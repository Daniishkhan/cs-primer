Design LLM Observability & Continuous Evaluation

An online marketplace runs a support agent that resolves two million conversations a day, and its team learns about breakages from angry social media posts. Design the traces, the sampling, the online evaluators, the dashboards and alerts, and the path from a spotted failure to a shipped fix, at a scale where nobody can read everything.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the design framework adapted to monitoring: clarify, estimate volume, decide what to record, draw the architecture, sample, evaluate online, build dashboards and alerts, close the loop to a fix, watch the watchers, weigh trade-offs, and summarise aloud. Every number is computed by a script on the page (seeded, so you get the same output) or labelled as an assumption. None of it describes a real company's system.

📐 The brief

The head of customer support at a marketplace says: "Our support agent answers questions about orders, refunds and returns, and can issue refunds up to a limit. We run two million conversations a day in fourteen countries. When a prompt change or a provider update makes it worse, we find out from social media or from the refund team a week later. Give us the monitoring we should have had from the start, and make sure what we find turns into fixes."

  • Each conversation averages about 3.2 turns. A turn runs retrieval over help articles, about 2.3 model calls and about 1.5 tool calls (order lookup, refund), plus guardrail checks. These are assumptions you can change in the scripts.
  • Prompts, retrieval settings and the model version change several times a week. The provider can change a model alias underneath you.
  • The company has a metrics and alerting stack for its other services, two people on a support-quality team who can read traces, and a pre-release eval suite from the earlier work (Studio 1).
  • Builds on tracing and observability for LLM systems, the improvement flywheel and cost, latency and quality, with the judges of chapter 3 and the statistics of chapter 4. It is the design-level partner of Lab 7. The foundations are metrics and percentiles, distributed tracing, SLOs and alerting.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes. The scripts need only the Python standard library.

2Estimate volume and cost

How many spans a second, how many gigabytes a day, and what does it cost to keep them? Compare "keep everything" with a tiered retention policy.

Compare with a worked answer

The estimate follows the habits of back-of-the-envelope estimation: name each assumption, then multiply. Content (prompts, retrieved passages, replies) is far larger than metadata (ids, timings, token counts, versions), and it is also the sensitive part, so the two are separated. All sizes and the price are assumptions.

  • The load is modest: about 580 spans a second on average and under 1,800 at three times the average. Ingestion is not the hard part; this is well within one tracing pipeline.
  • Content is 73 percent of the bytes. 222 GB a day with content against 60 GB without. Keeping every prompt for 30 days costs about $665 a month at the assumed price, which is cheap in money and expensive in risk: it is two million conversations of personal data.
  • Retention, not volume, drives the bill for metadata. Keeping the small records of every turn for 400 days (enough to compare year over year and to find when a regression began) costs about $2,400 a month, three and a half times more than keeping full content for 30 days.
  • The tiered policy costs about the same as metadata alone. Metadata for everything for 400 days, plus content only for flagged traces (about 3 percent) and a random half percent for 30 days, is about $2,400 a month and holds 224,000 content traces a day, which is more than any person can read. The point of tiering is privacy as much as money.
  • Reading capacity is the binding limit. 224,000 stored content traces a day against 200 human reads means the question is not whether to store, but how to rank. Stage 5 and stage 9 deal with that.

3What to record

Define the spans and the attributes on each. Which attributes would you require, which are optional, and which content must never be stored?

Compare with a worked answer

Use the shape from chapter 17: one trace per turn, a root span for the request, child spans for retrieval, each model call, each tool call and each guardrail check, using OpenTelemetry's GenAI conventions for the names and standard attributes (span names such as chat {model} and execute_tool {tool}, and attributes such as gen_ai.usage.input_tokens). Those conventions were still in Development status when I last read them (October 2026), so pin the version of the conventions and your instrumentation library, and keep your own attributes under your own prefix. The rest is a schema decision.

SpanAlways record (metadata)Content (opt-in, redacted, sampled)
Request (root)Conversation id (pseudonymised), turn index, country, route, channel, app.release, app.prompt_version, app.retrieval_config, total latency, outcome (resolved, escalated, abandoned)The member's message and the final reply
RetrievalIndex version, top k, document ids and scores, latency, number of passages usedQuery text, passage text

Guardrail checkWhich check, verdict, score, whether it was overriddenThe text that triggered it

  • Three attributes pay for the whole design. The release, the prompt version and the retrieval configuration on the root span turn "quality dropped on Tuesday" into "quality dropped on release 412 for the refund route". Without them you can see regressions and cannot attribute them.
  • Money-moving facts are metadata, not content. The refund amount, the order's age and whether a limit stopped it are numbers and flags; they can be aggregated and alerted on without storing a word the member wrote.
  • Redact at the collector, not in the application. The collector is the one place where rules change without a code release. Remove or tokenise names, emails, addresses, phone numbers and payment details before content is written (chapter 15's PII filters apply here, and so does the stricter reading in chapter 22). Keep variable text out of span names and metric labels, where redaction rules do not look.
  • Regional rules shape storage. Content for members in some countries stays in that region and is read there; evaluators that call an external model API are only enabled where the data-processing terms allow it.
  • Propagate context across every boundary. A tool that runs in another service must carry the trace context, or its spans become orphans (distributed tracing).

4Architecture

Draw the path from a user's turn to a fixed bug. Where are the traces redacted and sampled, where do the numbers come from, and where do people enter?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Counters before sampling. Volume, error rates, latency histograms, token counts and cost are derived from every span at the collector (or from metrics emitted by the application), so they are exact. Only the expensive-to-store content is sampled.
  • Evaluators are asynchronous. They read from the store and write scores back onto the traces; nothing in the user's request waits for a judge. Scores are attributes with an evaluator name and version, so the history survives a rubric change.
  • Do not put the model call on the path to the user's reply. The exporter buffers and sends in batches; if the collector is down the SDK drops spans after a bounded buffer rather than adding latency. The tracing overhead target is under 1 percent of the p95 request latency, checked in the performance tests (performance engineering).
  • Use what exists. The company's metrics and alerting stack carries the counters and pages the on-call. A tracing and evaluation tool in the Langfuse or Phoenix family adds trace views, datasets and online scorers; the toolbox card says what I did and did not run.

5Sampling: keep what you will read, weight what you count

Design the sampling policy. What do you keep with certainty, what is the baseline rate, and how do you avoid biased statistics from a deliberately biased sample?

Compare with a worked answer

Chapter 17 explains why head sampling is blind and tail sampling is not. The new problem at this scale is statistical: a sample that over-keeps failures overstates the failure rate. If you compute "refund route failures" from the stored traces you get a number several times too high. The fix is to record the probability with which each trace was kept and weight it by the inverse (the Horvitz-Thompson idea, in its simplest form). The script simulates one day scaled down to 200,000 turns: a cheap evaluator flags 70 percent of failing traces and 5 percent of good ones, flagged traces are always kept, and the rest are kept with probability 0.02.

"""Tail sampling keeps the interesting traces. Weight them back to get unbiased rates. Seeded; rates are assumptions."""
import random, math
random.seed(30)
N = 200_000                                   # one day, scaled down 10x from 2M turns
routes = {"order_status": (.50, .030), "refund": (.30, .110), "returns": (.20, .060)}   # (traffic share, true failure rate)
BASE = 0.02                                   # baseline keep-rate for unflagged traces

def flagged(fails):
    # a cheap evaluator flags 70% of failing traces and 5% of good ones
    return random.random() < (0.70 if fails else 0.05)

kept = []
truth = {r: [0, 0] for r in routes}
for _ in range(N):
    r = random.choices(list(routes), [v[0] for v in routes.values()])[0]
    fails = random.random() < routes[r][1]
    truth[r][0] += 1; truth[r][1] += fails
    flag = flagged(fails)
    p = 1.0 if flag else BASE                 # keep probability
    if random.random() < p:
        kept.append((r, fails, p))

print(f"traces {N:,}; kept {len(kept):,} ({len(kept)/N:.1%})")
print(f"\n{'route':14} {'true rate':>10} {'naive (kept only)':>18} {'weighted (1/p)':>15} {'95% interval':>20}")
for r in routes:
    rows = [(f, p) for rr, f, p in kept if rr == r]
    naive = sum(f for f, p in rows) / len(rows)
    num = sum(f / p for f, p in rows); den = sum(1 / p for f, p in rows)
    est = num / den
    # design-based variance of a ratio estimator when each trace is kept independently with probability p
    var = sum(((f - est) / p) ** 2 * (1 - p) for f, p in rows) / den ** 2
    se = math.sqrt(var)
    print(f"{r:14} {truth[r][1]/truth[r][0]:10.2%} {naive:18.2%} {est:15.2%} {est-1.96*se:9.2%} to {est+1.96*se:6.2%}")
traces 200,000; kept 21,421 (10.7%)

route           true rate  naive (kept only)  weighted (1/p)         95% interval
order_status        2.98%             23.31%           3.03%     2.60% to  3.46%
refund             10.89%             55.93%          11.28%    10.19% to 12.37%
returns             5.96%             39.56%           5.71%     4.84% to  6.59%
  • The naive rate is wildly wrong and the weighted rate is close. On the refund route, 56 percent of stored traces are failures (the keep rule loads them) against a true 10.9 percent. Weighting each kept trace by 1/p recovers 11.3 percent, with an interval that covers the truth on all three routes.
  • The estimator is unbiased whatever the flagger's quality; the flagger's quality sets how narrow the interval is. A flagger that finds failures makes the sample informative; a useless one still gives correct but wider numbers. That is why you can improve flagging without invalidating history, provided each trace stores the keep probability it was given.
  • Record the keep probability on the trace. Store app.sample_p beside the decision. If the policy changes, old and new traces still combine correctly, and anyone who analyses a dashboard export can weight it.
  • Keep with certainty: errors and timeouts, guardrail blocks and overrides, any money-moving tool call above a threshold, thumbs-down, repeated rephrasing, an escalation, the first N traces of every new release, and traces flagged by an evaluator. Baseline: a small random share of everything else (here 2 percent), because it is the only unbiased view of what the flags miss.
  • Per-slice floors. Make sure each route, country and release gets a minimum number of kept random traces a day, or a small slice will have too few to say anything.

6Online evaluators and their budget

Which evaluators run on which traces? Price them for 6.4 million turns a day, and say how many judged traces a slice needs for a useful daily number.

Compare with a worked answer

An online evaluator is a function that scores a production trace after the fact. The hierarchy from chapter 2 holds: deterministic checks first (run on every trace because they are free), then calibrated judges on a sample (chapter 3), then people. Candidates for this system: code checks that the reply's order id exists, that quoted amounts match the tool result, that links are on the allow-list, and that the language matches; a groundedness judge (is each claim supported by the retrieved articles); a resolution judge (did the reply answer the question or hand off properly); and a safety judge for policy-sensitive turns. The script prices them with assumed token counts and prices.

  • Code checks cost nothing and see everything in the class of failures they can express. The money-moving ones (refund amount against the tool result and the policy cap) become exact counters, which is the right foundation for an alert on wrongly issued refunds.
  • Judges at 1 percent cost about $0.88 per 1,000 conversations in total with the assumed prices, inside the 1 percent budget of the serving cost. Judging the safety evaluator on every flagged turn (7 percent) would cost ten times the cheaper design. A small, cheap model that flags candidates for the expensive judge is the usual pattern; its misses are measured on the random baseline.
  • The sample budget buys intervals, not certainty. A slice with 200 judged traces a day has a 95 percent half-width of 4.2 points; with 1,000 it is 1.9. With 64,000 judged turns spread over 20 routes and 3 live versions, a route carrying 3 percent of traffic gets about 640 per version per day (2.3 points) and about 0.9 points over a week. So small slices need weekly numbers or a higher sampling rate, and the dashboard shows the count next to every rate.
  • Every judge is calibrated and re-calibrated. Calibrate each judge against 200 or more adjudicated human labels before it feeds an alert (Studio 1, stage 7), and re-run the calibration set weekly. A silent change in the judge model looks exactly like a change in quality.
  • Scores are versioned. A rubric edit creates evaluator groundedness@v4; dashboards show v3 and v4 side by side for a week so the break in the series is visible, not mistaken for a regression.
  • Judge the judge's blind spots with a random sample read by a person. The analysts' 100 weekly random reads are also the audit of every automated signal.

8Alerts that fire for real drops

Write the alert rule for "the groundedness pass rate on a route fell". You have about 42 judged traces per route per hour and a baseline of 92 percent. Compare simple rules by false alarms and detection time.

Compare with a worked answer

An alert is a statistical test run many times. If you test every hour, even a harmless threshold fires often by chance (alerting and on-call makes the same point about noisy pages). The script simulates 30 days with no real change (to count false alarms) and then a real drop that starts at hour zero, for four rules: two fixed hourly thresholds, a 12-hour window whose upper confidence bound must fall below the baseline, and a CUSUM, which accumulates each hour's shortfall below the baseline minus a small allowance.

"""Four alert rules on an hourly online pass rate: false alarms in 30 clean days, and how fast a real drop is caught. Seeded."""
import random, math
random.seed(5)
BASE, N_HOUR = 0.92, 42                       # baseline pass rate; judged traces per route per hour

def hourly(p, hours):
    return [sum(random.random() < p for _ in range(N_HOUR)) for _ in range(hours)]   # passes per hour

def wilson_upper(k, n, z):
    p = k / n
    return ((p + z*z/(2*n)) + z*math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n))) / (1 + z*z/n)

def threshold(limit):                         # alert when any single hour falls below `limit`
    def rule(passes):
        for h, k in enumerate(passes):
            if k / N_HOUR < limit: return h
    return rule
def window(w, z):                             # alert when a w-hour window's upper bound is below the baseline
    def rule(passes):
        for h in range(w - 1, len(passes)):
            if wilson_upper(sum(passes[h-w+1:h+1]), w * N_HOUR, z) < BASE: return h
    return rule
def cusum(k_ref, limit):                      # accumulate shortfall below baseline, minus an allowance
    def rule(passes):
        s = 0.0
        for h, k in enumerate(passes):
            s = max(0.0, s + (BASE - k / N_HOUR) - k_ref)
            if s > limit: return h
    return rule

rules = {
    "A1 any hour below 85%":                 threshold(0.85),
    "A2 any hour below 78%":                 threshold(0.78),
    "B  12 h window, 99.9% bound below 92%": window(12, 3.3),
    "C  CUSUM (allowance 0.02, limit 0.30)": cusum(0.02, 0.30),
}
trials = 150
print(f"{'rule':40} {'false alarms in 30 clean days':>30}")
for name, rule in rules.items():
    total = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        passes = hourly(BASE, 30 * 24)
        i = 0
        while i < len(passes):                # restart the detector after each alarm
            h = rule(passes[i:])
            if h is None: break
            total += 1; i += h + 1
    print(f"{name:40} {total / trials:30.2f}")

print(f"\nhours until the alert after a real drop begins (median of those caught; runs of 72 h that never alerted)")
print(f"{'rule':40} {'drop 92 -> 89%':>18} {'drop 92 -> 86%':>18}")
for name, rule in rules.items():
    cells = []
    for p in (0.89, 0.86):
        delays, missed = [], 0
        for _ in range(trials):
            h = rule(hourly(p, 72))
            if h is None: missed += 1
            else: delays.append(h + 1)
        delays.sort()
        cells.append(f"{delays[len(delays)//2] if delays else 0:3d} h (missed {missed:3d})")
    print(f"{name:40} {cells[0]:>18} {cells[1]:>18}")

B  12 h window, 99.9% bound below 92%     19 h (missed   9)  12 h (missed   0)
C  CUSUM (allowance 0.02, limit 0.30)     21 h (missed   4)   8 h (missed   0)
  • A fixed hourly threshold forces a bad choice. At 85 percent it fires 34 times in a clean month (an on-call engineer would mute it); at 78 percent it is nearly quiet but takes 28 hours to see a drop to 89 percent and misses it in 53 of 150 runs.
  • The window rule and the CUSUM both give under 0.3 false alarms a month and catch a drop to 86 percent in about 8 to 12 hours every time. They are slower than the noisy rule on large drops and much better on moderate ones: a fall to 89 percent is caught in 19 to 21 hours, missed in 3 to 6 percent of 72-hour runs.
  • Detection delay is a budget you set. Raising the sample rate narrows the intervals and shortens the delay; lengthening the window lowers false alarms and slows detection. State the target in the SLO: "detect a 6-point drop within 12 hours with fewer than one false page a month".
  • Two tiers. Exact signals (a wrongly issued refund, a spike in tool errors, a provider outage) page immediately because they are exact counters with large samples. Judge-based drops open a ticket for the quality team in working hours unless the drop is large.
  • An alert carries its next step. The page includes the slice (route, release, prompt version), the interval, a link to ten example traces, and the last deploys. "What changed?" should be answered by the alert, not discovered by the on-call engineer.
  • Deploy markers and canaries. A drop that begins at a release time is a rollback candidate; the canary comparison (stage 7) catches it before most traffic is exposed (deployment strategies).

  • Ten times the traffic: ingestion scales with the collectors (a few thousand spans a second is still ordinary), the counters stay exact, and the sampling rate falls, not the number kept. The human read does not grow, so triage ranking and automatic clustering become the main work.
  • A second model provider: the same attributes on every model span (provider, requested and returned model), a model dimension on every dashboard, and a comparison of providers on the same routes through the canary view.   • No content storage for an enterprise tenant: metadata and code-check scores only, evaluated in the tenant's region or in a memory-only evaluator that discards content after scoring, with the loss of human reading explicitly accepted. Failure analysis then relies on synthetic reproductions and on customer-supplied examples.
  • A new agentic capability (the agent now edits orders): end-state checks against the order database become an online evaluator, as in agent evals, and the guardrails of the previous studio add approval and policy-decision spans to the trace.
  • Regulators ask what you monitor: the record of releases, evaluator versions, alerts and fixes is the evidence trail for post-market monitoring duties (governance).

Say your design aloud in five minutes to the head of support, then compare with the model summary.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The scarce resource is attention: two million conversations a day against about two hundred human reads. So the design is a selection machine plus exact counters. Every turn is a trace with spans for retrieval, each model call, each tool call and each guardrail, using the OpenTelemetry GenAI names, and the root span carries the release, prompt version and retrieval configuration, because those turn 'it got worse' into 'release 412 on the refund route'. About 580 spans a second is easy; the real cost is retention and privacy, so I keep metadata for all traces for 400 days and content only for flagged traces and a random half percent for 30 days, redacted at the collector. Counters are computed from everything before sampling, so rates are exact; stored traces are a tail sample that keeps every error, money-moving call, guardrail hit and thumbs-down plus a 2 percent baseline, with the keep probability stored on each trace so I can weight back to unbiased rates, because the naive rate on the refund route reads 56 percent against a true 11. Online, code checks run on everything: refund amount against the tool result is an exact counter; calibrated judges run on 1 percent, about 88 cents per thousand conversations, and a small model gates the expensive safety judge. Every rate shows its count and interval. Alerts are sequential tests, a window or a CUSUM, not hourly thresholds: under 0.3 false pages a month and a six-point drop caught in under twelve hours, while exact signals like a wrong refund page at once. Each alert carries the slice, the interval, ten traces and the last deploys. A failure then goes through a clock: contain within the hour, triage same day, label within two days, a regression case in three, a fix at the right layer in a week, gated and canaried; I measure repeat failures as the test of whether the loop works. And I watch the watchers: heartbeats with a planted failure, missing-data alerts, weekly judge recalibration and a scanner for personal data in stored content."

Notice the shape: attention is the constraint → versioned traces → tiered retention → counters before sampling → weighted tail sampling → budgeted evaluators → sequential alerts → a failure-to-fix clock → monitoring the monitors.

Key takeaways

  • At this scale the problem is selection. Humans can read hundreds of traces a day; the architecture decides which ones, and turns the rest into numbers.
  • Put the release, prompt and retrieval versions on every trace. They are what make a regression attributable.
  • Compute counters from all traces before sampling. Keep content for a flagged and random few, redacted at the collector, and weight sampled statistics by the stored keep probability.
  • Code checks run on everything; calibrated judges run on a priced sample; every rate shows its count and interval.
  • Alert with sequential tests, not hourly thresholds, and state the false-alarm rate and detection delay as targets.
  • Give the failure-to-fix path owners and clocks, and measure repeat failures to learn whether the loop works.
  • Monitor the monitoring: missing data, judge drift, redaction holes and the weekly read that quietly stops.
