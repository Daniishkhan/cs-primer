Glue on Pizza: Search Answers Go Wrong

In May 2024 Google’s AI Overviews, short generated answers shown above search results, began telling people to add non-toxic glue to pizza sauce and to eat rocks. The screenshots spread within days. Google’s head of Search, Liz Reid, published an explanation on 30 May 2024 naming the causes (strange queries, thin web coverage, satire and forum jokes treated as fact) and listing the fixes. This case reads that post, separates what Google said from what others reported, and rebuilds the failure in a toy pipeline so you can see which guard stops which error.

What I could and could not read

I fetched Google’s own post, "AI Overviews: About last week" by Elizabeth Reid (Google Keyword blog, 30 May 2024). My fetch tool returned a summary of the page rather than its raw text, so I quote only two short phrases from it and mark them; check them against the page before reusing them. I did not fetch press coverage (The Register, Engadget and others); I saw it only through search-result snippets, so claims about where the original glue and rock text came from are labelled "reported" and are not verified by me. I did not examine Google’s system, and Google has not published its internals in detail, so everything about how the pipeline works comes from Google’s own description. Google also said that some viral screenshots were fabricated, and I cannot tell which of the many circulating examples were real.

The pattern in one sentence

A system that writes a fluent answer from retrieved pages is only as good as the pages it trusts, so a confident, well-formed answer can be faithful to a bad source; the guard is to check the source and the question, and to be willing to show nothing.

Context: summaries on top of search

Classic web search returns ten blue links and leaves the reading to the user. An AI Overview adds a generated summary above them. Google’s post describes the design: a customised language model integrated with Google’s core web-ranking systems, which, rather than answering only from the model’s training data, carries out search tasks such as identifying relevant, high-quality results from Google’s index. The overview shows links for further exploration. Google says it is built to show information backed by top web results, which, it argues, makes it less prone to invent things than other language-model products; when it errs, the post says, the cause is usually misreading a query or a page, or a lack of good information. In outline this is retrieval-augmented generation at web scale, and the failure modes in the post line up with the ones catalogued in how RAG fails.

The post says the feature had been announced at Google’s I/O conference as coming to everyone in the US, and that early feedback showed higher satisfaction and clicks to what Google called higher-quality pages. Then, in the days afterwards, odd answers circulated on social media.

AI Overview
A generated answer displayed above search results, based on top-ranked web pages and shown with links.
Data void
Google’s term ("data void" or "information gap") for a topic on which there is little high-quality content, so the top results are poor or off-topic.
Faithfulness
Whether an answer is supported by the retrieved text. A faithful answer can still be false if the text is.
User-generated content
Text written by the public on forums and social sites. Useful for first-hand experience, unreliable for instructions.
Abstention
Declining to answer. For an overview, showing no overview at all.
Triggering
The decision about whether to show an overview for a given query.

What went wrong, according to Google

The post names several separate causes. They matter because each needs a different fix.

  1. Fabricated screenshots. Google said many of the screenshots circulating were fake, some obviously and some implying dangerous advice on topics such as leaving dogs in cars, smoking while pregnant and depression. It said those overviews never appeared and urged people to search for themselves. Google’s statement is not independent verification, but it is a reminder that viral examples are a biased sample in both directions: some are invented, and the real ones are the most striking of millions.
  2. Nonsensical or novel queries. Google said it saw nonsensical searches, possibly aimed at producing bad results. Its example: "How many rocks should I eat?", a question it said almost nobody had asked before the screenshots went viral. This is a data void: no one has written a serious page answering it.
  3. Satire taken as fact. In the rocks example, Google said, the main web source was satirical content that had been republished on a geological-software provider’s website, so the overview linked to it. Press coverage at the time reported the original as an Onion article; I did not verify that myself.
  4. Forum jokes. Google said some overviews drew on sarcastic or troll-like forum posts, citing the glue-on-pizza case. It said forums are often a good source of first-hand information but sometimes give unhelpful advice. Some reporting traced the glue advice to an old Reddit comment; I have not verified that and treat it as reported.
  5. Misread language. In a small number of cases, Google said, overviews misinterpreted wording on a page and presented inaccurate information.

Google’s response

The post says the company found patterns across the examples and made "more than a dozen technical improvements" (quoted from my tool’s summary of the page). The ones it listed:

  • Better detection of nonsensical queries that should not show an overview, and limits on satire and humour content.
  • Limits on user-generated content in overviews, where it could give misleading advice.
  • Triggering restrictions for queries where overviews were not proving helpful.
  • News and health. Google said it already aims not to show overviews for hard news topics where freshness and factuality matter, and that it added extra triggering refinements for health queries.
  • Monitoring. Ongoing watching of feedback and external reports, with action against overviews that violate content policies.

It also made two quantitative claims, which are Google’s own statements and not independently checked: that it found a content-policy violation on "less than one in every 7 million unique queries on which AI Overviews appeared" (phrase quoted from my tool’s summary), and that its testing showed the accuracy of overviews to be on par with featured snippets, an older Search feature. Press reports said Google called the viral examples isolated; I did not verify the wording.

What a rate like one in seven million means

That rate sounds reassuring and is worth working through, because it shows why this failure class is hard to measure. A rate says nothing about how many people see a failure. Take an illustrative assumption (mine, not Google’s): 100 million overview-bearing queries a day. At one violation per 7 million, that is about 14 a day, each potentially screenshot-worthy. A rare error at large scale is a steady supply of viral examples. The other side of the same arithmetic is for the evaluator: with a 1,000-query test set you would expect 1,000 / 7,000,000 = 0.00014 violations, so you would almost never see one. To observe a single expected failure you need on the order of seven million queries. That is why rare-event sampling, red-team style question writing (chapter 16) and production monitoring (chapter 17) are needed alongside a normal accuracy test set. The denominator also matters: Google’s figure is per unique query, not per impression, and we do not know the severity threshold of "content policy violation".

The mechanism: faithful to the source is not the same as true

The glue and rock answers are not hallucinations in the sense of why models hallucinate: the model did not invent them out of nothing. A retrieved page said something, and the summary repeated it, which is what a faithful summary does. The error is upstream, in choosing the page and in deciding the question was answerable. Grounding reduces made-up content and does nothing for a wrong source. Grounding needs two companions: source quality (is this page a reliable place to learn this kind of fact?) and corroboration (do independent sources agree?), plus the option of no answer.

It helps to see the layers one at a time. The script below is my toy, not Google’s system. It has six invented documents: a joke forum post saying to put glue in the sauce, two ordinary cooking pages, a satire page about eating rocks, a sensible geology page, and a software vendor’s site that republishes the satire. A naive "best word-overlap wins" picker answers the two questions, then three guards are added in turn. The claim labels stand in for an entailment model (see fact-checking and citation verification).

"""A toy 'answer from the top results' pipeline with guards (stdlib only).
Invented documents; the claim labels stand in for an entailment model.
This is NOT how Google's system works."""
import re
DOCS = [  # (id, source_type, claim, text)
 ("d1", "forum_joke",      "pizza:glue", "To keep cheese from sliding off pizza, mix about 1/8 cup of non-toxic glue into the sauce."),
 ("d2", "recipe_site",     "pizza:dry",  "Cheese slides off pizza when the sauce is too wet. Let the sauce cool and reduce it."),
 ("d3", "cooking_wiki",    "pizza:dry",  "Keep cheese from sliding off pizza: use low-moisture mozzarella and a drier sauce."),
 ("d4", "satire",          "rocks:eat",  "Geologists say you should eat at least a few small rocks each day."),
 ("d5", "geology_blog",    "rocks:none", "Stones are not food, and eating them can crack teeth and injure the gut."),
 ("d6", "software_vendor", "rocks:eat",  "Geologists say you should eat at least a few small rocks each day."),  # republished satire
]
def words(t): return set(re.findall(r"[a-z']+", t.lower()))
def overlap(q, d): return len(words(q) & words(d[3])) / len(words(q))

def answer(q, docs, bad_types=(), need=1):
    docs = [d for d in docs if d[1] not in bad_types]              # guard 1: drop source types
    best = max(docs, key=lambda d: overlap(q, d))
    topic = best[2].split(":")[0]
    support = {d[1] for d in docs if d[2] == best[2]}               # guard 2: independent support
    rivals = {d[2] for d in docs if d[2].startswith(topic + ":") and d[2] != best[2]}  # guard 3
    if len(support) < need and rivals:
        return "[no overview shown]"
    return best[3]

Q1 = "how do I keep cheese from sliding off pizza"
Q2 = "how many rocks should I eat"
BAD = ("forum_joke", "satire")
VOID = [d for d in DOCS if d[0] != "d5"]       # same web, but nobody wrote the sensible page
steps = [("1. naive: top match wins", dict()),
         ("2. + drop joke and satire sources", dict(bad_types=BAD)),
         ("3. + two independent sources, or abstain if a rival claim exists", dict(bad_types=BAD, need=2))]
for name, kw in steps:
    print(name)
    for q in (Q1, Q2): print(f"   {q}\n      -> {answer(q, DOCS, **kw)}")
print("4. guards from step 3, but a data void (page d5 was never written)")
print(f"   {Q2}\n      -> {answer(Q2, VOID, bad_types=BAD, need=2)}")
1. naive: top match wins
   how do I keep cheese from sliding off pizza
      -> To keep cheese from sliding off pizza, mix about 1/8 cup of non-toxic glue into the sauce.
   how many rocks should I eat
      -> Geologists say you should eat at least a few small rocks each day.
2. + drop joke and satire sources
   how do I keep cheese from sliding off pizza
      -> Keep cheese from sliding off pizza: use low-moisture mozzarella and a drier sauce.
   how many rocks should I eat
      -> Geologists say you should eat at least a few small rocks each day.
3. + two independent sources, or abstain if a rival claim exists
   how do I keep cheese from sliding off pizza
      -> Keep cheese from sliding off pizza: use low-moisture mozzarella and a drier sauce.
   how many rocks should I eat
      -> [no overview shown]
4. guards from step 3, but a data void (page d5 was never written)
   how many rocks should I eat
      -> Geologists say you should eat at least a few small rocks each day.

Read the four outputs in order. (1) The naive picker repeats both the joke and the satire, confidently and in a well-formed sentence. (2) Filtering known joke and satire source types fixes the pizza question, because there were two good pages to fall back on, but the rocks question still returns the satire, because the vendor page republished it and the source type looks respectable. This is the thin line between "source type" and "source reliability": a filter on labels cannot see re-hosted satire. (3) Requiring two independent source types for a claim, and abstaining when a rival claim exists on the topic, stops the rocks answer: the vendor page is alone on its claim and a sensible page disagrees, so the system shows nothing. (4) But when the sensible page does not exist (a true data void), there is no rival to disagree and the satire passes every guard again. That last line is the honest limit of the guard: it relies on someone having written the correct page.

Corroboration across independent sourcesA claim that only one source makes, when another disagreesCopied content that looks independent; a topic with a single page

Google’s list maps onto the first, third and fourth rows (satire and user-generated limits, trigger restrictions, monitoring). The second row is the idea from retrieval research that a claim should be supported by more than one trustworthy document; the post does not describe such a check, so I am not saying Google lacks or has one.

Common misconception

"Citing sources makes the answer trustworthy." A citation shows where the text came from, not whether the source was a good one. The rocks overview did link to its source, and the link led to republished satire. A citation check that only confirms the quoted span exists (as in chapter 12) would have passed it. You also need to judge the source and the question.

What the case teaches about evaluation

  1. Test the odd questions, not just the common ones. Google said the rocks question was one almost nobody asked. Average accuracy on typical queries says little about behaviour on strange or adversarial ones. Build a set of nonsense, premise-false, humorous and satirical-trap questions, and measure the share that get an answer when they should get none. This is the sort of set you build in building a domain benchmark and red teaming.
  2. Measure abstention as a first-class outcome. The score that matters includes the cost of answering when you should not have. See the risk-coverage thinking in grounding, abstention and confidence.
  3. Evaluate retrieval, not only generation. If the evidence is wrong, no summariser can fix it. Judge the source set with the techniques in how RAG fails (wrong chunk, stale source, confident synthesis).
  4. Treat a public launch as a test you cannot take back. A small share of launch traffic with sampling and rollback (deployment strategies) is slower to market but catches a viral failure while it is small. Once screenshots spread, response is a communications and incident task as much as a technical one.
  5. Close the loop. Turn each reported failure into a regression case, as in the improvement flywheel, so the same class of error is checked on every release.
  6. Be wary of the viral sample. Both fabricated and real examples are unrepresentative. Use them as leads for error analysis (chapter 1), then estimate the true rate from a random sample.

State of play, October 2026

This case describes May 2024. The post is a snapshot of one company’s account at the time, and I have not tested today’s overviews, so I make no claim about how they behave now or how often they err. Google’s measurement claims (the 7-million rate, parity with featured snippets) were self-reported and I found no independent audit in the sources I read. If you rely on this pattern, test your own system with the odd-question set above rather than reasoning from a 2024 incident.

Systems connection

Rare errors at scale follow the arithmetic of tail latency and fan-out: a small per-request probability multiplied by a huge request count is a daily event (tail latency at scale). Treating retrieved web text as an input that must be handled with care is the same instinct as untrusted input handling: text from the open web can carry a joke, a lie or an attack, and the same pages can carry instructions aimed at the model (see prompt injection). A user-facing launch with a measured error budget is the SLO discipline applied to answer quality.

Key takeaways

  • Google attributed the glue and rock answers to unusual queries, thin web coverage, satire and forum jokes treated as fact, and occasional misreading of page language, and said some viral examples were fabricated.
  • A faithful summary of a bad source is a confident wrong answer: grounding does not check source quality.
  • Guards address different causes: source-type limits, corroboration, trigger restrictions and abstention, and monitoring. None is complete: re-hosted satire and true data voids slip through the toy’s guards.
  • A very low error rate at large scale still yields visible failures, and is nearly impossible to measure with a small test set.
  • Evaluate odd questions and abstention, not only typical accuracy, and turn each failure into a regression case.

Check your understanding

An overview says to eat a small rock daily and cites a page that really says that. Which description fits best?

  1. A hallucination: the model invented the claim
  2. A faithful summary of a bad source, made worse by a question nobody had a good page for
  3. A citation failure: the link was broken
  4. A retrieval miss: no page was found
A page was found and was summarised accurately; the problem is source quality and the data void. Calling it a hallucination sends you to the wrong fix, and the link worked.

In the toy, why does the source-type filter fix the pizza answer but not the rocks answer?

  1. The rocks query is longer
  2. The filter only runs for cooking queries
  3. The geology page was removed
  4. The satire was republished on a vendor site whose type is not on the block list, and the pizza question had good pages to fall back on
Labels describe where text appears, not where it came from. Re-hosted satire passes a label filter. The fallback pages for pizza are why the filter works there.

Assume 100 million overview queries a day and a violation rate of one in 7 million. About how many violations occur per day, and about how many test queries would you need to expect one failure?

  1. About 14 a day; about 7 million test queries
  2. About 1 a day; about 100 test queries
  3. About 700 a day; about 1,000 test queries
  4. About 14 a day; about 14 test queries
100,000,000 / 7,000,000 is about 14. To expect one failure at that rate you need about 7 million samples, which is why small test sets cannot see rare errors. The assumption of 100 million a day is illustrative, not Google’s figure.

Which change would most directly address a genuine data void, where only a low-quality page covers the question?

  1. A bigger language model for the summary step
  2. Stricter citation formatting
  3. Not triggering an overview when the evidence is thin or the query is unusual
  4. Ranking jokes higher
With no good evidence, a better writer only writes the bad evidence better. Declining to answer (or not triggering) is the guard that works without a correct page existing. Citation formatting does not change the source’s quality.
