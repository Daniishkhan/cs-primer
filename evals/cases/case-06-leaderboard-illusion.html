The Leaderboard Illusion

Chatbot Arena ranks language models by asking real people which of two anonymous answers they prefer. In April 2025 a team led by researchers at Cohere Labs published "The Leaderboard Illusion", a study of how the Arena actually ran: some providers could test many private variants and publish only the best, proprietary models were shown to voters far more often, and many models were quietly retired. The Arena’s organisers published a point-by-point reply. This case reads both, and shows with a short simulation why the mathematics of "publish the best of N" is not in dispute even though its size is.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the full HTML version of the paper (arXiv:2504.20879, v2 of 12 May 2025) and the organisers’ reply, "Our Response to ‘The Leaderboard Illusion’ Writeup’" (published 9 May 2025, last updated 13 June 2025, now hosted at arena.ai). I did not read the Arena’s original 2024 policy post (linked from the paper), the Arena’s own research paper (arXiv 2403.04132), or the raw data, and I could not independently verify either side’s counts. The paper’s numbers are the authors’ estimates from data they scraped and from the Arena’s released datasets; the reply’s numbers are the organisers’ claims. I report both and mark whose is whose. One more disclosure the paper makes and I repeat: several authors work at Cohere, which submits models to the Arena, and the authors ran experiments on the Arena with Cohere models.

The pattern in one sentence

A ranking built from noisy measurements is biased upwards if some contestants may measure several versions and keep only the best score, and it is skewed further if some contestants get more measurements than others, so a public leaderboard needs published rules for who can test what, how often, and what must be disclosed.

Context: why the Arena mattered

Static benchmarks (multiple-choice exams, coding problems) were saturating and often did not reflect how people use chatbots, a story told in contamination, saturation and decay. Chatbot Arena, created in 2023, took a different route: anyone can submit a prompt, two anonymous models answer, and the user votes for the better answer or declares a tie. A rating system turns the votes into a ranking. By 2025 the paper’s authors describe it as the de facto standard for comparing frontier models, with influence over media, industry and research. That influence is why its internal rules matter: when a number steers purchasing, press and research agendas, anything that biases it has consequences, an instance of Goodhart’s law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"), which the paper itself quotes.

Arena score
The leaderboard rating, a normalised Bradley-Terry model fitted to pairwise votes. It estimates a skill for each model so that the chance a model wins a battle depends on the difference between the two skills.
Battle
One comparison: one prompt, two anonymous answers, one vote.
Private testing
Running a model in the Arena under an anonymous alias before release, so voters rate it without knowing whose it is, and the result is not shown publicly.
Selective disclosure (retraction)
Publishing the score of only some privately tested variants and withdrawing the rest.
Best-of-N
Test N variants, keep the highest score. The kept score is the maximum of N noisy measurements.
Sampling rate
How often a model is chosen to appear in a battle. A higher rate means more votes, a tighter error bar, and more prompts that the model’s provider can see.
Silent deprecation
Removing a model from active battles without listing it as retired.

The mechanism: how an Arena rating is built

A rating from pairwise votes needs far fewer comparisons than all-against-all. The Bradley-Terry model (Bradley and Terry, 1952) gives each model a skill and says the probability that model A beats B is determined by the gap between their skills. Fitting that to the votes yields one number per model, plus a confidence interval that shrinks as votes accumulate (the statistics behind it are in statistics for evals). The paper’s analysis covers about 2 million battles, 243 models and 42 providers, from January 2024 to April 2025.

The model assumes the sampled models are a fair draw: each model’s votes are an unbiased measurement of its skill. The paper’s argument is that three practices break that assumption, or distort who benefits.

Practice 1: private testing with retraction

The paper reports that, though not an officially stated policy in the authors’ reading, providers could test several private model variants at once and decide afterwards what to publish. Using scraped battles from January to March 2025 and the models’ self-identification as a clue to who owned each anonymous alias (an approximate method the authors flag as such), they counted active private variants: Meta 27, Google 10, with Amazon also high; Reka had one. Meta’s 27 came in the lead-up to its Llama 4 release; the authors add that counting Meta’s private variants on the vision leaderboard brings the total to 43. They observed no private models from academic labs in the period. They also point to rapid changes at the top of the board, for example one provider holding first place on 14 November 2024, another on 20 November, the first again on 21 November, and argue such turnover is more plausible with parallel variants than with one new model each time. That is circumstantial reasoning, and the reply below disputes the framing.

The statistical point is simple. Suppose each variant has the same true skill and each score is that skill plus noise from a finite number of votes. The maximum of N noisy scores is higher, on average, than one score; with N≥2 and any noise at all, the expected maximum strictly exceeds the expected single score. The paper shows this in a simulation (a family with a latent skill of 1200: testing 20 variants raised the expected best score by about 50 points over no private testing, with their assumed variance), and in a real experiment. The authors submitted two identical checkpoints of their own model, Aya-Vision-8B, in March 2025 and got Arena scores of 1052 (±21/22) and 1069 (±19/23), with four other models ranked between them. They also tested two different Aya-Vision-32B checkpoints: 1097 (±29/25) and 1059 (±18/23), with nine models between them.

Practice 2: unequal data

The second set of findings concerns who gets votes. Every battle shows two model answers to a real user prompt, so a model that appears in more battles collects more votes and its provider can see more of the prompts sent to it through its API. The paper estimates, from battle counts, that Google and OpenAI each received about a fifth of all Arena data (19.2% and 20.4%), against a combined 29.7% for 83 open-weight models, and 8.9% for 41 fully open-source models. It reports that proprietary models together received 61.4% of data, and that monthly shares for proprietary models ranged from 54.3% to 70.1%. It found maximum daily sampling rates of up to 34% for OpenAI or Google models, about ten times the maximum seen for providers like Allen AI.

Practice 3: silent removal

Of the 243 public models the authors tracked, they say 205 had been silently deprecated, against 47 officially listed as deprecated in the Arena’s open-source backend. Of the silently deprecated models, 64% were open-weight or fully open-source. Deprecation matters for the mathematics, too: Bradley-Terry works because models are compared against a connected web of opponents, and dropping models or leaving a model with a thin, old battle history makes its rating less reliable.

Does the data help? The overfitting experiment

The paper also asks whether extra Arena data actually helps a model do better on the Arena. In a controlled fine-tuning experiment with a fixed training budget, they varied the share of Arena-style data from 0% to 70% and measured win-rate on ArenaHard, a test set drawn from the Arena distribution: it more than doubled, from 23.5% to 49.9%, and the abstract cites relative gains of up to 112%. They report limited benefit on other tasks, and conclude that access to Arena data can buy Arena-specific skill, not general quality. They call their estimate conservative, since larger providers may hold 5 to 10 times more data.

The reply

The Arena team’s reply (9 May 2025) accepts some points and disputes others. It states:

  • Planned changes. The policy on pre-release testing would be updated to state explicitly that all providers may test multiple variants before release; retired models would be explicitly marked; and if more than 10 models are pre-release tested in parallel, scores would be labelled "provisional" until 2,000 additional fresh votes arrive after release.
  • The size of the best-of-N effect. The reply says the paper’s curve is a simulation with Gaussians centred on 1200 and an arbitrary variance, so it "does not reflect Chatbot Arena data"; the organisers put the real effect at "around +11 Elo after 50 tests and 3000 votes", shrinking toward zero as fresh data accumulates, because selection bias fades. They do not dispute that selection bias exists.
  • Identical checkpoints. The reply says the paper omitted the confidence intervals and gives 1069 (±27) and 1054 (±18/22), which it calls overlapping and within noise. The paper, for its part, reports 1052 and 1069 with its own intervals; the figures differ between the two documents and I cannot say why.
  • Open versus proprietary. The reply says the paper counted only some open models and reports official stats showing "Open Models at 40.9%" (27 April 2025), against the writeup’s 8.8% for open-source models. The two sides appear to be using different definitions of "open".
  • "Unstated" policy. The reply says its pre-release testing policy has been public since 1 March 2024, that any provider can submit as many variants as capacity allows, and that larger labs submit more because they build more models. It says it helped Cohere evaluate nine pre-release models since January 2025, which it describes as 2 to 3 times more than xAI or OpenAI.
  • The 112% figure. The reply says the experiment used ArenaHard, a static benchmark of 500 data points judged by a model, with no human votes, so it does not show what happens on the live Arena.
  • Sampling. The reply says the best models, regardless of provider, are up-sampled to improve users’ experience, while models from other providers are still sampled.

Read together, the two documents agree on more than the tone suggests. Both accept that selecting the best of several private runs inflates a score, and the organisers’ planned "provisional" label for large parallel tests is a remedy for exactly that. They disagree on size, on how to count open models, on whether the policy was visible enough, and on what the fine-tuning result shows. Those are the questions to ask of any leaderboard, which is why this case sits beside reading leaderboards critically.

A toy you can run

This is my own simulation, not the paper’s or the Arena’s. Each of N variants has exactly the same true skill: against a reference opponent of equal skill, each battle is a coin flip. Each variant gets 3,000 votes (the vote count mentioned in the organisers’ reply), and its measured rating gap is the Elo-style value 400·log10(p/(1−p)) from its win fraction p. We publish the best-scoring variant, then re-measure that variant on 3,000 fresh votes. The simulation repeats this 2,000 times per N.

"""Best-of-N selection bias on an arena-style rating (stdlib only).
Every variant has the SAME true skill, so any lift is pure selection."""
import random, math, statistics as st

def elo_estimate(votes, rng):
    """Rating gap vs an opponent of equal skill, from `votes` coin-flip battles."""
    wins = sum(rng.random() < 0.5 for _ in range(votes))
    p = min(max(wins / votes, 1e-6), 1 - 1e-6)
    return 400 * math.log10(p / (1 - p))          # Elo points relative to opponent

def trial(n_variants, votes, rng):
    scores = [elo_estimate(votes, rng) for _ in range(n_variants)]
    best = max(scores)
    fresh = elo_estimate(votes, rng)               # re-measure the winner on new votes
    return best, fresh

rng = random.Random(2025)
VOTES, TRIALS = 3000, 2000
print(f"true skill identical; {VOTES} votes per variant; {TRIALS} simulated releases")
sd = st.pstdev([elo_estimate(VOTES, rng) for _ in range(4000)])
print(f"sd of one variant's score: {sd:.1f} Elo")
print(" N  published(avg)  fresh re-test(avg)")
for n in (1, 3, 10, 27, 50):
    res = [trial(n, VOTES, rng) for _ in range(TRIALS)]
    pub = st.mean(b for b, _ in res); fr = st.mean(f for _, f in res)
    print(f"{n:2d}  {pub:+9.1f}      {fr:+9.1f}")

sd of one variant's score: 6.5 Elo
 N  published(avg)  fresh re-test(avg)
 1       +0.2           -0.2
 3       +5.5           +0.2
10       +9.8           -0.1
27      +12.7           -0.2
50      +14.3           +0.1

What to read from it. A single variant’s score has a spread of about 6.5 Elo at 3,000 votes. Publishing the best of 10 identical variants adds about 10 points; of 27, about 13; of 50, about 14. My numbers are the same order as the organisers’ figure (+11 after 50 tests), and far below the paper’s simulated 50 points at 20 variants. That is no contradiction: the paper’s simulation assumed a different spread of true skills and noise, and the size of best-of-N depends strongly on both. Two things hold in every setting: the lift grows with N but more slowly each time (it is roughly the expected maximum of N normal draws), and a re-measurement on fresh data shows no lift. The real question for the Arena is not whether this bias exists but how large it is under its actual vote counts, how different its variants really are, and whether the gap between two close models is bigger than the bias. Where the gap between neighbours is only a few Elo, a 10-point bias reorders them.

Try it yourself

Change VOTES from 3000 to 300 and to 30000 and re-run. Predict first: does the published lift at N=27 grow or shrink with more votes, and by roughly what factor? (Hint: the noise falls with the square root of the number of votes.) Then explain why a leaderboard that collects more votes per model is more resistant to best-of-N.

Common misconception

"If every provider is allowed to test privately, the playing field is level." Permission is not the same as capacity or knowledge. The reply says any provider may submit as many variants as capacity allows, but the paper’s point is that using that room needs engineering effort and awareness, and the data it brings (votes and prompts) goes to whoever uses it most. A rule that is formally open can still produce unequal outcomes, and bias from selection exists even when everyone has the same option.

State of play, October 2026

The Arena organisation now publishes at arena.ai (the old news.lmarena.ai address redirected there when I fetched it). The reply’s commitments (a clearer pre-release policy, explicit retired-model marking, "provisional" labels for parallel tests of more than 10 models) were stated in May 2025 and updated in June 2025. I did not check whether each has since been implemented, nor read any later independent audit. Leaderboard positions change constantly and I deliberately state none.

Lessons

  1. Selection is a form of tuning on the test. Any process that picks the winner by the benchmark score carries a winner’s-curse bias, whether it is the variants of a model, the prompts of a team, or the checkpoints of a training run. Re-measure the winner on fresh data, as in chapter 7 and the holdout discipline of chapter 2.
  2. Ask what is disclosed. For any ranking: how many versions were tried, who could try, which scores were withdrawn, how many votes or samples each entrant got, and whether the entrant could see the test prompts. The paper’s five recommendations map to these questions: no retraction, public limits on private variants, equal removal across licence types, fair sampling and transparency about removed models.
  3. Error bars decide whether ranks mean anything. A gap smaller than the interval is not a ranking (statistics for evals). Look at intervals, not positions.
  4. Human preference is a real signal, and also a target. People favour some styles; a model trained for the Arena’s votes can score higher without being more useful elsewhere. See reward models and reward hacking for the same dynamic in training.
  5. Trust your own eval more than a public one. A leaderboard can shortlist; the decision should come from a test on your tasks, as in building a domain benchmark.
  6. Be fair to organisers. The paper stresses that the Arena began as an academic project and that the organisers carry a large load, and the reply shows an organisation responding in public with changes. Both are what good evaluation governance looks like in practice: findings, a reply, and amended rules.

Systems connection

Selecting the best of N noisy measurements is the same effect as reporting the best of several benchmark runs in load testing, or the fastest of N timing runs in a performance claim. Unequal sampling is a fairness and capacity question like the allocation of shared resources in multi-tenancy. And "who sees whose data" is a data-protection question of the kind in data protection and privacy.

Key takeaways

  • Publishing the best of N noisy scores biases a rating upwards, even when all variants are equal; fresh data removes the bias.
  • The paper reports undisclosed private testing (Meta 27 variants before Llama 4), uneven sampling (Google 19.2%, OpenAI 20.4% of data, 83 open-weight models 29.7% combined) and silent deprecation (205 of 243 models).
  • The organisers dispute the size of the effects and several counts, but say they will clarify policy and add "provisional" labels.
  • The direction of the bias is not in dispute; its size, and what counts as "open", are.
  • When you use a leaderboard, ask how many versions were tried, what was disclosed, and how wide the error bars are.

Check your understanding

A provider privately tests 10 variants with identical true skill and publishes the highest score. What do you expect on re-measuring the published variant on fresh votes?

  1. The same score, because the variant has not changed
  2. A higher score, because more votes sharpen the estimate upward
  3. A score near the true skill, lower than the published one on average
  4. A random score unrelated to the first
The published score is the maximum of ten noisy estimates, so it contains the luckiest noise. Fresh votes have independent noise, so on average the score returns to the true value. It is not unrelated (the true skill is shared), which rules out the last option.

Which statement do both the paper and the organisers’ reply support?

  1. Selecting the best of several privately tested variants introduces upward selection bias
  2. Open models received only 8.8% of Arena data
  3. Private testing gives a lift of more than 100 points in practice
  4. The ArenaHard result proves the live Arena can be overfitted by 112%
The reply says the bias exists but is smaller and fades with fresh votes. The other three statements are each contested by the reply (open-model share, size of the lift, relevance of the 112% result).

Why do the authors argue that the 112% ArenaHard gain suggests overfitting rather than general improvement?

  1. Because ArenaHard questions are public
  2. Because the fine-tuned model was larger
  3. Because the human voters were biased
  4. Because the gain was large on a test set from the Arena distribution but limited on other tasks
The paper reports large gains on ArenaHard and limited benefits on other tasks of interest. Specific gains without general ones are the signature of fitting a distribution. The reply separately says ArenaHard is a static, model-judged set and not the live Arena.

You see two models separated by 6 Elo on a leaderboard where one entrant tested 27 variants privately. What is the most defensible conclusion?

  1. The higher model is better by 6 points
  2. The difference is smaller than plausible selection bias and noise, so treat them as tied unless you have fresh evidence
  3. The lower model must have been retracted
  4. Rankings are meaningless, so ignore the leaderboard entirely
With a single-score spread near 6 Elo and tens of points possible from selection under some assumptions, a 6-point gap is within the noise. The last option overcorrects: leaderboards remain useful for large gaps and for shortlisting.
