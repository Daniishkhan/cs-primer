Fake Citations in Court

In 2023 two New York lawyers filed a brief that cited six court decisions which did not exist. A chatbot had written them. The judge's opinion on sanctions is one of the most complete public records we have of a generative-AI failure in a professional workflow, because it reproduces the prompts, the chatbot's answers and the lawyers' shifting explanations, and states what the court thought the real mistake was.

What I could and could not read

Fetched and read in full: Judge P. Kevin Castel's 43-page "Opinion and Order on Sanctions" in Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (S.D.N.Y., 22 June 2023), docket entry 54, downloaded as a PDF from CourtListener and read as extracted text; and the court's separate seven-page "Opinion and Order" of the same day granting Avianca's motion to dismiss (docket entry 55). Read in part or through coverage: LawNext's report on the sanctions (fetched and read); Wikipedia's entry on the case (fetched; used only for citation details and a pointer to later cases). Not read: the hearing transcript, the lawyers' declarations and the ChatGPT screenshots themselves (the opinion quotes from them and annexes images that did not extract as text), and any later proceedings or ethics opinions. Quotations below are from the opinion unless stated; "para" means a numbered finding of fact in docket entry 54.

The pattern in one sentence

A generator that produces fluent, specific and wrong output on request will also confirm that output if you ask it to check itself, so verification must come from an independent source and from a person who owns the result.

Context

Roberto Mata sued Avianca, an airline, saying a metal serving cart struck his left knee on a flight from El Salvador to New York on 27 August 2019. The case was filed in New York state court on 2 February 2022 and removed to federal court on 22 February 2022 (para 1; ECF 55). Avianca moved to dismiss on 13 January 2023 on a narrow legal ground: international flights are governed by the Montreal Convention, which extinguishes the right to damages if no action is brought within two years, and Mata's second suit came after that. Mata's lawyers needed an argument that the clock had stopped, and their theory was that Avianca's bankruptcy, which triggered an automatic stay of lawsuits, had tolled (paused) the two years.

Citation
A standard reference to a court decision: names of the parties, the volume and page of a printed reporter, the court and year. For example, "925 F.3d 1339 (11th Cir. 2019)" says volume 925 of the Federal Reporter, third series, page 1339.
Reporter
A published series of court decisions, searchable in databases such as Westlaw and LexisNexis.
Rule 11
The federal rule that makes a lawyer certify, by signing, that a filing's legal contentions are warranted by existing law; the court may sanction a violation.
Order to show cause
A court order telling a party to explain why it should not be sanctioned or otherwise penalised.
Subjective bad faith
The finding the court needed for some sanctions: that a person knew, or consciously avoided knowing, that what they were doing was improper.
Fabricated citation
A reference to a decision that does not exist, or that exists but does not say what is claimed; the course's word for the general problem is hallucination (chapter 10).

The associate who did the work, Steven A. Schwartz, practised in state court and was not admitted to the federal court, so his colleague Peter LoDuca signed and filed the papers. Mr. Schwartz's firm, Levidow, Levidow & Oberman, used the research service Fastcase, which had limited access to federal cases, and did not subscribe to Westlaw or LexisNexis (para 37). He testified that he "heard about this new site which I assumed -- I falsely assumed was like a super search engine called ChatGPT" (para 37, quoting the transcript). He had not used it before.

The mechanism: how the fake cases were produced

The opinion annexes Mr. Schwartz's chat history (para 39). His first prompt was "argue that the statute of limitations is tolled by bankruptcy of defendant pursuant to montreal convention." ChatGPT answered with general descriptions and said the period was tolled, without citing cases. The court adds in a footnote that courts have generally held that the Convention aims for uniform limitation periods, so the claim itself was shaky. Mr. Schwartz then asked it to "provide case law in support", "show me specific holdings in federal cases where the statute of limitations was tolled due to bankruptcy of the airline", "show me more cases" and "give me some cases where the Montreal Convention allowed tolling". In the court's words: "When directed to 'provide case law', 'show me specific holdings', 'show me more cases' and 'give me some cases', the chatbot complied by making them up." (para 39). ChatGPT produced summaries and excerpts, not full opinions (paras 38, 40), and those excerpts are what the brief quoted.

My reading, not the court's: a prompt that asks for support for a conclusion, to a system that produces likely continuations, invites made-up support. The more specific the request, the more specific the fabrication. The hallucination chapter explains why a model that was trained and scored to always answer will supply an answer-shaped thing when it has none, and the grounding and abstention chapter covers the design choice that would have mattered here: a research tool that answers only from retrieved documents and says "not found" otherwise.

What the fakes looked like

The court examined three of the supposed federal decisions in detail (paras 24 to 34). "Varghese v. China Southern Airlines" was presented as an Eleventh Circuit panel decision. The opinion records that the Eleventh Circuit's clerk confirmed it is not an authentic ruling; that its docket number belongs to a different case; that its citation is associated with a different case (J.D. v. Azar); and that it names a judge from another circuit. The decision "shows stylistic and reasoning flaws that do not generally appear in decisions issued by United States Courts of Appeals. Its legal analysis is gibberish." (para 27). It mixes a wrongful-death claim with an overbooked-flight claim, and it ends without a conclusion.

Its internal citations were also unreliable (para 29). A cited "Holliday v. Atl. Capital Corp., 738 F.2d 1153" does not exist; that citation is a case called Gibbs v. Maxwell House. A real Supreme Court case with the same name as the cited "Zicherman" exists, but at a different citation and for a different point. Other cited cases do exist, but "do not contain the language quoted or support the propositions for which they are offered". Another fake, "Miller v. United Airlines", lists as a defence lawyer the name of a later U.S. Attorney General and describes a bankruptcy by United Airlines in 1992, when the court found no such proceeding. The court noted that the fakes had "some traits that are superficially consistent with actual judicial decisions" (para 24).

The verification that was not

Two parts of the record bear directly on verification. First, Mr. Schwartz knew that free websites let you enter a citation and see the decision. He testified that he entered "Varghese" and "couldn't find it", and that he cited it anyway "operating under the false assumption and disbelief that this website could produce completely fabricated cases" (para 12). Second, he later asked ChatGPT itself: "Is Varghese a real case" and "Are the other cases you provided fake". ChatGPT replied that the authorities were real and could be found through Westlaw, LexisNexis and the Federal Reporter (paras 45, 47), and, in his June declaration, that Varghese "does indeed exist". He wrote that this confirmed his suspicion that the system was "responding to language prompts without regard for the truth of the answers it was providing", but by then the cases were already before the court.

Look at what the lookup attempt shows. A deterministic, independent check existed, was tried once, and gave the right answer. The failure was in the process after it: a "not found" result did not stop the filing. Asking the model, in contrast, produced a confident wrong answer, exactly as it would for any other claim it had made. Chapter 12 develops the two-step citation check (does the cited source exist, and does it support the claim) and Lab 5 builds one.

What the court decided

The judge opened with a position that remains the part most quoted: "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance. But existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings." The respondents, he wrote, "abandoned their responsibilities when they submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations created by the artificial intelligence tool ChatGPT, then continued to stand by the fake opinions after judicial orders called their existence into question."

The court's decision on bad faith rests on conduct, and the opinion says outright that the record would "look quite different" if the lawyers had come forward after Avianca's 15 March brief or after the April orders. It found subjective bad faith in "acts of conscious avoidance and false and misleading statements to the Court". Examples in the findings:

  • Mr. LoDuca asked for an extension on the ground that he was "out of the office on vacation", which he admitted at the hearing was not true; the court found it was knowingly false and meant to conceal Mr. Schwartz's role (paras 16 and 17).
  • Mr. LoDuca signed an affidavit he did not write and did not read in advance, annexing excerpts of the fake decisions (paras 20 to 22).
  • Mr. Schwartz described ChatGPT in a sworn affidavit as supplementing his research, then admitted at the hearing that it was the only source of his arguments after a failed Fastcase search; the court called the earlier statement "a misleading attempt to mitigate his actions" (para 41).
  • His account of when he suspected a problem shifted between filings, which the court said undermined his credibility (para 47).

The sanctions were modest and specific: a $5,000 penalty, imposed jointly and severally on the two lawyers and the firm and paid into the court's registry; a letter to the client attaching the opinion, the hearing transcript and the affirmation; and a letter to each of the six judges falsely named as authors of the fake decisions (Varghese, Shaboon, Petersen, Martinez, Durden and Miller), enclosing the fake opinion attributed to that judge. The court did not order an apology ("a compelled apology is not a sincere apology"), did not order fee reimbursement because Avianca had not sought it, and found additional mandatory training redundant because the firm had already arranged an AI-competence course (paras 30 to 34). It credited the respondents' "embarrassment and remorse" and found they had acted without financial motive or animus (para 32). It also found no violation of the federal statute on forged judicial signatures and seals, because the fake opinions carried neither (conclusions of law, paras 7 and 8).

On the same day, in a separate opinion (ECF 55), the court granted Avianca's motion and dismissed the case: the claim arose under the Montreal Convention, the two years ran out by 28 August 2021, and the Convention's time limit is a strict condition precedent, so the bankruptcy stay did not toll it. Put plainly, the legal theory that the fake cases were invented to support also lost on the real law.

Try it yourself

This script is a toy citation checker. Its tiny index contains only entries that the sanctions opinion says exist at those citations (paragraphs 28 to 34), plus the two real Supreme Court decisions the opinion names. It checks each citation from the brief for two things: does anything exist at that citation, and does the case there match the name? It is deliberately an existence check, the first step of the two in chapter 12.

"""C3 toy: check each citation against a reporter index instead of asking the model that produced it.
The index holds only entries that Judge Castel's opinion (ECF 54, findings 28 to 34) says exist at those citations."""
import re

INDEX = {   # citation -> case name that actually appears there (from the opinion)
    "738 F.2d 1153":  "Gibbs v. Maxwell House",
    "925 F.3d 1339":  "J.D. v. Azar (925 F.3d 1291)",
    "92 F.3d 1074":   "two brief Eleventh Circuit orders in other cases",
    "516 U.S. 217":   "Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co.",
    "905 F. Supp. 2d 121": "United States v. ISS Marine Services",
    "525 U.S. 155":   "El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng",
}
BRIEF = [   # (name as cited, citation as cited)
    ("Holliday v. Atl. Capital Corp.", "738 F.2d 1153"),
    ("Varghese v. China Southern Airlines Co., Ltd.", "925 F.3d 1339"),
    ("Shaboon v. Egyptair", "2013 IL App (1st) 111279-U"),
    ("Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co.", "516 U.S. 217"),
    ("El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng", "525 U.S. 155"),
    ("Petersen v. Iran Air", "905 F. Supp. 2d 121"),
    ("Hyatt v. N. Cent. Airlines", "92 F.3d 1074"),
]
def words(name): return set(re.findall(r"[a-z]+", name.lower())) - {"v", "inc", "co", "ltd", "of", "the", "united", "states"}
def check(name, cite):
    found = INDEX.get(cite)
    if found is None: return "UNVERIFIED: not in this index (not proof it is fake)"
    if words(name) & words(found): return "ok: citation resolves to %s" % found
    return "MISMATCH: citation resolves to %s" % found
for name, cite in BRIEF:
    print("%-50s %-27s %s" % (name[:50], cite, check(name, cite)))
Holliday v. Atl. Capital Corp.                     738 F.2d 1153               MISMATCH: citation resolves to Gibbs v. Maxwell House
Varghese v. China Southern Airlines Co., Ltd.      925 F.3d 1339               MISMATCH: citation resolves to J.D. v. Azar (925 F.3d 1291)
Shaboon v. Egyptair                                2013 IL App (1st) 111279-U  UNVERIFIED: not in this index (not proof it is fake)
Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co.                  516 U.S. 217                ok: citation resolves to Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines Co.
El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng     525 U.S. 155                ok: citation resolves to El Al Israel Airlines, Ltd. v. Tsui Yuan Tseng
Petersen v. Iran Air                               905 F. Supp. 2d 121         MISMATCH: citation resolves to United States v. ISS Marine Services
Hyatt v. N. Cent. Airlines                         92 F.3d 1074                MISMATCH: citation resolves to two brief Eleventh Circuit orders in other cases

Read the results with care. The "Holliday", "Varghese", "Petersen" and "Hyatt" rows are mismatches: something exists at the citation, but it is a different case. The "Shaboon" row is "unverified", and the wording matters: a missing entry in a small index is not proof of fabrication, which is why real systems escalate to a human or a bigger database instead of declaring the case fake. The "ok" rows pass the existence check, and the opinion says one of them, El Al Israel Airlines v. Tsui Yuan Tseng, "does not contain the quoted language" that the fake Varghese attributes to it. So an existence check is necessary and not sufficient; the second step must compare the claim with the source's text. Try adding a "quote appears in the case text" check with a small text store of your own.

Common misconception

"The lawyers were sanctioned for using ChatGPT." The opinion says the opposite in its first paragraph. The sanction rested on submitting fake opinions, continuing to stand by them after the court questioned them, and misleading statements to the court. The court wrote that if the lawyers had come clean early, the record would look quite different. The lesson for engineers is not "do not use the tool"; it is that the person who files the work owns it, and that a process which can ship output nobody checked will eventually ship a fabrication.

Analogy: a confident intern with no access to the library

Picture a very well-read intern who has no library access but is unable to say "I do not know". Asked for cases supporting a position, the intern writes what such cases would sound like. Asked whether the cases are real, the intern says yes. Nothing in the intern's behaviour changed between the two answers; only your trust did.

Where it breaks down: a human intern can eventually be taught to say "I could not find any". A language model needs that behaviour built in through design, such as retrieval-only answers and abstention, and it can still be bypassed by a prompt that demands an answer.

State of play, October 2026

This case is from 2023. The details of the sanctions are settled history; the surrounding landscape is not. Wikipedia's article on the case lists later court decisions that cite it and a 2024 bar association opinion on lawyers' use of generative AI, but I did not read those and cannot say how sanctions have evolved since, or whether $5,000 is typical. Tools that retrieve from legal databases have also changed since 2023. Treat any claim about current practice as needing a fresh check.

What critics and later commentators said

  • Where the fault lay. The court placed responsibility on the lawyers, not the tool: Rule 11 makes the signer the gatekeeper. Mr. Schwartz's own explanation was that he did not understand the tool ("I just never thought it could be made up", Tr. 33, quoted in para 11). The court did not treat ignorance of the tool as an excuse for ignoring a failed lookup.
  • Fairness to the people. The opinion records mitigating facts: no history of disciplinary violations, no financial motive, genuine remorse, firm-level training already under way. The penalty was set at what was "sufficient but not more than necessary" for deterrence (para 34). It is a calibrated response, not an attempt to make an example.
  • Publicity as a factor. The court weighed the "significant publicity" of the matter when deciding on deterrence (para 32). Public failures teach more people than private ones, which is why an honest postmortem is valuable.

Lessons, linked to the course

  1. Fluency is not evidence. The fakes had captions, judges' names and plausible fact patterns (chapter 10).
  2. Verify from an independent source, then check support. Existence first, then whether the source says what the claim says (chapter 12, Lab 5).
  3. Design for "I could not find it". Retrieval-only answers with abstention remove the failure at its source (chapter 11).
  4. A failed check must stop the line. The lookup that returned nothing was the cheapest control and was overridden. Treat a negative result as a block, not a nuisance.
  5. When something goes wrong, say so early. The sanctions turned on what happened after the first warning. The same lesson appears in Incident Response and Postmortems.

Systems connection

  • Incident Response: disclosure timing and ownership of the incident.
  • Postmortems & Learning from Incidents: a record such as this opinion is the kind of artefact a good postmortem produces.

Key takeaways

  • A model asked to "show me more cases" can invent them with realistic captions, courts and quotations.
  • Asking the same model whether its output is real does not verify anything.
  • Check existence against an independent database, then check that the source supports the claim.
  • The court sanctioned the failure to verify and the conduct after warnings, not the use of AI.
  • A negative result from the cheapest check must stop the release.
