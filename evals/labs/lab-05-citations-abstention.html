Lab 5: Citation Checking & Abstention

A citation is a promise that a sentence rests on a source you can open. In this lab you build the machinery that checks the promise and then the policy that decides when a system should answer at all. You confirm that each quotation really occurs in a document that really exists, score whether the cited text supports the sentence it is attached to, compute citation recall and precision, turn the checks into one support score per answer, and sweep an answer-or-abstain threshold along a risk-coverage curve. The threshold comes from the price of a wrong answer, and you score it on answers you did not use to choose it.

Why this lab matters

A system that cites its sources looks trustworthy, which is exactly why a bad citation is worse than none: the reader sees a footnote and stops checking. Chapter 12 lists four ways a citation can fail (the source does not exist, the quotation is not in it, the passage does not support the sentence, or the sentence has no citation at all), and the court case in this course is what the first failure costs. Chapter 11 adds the other half: even a perfectly cited system must sometimes say "I can't tell", and where it draws that line is a choice about cost, not a property of the model. Here you write the checks and the choice. The first two checks are exact and nearly free, the third is a judgement you must measure like any judge (as in Lab 2), and the last is arithmetic on a curve. By the end you can say, with numbers, how much more reliable an answer is when a verifier stands behind it, and what that reliability costs in unanswered questions. The retrieval side of grounded answers is in Grounded Answers & Citations; the threshold arithmetic is the one of Imbalance, Thresholds & Calibration with a price attached.

Deterministic, offline and fast

The handbook, the questions, the stand-in assistant's answers and its confidence are all generated from fixed seeds in data.py, so nothing touches the network and every run prints the same numbers in about a second. The assistant is a stand-in, not a model: it is built to fail in the seven ways listed below, in known proportions, and every answer carries the gold labels the generator knows. That is what lets you measure your checks against the truth, which you cannot do with a hosted model. The venues and figures are invented. A failure that involves random cases prints the seed: python3 check.py solution 1 7 replays milestone 1 with seed 7. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. An optional section at the end shows how to put a real model in the judge's seat; check.py never does that.

What you'll build

Five milestones, each building on the last:

  1. Quotes that exist: normalise text while remembering where each character came from, find a quotation (with gaps) in a document and report its span, and classify a citation as ok, quote_not_found or no_such_source.
  2. Does the cited text support the claim? A rule-based judge that returns supported, contradicted or not enough information, and a confusion matrix that shows its errors.
  3. Recall, precision and one support score: the ALCE-style citation metrics of chapter 12, and a per-answer support score that is only as high as the weakest statement.
  4. The risk-coverage curve: coverage and risk at every threshold, the area under the curve, and the coverage you can keep at a risk you can accept.
  5. Where to put the threshold: expected score from the price of a wrong answer, a threshold chosen on one set and scored on another, and a recalibration that makes the textbook formula work.

Milestone 1: do the quoted words exist?

The cheapest checks come first because they catch the most embarrassing failures at almost no cost. A citation is a pair: a document identifier and a quotation. Two questions need no model at all. Is there a document with that identifier? And does the quotation occur in it? If the answer to either is no, nothing else about the citation matters, and a fabricated reference (the failure of Fake Citations in Court) is stopped by a dictionary lookup.

Matching a quotation against a document sounds like quote in text, and it nearly is. Three details turn it into real work. First, models and people copy loosely: they change case, collapse a line break into a space, turn a curly apostrophe into a straight one. A check that rejects those would block honest quotations, so both sides are normalised before comparing: lower-cased, with quotes, dashes and non-breaking spaces replaced by their plain forms and each run of whitespace collapsed to one space. (Normalisation is a security topic too: Injection & Untrusted Input explains why canonical forms matter whenever text is compared.) Second, you want to show the reader where the quotation sits, to highlight it or to log which span backed the claim, and that span must be in the original document, not in the normalised copy. So normalisation has to remember where each character came from.

Third, quotations have gaps. A model that wants to quote half a sentence writes "Adult entry ... costs 14 pounds", and the ellipsis means that words were left out. The honest reading is that the fragments must occur in order, each after the end of the previous one. Allowing them to overlap or go backwards would accept a quotation assembled from the same four words used twice. A fourth detail guards against the opposite error: a quotation of one or two words ("14 pounds") occurs somewhere in nearly every document by chance and proves nothing, so a quotation whose fragments total fewer than MIN_QUOTE_CHARS (12) characters is rejected.

Common misconception

"The quotation is in the document, so the citation is fine." The quotation check proves the words exist and nothing more. Answer A0005 above quotes a real sentence from a real document, about a different venue. The check you are writing here answers "did the model make this up?", not "does this source say what the sentence says?". Mixing the two up is how teams ship a citation checker that reports 95% and a product that is still wrong a third of the time.

def normalise_with_map(text):
    """Normalise a text for comparison and remember where every kept character came from.

    Returns (norm, idx). norm is the text with: every character lower-cased (if lower() gives one character),
    curly quotes, dashes and non-breaking spaces replaced as in _QUOTE_MAP, and each run of whitespace collapsed
    to a single space. idx has one entry per character of norm: idx[i] is the offset in the ORIGINAL text of the
    character that norm[i] came from (for a collapsed run, the offset of its first whitespace character).
    Nothing is stripped. len(norm) == len(idx).
    """
    norm, idx, in_space = [], [], False
    for i, ch in enumerate(text):
        ch = _QUOTE_MAP.get(ch, ch)
        low = ch.lower()
        if len(low) == 1:
            ch = low
        if ch.isspace():
            if not in_space:                      # the first whitespace character of a run stands for the run
                norm.append(" ")
                idx.append(i)
            in_space = True
        else:
            norm.append(ch)
            idx.append(i)
            in_space = False
    return "".join(norm), idx

def verify_quote(quote, passage):
    """Where, in the passage, does the quotation appear? Return (start, end) with passage[start:end] the span
    (offsets into the ORIGINAL passage), or None.

    Split the quote into fragments at '...' or '…'; normalise and strip each fragment (drop empty ones); the
    quote is rejected (None) if there are no fragments or all fragments together have fewer than MIN_QUOTE_CHARS
    characters. Normalise the passage with normalise_with_map. Find the fragments in order with str.find in the
    normalised passage, each search starting where the previous fragment ended (so they cannot overlap or go
    backwards). If any fragment is missing, return None. start is the original offset of the first fragment's first
    character; end is one past the original offset of the last fragment's last character.
    """
    frags = []
    for part in split_fragments(quote):
        f, _ = normalise_with_map(part)
        f = f.strip()
        if f:
            frags.append(f)
    if not frags or sum(len(f) for f in frags) < MIN_QUOTE_CHARS:
        return None
    norm, idx = normalise_with_map(passage)
    pos, first, last = 0, None, None
    for f in frags:
        at = norm.find(f, pos)
        if at < 0:
            return None
        if first is None:
            first = at
        last = at + len(f) - 1
        pos = at + len(f)
    return idx[first], idx[last] + 1

def check_citation(cite, corpus):
    """Check one citation {"doc": id, "quote": text} against the corpus {id: text}. Return one of:
         "no_such_source"   the document id is not in the corpus (exact match)
         "quote_not_found"  the document exists but verify_quote finds no such span
         "ok"               the quotation is there
    """
    text = corpus.get(cite["doc"])
    if text is None:
        return "no_such_source"
    return "ok" if verify_quote(cite["quote"], text) is not None else "quote_not_found"

Milestone 2: does the cited text support the claim?

A quotation can be real and irrelevant. The question that matters is whether the cited text entails the claim: if the passage is true, must the claim be true? Models that answer this are doing natural language inference, with three labels (SUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED, NOT_ENOUGH_INFO), exactly as in chapter 12. Here the judge is a small rule-based stand-in so that the lab runs offline and you can read every decision. Its rules are those of the chapter's verifier:

  1. Split the cited documents into sentences, and each claim and sentence into content words (words_of: letters only, no stop words, synonyms folded, so "can hold" and "has capacity for" meet). Numbers are handled separately (numbers_of).
  2. For each sentence, overlap is the share of the claim's content words that the sentence contains. The best sentence is the one with the highest overlap (the first on a tie).
  3. If the best overlap is below MIN_OVERLAP (0.6), nothing in the text addresses the claim: NOT_ENOUGH_INFO.
  4. If one side contains a negation ("not", "no", "never") and the other does not, the text says the opposite: CONTRADICTED.
  5. If the claim contains a number that the best sentence does not, it states a different figure: CONTRADICTED. Otherwise SUPPORTED.

The score of a verdict is the overlap, whatever the label; you will use it in milestone 3. Two claims against the first document ("Marlow Pool has capacity for 300 visitors. Adult entry to Marlow Pool costs 14 pounds. ..."): "Marlow Pool can hold 300 visitors." has all four content words in the first sentence, so it is SUPPORTED with score 1.0; "Marlow Pool can hold 250 visitors." has the same words and a different number, so it is CONTRADICTED. "Marlow Pool has 40 car parking spaces." shares only the venue name with any sentence (2 of 5 words, 0.4), so it is NOT_ENOUGH_INFO.

The rules are simple on purpose, and every rule has a way to fail. That is the point of the second half of the milestone: evaluate_judge scores a judge against gold labels and returns the confusion matrix, the accuracy, and two numbers that matter more than accuracy. Precision of SUPPORTED is the share of claims the judge approves that really are supported; its complement is the false-support rate, the dangerous error because it stamps a wrong claim as checked. Recall of SUPPORTED is the share of supported claims it approves; its complement is the share of good answers it needlessly vetoes. Here is the finished judge on the claims of the dev set:

def judge_claim(claim, passages):
    """Decide whether the passages support a claim. A rule-based stand-in for an entailment model.

    Take every sentence of every passage (split_sentences, passages in the given order). For each sentence compute
    overlap = |W(claim) & W(sentence)| / |W(claim)|, where W is the SET of words_of(...) (0.0 if the claim has no
    content words). The best sentence is the one with the highest overlap, the first one on ties. Then, in order:
      no sentences at all, or overlap < MIN_OVERLAP           -> NOT_ENOUGH_INFO
      has_negation(best) != has_negation(claim)               -> CONTRADICTED
      claim has numbers and they are not all in the best sentence's numbers   -> CONTRADICTED
      otherwise                                               -> SUPPORTED
    Return Verdict(label, score=overlap of the best sentence (0.0 if no sentences), sentence=the best sentence or "").
    """
    claim_words = set(words_of(claim))
    best, best_score = "", 0.0
    for passage in passages:
        for sent in split_sentences(passage):
            sw = set(words_of(sent))
            score = len(claim_words & sw) / len(claim_words) if claim_words else 0.0
            if best == "" or score > best_score:
                best, best_score = sent, score
    if best == "" or best_score < MIN_OVERLAP:
        return Verdict("NOT_ENOUGH_INFO", best_score, best)
    if has_negation(best) != has_negation(claim):
        return Verdict("CONTRADICTED", best_score, best)
    nums = numbers_of(claim)
    if nums and not nums <= numbers_of(best):
        return Verdict("CONTRADICTED", best_score, best)
    return Verdict("SUPPORTED", best_score, best)

def evaluate_judge(items, judge=None):
    """Score a judge against gold labels. items is a list of (claim, passages, gold_label). Return a dict:
         confusion   {(gold, predicted): count} with an entry for every pair of LABELS (zeros included)
         accuracy    correct / total
         precision   precision of SUPPORTED: of the claims predicted SUPPORTED, the share whose gold is SUPPORTED
                     (1.0 if nothing was predicted SUPPORTED)
         recall      recall of SUPPORTED: of the claims whose gold is SUPPORTED, the share predicted SUPPORTED
                     (1.0 if there are none)
         false_support  the (claim, gold) pairs predicted SUPPORTED whose gold is not, in input order
    """
    judge = judge or judge_claim
    conf = {(g, p): 0 for g in LABELS for p in LABELS}
    false_support, right = [], 0
    for claim, passages, gold in items:
        pred = judge(claim, passages).label
        conf[(gold, pred)] += 1
        right += pred == gold
        if pred == "SUPPORTED" and gold != "SUPPORTED":
            false_support.append((claim, gold))
    pred_sup = sum(conf[(g, "SUPPORTED")] for g in LABELS)
    gold_sup = sum(conf[("SUPPORTED", p)] for p in LABELS)
    return {"confusion": conf, "accuracy": right / len(items) if items else 1.0,
            "precision": conf[("SUPPORTED", "SUPPORTED")] / pred_sup if pred_sup else 1.0,
            "recall": conf[("SUPPORTED", "SUPPORTED")] / gold_sup if gold_sup else 1.0,
            "false_support": false_support}

Milestone 3: recall, precision and one support score

Chapter 12 defines two citation metrics after Gao et al. (2023), the ALCE paper. Take one statement and the documents it cites.

Citation recall (per statement)
1 if the statement has at least one citation and the cited documents, taken together, entail it; otherwise 0. It asks whether the sentence is backed.
Citation precision (per citation)
A citation is irrelevant if its document alone does not entail the statement and the statement's other citations still do. A citation scores 1 if its statement has recall 1 and the citation is not irrelevant. It asks whether every footnote is pulling its weight; a statement that stacks four footnotes where one would do is padded, not better sourced.

These sit beside the two checks of milestone 1, which you report separately: the share of citations that point at a real source (existence) and the share whose quotation is verbatim (quotes). Four numbers, four questions. The six-sentence answer of chapter 12, with the same corpus, makes the difference vivid. This is its check by your finished functions:

def entails(statement, doc_ids, corpus, judge=None):
    """Do the cited documents, taken together, support the statement? Documents that do not exist are ignored; if
    none exist the answer is False. True exactly when judge_claim(statement, [texts]).label == "SUPPORTED"."""
    judge = judge or judge_claim
    texts = [corpus[d] for d in doc_ids if d in corpus]
    return bool(texts) and judge(statement, texts).label == "SUPPORTED"

def check_statement(stmt, corpus, judge=None):
    """Check one statement {"text", "cites": [{"doc", "quote"}, ...]} against the corpus. Return a dict:
      cites      one dict per citation, in order: {"doc", "status" (check_citation), "alone" (entails with only this
                 document), "irrelevant" (not alone, but the OTHER citations of the statement do entail it)}
      recall     1 if the statement has at least one citation and all its cited documents together entail it, else 0
      precision  one 0/1 per citation: 1 if recall == 1 and the citation is not irrelevant
      support    0.0 unless the statement has citations, EVERY citation has status "ok" and the judge's label on all
                 cited documents together is SUPPORTED; then the judge's score
    """
    judge = judge or judge_claim
    text, cites = stmt["text"], stmt["cites"]
    ids = [c["doc"] for c in cites]
    rows = []
    for k, c in enumerate(cites):
        others = ids[:k] + ids[k + 1:]
        alone = entails(text, [c["doc"]], corpus, judge)
        rows.append({"doc": c["doc"], "status": check_citation(c, corpus), "alone": alone,
                     "irrelevant": (not alone) and entails(text, others, corpus, judge)})
    recall = 1 if cites and entails(text, ids, corpus, judge) else 0
    support = 0.0
    if cites and all(r["status"] == "ok" for r in rows):
        v = judge(text, [corpus[d] for d in ids])
        if v.label == "SUPPORTED":
            support = v.score
    return {"cites": rows, "recall": recall, "precision": [1 if recall and not r["irrelevant"] else 0 for r in rows],
            "support": support}

def verify_answer(answer, corpus, judge=None):
    """Check every statement of an answer. Return {"statements": [check_statement(...)...], "support": s} where the
    answer's support is the MINIMUM statement support (an answer is only as good as its weakest claim; 0.0 for an
    answer with no statements)."""
    res = [check_statement(s, corpus, judge) for s in answer["statements"]]
    return {"statements": res, "support": min((r["support"] for r in res), default=0.0)}

def citation_report(answers, corpus, judge=None):
    """Aggregate the checks over many answers. Return a dict of rates (each 1.0 when its denominator is 0):
      existence   share of citations whose status is not "no_such_source"
      quotes      share of citations whose status is "ok"
      recall      share of statements with recall 1
      precision   share of citations with precision 1
      uncited     share of statements with no citation (a COUNT-based share of statements, 0.0 if none)
      statements, citations   the two counts
    """
    n_s = n_c = ex = ok = rec = prec = unc = 0
    for a in answers:
        for st in verify_answer(a, corpus, judge)["statements"]:
            n_s += 1
            rec += st["recall"]
            unc += not st["cites"]
            for row, p in zip(st["cites"], st["precision"]):
                n_c += 1
                ex += row["status"] != "no_such_source"
                ok += row["status"] == "ok"
                prec += p
    one = lambda a, b: a / b if b else 1.0
    return {"existence": one(ex, n_c), "quotes": one(ok, n_c), "recall": one(rec, n_s), "precision": one(prec, n_c),
            "uncited": unc / n_s if n_s else 0.0, "statements": n_s, "citations": n_c}

Milestone 4: the risk-coverage curve

Checks tell you which answers look sound. Now suppose the system may decline. Each answer has a confidence, a number that is higher when the answer is more likely right, and the system answers when the confidence reaches a threshold t. Two quantities describe the policy (chapter 11):

Coverage
The share of all questions the system answers. Low coverage means many refusals.
Risk
The share of the answered questions that are wrong (the selective risk). It is a leading indicator of how much users can trust an answer when one is given.

Lower the threshold and coverage rises while risk, usually, rises with it, because the answers you add are the ones the signal liked least. Plot risk against coverage for every threshold and you have the risk-coverage curve. The area under it (AURC, lower is better) summarises the ranking quality of the signal in one number: a signal that put every correct answer first would keep risk at zero until coverage reached the accuracy, and one with no information would give a flat line at the overall error rate.

Two details of the computation matter. First, ties: if two answers have the same confidence, no threshold can answer one without the other, so equal confidences enter the curve together as one step. (A verifier's support score is full of ties: most good answers score exactly 1.0.) Second, the area is the sum, over the steps, of the coverage gained times the risk at that step. For a hand-worked example take five answers with confidences 0.9 (right), 0.8 (right), 0.8 (wrong), 0.5 (right) and 0.4 (wrong). The points are (0.9, coverage 0.2, risk 0), (0.8, 0.6, 1/3), (0.5, 0.8, 0.25) and (0.4, 1.0, 0.4). The 0.8 answers join in one step. AURC is 0.2×0 + 0.4×(1/3) + 0.2×0.25 + 0.2×0.4 = 0.2633.

The operational query is the third function: how much of the traffic can we answer if we will accept at most 5% wrong answers? That is max_coverage_at_risk(points, 0.05). def risk_coverage(rows):
    """The risk-coverage curve of a confidence signal. rows is a list of (confidence, correct). For each DISTINCT
    confidence value t, highest first, the system that answers every row with confidence >= t has
         coverage = answered / total      risk = wrong answers / answered
    Return [(t, coverage, risk), ...], highest threshold first. Rows with equal confidence enter together (a tie
    cannot be split by a threshold). Empty input gives []."""
    n = len(rows)
    groups = {}
    for conf, ok in rows:
        c, w = groups.get(conf, (0, 0))
        groups[conf] = (c + 1, w + (not ok))
    points, answered, wrong = [], 0, 0
    for t in sorted(groups, reverse=True):
        c, w = groups[t]
        answered += c
        wrong += w
        points.append((t, answered / n, wrong / answered))
    return points

def aurc(points):
    """Area under the risk-coverage curve as a step function: the sum over points of (coverage - previous coverage)
    * risk, with previous coverage 0.0 before the first point. Lower is better. [] gives 0.0."""
    area, prev = 0.0, 0.0
    for _, cov, risk in points:
        area += (cov - prev) * risk
        prev = cov
    return area

def max_coverage_at_risk(points, max_risk):
    """The largest coverage among points whose risk is at most max_risk; 0.0 if no point qualifies."""
    ok = [cov for _, cov, risk in points if risk <= max_risk]
    return max(ok) if ok else 0.0

Milestone 5: where to put the threshold

A risk-coverage curve shows every trade; it does not choose one. The choice is about money: what does a wrong answer cost compared with an answer that was right? Score each answered question +1 if it is right and −λ if it is wrong, and each declined question 0. The penalty λ is yours to set: 1 for an internal search helper where a wrong answer costs about what a right one earns, 9 for a medical or legal assistant where one error undoes nine good answers. The expected score of a threshold t is the mean of these scores over all questions, answered or not.

If the confidences were calibrated (an answer given 80% confidence is right 80% of the time), answering would pay c − λ(1 − c) on average, which beats declining exactly when c > λ/(1 + λ). So λ = 1 gives a threshold of 0.5, λ = 3 gives 0.75, and λ = 9 gives 0.9. But the assistant's confidence is not calibrated: it is inflated, and the formula applied to it picks the wrong cut. Two remedies, and you write both:

  • Search. Try every distinct confidence as a threshold on a calibration set of answers with known outcomes, and keep the one with the best expected score (best_threshold). On a tie, take the higher threshold: when two thresholds earn the same, prefer to answer less. If no threshold earns more than 0, the best policy is to answer nothing (threshold math.inf, score 0).
  • Recalibrate, then use the formula. Fit a table that maps each confidence to the accuracy actually observed at that confidence (histogram binning, fit_binning), apply it (calibrate) and use λ/(1 + λ) on the recalibrated numbers. This gives the same sort of threshold with an explanation you can defend, and it needs no search.

Either way, the threshold must be scored on answers that were not used to choose it. A threshold picked on the questions you report is optimistic by construction, because you picked its best-looking cut (the held-out-set rule from the start of the course, as in Lab 1). def expected_score(rows, t, penalty):
    """Mean score per row of the policy "answer when confidence >= t": +1 for a right answer, -penalty for a wrong
    one, 0 for an abstention. The mean is over ALL rows, answered or not. [] gives 0.0."""
    if not rows:
        return 0.0
    return sum((1.0 if ok else -penalty) for conf, ok in rows if conf >= t) / len(rows)

def best_threshold(rows, penalty):
    """The threshold with the highest expected_score on these rows. Candidates are the distinct confidence values
    plus math.inf (answer nothing, score 0.0). On a tie take the HIGHER threshold. Return (t, score)."""
    best_t, best_s = math.inf, 0.0
    for t in sorted({c for c, _ in rows}, reverse=True):
        s = expected_score(rows, t, penalty)
        if s > best_s:                       # strict: scanning from the top, an equal score keeps the higher t
            best_t, best_s = t, s
    return best_t, best_s

def fit_binning(rows, n_bins=10):
    """Histogram-binning recalibration. Bin i covers confidences in [i/n_bins, (i+1)/n_bins), the last bin also
    includes 1.0 and anything above. Return a list of n_bins numbers: the share of correct answers in each bin, or
    the bin's midpoint (i + 0.5) / n_bins if the bin is empty."""
    right, total = [0] * n_bins, [0] * n_bins
    for conf, ok in rows:
        b = min(max(int(conf * n_bins), 0), n_bins - 1)
        total[b] += 1
        right[b] += bool(ok)
    return [right[i] / total[i] if total[i] else (i + 0.5) / n_bins for i in range(n_bins)]

def calibrate(conf, table):
    """Map a confidence to the accuracy of its bin in a table from fit_binning (same binning rule)."""
    n = len(table)
    return table[min(max(int(conf * n), 0), n - 1)]

Read the last block as a decision. If a wrong answer costs three right ones, answer only when the verifier's support is at least about 0.86, and you earn +0.46 per question where answering everything loses 0.77; you answer about half the questions and are wrong on about 3% of those. The remaining cost is the other half, the 44% of answers that never reach the user: 29 of them were correct and were vetoed because a rule-based judge cannot compare quantities. That figure is the budget for improving the judge: better entailment checking lifts coverage without touching the threshold logic.

Look under the hood

The judge's one parameter, MIN_OVERLAP, trades the two errors of milestone 2 against each other. The dev-set sweep printed above shows the pattern in miniature: accuracy peaks at 0.8 (0.923); a bar of 0.9 removes every false support (precision 1.000) and cuts recall to 0.819, which in the full pipeline means a lot more good answers vetoed. Choose it as you chose the abstention threshold, from the cost of each error, on a set you did not tune on.

Two weaknesses of this judge are worth fixing yourself, because each one maps to a failure of real entailment models. The first is that entity names count as evidence: a claim about "Orchard Theatre" and a sentence about "Orchard Theatre" share two of the claim's words before anything else is compared. A stronger judge would remove the words that name the subject, or weight rare words more than common ones. The second is that numbers are compared for equality only: "more than 280" and "280" are different strings. Handling quantifiers and units is where small rule-based verifiers stop and trained entailment models begin, and it is also where LLM judges are least reliable (chapter 12 reports the same failure for "most"). The cases in this lab are a faithful miniature of that trade.

Run it against a real model

Optional, and not used by check.py. Every function that takes a judge accepts a judge= argument with the signature judge(claim, passages) -> Verdict, so a model can sit in the judge's seat without changing any other code. Set LAB_MODEL to anthropic:<model-id>, openai:<model-id> or ollama:<name> first. The listing was not run here, so no output is shown.

from llm import get_model, text_of
from solution import *      # or: from starter import *

PROMPT = ("Passage:\n{passage}\n\nClaim: {claim}\n\n"
          "Does the passage support the claim? Answer with exactly one of: SUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED, NOT_ENOUGH_INFO.")
model = get_model()

def llm_judge(claim, passages):
    reply = text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT.format(passage="\n".join(passages), claim=claim)}],
                                   temperature=0.0)).upper()
    label = next((l for l in LABELS if l in reply), "NOT_ENOUGH_INFO")
    return Verdict(label, 1.0 if label == "SUPPORTED" else 0.0, "")

items = []
for a in dev_set():
    for s in a["statements"]:
        texts = [CORPUS[c["doc"]] for c in s["cites"] if c["doc"] in CORPUS]
        if texts:
            items.append((s["text"], texts, s["gold"]))
r = evaluate_judge(items, llm_judge)
print(r["accuracy"], r["precision"], r["recall"], len(r["false_support"]))
rows = [(verify_answer(a, CORPUS, llm_judge)["support"], a["correct"]) for a in test_set()]
print(aurc(risk_coverage(rows)))

What to look for: whether the model catches the near misses ("child" for "adult") that the rules cannot, whether it handles "more than 280", and whether it too is fooled by some other class (a long passage, a claim that contradicts by omission). Its support score here is 1.0 or 0.0, so the risk-coverage curve has only two steps; ask for a probability or sample several times to get a ranking. Remember what chapter 3 says about judges: calibrate this one against labels before you trust its numbers, and re-check it whenever the model or the prompt changes.

Systems connection

Quotation checking is input validation with a canonical form: compare text only after normalising it, and keep the map back to the original so errors can be reported where the user can see them (Injection & Untrusted Input). A citation that points at a document that does not exist is a dangling foreign key, and a referential-integrity check is the cheap guard (SQL & the Relational Model). The risk-coverage curve is the precision-recall trade-off of an alert, with abstention in place of paging: the same logic as choosing a burn-rate threshold in the reliability course's Lab 1, where the cost of a false page replaces the cost of a wrong answer. Putting a price on an error and choosing the cut from it is the habit of error budgets. And the stage that stands between the model and the user, where a failed check turns into a refusal or a retry, is the validation layer of Designing with ML & LLMs.

What you learned

  • Check the exact things first. A citation to a document that does not exist, or a "quotation" that is not in it, is stopped by a lookup and a string search, after normalising case, whitespace and quotes. Keep the map back to the original text so the span can be shown.
  • A real quotation proves nothing about support. Judge whether the cited text entails the statement, and report recall (is it backed?) and precision (is every footnote needed?) beside existence and quotation rates: four numbers, four questions.
  • Measure the judge by its confusion matrix. The false-support rate is the dangerous one; the rule-based judge here is fooled by changed qualifiers and cannot compare quantities.
  • Make the support of an answer the minimum over its statements, and zero if any citation fails a hard check.
  • Plot risk against coverage, group ties, and read the curve at the risk you can accept. Neither signal need dominate: the assistant's own confidence did better at high coverage, the verifier at low risk.
  • Choose the threshold from the price of a wrong answer, on one set, and report it on another. The formula λ/(1 + λ) is right only for calibrated confidences; recalibrate first or search.

Check your understanding

An answer says "Orchard Theatre has capacity for 360 visitors" and cites document D05 with the quotation "capacity for 360 visitors". D05 is about a different venue and does contain that exact phrase. Which of the lab's checks first rejects the citation?

  1. The existence check: D05 might not be in the corpus
  2. The quotation check: the phrase is not in D05
  3. The support check: the cited text is about another venue, so the venue name in the claim is not backed
  4. None of them: the citation is fine because the quotation is verbatim
The document exists and the quotation is verbatim, so milestone 1 passes it. Only the judge notices that the claim's content words (the venue's name) are not in the cited sentence, which makes the overlap fall below the bar. Option 4 is the misconception of the lab: a real quotation says nothing about whether it supports the sentence.

Why does normalise_with_map return an index map as well as the normalised text?

  1. So that a span found in the normalised text can be reported at the right place in the original document
  2. So that the quotation check can run in constant time
  3. So that two different texts can never normalise to the same string
  4. Because Python strings cannot be searched without an index
Collapsing whitespace and replacing characters changes lengths, so an offset in the normalised string is not an offset in the original. The map restores it, which is what you need to highlight the quotation or log the evidence. Option 3 is false (different texts can normalise equal, which is the point of normalising); the map does not speed anything up.

You raise MIN_OVERLAP from 0.6 to 0.9 and rerun the dev-set evaluation. What do you expect, and what does the lab's sweep show?

  1. Accuracy and recall of SUPPORTED both improve, because the judge is stricter
  2. Nothing changes, because the number-matching rule decides every verdict
  3. More false supports, because fewer sentences count as evidence
  4. Fewer false supports, down to none in the dev set, and more good claims vetoed: recall of SUPPORTED falls from 0.93 to 0.82
A higher bar demands that nearly every word of the claim appear in the sentence. That removes the near misses but also rejects honest paraphrases. Precision rises to 1.000 and recall falls to 0.819. A stricter judge is not a better judge; it is a different point on the same trade-off.

In risk_coverage, three answers share the confidence 0.8. Why do they enter the curve as a single step?

  1. To make the curve smoother and easier to plot
  2. Because a threshold cannot answer some of them and not the others, so a point that included only some would not correspond to any real policy
  3. Because tied answers always have the same correctness
  4. Because sorting is not stable in Python
A policy is "answer everything with confidence at least t". Equal confidences are in or out together. Splitting a tie would invent policies that cannot be implemented and would make the curve depend on the order of the input. Option 3 is wrong: tied answers can be a mix of right and wrong, which is exactly the verifier's problem at support 1.0.

A wrong answer costs three right ones (λ = 3). The assistant's confidence is inflated, and you use the formula λ/(1 + λ) = 0.75 on the raw numbers. What is the likely result, compared with the threshold searched on a calibration set (0.83 in the lab)?

  1. The same threshold, because the formula is exact
  2. A higher threshold, because inflation makes the model look more certain
  3. A lower threshold than is optimal, so it answers some questions it should decline, and scores worse on the test set (+0.088 against +0.107)
  4. No answers at all
The formula assumes that a confidence of 0.75 means 75% right. When the real accuracy at 0.75 is lower, the cut is too generous. Recalibrating first (mapping each confidence to its observed accuracy) and then applying the formula repairs this and scores +0.105. Option 2 has the effect backwards: inflation is the reason the true cut sits above the formula's.

You choose the best threshold on the 600 calibration answers and report its score on the same 600. Why is that figure optimistic?

  1. You selected the cut that looks best on exactly those answers, so part of the score is luck that will not repeat on new ones
  2. The calibration set is always easier than the test set
  3. The expected score is only defined on test data
  4. Because ties always go to the lower threshold
Searching over many thresholds and keeping the best of them fits noise in the particular answers. The held-out score shows what to expect. The lab's two sets agree closely (+0.118 and +0.107 at λ = 3) because 600 answers is enough for a one-parameter search, but with a small set or many parameters the gap grows.
