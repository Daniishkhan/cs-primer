Building a Domain Benchmark

Public benchmarks answer someone else's question. When you need to know whether an assistant can answer your city's permit questions, read your firm's contracts or follow your clinic's booking rules, you build a benchmark: a curated, versioned set of items with trusted answers. This chapter covers how to define the task, source and label items, measure difficulty, split the data, document it and keep it honest as it ages.

Why this matters

The previous chapters gave you the measurement tools: traces, harnesses, judges, error bars and human labels. A benchmark is where they come together into something that outlives a single experiment and lets you compare systems across months. A weak benchmark is worse than none: it gives a number that people trust, optimise against and quote to customers, while measuring something slightly different from what matters. After this chapter you will be able to write a task specification, plan coverage, produce gold answers that survive review, find broken items before users do, split and version the data, and publish a data card that tells the next person what the numbers can and cannot support.

Benchmark, regression suite or eval set?

The words overlap, so fix them first. An eval set is any collection of cases you run a system against, such as the cases in chapter 2's harness. A regression suite grows from failures: every time something breaks in production, a case is added so it never breaks silently again (chapter 18 builds this loop). A benchmark is the stable end of the spectrum: a curated set with a written task definition, trusted answers, fixed splits and version numbers, designed so that different systems and different dates can be compared. You build one when several systems, prompts or models will be ranked against each other over time, when a customer or regulator needs evidence, or when the set is shared between teams.

Build it only after you have looked at your own data. The error analysis of chapter 1 tells you what the failures are; a benchmark designed without that tends to test what was easy to write down. If you are choosing between public benchmarks instead, Public Benchmarks & Their Limits (A5) and chapter 9 discuss reading them; the customer-facing side of the same work is in Evaluation Plans Customers Trust (A8).

Define the task before the items

Every defect that is hardest to fix later starts as a vague task definition. A task specification is a page that answers these questions in plain words.

  1. What ability or behaviour is being measured? "Answers questions about building permits correctly from the city's code." Not "is good at permits".
  2. What is the input? A question alone, or a question plus documents, a conversation history, a user profile, tools? Be exact, because a system that retrieves its own documents is measured differently from one that is handed them.
  3. What is the output and how is it scored? A short string compared after normalisation, a structured record checked field by field, a free-text reply graded by a calibrated judge. The scoring rule is part of the benchmark, not an afterthought.
  4. What is out of scope? Appeals, other cities, legal advice. Out-of-scope inputs are either excluded or included deliberately with an expected refusal.
  5. Who is the user and what would make an answer harmful? A wrong fee is an annoyance; wrongly saying no permit is needed for structural work is a safety problem. Severity decides how to weight items and what to sample on purpose.
  6. What decision will the score feed? Choosing between two models, gating a release, reporting to a customer. The decision sets the precision you need (chapter 4) and the cost of error.

The last gap deserves a check you can run. Take five or six systems you already have, score them on the benchmark, and also have an expert rate their answers on a small sample of real production questions. If the benchmark ranking and the expert ranking disagree, the benchmark is not measuring what you want, whatever its other qualities. The agreement of rankings is the practical meaning of what researchers call validity: does the measure track the thing it stands for?

Throughout this chapter the running example is a fictional benchmark, ashford-permits-qa: questions to an assistant that answers from a municipal building code. The code, fees and numbers are invented for the example; the machinery is real.

Sourcing items

An item is one case: an input, any context it needs, and the information required to score a response. Where items come from decides what the benchmark can represent.

Real traffic

Sample actual questions (from logs, tickets, search queries). Best for realism, including the odd phrasing and typos real users produce. Needs consent and a privacy review, de-identification and a time spread so a single bad week does not dominate. See Data Protection & Privacy.

Expert-written

Specialists write questions and answers from the source documents. Best for hard cases and rare situations that traffic rarely contains. Slow and expensive, and experts write in their own register, which may differ from users'.

Synthetic, then filtered

A model drafts items from documents; experts review each one. Cheap and scalable, but the items inherit the generator's style and blind spots, and near-duplicates multiply. Treat generated items as drafts, never as gold.

Most good benchmarks mix sources: real questions for realism, expert-written ones for the hard and rare cases, and synthetic drafts for volume, with the proportions recorded in the data card. Whatever the source, sample with a plan, not with convenience.

Plan coverage with a matrix

A coverage matrix lists the dimensions along which questions differ (topic, difficulty, user type, input format, language) and how many items you want in each cell. It does two jobs: it makes you decide what the benchmark covers, and it exposes holes afterwards. Add a dimension for severity and deliberately over-sample the rare, high-stakes cases, then report both the plain average and per-cell results, since a weighted average that hides a failing critical cell is dangerous.

How many items per cell? The statistics of chapter 4 apply: a cell of 10 items has an error bar of about ±30 points, so it supports "we have covered this" but not "we measured this". Decide whether each cell is there to cover or to report, and size it accordingly. Beware also of items that are not independent: ten rewordings of one question are one question for the purpose of error bars (the clustering problem).

The toy benchmark

Here is the format used for the examples below: one JSON object per line, with an id, category, an author-assigned difficulty, the question, the gold answer and where it comes from in the code. The file has 31 items and four planted defects of the kind real benchmarks contain.

{"id": "p001", "category": "fees", "difficulty": "easy", "question": "What is the fee for a residential deck permit under 30 square metres?", "gold": "120", "source": "fee-schedule:B2"}
{"id": "p002", "category": "fees", "difficulty": "easy", "question": "How much does it cost to renew an expired building permit?", "gold": "60", "source": "fee-schedule:B7"}
{"id": "p003", "category": "fees", "difficulty": "medium", "question": "What is the plan-check fee for a commercial alteration valued at 80,000?", "gold": "900", "source": "fee-schedule:C3"}

A validator is the first tool to write, before there are many items. It checks the schema, flags near-duplicates (here by the overlap of three-word shingles, measured with Jaccard similarity), prints the coverage matrix, assigns splits and fingerprints the content.

import json, hashlib, re
from collections import Counter

CATEGORIES = {"fees", "timelines", "documents", "inspections", "exemptions", "setbacks"}
LEVELS = ["easy", "medium", "hard"]
items = [json.loads(line) for line in open("items.jsonl")]

def shingles(text, k=3):
    words = re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())
    return {" ".join(words[i:i + k]) for i in range(len(words) - k + 1)}

def jaccard(a, b):
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b) if a | b else 0.0

problems = []
for it in items:
    if not it.get("gold"):                       problems.append((it["id"], "missing gold answer"))
    if it["category"] not in CATEGORIES:         problems.append((it["id"], f"unknown category {it['category']!r}"))
    if it["difficulty"] not in LEVELS:           problems.append((it["id"], "unknown difficulty"))
for i, a in enumerate(items):                     # pairwise near-duplicate scan (fine up to a few thousand items)
    for b in items[i + 1:]:
        sim = jaccard(shingles(a["question"]), shingles(b["question"]))
        if sim >= 0.5:
            problems.append((b["id"], f"question {sim:.2f} similar to {a['id']}"))

print(f"{len(items)} items, {len(problems)} problems")
for pid, msg in problems:
    print(f"  {pid}: {msg}")

grid = Counter((it["category"], it["difficulty"]) for it in items)
print("\ncoverage (category x difficulty)")
print(f"  {'':11}" + "".join(f"{lv:>8}" for lv in LEVELS))
for c in sorted(CATEGORIES):
    print(f"  {c:11}" + "".join(f"{grid[(c, lv)]:>8}" for lv in LEVELS))

def split_of(item_id, dev_share=0.3):            # stable: depends only on the id, not on the other items
    h = int(hashlib.sha256(item_id.encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % 1000
    return "dev" if h < dev_share * 1000 else "test"

splits = Counter(split_of(it["id"]) for it in items)
canonical = "\n".join(json.dumps(it, sort_keys=True) for it in sorted(items, key=lambda x: x["id"]))
print("\nsplits:", dict(splits))
print("content hash:", hashlib.sha256(canonical.encode()).hexdigest()[:16])

Duplicates matter beyond tidiness: they double-count a question and, if one copy lands in dev and the other in test, leak the answer.

Gold answers

The gold answer (or key, or reference) is what a response is scored against. Its form follows the task, and the form decides how much of the scoring can be mechanical.

FormExampleScoringWatch out for
Single closed answer"120" (a fee)Normalised exact matchUnits, formatting ("$120", "120 dollars"), valid paraphrases
Set of accepted answers"one extension, 90 days" or "a single 90-day extension"Match any accepted variantWriting the variants by looking at model outputs, which biases towards what models say
Programmatic checkSQL result equals expected rows; unit tests pass; JSON matches schemaRun the checkCheckers with loopholes (see chapter 2); tests that do not test the right thing
Reference plus rubricA model answer and a list of required factsCalibrated LLM judge (chapter 3)Judge bias; rubric drift; contested items
Preference or ranking"A is better than B", by expert votePairwise win ratePosition and length bias; expert disagreement (chapter 5)

Four habits keep gold answers trustworthy.

  • Every answer cites its source. The source field above ("code:4.6") lets a reviewer verify the key in seconds, and lets you re-check when the underlying document changes. Add an "as of" date for facts that expire.
  • A second expert checks each key independently, blind to the first expert's answer, following the procedure and agreement measures of chapter 5. Items they disagree on are fixed or dropped, never silently kept.
  • Include unanswerable items on purpose. Questions whose answer is not in the supplied material, with "cannot be determined from the code" as the correct response, test whether the system invents answers. Chapter 11 develops abstention.
  • Assume some keys are wrong, and budget for finding them. Northcutt and colleagues (2021) estimated at least 3.3 percent label errors on average across ten widely used test sets; the MMLU-Redux re-annotation of 5,700 questions (Gema et al., 2024) estimated about 6.5 percent of MMLU questions contain errors; and in August 2026 Epoch AI reported that 22 of 48 randomly sampled questions (46 percent) in Humanity's Last Exam had accuracy-altering errors and rated the benchmark "Flawed". Your own set, built faster, will not be cleaner unless you audit it.

Difficulty and discrimination

A benchmark where every system scores 99 percent tells you nothing, and neither does one where every system scores 3 percent. A useful benchmark has items spread across a range of difficulty, so that better systems score higher. Two item statistics make this concrete. The solve rate of an item is the share of systems (or attempts) that get it right. The discrimination of an item is how well it separates strong systems from weak ones, commonly measured as the correlation between success on the item and the total score on the other items. A good item has a mid-range solve rate and positive discrimination.

You get these statistics from a pilot run: score a handful of systems of different strengths (a small model, a large model, a retrieval-less variant, a rules baseline) on the draft benchmark, several attempts each. The most valuable output is not the difficulty ranking but the odd items:

  • Items nobody solves are more often wrong keys, missing context or ambiguous questions than genuinely impossible ones. Send every one back to an expert. This is a cheap, powerful way to find errors: you are asking the systems to disagree with your key and then checking who is right.
  • Items everybody solves carry no information for ranking. Keep a few as a sanity check and for regression detection, but do not let them dominate the average, because they compress differences and make a benchmark look easier than it is.
  • Items where stronger systems do worse (negative discrimination) are suspicious: the question may be ambiguous in a way that more careful systems notice.

The simulation below uses eight stand-in systems of increasing ability on the 31 items, with three broken keys and one ambiguous question planted. It is a simulation I wrote, with a logistic model of success, so it shows what the analysis looks for and not how any real model behaves.

import json, math, random
random.seed(31)
items = [json.loads(line) for line in open("items.jsonl")]
level = {"easy": -1.5, "medium": 0.0, "hard": 1.2}          # how hard each level is, on a logistic scale
BROKEN = {"p010", "p018", "p025"}                            # planted: the gold answer is wrong or missing
AMBIGUOUS = {"p030"}                                         # planted: strong systems read it another way
abilities = [-1.0, -0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5]      # eight stand-in systems, weakest to strongest
RUNS = 4

def p_correct(ability, item):
    if item["id"] in BROKEN:
        return 0.02                                          # nobody can match a wrong key, apart from luck
    if item["id"] in AMBIGUOUS:
        return 1 / (1 + math.exp(ability - 0.5))             # better systems do worse
    x = ability - level[item["difficulty"]] + random.gauss(0, 0.3)
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-x))

# results[item][system] = fraction of the RUNS in which that system solved the item
results = {it["id"]: [sum(random.random() < p_correct(a, it) for _ in range(RUNS)) / RUNS
                      for a in abilities] for it in items}

def corr(x, y):
    mx, my = sum(x) / len(x), sum(y) / len(y)
    sx = math.sqrt(sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)); sy = math.sqrt(sum((b - my) ** 2 for b in y))
    return sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(x, y)) / (sx * sy) if sx and sy else 0.0

totals = [sum(results[i][s] for i in results) for s in range(len(abilities))]
print("system scores on all", len(items), "items:", "  ".join(f"{t/len(items):.0%}" for t in totals))
print(f"\n{'item':5} {'level':7} {'solve rate':>10} {'discrimination':>15}  flag")
flagged = []
for it in items:
    r = results[it["id"]]
    rate = sum(r) / len(r)
    rest = [totals[s] - r[s] for s in range(len(r))]          # each system's total without this item
    d = corr(r, rest)
    flag = ("REVIEW KEY" if rate <= 0.10 else "no signal" if rate >= 0.97
            else "negative discrimination" if d < -0.2 else "")
    if flag:
        flagged.append(it["id"])
        print(f"{it['id']:5} {it['difficulty']:7} {rate:10.0%} {d:15.2f}  {flag}")
print(f"\n{len(flagged)} of {len(items)} items flagged: {flagged}")
print(f"planted: broken keys {sorted(BROKEN)}, ambiguous {sorted(AMBIGUOUS)}")

Flags are leads for a human, not verdicts: with eight systems the discrimination figures are noisy (p025 shows a healthy-looking 0.43 despite a broken key, and is caught only by the zero solve rate). With real models, check the flagged items by reading the responses.

Common misconception

"A harder benchmark is a better benchmark, so filter out everything the current models get right." Filtering against specific models produces a benchmark that is hard for those models in the particular ways they fail now, not necessarily one that reflects real difficulty, and it ages fast as models change. It also concentrates the broken items: when you keep only items that models fail, wrong keys are over-represented among them. Keep a spread of difficulty, review hard items most carefully, and keep easy items as a floor.

Item response theory in one paragraph

Item response theory (IRT) is the statistical framework behind standardised exams. It models the chance that system s solves item i as a function of the system's ability and the item's difficulty (and, in richer versions, its discrimination), fitting both from the matrix of results. It lets you compare systems that were scored on different subsets of items and pick small, informative subsets: tinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024) reports that about 100 curated examples can be enough to estimate performance on the 14,000-question MMLU. As I understand the paper it builds on IRT-style models, though I read only its abstract. You do not need IRT to build a good domain benchmark, but the solve rate and discrimination above are its simplest ingredients, and the idea explains why a few well-chosen items can carry more information than many redundant ones.

Splits: dev, test and what to hide

If you tune a prompt or a retriever against the same items you report on, the reported score is optimistic, which is the evaluation version of overfitting. Split the items:

  • Dev: visible to everyone, used freely for prompts, thresholds and error analysis. Scores here are for iteration, not for claims.
  • Test: used rarely, for decisions and reports. For a shared benchmark, withhold the gold answers (or run scoring as a service) so the test set cannot be copied into training data or prompts.
  • Optionally a fresh reserve: items kept in a drawer, to replace the test set when it is spent (chapter 7).

Three rules make splits trustworthy. First, split by source and template, not by row: if three questions come from the same code section, or ten are generated from one seed, they belong together in one split, or the dev set quietly teaches the test (the grouped splits of cross-validation and the leakage patterns in Leakage apply directly). Second, make assignment stable: the validator hashes each id, so adding items never moves an existing item between splits. Third, log every look at the test set; repeated peeking is overfitting by committee.

Data cards and versioning

A benchmark without documentation gets used for things it was never designed to measure. Gebru and colleagues proposed "Datasheets for Datasets" (2018, published 2021): every dataset should come with documentation of its motivation, composition, collection process and recommended uses. Pushkarna, Zaldivar and Kjartansson (2022) described Data Cards, structured summaries of a dataset's provenance, collection and annotation, intended use and factors that affect performance, from deploying more than 20 of them. For a benchmark, a compact card should state:

  • the task specification and scoring rule, and what the scores are not evidence of;
  • where items came from, in what proportions, and when;
  • the labelling procedure, guideline version and agreement figures (chapter 5);
  • the coverage matrix, known gaps and known errors;
  • the splits, who may see what, and the contamination precautions (a canary string, withheld answers);
  • licence, privacy handling, maintainers and how to report errors;
  • the version, the content hash and the baseline scores with confidence intervals.

Version it like an API

A benchmark changes: keys are corrected, items are added, scoring improves. Changes must never silently alter what a score means. The convention that works is versioning in the style of API versions, with a content hash as the unambiguous identity.

The script below applies the rule mechanically: it compares two versions, classifies the change, and prints a compact data card as the record that travels with the release.

import json, hashlib

def load(path):
    return {it["id"]: it for it in map(json.loads, open(path))}

def content_hash(items):
    canonical = "\n".join(json.dumps(items[i], sort_keys=True) for i in sorted(items))
    return hashlib.sha256(canonical.encode()).hexdigest()[:16]

SCORED = ("question", "gold")            # fields that change what a correct answer is

def classify(old, new, scoring_changed=False):
    """Which part of MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH should change? Scores are only comparable within one MAJOR.MINOR."""
    if scoring_changed:
        return "major", "scoring rule changed: all earlier scores are void"
    removed, added = set(old) - set(new), set(new) - set(old)
    edited = [i for i in set(old) & set(new) if any(old[i].get(f) != new[i].get(f) for f in SCORED)]
    if removed or added or edited:
        return "minor", f"{len(added)} added, {len(removed)} removed, {len(edited)} edited: re-run baselines"
    return "patch", "metadata only: scores unchanged"

v1 = load("items.jsonl")
v11 = {i: dict(it) for i, it in v1.items()}
del v11["p012"], v11["p031"]                                  # the duplicate and the near-duplicate
v11["p018"]["gold"] = "the inspection is rescheduled and a re-inspection fee applies"
v11["p029"]["category"] = "setbacks"                          # metadata only
level, why = classify(v1, v11)
print(f"v1.0.0  {len(v1)} items  hash {content_hash(v1)}")
print(f"v1.1.0? {len(v11)} items  hash {content_hash(v11)}  -> bump {level}: {why}")

From items to a score you can defend

With the set built, a few practices turn it into evidence.

  1. Always include baselines. A trivial baseline (return the most common answer, copy the question), a simple one (keyword search plus rules) and the strongest system you can run. Their scores define the useful range. Without them, "68 percent" has no meaning.
  2. Add a human ceiling. Have the domain experts answer a sample under the same conditions. If the experts score 90 percent, the benchmark's own noise and ambiguity cap what anyone can reach; if they score 100 percent, there is headroom to measure.
  3. Report with error bars and a minimum detectable effect (chapter 4). With 381 test items, differences below about 9 points between two systems are unlikely to be detected at 80 percent power with one answer per question and moderate agreement between the systems, so say so.
  4. Pair every aggregate with slices. The coverage matrix is also the reporting layout: per-category results, with intervals, and the critical cells separately.
  5. Re-validate against reality. Compare benchmark scores with a sample of production outcomes each quarter. If the two drift apart, the benchmark is going stale; chapter 7 covers decay.
Step through

From a draft to release v1.0.0 (illustrative counts)

  1. Draft. Two permit officers write 520 items guided by the coverage matrix, with a gold answer and a code citation for each. Counts in this walk-through are illustrative, chosen to be plausible for a small team, not taken from a real project.
  2. Mechanical checks. The validator removes 18 exact and near-duplicates and rejects malformed rows. This is the cheapest stage and runs on every change from now on.
  3. Second expert. A different specialist answers every item blind. They disagree with the key on 41. Of those, 27 turn out to be errors in the key and are fixed; 14 are ambiguous or unanswerable as written and are dropped.
  4. Pilot run. Several stand-in systems answer every item several times. Twenty-two items are flagged (nobody solves them, or stronger systems do worse). An expert reads them: 15 had wrong or incomplete keys and are corrected, 7 are dropped.
  5. Split. Assignment by hash of id, grouped by source section, gives 100 dev items and 381 test items. Gold answers for the test items are withheld from the public copy.
  6. Release. The data card, content hash and canary string are attached, baseline scores with intervals are recorded, and the set is frozen as v1.0.0. The next change, however small, gets a new version.

Analogy: setting a driving test

A licensing authority does not test every road in the country. It defines what a safe driver must do, builds a route and a set of manoeuvres that sample those skills, writes marking rules so that examiners agree, trials the test on experienced drivers (who should pass) and beginners (who should not always), retires the exam once the route leaks, and versions the rules when they change. The pass mark means something because of that groundwork.

Where it breaks down: a human candidate cannot memorise the examiner's answer sheet in one night and then forget everything else. A model can have the test paper in its training data without anyone intending it, which is why the exam's secrecy and the question of leakage get a whole chapter next.

Systems connection

Much of a benchmark is data engineering you already know. The schema check is a contract on a file format, like the API schemas in APIs. The content hash is content addressing, the same idea that makes a git commit id name a state. Hash-based split assignment is a stable partition function, the idea behind consistent sharding. And "never edit in place; publish a new version" is the immutability rule that makes a release reproducible. When you evaluate a model you trained yourself, the checks in Evaluating a Model You Trained (A3) also depend on a clean, versioned held-out set.

Try it yourself

Put an items.jsonl file in the format shown (write 30 items for a domain you know, and plant a few defects of your own) and the three scripts in one folder. Run validate.py. Then break the benchmark on purpose: add ten reworded copies of one question and see which are flagged at the 0.5 threshold, then lower the threshold to 0.35 and watch false alarms appear between different questions about the same topic. Change dev_share to 0.5 and check that the items already in dev stay in dev when you add a new item. Finally, write the task specification for a domain you know, answering the six questions above in under 200 words.

State of play, October 2026

Benchmark quality is now an active research topic. BetterBench (Reuel et al., 2024) assessed 24 AI benchmarks against 46 best practices across the benchmark life cycle and reported that quality varies widely, that most did not report statistical significance and that most were not easy to replicate; it also offers a minimum quality checklist and a living repository of assessments. I read the abstract, not the full paper. Audits of prominent benchmarks keep finding errors in the items (Epoch's August 2026 review of Humanity's Last Exam, described above). The life cycle of a widely used coding benchmark that its main sponsor stopped reporting is the subject of the case study Retiring SWE-bench Verified; chapter 7 explains the mechanisms. Scores, leaderboards and sponsors' positions change quickly, so treat any specific ranking as dated.

Key takeaways

  • A benchmark is a curated, versioned set designed for comparison over time. Build it after error analysis, not before, and start from a one-page task specification covering input, output, scoring, scope and the decision it feeds.
  • Plan coverage with a matrix, mix item sources (real, expert-written, synthetic-then-reviewed), over-sample rare high-stakes cases, and decide per cell whether it is there to cover or to report.
  • Gold answers cite sources, are checked blind by a second expert, include unanswerable items on purpose and are assumed to contain errors until audited. Published benchmarks show error rates from about 3 to 46 percent depending on the set.
  • A pilot run with several systems finds broken keys (nobody solves), useless items (everybody solves) and ambiguous ones (strong systems fail). Flags are leads for an expert to read.
  • Split by source and template, assign by a stable hash, hide the test answers and log every look at the test set.
  • Document with a data card and version like an API, with a content hash. Compare scores only within one MAJOR.MINOR, and always report baselines, a human ceiling and error bars.

Check your understanding

In a pilot run, 8 systems of varied strength all fail item 214 across 4 attempts each. What is the most useful first step?

  1. Keep it: it shows the benchmark is hard
  2. Delete it, since it carries no ranking information
  3. Have an expert re-check the gold answer and the wording, because a key error or ambiguity is the most likely cause
  4. Re-run with a higher temperature to see if any system solves it
Items that no system solves are more often wrong keys or unanswerable questions than genuinely unsolved ones. Reading them is the cheapest audit available. Keeping it blindly risks a broken key, deleting it blindly throws away a possibly hard, valid item, and temperature changes only add noise.

A benchmark has 12 questions generated from each of 40 source passages (480 items). You split items at random into dev and test. What is the main risk?

  1. Questions from the same passage land in both splits, so tuning on dev leaks information about test
  2. The test set will be too small to compute a mean
  3. The two splits will have different difficulty by construction
  4. Hash-based assignment cannot be used
Items from one source are related, and a prompt or retriever tuned on dev questions about a passage can benefit on its test siblings. Split by source group. Size is not the issue (480 items is ample), and hashing can be done on the group id.

You correct the gold answers of three items in a published benchmark and nothing else. Under the versioning convention in this chapter, what should happen?

  1. A patch release, because only three items changed
  2. Overwrite the file in place, since the fix is an improvement
  3. A major release, because the answers changed
  4. A minor release, re-run the baselines and note the change in the errata log
Changing what counts as correct changes scores, so earlier numbers are no longer comparable for those items; that is a minor bump with baselines re-run. A patch must leave every score unchanged. A major release is for changes to the task or scoring rule. Silent overwriting makes two scores with the same name incomparable.

A team generates 5,000 questions by asking a model for ten variations of each of 500 seed questions, and reports "5,000 items". What is wrong with the claim of precision?

  1. Nothing: variations are independent items
  2. Variants of one seed are strongly related, so the effective sample is nearer 500 than 5,000, and the error bars must be clustered
  3. Model-generated questions cannot be used in a benchmark at all
  4. 5,000 is too many items to score
The seed is the sampling unit (chapter 4's clustering). Treating the variants as independent understates the error bars. Synthetic items can be used when reviewed by experts, and size is not a problem in itself.

Which statement about "filter the benchmark to the items the current best model gets wrong" is most accurate?

  1. It guarantees the benchmark measures real difficulty
  2. It removes all errors from the answer keys
  3. It tends to produce a set that is hard for those models in their current failure modes and has a higher share of broken keys among what remains
  4. It prevents contamination
Adversarial filtering selects for the model's current weaknesses and also for items whose keys are wrong, since the model cannot match a wrong key. The set may not reflect real-world difficulty and ages as models change. It does nothing for contamination.

Why include a human ceiling and baselines when reporting a benchmark score?

  1. To make the report longer and more convincing
  2. To give the score a scale: how much is chance or trivial, how much headroom exists, and whether noise in the benchmark caps results
  3. To show that the benchmark's items are not contaminated
  4. Because regulators require exactly three baselines
A bare "68 percent" is uninterpretable. Baselines and a human ceiling give it a floor and ceiling. They say nothing about contamination, and there is no fixed regulatory count.
