Error Analysis

Before you write a metric, read the outputs. This chapter teaches a method that turns a pile of model traces into a ranked list of failures you can fix. You read each trace and write a note. You group the notes into named kinds of failure. You count only the first thing that went wrong in each trace. Then you keep the resulting list honest.

Why this matters

Almost every team starts evaluation by choosing a metric: "helpfulness", "faithfulness", a score out of five from a model. Almost every one of those metrics later turns out to measure something other than what hurts users. Error analysis fixes the order of work. You look first, name what is wrong in your own words, count it, and only then decide how to measure it. After this chapter you will be able to run a review session on a hundred traces. You will produce a failure taxonomy with counts and say which fix is worth making first. Chapters 2 to 5 (harnesses, judges, statistics, labels) all take this taxonomy as their input.

Why you look before you measure

A conventional program fails in ways its author can mostly predict: a null pointer, a timeout, a wrong branch. A system built around a language model fails in ways nobody listed in advance. Its behaviour comes from a prompt, retrieved text and learned weights acting together. You cannot write the check for a failure you have not seen.

There is a second reason. When people grade outputs, they discover what they care about. Shankar and colleagues (2024), in "Who Validates the Validators?", studied developers building evaluators for language-model pipelines. They named the problem criteria drift. People need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps them define the criteria. A developer who starts with "answers should be concise" reads twenty outputs and realises the real requirement is "never invent a price". Written down first, the criteria would have been wrong. Read first, they improve.

Hamel Husain, who teaches this workflow widely, calls error analysis the highest-return activity in AI development. He gives an example from a property-management assistant. Reviewing real conversations showed that date handling failed in about two thirds of the cases that involved dates. Targeted fixes took the success rate on that problem from 33 percent to 95 percent. This is his report of one engagement, not a controlled study (Husain, 2024). The lesson that generalises is not the numbers. It is that one named, countable failure kind was hiding in the data, and no generic quality score would have pointed at it.

Analogy: the kitchen's returned plates

A restaurant that wants to improve does not start by buying a "taste score" meter. The chef looks at the plates that come back, sorts them into piles (too salty, cold, wrong order), and counts the piles. If forty percent of returns are cold dishes, the fix is in the pass between kitchen and floor, not in the recipes. Error analysis is the same sorting of returned plates, done on model outputs.

Where it breaks down: a returned plate has one flaw and one cause. A trace is a chain of steps, and a mistake in step two can show up as a different-looking mistake in step five. That is why this chapter spends time on finding the first failure.

What you are reading: traces

A trace is the full record of one request, in order. It holds the user input and the system prompt in force. It also holds every model call, every tool call with its arguments and result, any retrieved documents, and the final output. If you know distributed tracing, the idea is the same as a distributed trace with spans. The difference is that the payloads (the actual text) matter as much as the timings. Chapter 17 covers how to produce traces at scale. For now, assume you can export a list of traces as JSON or paste them into a spreadsheet.

Reading only the final answer is the most common mistake. A wrong final answer might have four causes. The wrong document may have been retrieved. The right document may have been ignored. A tool argument may have been malformed. Or the model itself may have erred. Those four causes have four different fixes. The trace is the only place that tells them apart.

Where the traces come from

The first batch should be a random sample of real usage. If you have no users yet, write down plausible inputs with people who would actually use the system. Or generate varied inputs with a model and edit them by hand. Vary the dimensions that matter. For the clinic assistant these are the kind of request, patient age, how vague the phrasing is, and how many details are given. Synthetic inputs are a bootstrap, not a substitute: replace them with real traces as soon as you have any.

Be careful with convenient samples. Reading only traces that got a thumbs-down will find some failures. It cannot tell you how common they are. Silent failures, where users left without clicking anything, stay invisible. Only reading traces from the loudest customer shows you that customer's problems. A random sample is the baseline. You can stratify it so that rare but important request types are represented. Targeted samples (low-confidence answers, long conversations, escalations) are additions to it.

How many traces

Husain and Shankar's published guidance is to start with about 100 diverse traces and continue while you are still learning. The stopping rule is borrowed from qualitative research. Keep reading until new traces stop revealing new kinds of failure or changing the ones you have. Social scientists call this theoretical saturation. A hundred is a starting point, not a law. A narrow system with one task may saturate in sixty. A broad assistant with twenty intents may need several hundred, or a separate pass per intent.

Open coding: write what you see

Open coding is the first pass. The word "coding" here is the social scientist's: attaching a short label or note to a piece of data. It is not programming. You read each trace and write a one- or two-sentence note about what, if anything, went wrong. You do not yet have categories. You are not asking "which of my ten failure types is this?" You are asking "what happened that I did not want?"

Good notes have three properties:

  • They describe an observation, not a diagnosis. "Booked 23 Oct when patient said 'next Friday' on Wed 14 Oct" is an observation. "The model is bad at dates" is a theory, and it may be wrong (perhaps the prompt gave the wrong current date).
  • They are specific enough to be recognised later. "Bad answer" tells you nothing at note 80. "Quoted the old 48 hour cancellation window" can be matched against other notes.
  • They are written fast. A minute a trace is plenty. Perfectionism here slows you down without improving the later grouping.

Tools for reviewing

Start with a spreadsheet: one row per trace, columns for the input, the output, a link to the full trace, and a free-text notes column. Teams that review regularly build a small custom viewer. It shows the whole conversation, tool calls and retrieved documents on one screen, with a text box for the note and keyboard shortcuts to move on. Husain reports that such viewers make review much faster. That is an experience claim, not a measurement. The investment pays back in the quality of the notes. When a trace takes five clicks to read, people read the last message and stop. The toolbox lists annotation tools.

Who reads

The reviewer must be able to tell a good answer from a bad one in your domain. For a clinic, that is someone who knows the booking rules, not only an engineer. For most small and medium projects, Husain and Shankar recommend appointing one domain expert as the final authority on quality. They call this person the "benevolent dictator". Committees produce vague standards and slow reviews. Chapter 5 returns to this with several annotators and measures of agreement. For a first pass, one expert reading 100 traces beats five people arguing over 20.

The first failure in each trace

Here is the most useful rule in this chapter. In each trace, find and record the first thing that went wrong. Not the worst, not the last, not the most visible: the first.

Why? Because a trace is a causal chain, and errors compound. Suppose step 2 resolves a date wrongly. Then the availability check in step 3 uses the wrong date. The booking in step 4 books the wrong day, and the confirmation in step 5 states the wrong day. A reviewer who starts from the final answer writes "confirmation is wrong" or "tool call has the wrong date". Both are true, and both are symptoms. Fixing the booking call alone will not help, because the call is faithfully copying a wrong value it was handed. Fixing the date resolution repairs all three.

Step through the example below. The reviewer reads backwards from the visible symptom and then finds where the chain began.

Step through

Reading back to the first failure

  1. A normal-looking trace. The patient wrote "next Friday" on Wednesday 14 October and meant Friday 16 October. Five spans: the user message, the model's reading of the date, an availability check, the booking and the reply.
  2. The visible symptom. The reviewer starts where the user sees the problem: the reply confirms 23 October, and the patient objects. A note written here, "confirmation has wrong date", is an observation but not yet a cause.
  3. One step back. The booking call also used 23 October. Is that the first error? It is wrong, but the tool did exactly what it was asked to do with the date it was handed. It is downstream.
  4. Another step back. The availability check used 23 October as well, and reported 3pm free on that day. Still faithful to its input, so still downstream.
  5. The first failure. Span 2 turned "next Friday" into 23 October. Everything after it is consistent with that wrong value. Record "date resolution" for this trace. A fix to the prompt or a date-parsing tool here would repair spans 3 to 5; patching span 5 alone would not.

Common misconception

"The last wrong thing in the trace is the failure." The last wrong thing is the one users see. That makes it the easiest to notice and the least useful to count. Counting last failures overstates the symptoms (wrong confirmations, wrong tool arguments) and understates causes (date resolution, retrieval). Count the first failure for each trace and the ranking changes, as the next section shows with numbers.

Exceptions: when there is no single first failure

Sometimes two independent things go wrong in one trace: the date is wrong and the answer is rude. Record the first failure for the main count, and note the second in a separate column. You are not trying to build a perfect causal model. You are trying to rank fixes. If a trace is so confusing that you cannot tell, mark it "unclear" and move on. If more than a few in ten are unclear, your traces are missing information. That is itself a finding (see Chapter 17).

Axial coding: from notes to a taxonomy

Axial coding is the second pass. Now that you have a hundred notes, you read them together. Group the ones that describe the same kind of problem, name each group, and count the members. The result is a failure taxonomy: a short list of named failure categories with counts.

Running it on 40 traces

The script below stands in for a reviewer's spreadsheet. It generates 40 synthetic traces (seeded), each with the notes a reviewer would have written in order, the first of which is the first failure. It then counts two ways: by the first failure only, and by every mention anywhere in the trace. The data is synthetic, built so that a wrong date often causes a later wrong tool call, which is the pattern in the stepper above. The point is the effect on the ranking, not the specific figures.

import random
from collections import Counter

random.seed(7)
NOTES = {
    "date":   ["said 'next Friday' -> booked the Friday after", "'tomorrow' resolved in UTC, patient is in Perth",
               "booked 03/04 as March 4, patient meant 3 April"],
    "policy": ["quoted old cancellation fee", "told patient no fee, policy says 24h notice", "missed the child-patient rule"],
    "tool":   ["passed dentist name where provider_id expected", "called book_slot twice", "forgot to call check_availability"],
    "ignore": ["patient said 'not mornings'; offered 9am", "ignored allergy note in profile"],
    "tone":   ["blunt reply to an anxious patient"],
}
WEIGHTS = {"date": 14, "policy": 9, "tool": 7, "ignore": 5, "tone": 2}
AXIAL = {"date": "date and time handling", "policy": "wrong or stale policy",
         "tool": "tool-call mistakes", "ignore": "ignored user context", "tone": "tone"}

traces = []
for i in range(40):
    if random.random() < 0.075:               # a clean trace
        traces.append({"id": i, "failures": []}); continue
    first = random.choices(list(WEIGHTS), weights=WEIGHTS.values())[0]
    fails = [(first, random.choice(NOTES[first]))]
    if first == "date" and random.random() < 0.7:   # a wrong date breaks the later tool call
        fails.append(("tool", "book_slot called with wrong date"))
    if first == "policy" and random.random() < 0.4:
        fails.append(("tone", "confident tone about the wrong fee"))
    traces.append({"id": i, "failures": fails})

first = Counter(t["failures"][0][0] for t in traces if t["failures"])
anyf = Counter(code for t in traces for code, _ in t["failures"])
bad = sum(1 for t in traces if t["failures"])
print(f"{bad} of {len(traces)} traces have at least one failure\n")
print(f"{'category':26} {'first failure':>13} {'any mention':>12}")
for code, n in first.most_common():
    print(f"{AXIAL[code]:26} {n:>13} {anyf[code]:>12}")
cum = 0
print("\ncumulative share of failing traces by first failure:")
for code, n in first.most_common():
    cum += n
    print(f"  {AXIAL[code]:26} {cum/bad:5.0%}")

Read the table. "Tool-call mistakes" appears in 20 traces if you count every mention, which would make it the top problem. By first failure it is only 6. In 14 of those 20 traces, the tool call was wrong because an earlier step handed it a wrong value. A team that counted mentions would have rewritten tool descriptions for a week. A team that counted first failures would have fixed date handling and removed about 44 percent of the failing traces in one change. The chart plots the first-failure column.

What makes a good taxonomy

A taxonomy is a working tool, so judge it by whether it lets you act. Five rules help:

  1. Name the failure, not the component. "Date and time handling" is a failure. "The LLM" is a component. A category should suggest a fix.
  2. Make categories mutually exclusive for the first failure. Each trace gets exactly one first-failure label, so the counts add up to the number of failing traces. If you cannot choose between two categories for a trace, your categories overlap: sharpen them.
  3. Keep an "other" bucket, and watch its size. If "other" grows past about a fifth of failures, you are missing a category.
  4. Aim for five to ten categories. One giant "wrong answer" bucket cannot be acted on; fifty tiny ones cannot be ranked. Merge categories that would get the same fix; split ones that would get different fixes.
  5. Write a one-line definition and one example for each. This is the beginning of an annotation guideline (Chapter 5) and of the rubric for a judge (Chapter 3).

Do not begin from a generic list ("hallucination, toxicity, relevance, coherence") and force traces into it. Generic lists are tempting because they save thinking, but they bias you toward the failures other people had and hide the ones your system has. The date failure in the example would be filed under "hallucination" or "incorrect" in most generic schemes, and the specific fix would be lost.

Using a model to help group notes

Once you have written your own notes, a language model is a reasonable assistant for the grouping step. Paste in the notes and ask for candidate clusters with names. This is quick, and it often catches synonyms ("wrong Friday", "off-by-a-week"). Treat the result as a first draft. Read each cluster's members and rename categories into your domain's words. Fix misfiled notes too. The model can only group what you wrote, and it may invent tidy-sounding categories that no trace supports. Do not skip the note-writing pass and ask a model to read the traces instead. The reading is where you learn what the system does, and that understanding is the product.

From categories to decisions

The ranked taxonomy tells you where the failures are. It does not tell you what to do about each one. For each of the top few categories ask four questions in order:

QuestionIf yesExample
Is it a plain bug or a missing instruction?Fix it now. No evaluation is needed to justify it.The prompt never states today's date or the patient's time zone.
Can code check it exactly?Write an assertion."The booked date equals the date parsed by a calendar library."
Does it need judgement but have a clear yes or no?Build a binary LLM judge and validate it against your labels."Reply respects the patient's stated time constraints."
Is it rare, or cheap and harmless?Note it and do not build an evaluator.Tone issues in 3 of 40 traces.

The first row matters more than it looks. Husain's guidance is to fix the obvious problems before building evaluation infrastructure. Writing an LLM judge for a failure whose cause is a missing sentence in the prompt is wasted effort. Fix the sentence, re-read a few traces, and confirm the failure is gone. Spend evaluation effort on failures that are subtle, recurring or costly if they return.

Systems connection

This is a postmortem practice applied to a whole sample instead of one incident. It is blameless and written in observable facts. It searches for contributing factors rather than the last visible symptom. The "first failure" rule is the same instinct as asking for the trigger and not only the impact in an incident review. The traces themselves are wide structured events. If you did not log the tool arguments, you cannot do this analysis later. Traces also contain user data, so retention and access rules from data protection apply to the review spreadsheet too.

Keeping the process honest

Saturation, and what to do when you reach it

Track how many new categories each batch of ten traces produces. In the early batches almost every trace adds something. By trace sixty you may find that nine of ten notes fit existing categories. When two successive batches add nothing new, you have probably saturated for this input distribution. That is a statement about the sample you read. If a new feature, customer or language arrives, the distribution has changed and you must sample again.

Rare failures

Random sampling finds common failures. A failure that occurs in 0.5 percent of traces will appear in about half of random samples of 100 and be missed by the rest. This uses the same arithmetic as Chapter 0. If the rare failure is dangerous (a wrong drug dose, a leaked document), do not rely on random reading. Use targeted searches instead. Try keyword filters, traces where the model abstained or the user rephrased, and traces with long tool chains. Add deliberately written adversarial inputs (Chapter 16). Rare-and-severe failures usually deserve a dedicated test set rather than a place in the main taxonomy.

Reviewer fatigue and drift

Review quality drops after an hour of reading. Work in sessions of twenty to thirty traces. Re-read your first few notes at the end of a session. If your standards have shifted, say so and re-label those. That drift is the criteria drift described earlier, and it is not a mistake to be ashamed of. Write the revised criteria down as you go.

Do not let one session become the truth

The taxonomy is a snapshot. After you fix date handling, the next sample will show a different ranking. The top failure is gone and the second now leads. Re-run the analysis after each significant change, on a fresh random sample. Never reuse the sample you used to fix things. This is the heartbeat of the loop. Chapter 18 turns this into a weekly process fed by production traces.

Taxonomies for common system shapes

Your own taxonomy should come from your own traces. After you have one, compare it against the failure kinds other people have found in similar systems. Use them as a checklist of things you might have missed, not as a starting scheme:

Retrieval-augmented answers

The right document was not retrieved. Or it was retrieved but ranked below the cut-off. Or it was in the context and ignored. Or it was stale. Or the answer was right but the citation was wrong. For retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems, see How RAG Fails and Chapter 12.

Tool-using agents

The agent chose the wrong tool. Or it chose the right tool with malformed arguments. Or it ignored an error result, looped, stopped early or took an unsafe action. Or it reached the right end state by a wasteful route. Chapter 8 grades these end to end.

Conversation and writing

Missed an instruction; invented a fact; wrong register; failed to ask a clarifying question; refused something harmless; complied with something it should have refused. Chapters 10 and 15.

Try it yourself

Run an error-analysis session on any assistant you can access. You need 20 minutes and a text file.

  1. Write 20 realistic requests in your own domain (work, study, cooking, code). Vary them: short and long, clear and vague.
  2. Run all 20. For each, write one note: what, if anything, went wrong. Write "fine" for good ones.
  3. Group the notes into categories and count them. If a trace had several problems, count only the first.
  4. Sort the categories by count. Which one would you fix first, and is it a bug you can fix right now or something you would have to measure?

Then modify ea.py above: change WEIGHTS and the cascade probability (0.7) and watch how the "any mention" column diverges from "first failure". What cascade probability makes the two columns agree?

State of play, October 2026

Open-source and commercial platforms such as Langfuse and Arize Phoenix offer trace viewers with annotation and tagging. Many teams build a custom viewer in an afternoon. LLM-assisted clustering of notes and traces is now common. Which tool you use matters much less than the habit, so check the toolbox for current options. The workflow described here (open coding, axial coding, first failure, saturation) follows Husain and Shankar's published guidance and the Evals FAQ, which they have updated since 2024.

Key takeaways

  • Look before you measure. You cannot write a check for a failure you have not seen, and reading outputs changes what you think matters (criteria drift).
  • Read whole traces from a random sample of real usage, about 100 to start, until new traces stop revealing new failure kinds.
  • Open coding writes specific, observational notes; axial coding groups them into five to ten named, actionable categories with counts.
  • Record the first failure in each trace. Counting every mention overstates downstream symptoms and sends fixes to the wrong place.
  • Fix plain bugs immediately, use code assertions for exact rules, build validated judges for the subtle ones, and ignore the rare and harmless.
  • The taxonomy is a snapshot: re-sample after each change, on traces you did not tune on.

Check your understanding

You review 50 traces of a research assistant and tally every failure mentioned in each. "Wrong citation format" appears 31 times and "searched for the wrong terms" 12 times. What should you check before fixing citation formatting?

  1. Whether the formatting rule is in the system prompt
  2. Whether the judge agrees with you
  3. How many of the traces with citation errors had a different failure earlier in the trace
  4. Whether the sample is large enough for a confidence interval
Counting every mention conflates symptoms with causes. If bad searches produced irrelevant sources, formatting them correctly was never going to help. Count the first failure in each trace before ranking. The prompt check is a reasonable later step, and confidence intervals come in Chapter 4, but neither addresses the ranking problem.

Which note is the best open-coding note?

  1. "Asked for refund within 30 days; assistant said refunds need 60 days"
  2. "Policy hallucination"
  3. "The model is weak at policy questions"
  4. "Bad"
The first states what happened and can be matched against other notes later. "Policy hallucination" is already a category, chosen before you have seen the other traces. The third is a theory about the model, and "Bad" carries no information.

After two successive batches of ten traces, none produced a new failure category. What does this license?

  1. Concluding the system has no other failures
  2. Stopping error analysis permanently
  3. Concluding the existing categories are all equally common
  4. Treating the taxonomy as adequate for this input distribution for now, and re-sampling when the inputs or the system change
Saturation is a statement about the sample read. Rare failures can still be missing, and the distribution will shift with new users or features. It does not tell you the categories are equally common, and it does not end the loop.

Why should the traces you use to find failures differ from the ones you use afterwards to check that a fix worked?

  1. Fixed traces are too easy to read quickly
  2. You tuned the fix to those traces, so improvement on them overstates improvement in general
  3. Old traces contain personal data
  4. A model cannot be re-run on the same input twice
This is the same overfitting you met in the machine-learning course: any fix shaped by specific examples will look better on them than on fresh data. Re-run on those traces as a regression check, but judge improvement on a fresh sample.

The prompt for a billing assistant never tells it today's date, and 14 of 100 traces fail on relative dates. What is the best next step?

  1. Build an LLM judge for date correctness first, so the effect of changes can be measured
  2. Add the current date and the user's time zone to the prompt, then re-read a fresh sample; build a code assertion if the failure matters enough to guard
  3. Fine-tune the model on date examples
  4. Switch to a larger model
A missing instruction is a plain bug with an obvious cheap fix. Evaluation infrastructure and bigger models are heavy responses to a one-line cause. After the fix, a deterministic assertion (compare the booked date to a library's parse) is the right guard because the rule is exact.

Which taxonomy is most useful for ranking fixes?

  1. Twelve generic categories chosen before reading any traces: hallucination, toxicity, relevance, coherence and so on
  2. One category, "bad answers", with a count
  3. Six categories named from the reviewer's own notes, mutually exclusive on the first failure, with an "other" bucket
  4. A separate category for every individual trace
Categories drawn from your notes reflect your system's real failures and suggest fixes. Generic lists hide system-specific problems, one bucket cannot be ranked, and one category per trace cannot be counted.
