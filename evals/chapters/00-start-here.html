Anyone can build a demo that works on the five examples they tried. This course is about the harder question that decides whether an AI system is worth shipping: how do you know it works, how do you know when it stops working, and how do you keep it from doing harm? This page gives you the map and one small calculation that shows why the habit of "trying a few things" fails.

Why this matters

The earlier AI courses taught you to build the pieces: models, agents, retrieval. A model-based system differs from ordinary programs in one awkward way: its behaviour is not specified by code you can read, so you cannot know what it does without observing it. Evaluation is how you observe. Teams that do it well ship faster, because every change comes with evidence; teams that do not spend their time arguing about anecdotes. After this page you will know the loop the whole course repeats, how the chapters fit together and how to study them.

Why "it looked good when I tried it" is not evidence

Suppose you have written a customer-support assistant for a clinic. You try five questions and all five answers look fine. How confident should you be?

Less than you think, and the arithmetic is short. Say the assistant gets 10 in every 100 answers wrong. Each question you try has a 90 percent chance of looking fine, so the chance that five questions all look fine is 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9, about 59 percent. A system that fails one answer in ten passes your five-question check more often than not. The next script computes the figure exactly and then confirms it by simulation (seeded, so you get the same numbers).

import math, random
random.seed(1)
fail_rate = 0.10
for n in (5, 20, 30, 100):
    print(f"{n:>3} spot checks: chance of seeing no failure at all = {(1-fail_rate)**n:6.1%}")
need = math.ceil(math.log(0.05) / math.log(1 - fail_rate))
print(f"checks needed to see at least one failure with 95% probability: {need}")
# and a simulation of the 'tried five prompts, looks fine' habit
trials = 10000
clean = sum(all(random.random() > fail_rate for _ in range(5)) for _ in range(trials))
print(f"simulated: {clean/trials:.1%} of 5-example sessions found nothing wrong")
  5 spot checks: chance of seeing no failure at all = 59.0%
 20 spot checks: chance of seeing no failure at all = 12.2%
 30 spot checks: chance of seeing no failure at all =  4.2%
100 spot checks: chance of seeing no failure at all =  0.0%
checks needed to see at least one failure with 95% probability: 29
simulated: 58.2% of 5-example sessions found nothing wrong

That is only the chance of seeing a failure. Knowing how often it happens, whether the last change made things better, and whether the failures cluster in one kind of question needs more still. A 10 percent failure rate is also a mild case: in medicine, law or finance a 1 percent rate of confident wrong answers can already be unacceptable, and finding it needs hundreds of checks. That is why evaluation is a skill with methods, and why this course is long.

Common misconception

"Evals are a test suite you write once, after the product works." In practice the evaluation work shapes the product. You cannot decide what to build next without knowing what fails today, and you cannot know what fails today without looking at real outputs. The suite grows out of that looking; it does not come before it.

The loop this course teaches

Every method in this course is one step of a single loop. Read the loop once now; the rest of the course fills in each box.

  1. Look. Read real inputs and the system's full trace (the record of everything it did to produce an answer: prompts, tool calls, retrieved documents, final text). Chapter 1.
  2. Name. Write down what went wrong in plain words, then group the notes into a failure taxonomy, a short list of named failure kinds with counts. Chapter 1.
  3. Measure. Turn each important failure kind into a check that a machine can run: a code assertion where the rule is crisp, an LLM judge (a model that grades another model's output) where it is not, and statistics so you know how much to trust the number. Chapters 2 to 5.
  4. Change. Edit the prompt, the retrieval, the tools or the model, and re-run the measurements. This is the step most people start with; the loop puts it fourth.
  5. Guard. Keep the checks as a regression suite (tests that fail if an old problem returns), watch production, and defend against people who attack the system on purpose. Chapters 13 to 20.

Notice what the loop does not contain: a leaderboard, a benchmark score or a vendor's "accuracy" number. Those come in Part 3 as inputs to the loop, useful for choosing a model and dangerous when mistaken for a measurement of your task. The point of the first six chapters is to produce evidence about your own system, from your own data.

Systems connection

If you have run production services, this course will feel familiar. An SLO is a measurable promise about behaviour, checked continuously. An eval is the same idea for a property that code cannot check directly, such as "the answer is supported by the document". The difference is that for model systems you must first discover which properties matter, by reading outputs, before you can write the SLO.

How to study this course

  • Depth tags. Every chapter is tagged Deep dive, Core or Quick intro. Deep chapters carry derivations and failure modes and take the longest; read a Quick intro in one sitting and come back for the others with the exercises done.
  • Labs run offline. Each lab is a Python folder with a check.py that tells you which milestones pass. Labs use the standard library and scripted stand-in models, so they cost nothing and give the same answer every time. Each lab page shows how to point the code at a real model afterwards.
  • Case studies and studios. Cases read one real event closely (a retired benchmark, a court filing built on invented citations). Studios are mock design sessions: write your own answer before opening the worked one.
  • Do the "Try it yourself" boxes. The ideas in this course are about data. You will retain them only if you have looked at some.
  • Keep a notebook of failures. From Chapter 1 onward, collect ten real outputs from any model you use, and write what is wrong with each. That notebook is your first dataset.

Try it yourself

Take any assistant you use regularly and write down five questions you would ask in normal use. Ask them, and for each answer write "pass" or "fail" and one sentence of why, before deciding what to improve. Then ask yourself: if the failure rate were 10 percent, how likely was it that your five questions showed you nothing? You have just done a tiny round of steps 1 and 2 of the loop, and met its limit.

Key takeaways

  • Trying a few examples proves little: at a 10 percent failure rate, five good-looking answers happen about 59 percent of the time.
  • The course teaches one loop: look, name, measure, change, guard, then look again.
  • Reading real traces comes before metrics, and metrics come with error bars.
  • Labs run offline and deterministically; case studies and studios apply the ideas to real situations.
  • Evaluation connects backwards to agents and retrieval, sideways to reliability and security, and forwards to self-improving systems and delivery.

Further reading

  • Hamel Husain, "A Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products" (2024). Argues that error analysis on real data is the highest-return activity in AI development; the spine of the first chapters.
  • Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar, "Evals FAQ". Practical answers on how many traces to read, who labels, and how to validate judges.
  • Shankar et al., "Who Validates the Validators?" (2024). Shows that people's criteria change as they read outputs, which is why evaluation is iterative.
  • Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena" (2023). The paper that made model-based grading standard, and named its biases.

Check your understanding

A system fails 5 percent of requests. You try 10 prompts and all look fine. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The system is probably free of failures
  2. Little: a clean run of 10 happens about 60 percent of the time at this failure rate
  3. The failure rate must be below 1 percent
  4. The failure rate is exactly 5 percent
0.9510 is about 0.60, so ten clean answers carry almost no information about a 5 percent failure rate. Option 3 confuses "I saw no failures" with "failures are rarer than one in a hundred", which would need hundreds of checks.

In the loop taught here, which step comes immediately before measuring?

  1. Change the prompt
  2. Choose a benchmark
  3. Deploy to production
  4. Name the failures you saw
You cannot write a useful check until you know what kind of failure you are checking for, and you learn that by reading traces and naming what you find. Changing the prompt comes after measuring, so you can tell whether the change helped.

Why does the course put public benchmarks after error analysis and harnesses?

  1. They measure general ability, not your task, so they are inputs to your own evidence rather than a replacement for it
  2. They are always contaminated
  3. They are too expensive to run
  4. They cannot be reproduced
Benchmarks are useful for shortlisting models, but they say little about whether your clinic assistant handles "next Friday" correctly. Contamination is a real concern (Chapter 7) but not the main reason for the ordering.

What does the dashed arrow from Guard back to Look represent?

  1. Repeating the same test until it passes
  2. Rolling back a bad release
  3. Production traffic producing new traces, and new traces revealing failures no earlier test anticipated
  4. Asking the model to review its own work
Users do things test authors did not imagine, so the loop restarts from real traces. Re-running a test until it passes would be flaky-test abuse, not evaluation.
