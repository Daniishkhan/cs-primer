Guardrail Classifiers & Content Policy

A guardrail is a check that sits beside the model and decides whether a message, a document, an answer or an action may pass. This chapter covers the policy that tells such a check what to look for, the main kinds of classifier, the PII filters that nearly every product needs, and the part teams skip: measuring how much of the bad traffic a guardrail actually catches, and what it costs in wrongly blocked good traffic.

Why this matters

The previous two chapters argued that you cannot rely on filters to stop a determined injection, and that the stronger defence is to limit what a fooled model can do. That does not make classifiers useless. They are the right tool for a different job: enforcing a content policy (no personalised investment advice from a support bot, no card numbers stored in logs, no abusive replies) against the large volume of ordinary, non-adaptive traffic. Teams get this wrong in two predictable ways. They ship a guardrail with no written policy behind it, so nobody can say what it should catch, and they report one accuracy number that hides both the misses and the false alarms. After this chapter you will be able to write a testable policy, choose a classifier type by its cost and failure modes, build and test a PII filter, and report guardrail recall with intervals, by category and by kind of wording, next to the false-positive rate.

What a guardrail is, and where it can sit

A guardrail is a component outside the main model that inspects something and returns a verdict: allow, block, rewrite or escalate to a person. It is deliberately separate from the model, for the same reason a payment system checks a transaction with code that is not the code that created it: the checker should not share the generator's blind spots, and you should be able to change, test and measure it on its own.

There are four natural places to put one, and a real product usually uses more than one.

  • Input check. Looks at what the user typed before the model sees it. It can refuse out-of-scope or abusive requests cheaply and strip personal data before it reaches a provider or a log.
  • Context check. Looks at retrieved passages and tool results before they enter the prompt. This is where you would screen a retrieved web page, as the Prompt Injection & Jailbreaks chapter describes, and where document-level permissions are enforced (Filters, Metadata & Permissions).
  • Output check. Looks at the draft answer before the user sees it: policy violations, leaked personal data, missing citations, unsupported claims (Fact-Checking & Citation Verification).
  • Action check. Looks at a proposed tool call (its name and arguments) before it runs. This is the one place a guardrail can be a strict rule in code instead of a statistical classifier, and for agents it matters most (Architectural Defences).
Content policy
The written statement of what the system may and may not say or do, with examples and the action to take for each case.
Classifier
A function that assigns a message to a category or gives it a risk score; in a guardrail, the score is compared with a threshold to produce a verdict.
Guardrail recall
Of the inputs the policy says must be caught, the share the guardrail did catch. Its partner is the false-positive rate: of the inputs that should pass, the share wrongly blocked.
Over-refusal
Blocking or refusing a legitimate request because it looks like a prohibited one.

Write the policy before the classifier

A classifier needs a definition of its target, and "unsafe" is not one. Consider a support bot for a retail bank. Its owners may decide that the bot must not give personalised investment advice (which regulation may treat differently from general information), must not reveal another customer's data, and must stay polite. Those are three different rules with different consequences, and a single "is this harmful?" score cannot tell them apart.

A usable policy has the same parts whatever the product.

  1. Scope. What the assistant is for, and what is out of scope. Out of scope is not the same as forbidden: "ask a financial adviser" is a good answer.
  2. Categories. A short list, each with a one-sentence definition, a handful of clear examples that must be blocked, a handful of near-miss examples that must pass, and the hard edge cases with a ruling.
  3. An action per category. Block with a fixed message, redirect, answer with a disclaimer, mask a part, send to a person, or log only. The right action for a slur in a user message differs from the right action for a card number.
  4. An owner and a review date. Policy changes when law, product or abuse patterns change, and someone has to decide.

The near-miss column is the one teams omit and the one that does the most work. It is what stops the guardrail from becoming a keyword blocker, and it gives you the negatives you need to measure false positives later. Every row of such a table becomes labelled test data: a policy you cannot turn into examples is a policy you cannot test. Disagreements when two people label the same example are information about the policy, not noise to be averaged away (see Human Labels).

Do not start from a blank page

Published taxonomies are a good checklist for general-purpose assistants. Meta's Llama Guard 3 model card, which I fetched and read, says the model was aligned to the MLCommons standardised hazards taxonomy and lists fourteen categories, from S1 Violent Crimes through S10 Hate and S11 Suicide & Self-Harm to S14 Code Interpreter Abuse. The same card makes a point worth keeping: some categories, such as S5 Defamation, S8 Intellectual Property and S13 Elections, may need factual, up-to-date knowledge to judge, and for use cases highly sensitive to them the authors say that more complex systems should be deployed. A generic taxonomy covers harms in general. It does not know your bank's investment-advice rule, so you add your own categories on top and treat the generic ones as defaults to review, not as the policy.

Kinds of classifier

All the options answer the same question, "does this text fall in a category?", and they differ in cost, speed and the way they fail.

KindHow it decidesStrengthsTypical failure

Llama Guard (Inan et al., 2023, "Llama Guard: LLM-based Input-Output Safeguard for Human-AI Conversations") is the standard example of the safeguard-model row. Its abstract describes a Llama 2 7B model instruction-tuned on a collected dataset, which classifies prompts and responses against a safety risk taxonomy, produces a binary decision score, and can be adapted to a different taxonomy through its instructions. That last point is the practical draw: you can put your own categories in the prompt, though you should then measure how well it follows them rather than assume it does. Llama Guard 3, per its model card, adds eight languages and checks for tool use such as search and code interpreter calls.

Two design decisions apply to every kind. The first is latency placement. An input check can run in parallel with the model call and cancel the result if it blocks, which hides its latency at the cost of paying for tokens you throw away; or it can run first, which is cheaper and slower. An output check must see the answer, so with streaming you either buffer until it passes or check chunks as they arrive and accept that some text may already be on screen (queueing and tail effects here follow Tail Latency at Scale: a guard on the critical path adds its own p99). The second is failing open or closed: when the classifier times out, does the request pass or stop? For a PII mask or an action check, closed is the usual answer. For a tone check on a chat reply, open with an alert may be right. Decide on purpose; the default in a library is rarely the one you want.

A worked comparison: rules against a trained classifier

The script below builds two classifiers for the bank's first rule, "block personalised investment advice". One is a keyword list. The other is a naive Bayes word model trained on only twenty labelled messages (ten to block, ten to allow), which is deliberately small so you can read every number. Both are tested on three kinds of blocked message, six of each: the plain wording the training data resembles, paraphrase that avoids the obvious words, and obfuscated text with digit-for-letter swaps and spacing. They are also tested on twelve benign messages, several of which are near misses. The intervals are Wilson 95% intervals, which stay sensible for small samples (Statistics for Evals).

import math, re
from collections import Counter

# ---- classifier 1: keyword rules -------------------------------------------------
KEYWORDS = ["invest", "shares", "stock", "fund", "bonds", "crypto", "portfolio", "pension"]
def rules_score(t):
    return 1.0 if any(k in t.lower() for k in KEYWORDS) else 0.0

# ---- classifier 2: naive Bayes over words (trained from 20 examples) -------------
def toks(t): return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9']+", t.lower())
cb, co = Counter(), Counter()
for t in TRAIN_BLOCK: cb.update(toks(t))
for t in TRAIN_OK: co.update(toks(t))
vocab = set(cb) | set(co)
def nb_score(t):
    lb = lo = 0.0
    nb, no = sum(cb.values()), sum(co.values())
    for w in toks(t):
        lb += math.log((cb[w] + 1) / (nb + len(vocab) + 1))
        lo += math.log((co[w] + 1) / (no + len(vocab) + 1))
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(lo - lb))      # probability of BLOCK with equal priors

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d; h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return max(0, c - h), min(1, c + h)

def report(name, score, thr):
    print(f"\n{name}  (block if score >= {thr})")
    for variant, texts in TEST_BLOCK.items():
        k = sum(score(t) >= thr for t in texts); lo, hi = wilson(k, len(texts))
        print(f"  recall on {variant:<11} {k}/{len(texts)} = {k/len(texts):.2f}   95% CI {lo:.2f} to {hi:.2f}")
    fp = [t for t in TEST_OK if score(t) >= thr]; lo, hi = wilson(len(fp), len(TEST_OK))
    print(f"  false-positive rate    {len(fp)}/{len(TEST_OK)} = {len(fp)/len(TEST_OK):.2f}   95% CI {lo:.2f} to {hi:.2f}")
    for t in fp: print(f"     wrongly blocked: {t!r}")

report("keyword rules", rules_score, 1.0)
report("naive Bayes", nb_score, 0.5)

Read the results in order. On plain wording both catch everything, and a team that tested only there would ship either. On paraphrase, the rules catch two of six (an overlap with "stocks" and "funds"), which fits the general lesson that a keyword list encodes your first guess at wording and nothing more. On obfuscated text the rules catch none: "sh4res" is not "shares".

The Bayes model scores 6 of 6 on the obfuscated text, which looks like robustness and is not. Look at what it wrongly blocks: "where can I see my savings balance", "tell me which branch is nearest" and "should I use a standing order or direct debit". Its twenty training messages let it learn that words like which, should and my lean towards "advice", so it blocks advice-shaped questions in general. It caught the obfuscated cases partly because they share that question style, not because it understood the obfuscation. This is a standard way for a small classifier to look good: check false positives on benign messages that share the style of the bad ones, and be suspicious when recall rises with no change in what the classifier can actually see.

Also look at the intervals. Six cases per variant give an interval for "6 of 6" of 0.61 to 1.00, and a false-positive rate of 3 of 12 spans 0.09 to 0.53. Nothing here is a number to quote. It is enough to see the pattern and to know that the real evaluation needs hundreds of examples, which the last section sizes.

The last block of output shows the threshold at work. A probability-style score lets you trade recall against false positives by moving one number. Here, moving it from 0.5 to 0.65 cuts false positives from 25% to 8% at no cost in recall on this tiny set, which usually means the set is too small to show the cost. The right threshold is a business decision made with the costs in view: a wrongly blocked customer question has a price (a call to the contact centre), a wrongly allowed piece of advice has a different price (a compliance report), and the threshold should sit where you would be least surprised by the next hundred cases of each.

Analogy: the airport security lane

A metal detector has a sensitivity dial. Turn it up and it catches smaller objects and stops more people with belt buckles and keys; turn it down and the queue moves but something gets through. Nobody asks whether the detector is "accurate". They ask how often it misses a real threat and how many innocent travellers it stops, and they pick the dial setting knowing both. Layers (a bag scan, a random check, a person with a wand) catch different things at different prices.

Where it breaks down: a traveller does not study the detector and redesign their luggage after each failed attempt, but an attacker on a text system can probe the guardrail thousands of times for free and adapt. Recall measured on a fixed list of past bad messages overstates what an adaptive attacker will find, which the next chapter measures directly.

PII filters

The most common guardrail in production is not a safety classifier at all. It is a filter for personally identifiable information (PII): data that identifies a person or can be linked to one, such as names, email addresses, phone numbers, card numbers and national identifiers. Personal data belongs in the data-protection discussion of Data Protection & Privacy, and the AI-specific retention and provider questions come in Privacy & Data Handling for AI. This section covers the mechanics, because a PII filter is a good model of every guardrail: part exact, part statistical, with a recall ceiling you can measure.

What a filter does with a hit

Finding the entity is half of the job. The action matters just as much, and there are four common choices.

  • Block the whole message. Safe, and unfriendly: the customer wanted to ask about a refund.
  • Redact: replace the span with a fixed marker such as [CARD]. The model cannot use the value, which is usually what you want.
  • Pseudonymise: replace each value with a consistent placeholder ([EMAIL_1]) and keep the mapping on your side, so the model can reason about "the email the user gave" and you can put the real value back into the reply or a tool call. This keeps the conversation coherent, and it moves the secret into a mapping table that you must now protect.
  • Hash or tokenise for analytics, so you can count distinct users without keeping the value.

Patterns, checksums and models

Structured identifiers suit rules. A regular expression finds candidate digit runs, and a checksum (a formula the number must satisfy) removes most false matches. Payment card numbers carry a Luhn check: double every second digit from the right, subtract 9 from any result above 9, add everything, and the total must be divisible by 10. An order reference with sixteen digits will match a card-shaped pattern, but only about one random sixteen-digit string in ten passes the checksum. Free-text entities such as names and street addresses have no fixed shape, so they need a trained named-entity model, a dictionary, or context words ("my name is…"), and none of these is exact. Microsoft's Presidio, an open-source toolkit for detecting and anonymising personal data in text and images, is built on exactly this mix of pattern recognisers, validation, context and NLP models; I read its README for this edition, which notes that the project has moved to a new GitHub organisation (data-privacy-stack), so check the current location before depending on a link.

The script builds a small filter with the three patterns above, a deliberately naive first version, and measures recall per entity type on synthetic messages. It then does what you would do after reading the misses: it widens the patterns, and measures again.

import random, re
from collections import defaultdict

random.seed(15)

def luhn_ok(digits):
    total = 0
    for i, ch in enumerate(reversed(digits)):
        d = int(ch)
        if i % 2 == 1:
            d = d * 2 - 9 if d * 2 > 9 else d * 2
        total += d
    return total % 10 == 0

V1 = {  # version 1: the obvious patterns
    "EMAIL": r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.-]+",
    "PHONE": r"(?:\+44\s?\d{3,4}|0\d{3,4})[ -]?\d{3}[ -]?\d{3,4}\b",
    "CARD":  r"\b(?:\d[ -]?){13,19}\b",
}
V2 = dict(V1)  # version 2: after reading the misses, tolerate dots and "at"/"dot"
V2["EMAIL"] = r"[\w.+-]+(?:@| at )[\w-]+(?:\.| dot )[\w.-]+"
V2["CARD"] = r"\b(?:\d[ .-]?){13,19}\b"

def detect(text, patterns, luhn=True):
    out = []
    for kind, pat in patterns.items():
        for m in re.finditer(pat, text):
            s = m.group(0).strip()
            if kind == "CARD" and luhn and not luhn_ok(re.sub(r"\D", "", s)):
                continue
            out.append((kind, s))
    return out

def make_card():
    while True:
        body = "".join(random.choice("0123456789") for _ in range(15))
        for c in "0123456789":
            if luhn_ok(body + c):
                return body + c

for i in range(30):

    ph = f"07{random.randint(100,999)} {random.randint(100,999)} {random.randint(100,999)}"
    CLEAN += [(f"Email me at user{i}@shop-mail.example please.", {("EMAIL", f"user{i}@shop-mail.example")}),
              (f"Ring {ph} after five.", {("PHONE", ph)}),
              (f"My card is {g} and it expired.", {("CARD", g)})]
    MESSY += [(f"mail me: user{i} at shop-mail dot example", {("EMAIL", f"user{i} at shop-mail dot example")}),
              (f"card number {d} thanks", {("CARD", d)}),
              (f"it is zero seven {random.randint(100,999)} {random.randint(100,999)} {random.randint(100,999)}", {("PHONE", "spelled out")})]
    REFS.append((f"Order ref " + " ".join(str(random.randint(1000, 9999)) for _ in range(4)) + " shipped.", set()))
    NAMES.append((f"Hello, this is {random.choice(['Priya Raman','Tom Okafor'])} from accounts.", {("NAME", "x")}))

def evaluate(title, data, patterns, luhn=True):
    stats = defaultdict(lambda: [0, 0, 0])  # tp, fn, fp per type
    for text, gold in data:
        got = set(detect(text, patterns, luhn))
        gold_kinds = {k for k, _ in gold}
        for kind, s in got:
            if (kind, s) in gold: stats[kind][0] += 1
            else: stats[kind][2] += 1
        for kind, s in gold:
            if (kind, s) not in got:
                stats[kind][1] += 1
    cells = []
    for kind in sorted(stats):
        tp, fn, fp = stats[kind]
        r = tp / (tp + fn) if tp + fn else None
        cells.append(f"{kind} recall={'n/a' if r is None else f'{r:.2f}'} (n={tp+fn}) false alerts={fp}")
    print(f"{title:<30}" + " | ".join(cells))

evaluate("messy, v1 patterns", MESSY, V1)
evaluate("messy, v2 patterns", MESSY, V2)
evaluate("names, v2 patterns", NAMES, V2)

Four things stand out. On clean formats the first version is perfect, which is why a demo always looks fine. On messy formats (an email written as "user at shop-mail dot example", a card number with dots) it catches nothing, and a second round of pattern tuning fixes email and card and not the phone number written out in words. Names are unreachable for these patterns at all: recall is 0 of 30, which is not a bug in the regular expressions but a limit of the method. And the Luhn check cut false alerts on card-shaped order references from 30 to 4, which is the expected one-in-ten pass rate for random digits, so a residual false alarm rate is built in.

The practical conclusion is a recall ceiling that you can state in advance: a pattern-based PII filter has high recall on well-formed structured data and low recall on everything else. A policy that says "no personal data reaches the logs" is therefore not met by this filter alone. You combine it with minimising what you collect, with a model-based recogniser for names, and with retention limits, and you measure what gets through by sampling the stored data and having a person look.

Common misconception

"The guardrail is 99% accurate, so it is safe." Accuracy is the share of all decisions that were correct. If 99.9% of messages are fine, a guardrail that blocks nothing is 99.9% accurate. What matters is recall on the bad class, the false-positive rate on the good class, and how often the bad class occurs. A second misconception sits beside it: that a guardrail is a security boundary. A content filter reduces the volume of policy violations in ordinary traffic. A person who wants to get past it can try variants until one works, so for anything with real consequences (data leaving, money moving) put the control in an access rule or a capability limit, and treat the classifier as the first layer, not the last.

Measuring guardrail recall

You measure a guardrail the way you measure any binary classifier, with the extra care that the bad class is rare and the people on the other side change their wording. The pieces are these.

Build the evaluation set from four sources

  1. The policy's own examples, including the near misses, as a regression floor.
  2. Production traffic that was flagged by the guardrail, by users, or by reviewers, plus a random sample of everything that was not flagged. The unflagged sample is the only source of cases the guardrail missed, and reading it is error analysis (Error Analysis).
  3. Perturbed variants of every positive: typos, spacing, character swaps, other languages, a polite wrapper, a role-play frame. Keep each kind of perturbation separate so you can report recall for each.
  4. Red-team findings (Red Teaming), added as they are found so the guardrail is never again tested only on what it already handles.

Report recall per category and per kind of wording, never as one pooled number. A pooled recall of 0.8 across plain, paraphrased and obfuscated variants hides the fact that it is 1.0, 0.8 and 0.4, and the last is the one an attacker will use.

How many examples

The width of an interval around a recall estimate depends on the number of bad examples, not on the size of the whole set. For a guardrail whose true recall is about 0.9, the normal approximation says you need roughly 1.962 × 0.9 × 0.1 / 0.052 ≈ 140 positive cases per category and variant to pin recall to ±5 points, and about 550 for ±2.5 points. That is why the six-case tests above are demonstrations, not results. If a category has too few real examples, say so in the report rather than quoting a point estimate: "recall 0.83 on 6 cases (95% interval 0.44 to 0.97)" is honest, and "83%" is not.

The base rate decides what the block queue looks like

Recall and false-positive rate are properties of the classifier. What the people downstream experience also depends on how often the bad class occurs. Watch it in production

Treat the guardrail as a service with indicators: block rate by category, the rate at which humans overturn blocks, the latency it adds, timeouts and which way it fails, and a weekly sample of allowed traffic read by a person for misses. A sudden drop in block rate is as important as a spike; it can mean a new wording the classifier does not know, a changed prompt upstream, or a broken integration that silently fails open. The traces behind these numbers come from the instrumentation in Tracing & Observability for LLM Systems, and the way misses become permanent tests is the subject of The Improvement Flywheel.

Systems connection

A guardrail is input validation with a statistical validator. Injection & Untrusted Input teaches the stronger rule that you should not rely on blocklists for untrusted input, and the same advice applies here: prefer an allow-list or a structured interface where you can (the action check), and use classifiers where the space of inputs is too open for a rule. The block-rate and override-rate indicators are ordinary service metrics, and the failing-open-versus-closed decision is the same one a load balancer's health check or a rate limiter faces. For agents, Agent Security shows where these checks sit in a tool loop.

Key takeaways

  • A guardrail needs a written policy first: scope, categories with must-catch and must-pass examples, an action per category, and an owner. The examples become the test set.
  • There are four checkpoints (input, context, output, action). Only the action check can be an exact rule, which is why it matters most for agents.
  • Rules are fast and exact for structured data and brittle for language. Trained and safeguard-model classifiers generalise further and cost more, and they learn shortcuts. Always decide what happens when the guard times out.
  • A PII filter is part pattern and checksum, part statistical, with a recall ceiling: high on well-formed identifiers, low on names and messy formats. Redact, pseudonymise or block, and measure what leaks.
  • Report recall by category and by kind of wording, with intervals, next to the false-positive rate. Roughly 140 positives per cell give ±5 points at 90% recall.
  • Prevalence decides precision. A guard with a good recall and an 8% false-positive rate produces mostly false alerts when the harm is rare, so estimate the base rate from a random sample.
  • Fixed-set recall overstates protection against adaptive attackers. Treat classifiers as one layer and put consequential controls in code.

Check your understanding

A guardrail has recall 0.94 and a false-positive rate of 0.08. About 1 message in 100 is actually in the blocked category. Roughly what share of the messages it blocks are truly in that category?

  1. About 94%
  2. About 11%
  3. About 50%
  4. About 8%
Per 10,000 messages, 100 are bad and 94 are caught; of the 9,900 good ones, 8% (792) are blocked wrongly. Real blocks are 94 of 886, about 11%. The 94% figure is recall, a different quantity. The false-positive rate (8%) is also not the share of blocks that are wrong, which depends on prevalence.

A keyword guardrail scores recall 1.00 on the team's plain test messages, 0.33 on paraphrases and 0.00 on obfuscated ones. Which conclusion and next step is best?

  1. Report 1.00, since that is the figure on the main test set
  2. Average the three into a recall of about 0.44 and report that
  3. Replace the keywords with a longer keyword list and stop
  4. Report the three figures separately, and add paraphrase and obfuscation to the training and test data of a classifier that sees more than keywords, while keeping the false-positive rate in view
Pooling hides which wording fails, and an attacker picks the weakest. A longer keyword list chases yesterday's wording. The useful reading is by variant, then a model or normalisation step that handles the variants, checked against benign near misses so the fix does not just block more of everything.

In the PII experiment the Luhn check cut false alerts on 16-digit order references from 30 to 4. Why not to 0?

  1. About one random 16-digit string in ten satisfies the checksum, so some order references look like valid cards by chance
  2. The regular expression is wrong and should be fixed
  3. Luhn only works on the first twelve digits
  4. The order references were generated from real card numbers
A checksum with a ten-valued check digit accepts about 10% of arbitrary digit strings, so a few false matches are expected (4 of 30 here). It is a strong filter, not an exact one. The pattern is not at fault, Luhn covers the whole number, and the references were random.

A team writes "block unsafe content" as their whole policy and trains a classifier on whatever labels are to hand. What is the main problem?

  1. Classifiers cannot be trained on small datasets
  2. The threshold will be too low
  3. There is no testable definition, so nobody can say what the guardrail should catch or pass, and labellers will disagree
  4. The classifier will be too slow
Without categories, examples and near misses, labels reflect each labeller's opinion and the false-positive rate cannot even be defined. Dataset size, threshold and latency are real concerns, but they are downstream of having a policy that can be turned into labelled examples.

A guardrail caught 6 of 6 obfuscated attacks in a team's test. A colleague says "so recall is 100%". What is the best reply?

  1. Agree: perfect recall means it is safe against obfuscation
  2. Disagree: a six-case test cannot have perfect recall
  3. The 95% interval for 6 of 6 runs from about 0.61 to 1.00, so the true recall could be much lower; collect far more cases and check false positives on benign look-alikes
  4. Retest on the same six cases to confirm
With few cases the interval is wide, and the honest statement is "at least about 60% plausible". Rerunning identical cases adds no information. The test can show 6 of 6; it cannot support a claim of 100%.

An agent can send emails to any address. The team adds an output classifier that blocks messages containing card numbers, reporting 97% recall on its test set, and considers data exfiltration handled. What is the better design?

  1. Keep the classifier as one layer, and enforce an action check in code: recipients must be on an allow-list and consequential sends need approval
  2. Raise the classifier's threshold until recall is 100%
  3. Replace the classifier with a larger one
  4. Log all emails and review them next quarter
A classifier on a fixed test set does not bound what an adaptive attacker can encode and send (spaced digits, other languages, other data types). An allow-list and approval step in code limits the action whatever the wording. A higher threshold trades false positives for recall without a guarantee, a bigger model has the same weakness, and quarterly review detects rather than prevents.
