Fact-Checking & Citation Verification

Grounding makes an answer checkable; this chapter does the checking. You will break an answer into single claims, find evidence for each, decide whether the evidence supports, contradicts or says nothing about the claim, and then verify that every citation in the answer really supports the sentence it is attached to. You will also learn to measure the checker itself, because a checker is a model with error rates of its own.

Why this matters

The last two chapters explained why models state false things (chapter 10) and how to build a system that answers from evidence and declines otherwise (chapter 11). Neither guarantees that a particular answer on a particular day is correct. A grounded system can still misread its documents, attach a citation that says the opposite, or cite a source that does not exist. Users see a footnote and trust the sentence above it, so a wrong citation is worse than none. After this chapter you will be able to build a verification stage that checks an answer claim by claim, report three separate citation metrics, estimate how often the verifier itself is wrong, and decide what the product does with a claim that fails.

Checking is a different job from writing

Writing a fluent paragraph and checking whether each sentence in it is true are different tasks, and the second is often easier. A reviewer who is handed one claim and one passage only has to compare them. The writer had to recall facts, choose which to include and phrase them, and each step is a place to go wrong. This asymmetry is why verification stages work at all: you can use a second pass, a smaller model, a rule or a person to catch what the first pass got wrong, as long as the second pass is given something concrete to compare against.

The word "fact-checking" hides three questions that have different answers, different costs and different failure modes. It helps to name them before building anything.

Claim
One statement that could be true or false on its own, such as "Harbourline's operating profit was 61 million pounds". Answers are made of claims; verification works on claims, not on paragraphs.
Evidence
Text (or data) that a person could point to when asked "how do you know?": a passage from a filing, a database row, a search result.
Entailment
The relation "if the evidence is true, the claim must be true". A model that predicts it is doing natural language inference (NLI): given a premise and a hypothesis, label the pair as entailment, contradiction or neutral. In fact-checking the labels are usually called supported, refuted (or contradicted) and not enough information.
Citation
A pointer from a sentence in the answer to the source it claims to rest on: a footnote number, a document identifier, a URL, sometimes with a quoted span.

The three questions are:

  1. Is the claim true in the world? This is fact-checking proper. It needs evidence from outside the answer and is limited by the quality of that evidence.
  2. Is the claim supported by the sources the system was given? This is faithfulness or groundedness. It is a closed-world question: the sources may be wrong, but did the answer say what they say?
  3. Does each citation support the sentence it is attached to? This is attribution. Rashkin et al. ("Measuring Attribution in Natural Language Generation Models", 2021) framed it as whether a statement is "attributable to identified sources" (AIS), and showed how to have people judge that consistently.

Most production systems check questions 2 and 3, because the system has the sources in hand and the check is cheap and decidable. Question 1 needs an independent source of truth and is much harder; a later section covers where it stays hard. Throughout the chapter we use one small, invented but realistic running example so that every number can be checked by hand.

The pipeline

Whatever the tools, a verification stage has the same five parts. The answer is split into claims, evidence is found for each claim, each claim is compared with its evidence, the verdicts are combined, and the product decides what to do with failures.

Step 1: extracting claims

A paragraph cannot be checked as a unit. "Harbourline's revenue rose 8 percent and it paid a 12 pence dividend" contains one true fact and one false one, and a verdict on the sentence would be wrong either way. The first step is claim extraction: splitting the answer into atomic claims, each stating one fact. Min et al. ("FActScore: Fine-grained Atomic Evaluation of Factual Precision in Long Form Text Generation", EMNLP 2023) popularised the idea: score a long text by the share of its atomic facts that a reliable source supports. Their paper reports that ChatGPT scored 58% on biographies, meaning that roughly four in ten of its atomic facts were not supported, a number that no sentence-level or document-level check would have revealed.

Two properties make a claim checkable.

  • Atomic. One fact. "Opened in March 2025 and cost 38 million pounds" is two facts, and one may be right.
  • Self-contained (the literature says decontextualised). It must make sense with the rest of the answer removed. "It paid a dividend" is useless to a checker; "Harbourline paid a dividend of 12 pence" can be searched for. Pronouns, "the company", "the centre", "this year" and "the above" all have to be resolved.

Real systems ask a language model to do this with a prompt such as "Break the text into independent factual claims. Rewrite each so it can be understood alone." That is a model call with its own errors, so it is checked like any other stage (the next sections show how). To keep the whole chapter runnable offline, the script below uses a small rule-based extractor hand-tuned to one answer. It is honest scaffolding, not a recommendation: it works on this text and would not generalise. The answer contains five errors of the kinds we care about, planted on purpose, and we will see which of them the later stages catch.

import re

ANSWER = ("Harbourline's revenue was 412 million pounds in 2025, an increase of 8 percent. "
          "Operating profit was 61 million pounds, and it paid a dividend of 12 pence. "
          "The company employs 3,200 people, most of them in Leeds. "
          "Its chief executive, Mara Quince, joined in 2019. "
          "It opened a distribution centre in Doncaster in March 2025 that can handle 90,000 parcels a day, "
          "at a cost of 38 million pounds. "
          "Analysts expect revenue to reach 450 million pounds next year.")

SUBJECT = "Harbourline"
# Words that start a new fact inside a sentence. A real extractor uses a model for this.
SPLITS = [r", an increase of", r", and it", r", most of them in", r", at a cost of", r" that can handle"]
LEAD = {"an increase of": "{S} revenue rose by", "and it": "{S}",
        "most of them in": "Most of {S}'s staff work in", "at a cost of": "The Doncaster centre cost",
        "that can handle": "The Doncaster centre can handle"}

def sentences(text):
    return [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=\.)\s+", text) if s.strip()]

def decontextualise(s):
    """Replace pronouns and bare nouns with the entity they refer to."""
    s = re.sub(r"^(It|The company)\b", SUBJECT, s)
    s = re.sub(r"^Its\b", SUBJECT + "'s", s)
    s = re.sub(r"^Operating profit", SUBJECT + " operating profit", s)
    return s

def extract(text):
    out = []
    for sent in sentences(text):
        parts = re.split("(" + "|".join(SPLITS) + ")", sent.rstrip("."))
        head = decontextualise(parts[0].strip())
        out.append(head)
        for i in range(1, len(parts), 2):
            key = parts[i].lstrip(", ").strip()
            tail = parts[i + 1].strip()
            out.append(LEAD.get(key, SUBJECT).format(S=SUBJECT) + " " + tail)
    return out

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for i, c in enumerate(extract(ANSWER), 1):
        print(f"c{i:<2} {c}")

c4  Harbourline paid a dividend of 12 pence

Eleven claims came out of six sentences. Notice what the extractor had to do besides splitting: it replaced "it" and "its" with the company name, turned "the company employs" into a sentence with the same subject, and wrote "The Doncaster centre" where the original said "that". If that last step is skipped, claim c9 reads "that can handle 90,000 parcels a day", which no retrieval step can use.

Extraction has a failure that is easy to miss: it can change a claim. A model that tidies "most of them in Leeds" into "Leeds is the largest site" has altered what is being checked, and the checker will then approve or reject a claim the answer never made. For that reason a sample of (answer sentence, extracted claims) pairs should be read by a person, as part of the error analysis described in chapter 1, before any number from the verifier is trusted.

Step 2: finding evidence

The evidence for each claim comes from one of three places, and the choice defines what "verified" means.

Evidence sourceQuestion it answersTypical useMain risk
The passages the answer citesDoes each citation support its sentence?Attribution checks on a RAG answerCannot catch a true claim with no citation
All passages retrieved for the questionIs the claim supported by anything the system was given?Faithfulness monitoring (see Grounded Answers & Citations)Supports a claim from a source the answer did not name
A separate trusted corpus or the webIs the claim true in the world?Fact-checking free text, benchmarks such as long-form factualityRetrieval misses; the source itself may be wrong

Wei et al. ("Long-form factuality in large language models", NeurIPS 2024) built the third kind into a checker called SAFE (Search-Augmented Factuality Evaluator). A language model splits a response into facts, then for each fact issues search queries, reads the results and decides whether the search supports it. They report that SAFE agreed with crowdsourced human annotators on 72% of about 16,000 individual facts, and that on a random sample of 100 disagreements SAFE was judged right 76% of the time, at more than 20 times lower cost than the humans. Read that carefully: the automated checker is cheaper and about as reliable as paid raters, and both sides make mistakes. It is a measuring instrument with a known error rate, not an oracle.

Retrieval deserves its own measurement. If the right passage is never found, the verifier will correctly say "not enough information", and a lazy policy will treat that as "probably fine". Keep the two apart: retrieval recall for evidence (did the supporting passage appear in the top results?) is measured exactly as in Relevance Metrics, using claims with known supporting passages. In our running example the evidence set is small enough to search every sentence; a real corpus needs an index (RAG End to End).

Step 3: judging a claim against evidence

Given a claim and a passage, the judge returns one of three labels. The scheme comes from the FEVER shared task (Thorne et al., 2018): supported, contradicted (the literature says refuted), or not enough information. The third label is not optional. A two-way scheme forces the judge to call an unverifiable claim either true or false, and that is exactly the case where hallucinations live: analysts' forecasts that the sources never mention.

There are four ways to build the judge, and real systems layer them.

JudgeStrengthWeakness
Rules: exact match of numbers, names, dates, quoted stringsFast, free, explainable, no false support on a changed numberBlind to paraphrase, units, arithmetic and quantifiers
An NLI model (a small classifier trained on entailment data)Cheap at scale, consistent, runs locallyShort context window; weak on numbers and multi-step reasoning; no partial support
An LLM judge with a binary rubric (chapter 3)Handles paraphrase and moderate reasoningBias, cost, latency; can be fooled by text in the evidence itself
A person (chapter 5)The referenceSlow, expensive, noisy without good guidelines

Rules are worth keeping even when a model is available, because the errors that matter most to a user (a changed figure, a flipped date, a missing "not") are the ones exact comparison catches reliably. The script below is a small verifier of the first kind. It retrieves the best-matching sentence for each claim by word overlap, then applies three rules in order: low overlap means not enough information; a negation in the evidence that the claim lacks means contradicted; a number in the claim that the evidence does not contain means contradicted. It is run on the eleven claims against two source documents and compared with the labels a careful person would give.

import re
from claims import ANSWER, extract

DOCS = {
 "annual_report": "Harbourline Ltd reported revenue of 412 million pounds in 2025, up 8 percent from 2024. "
                  "Operating profit was 61 million pounds. The company employs 3,200 people, of whom 1,100 work in Leeds. "
                  "The board did not recommend a dividend for 2025. The chief executive, Mara Quince, joined in 2021.",
 "press_release": "Harbourline opened a second distribution centre in Doncaster in March 2025. "
                  "The site cost 38 million pounds and has capacity for 90,000 parcels a day.",
}
STOP = set("the a an of to in is are was were for and or it its be by at on that".split())
WORDS = {"rose": "up", "increase": "up", "handle": "capacity", "paid": "dividend", "staff": "people"}

def toks(s):
    s = s.lower().replace(",", "")
    return {WORDS.get(w, w) for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s) if w not in STOP}

def numbers(s):
    return set(re.findall(r"\d+", s.replace(",", "")))

def sentences(text):
    return re.split(r"(?<=\.)\s+", text)

def verdict(claim, docs):
    """Return (label, best_sentence). Labels: SUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED, NOT_ENOUGH_INFO."""
    best, best_s = 0.0, None
    for text in docs.values():
        for sent in sentences(text):
            words = {w for w in toks(claim) if not w.isdigit()}
            score = len(words & toks(sent)) / max(1, len(words))
            if score > best: best, best_s = score, sent
    if best < 0.5:
        return "NOT_ENOUGH_INFO", best_s
    if re.search(r"\b(did not|not|no)\b", best_s) and not re.search(r"\b(not|no)\b", claim):
        return "CONTRADICTED", best_s
    if numbers(claim) and not numbers(claim) <= numbers(best_s):
        return "CONTRADICTED", best_s
    return "SUPPORTED", best_s

GOLD = ["SUPPORTED", "SUPPORTED", "SUPPORTED", "CONTRADICTED", "SUPPORTED", "CONTRADICTED",
        "CONTRADICTED", "SUPPORTED", "SUPPORTED", "SUPPORTED", "NOT_ENOUGH_INFO"]

    print("accuracy:", sum(conf[(l, l)] for l in labels), "/", len(GOLD))

Nine of eleven is respectable for fifteen lines of code, and the two misses are instructive.

  • c6, "most of the staff work in Leeds", was marked supported. The retrieved sentence says 1,100 of 3,200 people work in Leeds, which is about 34%, so the claim is false. Every word and number in the claim does appear in the evidence; the claim is wrong because of an arithmetic comparison ("most" means more than half). Rules and many NLI models fail here, and LLM judges fail on it more often than people expect. This is a false support, the most dangerous error a verifier can make because it stamps a wrong claim as checked.
  • c4, the 12 pence dividend, was marked not enough information. The right sentence ("The board did not recommend a dividend for 2025") was retrieved but scored below the overlap threshold because the claim also mentions "pence". The claim does not get through, which is the safe direction, but the user is told "unverifiable" when the truth is "contradicted", and a regression suite that expects "contradicted" would flag it.

Look at the confusion matrix, not the accuracy. Precision of the supported label is 7 of 8. For a verifier whose job is to reassure, that figure (and its confidence interval, which with eight claims is very wide) matters more than overall accuracy, for the reason covered in chapter 4: the number of cases behind a rate decides how much to believe it.

Analogy: the magazine fact-checking desk

At a magazine with a checking desk, the writer submits a draft and a stack of source documents. A checker highlights every checkable statement, finds a source for each, and marks it with a tick, a query or a cross. Statements with no source go back to the writer. The writer is not trusted less than before; the process just assumes any one person will miss things, and a second reader with the sources in hand misses different things. Verification stages work the same way: a second pass, given evidence, catches a different set of errors than the first.

Where it breaks down: a human checker understands the story and sometimes notices that a source is itself unreliable. A verifier model matches text. It cannot tell that a satirical post, an outdated filing or a forum joke is a poor source (the topic of Case 7) unless you give it a trust signal to use. The model also shares training data and habits with the writer model, so its blind spots often match the writer's.

A worked pass, step by step

The stepper below follows three of the eleven claims through the pipeline: the false dividend, the wrong chief executive start year, and the analyst forecast no source mentions. The three end with three different verdicts and, importantly, three different treatments.

Step through

Three claims, three verdicts

  1. Three claims enter. They came out of extraction already decontextualised. Each is a single fact that can be checked alone.
  2. Evidence is found. For c4 and c7 the best-matching sentences are the two in the annual report that say the opposite. For c11 nothing in the sources comes close.
  3. Each claim gets a verdict. Two are contradicted: one by a negation (the board recommended no dividend), one by a different year. The third is "not enough information", a different outcome that is not the same as "false".
  4. The product acts, and logs. Contradicted claims are removed or the answer is regenerated. The unverifiable forecast stays only with a visible marker. All three go to the log, because each is a candidate for the regression suite.

Verifying citations

Citations add a second check on top of claim verification, because the answer makes an additional promise: "this sentence is supported by that source". A user who clicks the footnote expects to find the evidence there. There are four distinct ways the promise can be broken, and they need four different tests.

The case of lawyers who filed a brief citing court decisions that a chatbot had invented (Case 3, 2023) fails check 1: nobody looked the cases up. A lookup against a database of real decisions would have caught it in milliseconds. That is the reason to put the cheap, exact checks first: they remove the most embarrassing failures at almost no cost, before any model is asked to judge.

Check 3 deserves a precise definition because "supports" is vague. Gao et al. ("Enabling Large Language Models to Generate Text with Citations", ALCE, EMNLP 2023) gave definitions that most citation evaluations still use. Take a sentence with its cited passages:

  • Citation recall (per sentence): 1 if the sentence has at least one citation and an NLI model says the cited passages, concatenated, entail the sentence; otherwise 0.
  • Citation precision (per citation): a citation is irrelevant if the passage alone does not entail the sentence and the sentence's other citations still do. A citation scores 1 if the sentence has recall 1 and that citation is not irrelevant.

Recall asks "is this sentence backed?"; precision asks "is every citation pulling its weight?". A sentence can have full recall and poor precision when it stacks four footnotes where one would do, which makes the answer look well-sourced when it is padded. In the ALCE human study, agreement between the automatic metric and people was moderate to substantial (Cohen's kappa 0.698 for recall, 0.525 for precision, from 100 examples), and the authors note that the NLI model cannot detect partial support. On ELI5-style questions, they report that even the best models lacked complete citation support about half the time. Kappa is explained in chapter 5.

The script below applies all four checks to a six-sentence answer with invented citations, using the verifier above as the "entailment" function. It checks existence, then whether the quoted span appears verbatim, then ALCE-style recall and precision.

from verify import DOCS, verdict

CORPUS = {"1": DOCS["annual_report"], "2": DOCS["press_release"]}   # what the system retrieved

# (statement, [(doc id, quoted span the model says supports it)])
ANSWER = [
 ("Harbourline's revenue was 412 million pounds in 2025.", [("1", "revenue of 412 million pounds")]),
 ("Harbourline operating profit was 61 million pounds.",   [("1", "Operating profit was 61 million pounds"), ("2", "capacity for 90,000 parcels")]),
 ("Harbourline paid a dividend of 12 pence.",              [("1", "dividend of 12 pence")]),
 ("The Doncaster centre cost 38 million pounds.",          [("2", "The site cost 38 million pounds")]),
 ("Harbourline was fined 2 million pounds by a regulator.", [("3", "fined 2 million pounds")]),
 ("Harbourline's chief executive joined in 2021.",         []),
]

def entails(statement, doc_ids):
    """Do these cited passages, taken together, support the statement?"""
    docs = {i: CORPUS[i] for i in doc_ids if i in CORPUS}
    return bool(docs) and verdict(statement, docs)[0] == "SUPPORTED"

def check(statement, cites):
    ids = [d for d, _ in cites]
    rows = []
    for d, quote in cites:
        exists = d in CORPUS
        quoted = exists and quote in CORPUS[d]
        alone = entails(statement, [d])
        rest = entails(statement, [x for x in ids if x != d])
        irrelevant = (not alone) and rest
        rows.append((d, exists, quoted, alone, irrelevant))
    recall = 1 if cites and entails(statement, ids) else 0
    return rows, recall

if __name__ == "__main__":
    tot_rec = prec_num = prec_den = n_exist = n_quote = n_cites = 0
    for stmt, cites in ANSWER:
        rows, recall = check(stmt, cites)
        print(f"{stmt}")
        if not rows: print("    (no citation)")
        for d, exists, quoted, alone, irr in rows:
            n_cites += 1; n_exist += exists; n_quote += quoted
            good = recall and not irr
            prec_num += good; prec_den += 1
            print(f"    [{d}] exists={exists!s:<5} quote_found={quoted!s:<5} supports_alone={alone!s:<5} irrelevant={irr!s:<5}")
        print(f"    statement citation-recall = {recall}")
        tot_rec += recall
    print()
    print(f"existence:  {n_exist}/{n_cites} citations point at a real source")
    print(f"quotes:     {n_quote}/{n_cites} quoted spans appear verbatim in the source")
    print(f"recall:     {tot_rec}/{len(ANSWER)} statements fully supported by their citations")
    print(f"precision:  {prec_num}/{prec_den} citations are relevant and support their statement")

Read the output sentence by sentence, because each one shows a different failure.

  • Sentence 2 cites two sources. The first alone entails it. The second exists, and its quoted span really appears in it, but it is about parcels per day, not profit, so it is irrelevant. Existence and quotation checks both pass; only the support check exposes it. Precision falls even though recall is perfect.
  • Sentence 3 points at a real document with a "quotation" ("dividend of 12 pence") that is not in it. The model invented a quote and the real text contradicts the sentence. This is the most damaging pattern, since the footnote looks like a proof.
  • Sentence 5 cites document 3, which was never retrieved and does not exist in the corpus. Existence fails; nothing else can even be tested.
  • Sentence 6, "the chief executive joined in 2021", is true but has no citation. It scores zero on recall. Whether that is a bug or acceptable depends on the product: for a legal or medical answer it is a defect, for a casual summary it may be fine. Decide it as a policy, not by accident.

The summary lines show why one number is not enough. Five of six citations point at something real, yet only half of the statements are fully supported. A team that reports only "citation accuracy 83%" (counting existence) would be pleased. A team that reports support would not.

Measuring the verifier

The verifier is a model, so it needs the same treatment as any judge: a labelled set, honest error rates, and a regular re-check. The recipe is the one in chapter 3, applied to claims.

  1. Collect claim-evidence pairs from real traffic, not only from examples you wrote. Include the easy cases (clearly supported) and the hard ones (numbers, negation, quantifiers, dates, units, partial support).
  2. Have people label them with a written guideline (chapter 5). Check that two labellers agree before trusting either. If people disagree on "partial support", the verifier cannot be held to a standard the people do not share.
  3. Report per-label rates, not accuracy: of the claims people marked unsupported, what fraction did the verifier catch (recall on the bad class)? Of the claims the verifier called supported, what fraction really were (precision of the good class)? These two numbers are the miss rate and the false-support rate.
  4. Attach intervals. Eight claims give a precision of 7 of 8 with a 95% interval of roughly 53% to 98% (a Wilson interval; see chapter 4). A few hundred labelled pairs are the minimum for a figure you would publish to a team.
  5. Break results down by claim type: numbers, names, dates, comparisons, negations. The overall rate averages over very different difficulties, and the weak types tell you where to add a rule.
  6. Re-run on every change to the extractor, the retriever, the judge model or its prompt. A new judge model can improve the average and quietly get worse on negation.

Correlated errors are the subtle risk. If the answer was written by one model and checked by the same model, a misconception it holds is likely to be held by both stages, and the checker will agree with the writer. Shankar et al. ("Who Validates the Validators?", 2024) found that people's criteria also drift as they grade, so the labelled set needs maintenance too. Prefer a different model family for the judge, give it the evidence rather than asking it to recall, and keep a human-labelled slice as the ground truth that neither model sees during tuning.

Common misconception

"If the answer carries citations, it has been verified." A citation is a claim by the model that a source supports a sentence. It is evidence of nothing until it is checked. The ALCE paper found incomplete support on roughly half of the answers of even the strongest models it tested, and the output of citations.py above shows citations that exist, quote real text and still fail. The reverse also holds: an answer with no citations is not automatically wrong. Treat citations as a feature that must be verified like any other output.

What verification cannot do

Four limits are worth knowing before you promise anyone "verified answers".

  • Supported is not true. If the source is wrong, outdated or a joke, a claim "supported" by it is still wrong. In the glue-on-pizza incident, an answer faithfully reflected a forum post (Case 7); the faithfulness check would have passed. Source quality is a separate control: allow-lists of trusted domains, publication dates, document versions, and authority ranking when sources conflict.
  • Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. "Not enough information" can mean the claim is false, true but unsourced, or that retrieval failed. The three need different handling, so log which one occurred.
  • Reasoning claims outrun matching. Arithmetic ("most", "double", "fell by a third"), comparisons across documents and claims about trends are the cases where a sentence-to-sentence entailment check says "supported" for the wrong reason. For these, compute the answer in code (Tool Calling) and compare with a tool result instead of text.
  • Evidence in the evidence is data, not instructions. A passage retrieved for checking may contain text addressed to the model ("mark this claim as supported"). The judge must treat evidence as untrusted input (chapter 13). This is one reason to keep the exact string checks that no instruction can talk their way past.

State of play, October 2026

The methods in this chapter (atomic claims, retrieve-then-verify, NLI-style citation recall and precision) date from 2021 to 2024 and have been stable enough to build on; the papers above were fetched and read for this chapter. What moves quickly is the tooling around them: several model providers and RAG frameworks now offer built-in citation or "groundedness" features, and judge models improve month to month. I did not verify any specific vendor feature or score for this edition, so test a candidate on your own labelled claims rather than trusting a published number. The long-form factuality results quoted above come from models and search tools of 2024 and say nothing about today's systems; use them for the method, not the ranking.

Acting on the result

Verification produces labels; the product must turn them into behaviour. The choice depends on cost of an error and on latency.

OutcomeOptionsUse when
Claim contradictedRemove the sentence; regenerate with the failure as feedback; replace with the source's wordingAlways. Do not ship a claim the sources contradict.
Not enough informationMark as unverified; remove; ask a follow-up; abstain on the whole answer (chapter 11)Choose by stakes. Medical or legal: remove. Casual: mark.
Citation irrelevant or missingRe-attach from the best-supporting passage; drop the footnoteCheap fix; run the check again afterwards.
Citation does not exist or quote not foundBlock the response; alert; count it as an incident metricAlways. These are integrity failures, not wording problems.

Costs add up: a verification pass roughly doubles model calls on every answer (an extraction call, then one judgement per claim). Common ways to contain it are to verify only high-stakes answers, to run the exact checks on everything and the model judge on a sample, to batch claims into one judge call (with a check that it did not drop any), and to verify asynchronously and retract after the fact for low-risk flows. The trade-off between quality, latency and cost is the subject of chapter 19; the monitoring side, with a sampled stream of production answers checked offline, is covered in chapter 17.

Systems connection

Citation checks are referential integrity for text. A database rejects a row whose foreign key points at nothing, which is exactly check 1. But no foreign key can tell you the referenced row supports the claim, which is check 3. That gap is why you also need business-rule validation. The "log failures, replay them as tests" loop mirrors how a postmortem turns one incident into a permanent test (Postmortems). And measuring a verifier with precision, recall and intervals is the same discipline as measuring any classifier in Metrics, Percentiles & Histograms: choose the number that matches the cost of the error.

Key takeaways

  • Fact-checking is three questions: true in the world, supported by the given sources (faithfulness), and attributed correctly (does each citation support its sentence). Most systems can only check the last two.
  • Check claims, not paragraphs. Extract atomic, self-contained claims; a claim that still says "it" cannot be verified.
  • Use three verdicts: supported, contradicted, not enough information. The third is where hallucinations hide.
  • A citation can fail by non-existence, a fake quotation, lack of support, or absence. Run the exact checks first, then judge support, then count uncited claims.
  • Report citation recall and citation precision separately (ALCE), and existence separately again. "Citations present" is not a quality number.
  • The verifier is a model. Measure its false-support rate on labelled claims, with intervals, broken down by claim type, and re-measure on every change.
  • Supported is not true: source quality, arithmetic claims and instructions hidden in evidence are the standing gaps.

Check your understanding

A RAG answer cites "[2]" after a sentence. The document with identifier 2 exists and the quoted span appears in it word for word, but the span is about a different product. Which check catches the problem?

  1. Existence: the lookup of the identifier
  2. Support: whether the cited passage entails the sentence
  3. Quotation: the exact string match
  4. Coverage: the share of claims with a citation
Support. Existence and quotation both pass, since the document is real and the string is really there. Only an entailment judgement notices that the passage does not say what the sentence says. Coverage counts missing citations and would count this one as present.

A sentence cites three passages. Passage A alone entails it. Passages B and C are on topic but entail nothing alone, and together with A they add nothing the sentence says. By the ALCE definitions, what are the sentence's citation recall and the precision of citation B?

  1. Recall 0, precision 0, because only one of three citations is needed
  2. Recall 1, precision 1, because B is on topic
  3. Recall 0, precision 1, because B does not contradict the sentence
  4. Recall 1, precision 0, because the sentence is covered and B is irrelevant (it does not entail alone and the others still do)
Recall depends on whether the cited passages together entail the sentence, and A does. B is irrelevant by definition: alone it does not entail, and the remaining citations still do. Being on topic is not enough to be relevant, which is why padding with related footnotes lowers precision.

Your verifier labels 1,000 claims. People marked 100 of them unsupported. The verifier flagged 60 of those 100 and wrongly flagged 20 of the supported 900. Which statement is most accurate?

  1. It misses 40% of the unsupported claims, so 40 wrong claims pass as verified; accuracy of 94% hides that
  2. It is 94% reliable, which is enough for production
  3. Its false-alarm rate is the main problem because 20 supported claims were flagged
  4. It cannot be evaluated until the retriever is fixed
Of 100 unsupported claims it caught 60, so 40 got through, a miss rate of 40%. Overall accuracy is (60 + 880) / 1,000 = 94%, dominated by the easy majority. The 20 false alarms cost reviewer time (about 2% of supported claims); the 40 misses are errors the user sees. Judge the verifier by the rate that matches the cost.

The answer says "most employees work in Leeds". The retrieved sentence reads "3,200 people, of whom 1,100 work in Leeds". A word-overlap verifier says supported. What is the underlying reason, and the best fix?

  1. Retrieval failed; use a larger index
  2. The claim was not atomic; split it into two claims
  3. The claim needs a comparison (1,100 of 3,200 is not more than half) that matching cannot do; compute it with a tool or a rule for quantifiers
  4. The verifier needs a fourth label, "partly true"
Retrieval did find the right sentence, and the claim is already atomic. The failure is reasoning: "most" requires dividing and comparing. Arithmetic belongs in code, with the result compared against the claim. A "partly true" label would not change the verdict for a claim that is simply false.

An answer is written by model X and verified by model X with the same prompt style. The verifier approves 98% of claims. Why is this number weak evidence of quality?

  1. 98% is too high to be plausible for any verifier
  2. The writer and verifier share training data and blind spots, so a wrong belief is likely to be approved; the verifier has to be validated against human labels and ideally be a different model
  3. A verifier should always reject at least 10% of claims
  4. Approval rates are only meaningful for human reviewers
The approval rate says how often the two stages agree, not how often they are right. With correlated errors the agreement is inflated. The check is its false-support rate on human-labelled claims. There is no fixed rejection quota; a very clean system may legitimately reject little.

A verification stage marks a claim "supported" because it matches a passage from a satirical website the system retrieved. What does this show?

  1. The entailment judge is broken
  2. The claim extraction was wrong
  3. Faithfulness to a source is not truth: the missing control is source quality (trusted domains, dates, authority ranking)
  4. The citation should have been removed
The judge did its job: the claim is supported by the retrieved text. The failure is upstream, in what counts as evidence. Truth needs trustworthy sources, which is controlled by retrieval filters and source ranking, not by the entailment check.
