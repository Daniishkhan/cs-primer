Human Labels

Every automated measurement in this course, from a judge's verdict to a benchmark's answer key, is ultimately checked against what a person decided. This chapter is about producing those decisions well: writing guidelines people can follow, measuring whether they agree, resolving the cases where they do not, and running the work as a small, repeatable process.

Why this matters

The judge calibration in chapter 3 compares a model's verdicts with human labels, and the benchmarks of chapter 6 are only as good as their gold answers. If the labels are inconsistent, no judge can agree with them and no system can score above their noise. Teams often treat labelling as a chore to hand off, then spend weeks puzzling over metrics that were limited by the labels all along. After this chapter you will write a guideline that survives contact with real data, compute and interpret agreement without being misled by it, settle disagreements in a way that improves the guideline, and estimate the time and cost of a labelling effort before you start.

What a label is, and who should make it

A label is a person's recorded judgement about one item against a stated criterion: this reply passes or fails "states the refund policy correctly"; this summary does or does not omit a safety-relevant fact. The people making the judgements are annotators (or raters, or labellers). A set of items whose labels you trust enough to measure against is a gold set. The word "gold" flatters it. A label is a measurement made by a person, with errors of its own, and the rest of this chapter treats it that way.

The first decision is who labels. The answer follows from what the criterion needs.

A domain expert

For criteria that need judgement: is this clause risky, is this triage advice safe, is this code review comment correct? A pharmacist, a paralegal or a senior engineer is slow and costly but their label is the standard. Husain and Shankar advise, for most small and medium organisations, appointing a single domain expert as the "benevolent dictator" of quality, to avoid the paralysis of too many opinions.

Trained generalists

For criteria a careful non-expert can apply from a written guideline: tone, formatting, whether the answer addresses the question. Cheaper and parallel, but the guideline must carry the expertise, and you must measure agreement.

The crowd or users

For large volumes of simple judgements, or preferences where the population's opinion is the target. Quality control is the main work: hidden checks, redundancy, statistical aggregation. Clicks and thumbs-up are signals, not labels, because they mix many unstated criteria.

Use the smallest group that can do the job. A single expert's labels are consistent by construction but cannot tell you whether the labels are reliable; to learn that, a second person has to label some of the same items, which is where agreement comes in. A cost-conscious pattern is one expert labelling everything with a second person double-labelling a sample (many teams use 10 to 20 percent; that figure is a rule of thumb, not a standard).

Writing the guideline

A labelling guideline is the document annotators follow. Its job is to make two competent people reach the same answer, which means it has to say what you mean by words that feel obvious. "Is the answer helpful?" produces as many standards as annotators. Compare with: "PASS if the reply tells the customer whether they are eligible for a refund and quotes the correct window (30 days for physical goods, 14 for digital). FAIL if the window is wrong, missing, or contradicts the policy text supplied."

A guideline that works usually contains:

  1. The purpose. What the label will be used for. It settles many edge cases ("if the reply is correct but rude, is it a PASS for accuracy?").
  2. The unit and the inputs. What exactly the annotator sees (the whole conversation? the retrieved documents?) and what they judge (the last reply? the entire trace?).
  3. Definitions of each label, with decision criteria rather than adjectives, and a short rule for the tie-break ("when unsure between PASS and FAIL, choose FAIL and tick the unsure box").
  4. Worked examples, including clear passes, clear fails and, above all, borderline cases with the decision and a one-line reason. Most agreement problems live in the borderline cases, and examples settle them faster than prose.
  5. An escape hatch. An "unsure" or "ill-formed item" option with a free-text note, so that annotators do not force a label on an item that cannot be judged.
  6. A version number and a change log. Labels are only comparable within one guideline version.

You cannot write the guideline before you see the data

Shankar and colleagues (2024), in "Who Validates the Validators?", studied people building evaluation criteria with LLM assistance and report criteria drift: users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs also helps them work out what the criteria are, and some criteria depend on which outputs they happened to see. In practice: expect version 1 of the guideline to be wrong, label a small batch, rewrite it, and re-label the early items under the new version. Chapter 1's error analysis is the same discovery process, from the other side.

Measuring agreement

Inter-annotator agreement asks: when two annotators label the same items independently, how often do they give the same label? The simplest answer is raw agreement, the fraction of items with identical labels. It is easy to read and easy to be fooled by.

Suppose a task is 90 percent PASS and two annotators who have not read the guideline, and just click PASS, will agree on about 81 percent of items without judging anything. Raw agreement does not know how much agreement chance would have produced. Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) corrects for it:

The idea

  1. 2 tables of (both PASS, A only, B only, both FAIL) counts
  2. p_o ← (both PASS + both FAIL) ÷ n; r_A, r_B ← each PASS rate
  3. p_e ← r_A × r_B + (1 − r_A) × (1 − r_B)
  4. kappa ← (p_o − p_e) ÷ (1 − p_e)
"""Cohen's kappa by hand for two tables of 100 items, both with 90 percent
raw agreement."""

# ── 1. Two annotator tables: (both PASS, A only, B only, both FAIL) ──────
TABLES = {
    "balanced": (45, 5, 5, 45),
    "skewed":   (85, 5, 5, 5),
}

# ── 2. Per table: p_o, then p_e from each annotator's PASS rate ──────────
print("table      p_o  A pass  B pass   p_e   p_o-p_e  1-p_e  kappa")
for name, (pp, pf, fp, ff) in TABLES.items():
    n = pp + pf + fp + ff
    p_o = (pp + ff) / n                   # observed agreement
    a_pass = (pp + pf) / n                # annotator A's PASS rate
    b_pass = (pp + fp) / n                # annotator B's PASS rate
    p_e = a_pass * b_pass + (1 - a_pass) * (1 - b_pass)   # step 1: chance
    above = p_o - p_e                     # step 2: agreement beyond chance
    room = 1 - p_e                        # step 3: most that is possible
    kappa = above / room                  # step 4: the fraction achieved
    print(f"{name:8s} {p_o:5.2f} {a_pass:7.2f} {b_pass:7.2f} {p_e:6.2f} "
          f"{above:8.2f} {room:6.2f} {kappa:6.2f}")
table      p_o  A pass  B pass   p_e   p_o-p_e  1-p_e  kappa
balanced  0.90    0.50    0.50   0.50     0.40   0.50   0.80
skewed    0.90    0.90    0.90   0.82     0.08   0.18   0.44

where po is observed agreement and pe is the agreement expected if each annotator labelled independently, keeping their own rate of PASS. It equals 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for agreement no better than chance, and can go negative. To compute pe multiply the two annotators' PASS rates, multiply their FAIL rates, and add. A worked example uses 100 items:

import random

def kappa(both_pass, a_only, b_only, both_fail):
    n = both_pass + a_only + b_only + both_fail
    po = (both_pass + both_fail) / n                       # observed agreement
    pa, pb = (both_pass + a_only) / n, (both_pass + b_only) / n
    pe = pa * pb + (1 - pa) * (1 - pb)                     # agreement expected by chance
    return po, pe, (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

tables = {
    "balanced (50% pass)": (45, 5, 5, 45),
    "skewed (90% pass)":   (85, 5, 5, 5),
}
print(f"{'two annotators, 100 items':26} {'agree':>6} {'chance':>7} {'kappa':>6}")
for name, t in tables.items():
    po, pe, k = kappa(*t)
    print(f"{name:26} {po:6.0%} {pe:7.0%} {k:6.2f}")

# Kappa is itself an estimate. How much does it move between two labelling sessions of the same size?
random.seed(4)
def session(n, p_pass=0.9, acc=0.95):
    cells = [0, 0, 0, 0]
    for _ in range(n):
        truth = random.random() < p_pass
        a = truth if random.random() < acc else not truth
        b = truth if random.random() < acc else not truth
        cells[(0 if a and b else 1 if a else 2 if b else 3)] += 1
    return kappa(*cells)[2]

print("\nsame annotators (95% accurate each), 90% of items pass, 300 sessions:")
for n in (40, 200, 1000):
    ks = sorted(session(n) for _ in range(300))
    print(f"  {n:5d} items: kappa 2.5th to 97.5th percentile = {ks[7]:.2f} to {ks[292]:.2f}")

A pilot of 50 items is good for finding guideline problems and not good for certifying a kappa to two decimals.

Reading kappa sensibly

  • Labels for ranges such as "substantial agreement" for 0.61 to 0.80 come from Landis and Koch (1977) and are conventions, not thresholds. Decide what you need: a judge calibration can tolerate more label noise than a safety-critical test set.
  • Always report raw agreement and the full table with kappa. A low kappa on a very skewed task (the right-hand table) may reflect only a handful of disagreements on the rare class, and those are exactly the ones to read.
  • Per-label agreement matters more than the overall figure when the rare class is the one you care about, such as unsafe replies or contract clauses with unusual terms.
  • More than two annotators, or gaps: Fleiss' kappa extends the idea to a fixed number of raters, and Krippendorff's alpha handles any number of raters, missing labels and ordered scales (for example a 1 to 5 rating). They answer the same question and are available in common statistics libraries.
  • High agreement is not correctness. Two annotators who share the same misunderstanding agree perfectly. Agreement shows that the guideline is reproducible; whether it measures the right thing is a separate check, by an expert reading a sample.

Common misconception

"Our annotators agree 92 percent of the time, so the labels are 92 percent correct." Agreement is not accuracy. Annotators can agree and both be wrong, which happens when the guideline has a systematic gap or when the model's output sets an anchor (a pre-filled label that reviewers tend to accept). The reverse also holds: on a skewed task 92 percent agreement can be close to chance. Measure agreement to find where the guideline is unclear, and check correctness separately by having an expert review a random sample, including some items on which everyone agreed.

Disagreement is data

Teams are tempted to treat disagreement as a nuisance to average away. The more productive habit is to read each disagreement and ask why it happened. In practice there are three causes, and each calls for a different response.

Adjudication

Adjudication is the process of resolving items on which annotators disagree. The usual form: the items with split labels go to a senior reviewer (or the benevolent dictator), who sees both labels and the annotators' notes, decides, and writes a one-line reason. Three habits keep it honest.

  • Keep the originals. Store each annotator's label, the adjudicated label, who decided and the reason. Later you may want to measure agreement again, or learn that one annotator was systematically stricter.
  • Feed decisions back into the guideline. An adjudication ruling that is not written down will be made again, differently, next week.
  • Do not rely on a vote. Majority-of-three is cheap and improves accuracy, but it can still be wrong on contested items.

The next script simulates three annotators labelling 400 items, 70 percent clear-cut, 20 percent needing judgement and 10 percent genuinely ambiguous. It shows what a majority vote buys and what adjudication of split items cannot reach.

import random
random.seed(12)

N = 400
items = []
for i in range(N):
    kind = random.random()
    if kind < 0.70:   truth, clear = random.random() < 0.6, 0.97      # clear-cut item
    elif kind < 0.90: truth, clear = random.random() < 0.6, 0.78      # needs judgement
    else:             truth, clear = random.random() < 0.5, 0.55      # genuinely ambiguous
    items.append((truth, clear))

def label(truth, clear):
    return truth if random.random() < clear else not truth

unanimous = split = unanimous_wrong = 0
single_correct = majority_correct = 0
needs_adjudication_correct = 0
for truth, clear in items:
    votes = [label(truth, clear) for _ in range(3)]
    single_correct += votes[0] == truth
    maj = sum(votes) >= 2
    majority_correct += maj == truth
    if len(set(votes)) == 1:
        unanimous += 1
        unanimous_wrong += votes[0] != truth
    else: split += 1

print(f"{N} items, 3 annotators each")
print(f"single annotator correct:      {single_correct/N:.1%}")
print(f"majority of three correct:     {majority_correct/N:.1%}")
print(f"unanimous items:               {unanimous/N:.1%}")
print(f"split items for adjudication:  {split/N:.1%}  ({split} items)")
print(f"unanimous but wrong:          {unanimous_wrong/N:.1%}  ({unanimous_wrong} items nobody will review)")

majority of three correct:     91.2%

Majority voting lifts accuracy from 87.8 to 91.2 percent, and a quarter of the items need a person to look at them. The last line is the uncomfortable one: 2.2 percent of items were unanimous and wrong, so adjudication of splits will never see them. Audit a small random sample of the unanimous items as well. For large crowds, statistical models such as Dawid and Skene's (1979) estimate each annotator's error pattern from the data and weight their votes accordingly, but for the small expert teams common in evaluation, careful adjudication is usually a better use of time.

Label noise sets a ceiling

Whatever you do, some labels will be wrong, and that has consequences for what your eval can show. If a fraction e of gold labels are wrong on a pass or fail task, a system with true accuracy a will score about a(1 − e) + (1 − a)e: a perfect system cannot score above 1 − e.

import random
random.seed(9)

def measured(true_acc, label_error):
    """Pass/fail task. A wrong gold label makes a right answer look wrong, and a wrong answer look right."""
    return true_acc * (1 - label_error) + (1 - true_acc) * label_error

print("measured accuracy when a fraction of the gold labels are wrong")
print(f"{'true accuracy':>14} " + " ".join(f"{e:>9.0%}" for e in (0.0, 0.03, 0.06, 0.10)))
for acc in (1.00, 0.97, 0.94, 0.90):
    print(f"{acc:14.0%} " + " ".join(f"{measured(acc, e):9.1%}" for e in (0.0, 0.03, 0.06, 0.10)))

# Does random label noise reorder two systems? A is truly 2 points better than B, 500 items.
def flip_rate(label_error, n=500, trials=2000):
    flips = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        wrong_gold = [random.random() < label_error for _ in range(n)]
        a_ok = [random.random() < 0.96 for _ in range(n)]
        b_ok = [random.random() < 0.94 for _ in range(n)]
        score = lambda ok: sum((o != w) for o, w in zip(ok, wrong_gold))
        flips += score(a_ok) < score(b_ok)
    return flips / trials

print("\nA truly 2 points better than B; how often does B score higher?")
for e in (0.0, 0.06, 0.15):
    print(f"  gold labels {e:4.0%} wrong: B ahead in {flip_rate(e):.0%} of 500-item evals")

With 6 percent wrong labels a perfect system measures 94 percent, and a system that is truly 94 percent measures 88.7. Random label errors compress differences and add noise but do not usually reverse the order on average; the last table shows the reversal chance rising from 6 percent (sampling noise alone) to 9 percent at 6 percent label error and 14 percent at 15 percent. Systematic errors (a guideline gap that mislabels a whole category) can reverse a ranking outright. Published benchmarks have this problem too: Northcutt, Athalye and Mueller (2021) estimated an average of at least 3.3 percent label errors across ten widely used test sets and at least 6 percent in the ImageNet validation set, and showed that rankings of models can change when the test labels are corrected. Chapter 6 returns to this when building a benchmark, and chapter 7 to what happens when a benchmark's ceiling is reached.

Running the effort

A labelling effort is a small project with a plan, a budget and quality controls. A rough budget exercise shows why it deserves one. Suppose 600 items, each needing about 90 seconds of expert reading and judgement, with 20 percent double-labelled: that is 600 × 90 s = 15 hours for the first pass, 120 × 90 s = 3 hours for the second labels, plus adjudication of roughly 25 percent of the doubled items and guideline revisions. Roughly 20 to 25 hours, three working days, for a set small enough to calibrate a judge. Double the time per item (long conversations, documents) and the figure doubles.

  1. Pilot before scaling. Label 30 to 50 items with two people, measure agreement, read every disagreement, revise. Repeat until the disagreements that remain are the genuinely contested ones.
  2. Randomise and blind. Show items in random order. Do not show a second annotator the first one's label, and do not pre-fill labels from a model unless you also measure how often annotators accept the pre-fill without checking (the anchoring risk).
  3. Hide known answers. Mix in a few percent of items with answers that the senior reviewer has settled. An annotator whose accuracy on these falls is tired, confused or drifting.
  4. Design the interface for the judgement. Show the whole context the criterion needs (the retrieved documents, the earlier turns), one decision per screen, a note field, and keyboard shortcuts. A spreadsheet is enough for hundreds of items; a purpose-built tool pays off for thousands.
  5. Watch for drift. Annotators' standards shift over days. Re-measure agreement on a repeating overlap sample, and hold short calibration meetings on the items that split.
  6. Protect people and data. Annotators may read personal data or distressing content. Redact what the task does not need, restrict access and keep the data where your policy allows, pay fairly, and give people a way to skip harmful items.
  7. Freeze and version. When the set is done, stamp it with the guideline version, annotator roles, date and agreement figures. Fix errors by publishing a new version, never by editing in place.

Analogy: umpires and the rulebook

Two umpires watching the same play will call it the same way far more often if the rulebook defines a strike by position and not by "looks fair", if they rehearsed with video of awkward cases, and if a third reviewer settles the disputed calls. Agreement is produced by the rulebook, the practice and the review, and not just by the umpires being good at the game.

Where it breaks down: a baseball rulebook has had a century to settle its edge cases. Your criterion ("is this reply appropriately cautious?") is new, and its edge cases appear only when you see real outputs, which is why the guideline keeps changing for the first few rounds.

Systems connection

A labelling queue is an ordinary work queue with an unusual consumer. Tasks can be handed out twice if a timeout fires while someone is still working, and two annotators can end up with the same item by accident. That is the at-least-once delivery problem from Logs & Exactly-Once: give each task an id, record who labelled what, and make storing a label idempotent.

How many labels do you need?

Enough to answer the question the labels serve, which is a statistics question (chapter 4). A guide from Husain and Shankar: for calibrating a judge on one failure mode, label roughly 100 to 200 examples, and when debugging a judge keep at least 50 passes and 50 fails, so that both the true positive and true negative rates have enough items behind them. With 50 items in a class, the 95 percent interval on a rate near 90 percent is wide, about ±8 points. Labels for the rare, important class are worth more than extra labels for the easy majority, so sample to include them: stratify by failure mode found in error analysis instead of taking a purely random draw.

Try it yourself

Label 30 customer messages from your own work (or any support dataset) against a one-sentence criterion of your choice, such as "the reply answers the question that was asked". Wait a day, then label the same 30 again in a shuffled order without looking at your first labels. Compute your own raw agreement and kappa with kappa.py by changing the four counts. Then write down, for each item where you changed your mind, whether it was a slip, an ambiguity in your own criterion, or a real judgement call. Few people reach kappa above 0.8 against themselves on a new criterion, which says something about the first guideline you will write.

State of play, October 2026

It is now common for a language model to propose a label or a rationale that a person then confirms or corrects, which can double throughput. It also anchors the reviewer, and reviewers confirm plausible model suggestions more often than a blind labeller would assign them. This is the practitioner's rule I would apply rather than a result I can cite: measure agreement on a sample labelled blind, and report the model-assisted and blind figures separately. Tool choices (labelling platforms, vendors) change quickly, and the ideas above do not depend on any one of them.

Key takeaways

  • A label is a measurement made by a person, with errors. Pick the smallest group of people who can judge the criterion; one accountable expert plus a double-labelled sample is a strong default.
  • Write guidelines with decision criteria, borderline examples, an escape hatch and a version number. Expect to rewrite them after the first batch, because criteria are discovered by looking at outputs.
  • Report raw agreement, the table and kappa together. Kappa corrects for chance but collapses on skewed tasks, is noisy on small samples, and says nothing about correctness.
  • Sort disagreements into slips, ambiguity and genuine subjectivity. Ambiguity is the most valuable to fix, because each fix improves the guideline for every later item.
  • Adjudicate split items, keep the original labels, audit some unanimous items, and version the final set.
  • Label noise caps the score a perfect system can reach and adds noise to comparisons. Budget the effort (hours, not hand-waves) before you commit.

Check your understanding

A safety test set is 95 percent "safe" replies. Two annotators agree on 94 percent of items, but kappa is only 0.37. What is the best interpretation?

  1. The annotators are unreliable and the labels should be discarded
  2. Most of the 96 percent is agreement expected by chance on the dominant class, so the disagreements on the rare unsafe class need reading
  3. Kappa is wrong whenever raw agreement is above 90 percent
  4. The labels are 94 percent correct
With 95 percent of items safe, two annotators agree about 90 percent of the time by chance, so 94 percent leaves little room above chance (about 90.5 percent). The few disagreements are the rare, important cases to read. Kappa is not wrong, it is answering a stricter question. Agreement is not accuracy, and discarding all the labels throws away the useful ones.

Your first pilot of 50 items gives kappa = 0.45, and reading the disagreements shows most come from the same borderline case (replies that are correct but quote an out-of-date policy). What should you do first?

  1. Replace the annotators with more experienced ones
  2. Average the two annotators' labels
  3. Collect another 500 labels to tighten the kappa estimate
  4. Add a rule and examples for that case to the guideline, bump its version and repeat the pilot
The disagreements share a cause, which is an ambiguity in the guideline. Fixing it improves every later label. Replacing people treats a guideline problem as a people problem, averaging hides the problem, and more labels measure the problem more precisely without fixing it.

Three annotators label every item and a majority vote is the final label. Which risk remains?

  1. Items on which all three agree on a wrong label are never reviewed
  2. Majority vote always has lower accuracy than a single annotator
  3. Annotators will stop disagreeing
  4. Kappa can no longer be computed
Voting and adjudication only look at the items that split. A shared misunderstanding produces unanimous wrong labels, so a random audit of unanimous items is needed. In the simulation majority voting raised accuracy from about 88 to 91 percent, and kappa can be computed between any two annotators.

A gold set has about 6 percent wrong labels on a pass or fail task. A system that is truly 100 percent accurate would score about what?

  1. 100 percent, since it is right on every item
  2. 97 percent, half the label error
  3. 94 percent
  4. 88 percent
On every item with a wrong gold label the correct answer is marked wrong, so a perfect system scores 1 minus the label error rate, here 94 percent. A system's measured score cannot exceed that ceiling, and scores near it carry little information about real differences.

Why should a second annotator not see the first annotator's label (or a model's suggested label) when you are measuring agreement?

  1. It would slow the work down
  2. Seeing a label anchors the second annotator, which inflates agreement without making the labels more reliable
  3. It violates data protection rules
  4. Kappa cannot be computed from non-independent labels, but raw agreement can
Agreement is only informative if the two judgements are independent. Anchoring makes people accept the visible label, so agreement rises while the underlying reliability does not. The statistical claim in the last option is true for both measures, not only kappa, and the first and third options are not the reason.

Two expert annotators disagree on 12 of 100 items about whether an answer is "appropriately cautious". On reading them, you find that for 9 of the 12 the experts defend opposite readings and both are reasonable, and you cannot write a rule that settles them. What is the best handling?

  1. Flip a coin so the label set stays balanced
  2. Drop all 100 items and start again
  3. Mark those items as contested, record both views, and report results on them separately from the settled items
  4. Let the more senior annotator decide and delete the other label
Genuine subjectivity is a feature of the task, not a defect in the labels. Hiding it with a coin flip or deleting a legitimate view gives a false sense of certainty; a system's behaviour on contested items is worth knowing separately. Dropping everything wastes the 88 settled items.
