Governance Frameworks & Regulation

Governance documents read like law and policy, but each clause asks for something an engineer can build or measure: a risk register, test evidence, logs, a human who can override. This chapter explains the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001 and the EU AI Act as three different kinds of document, and shows how the work you have done in this course becomes the evidence they ask for.

Why this matters

Sooner or later a customer's procurement team, an auditor or a regulator will ask how you know your AI system is safe, and "we ran some evals" will not satisfy them. They will ask for a risk assessment, for test results tied to each risk, for logs, for proof that a person can intervene, and for what you do when it goes wrong. Teams that built those things as they went answer in a day. Teams that did not discover that the evidence cannot be reconstructed afterwards. After this chapter you can read a framework as a list of engineering requirements, say which of its clauses your system triggers, and keep the evidence current by generating it from your evals and traces.

Not legal advice

This chapter explains the structure of these documents so that engineers can work with lawyers and compliance staff. It is not legal advice and it describes law as of 14 October 2026. Which obligations apply to you depends on your role, your sector and where your users are; ask someone qualified before you rely on any of it.

Three kinds of document

The three names in the title are often lumped together, but they are different kinds of thing and ask different questions. Mixing them up is the commonest early mistake.

A useful way to hold them: the AI Act says what you must achieve for certain systems, ISO 42001 describes a management system that makes achieving things routine, and the NIST framework gives a vocabulary and a menu of practices for the risk work inside both. Many organisations use all three, with one body of evidence behind them.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework

NIST released the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) on 26 January 2023. It is voluntary, sector-neutral and built around four functions, which are groups of activities rather than steps in a sequence.

Govern

Policies, roles, accountability and culture. Who owns AI risk, what is the organisation's risk tolerance, how are third-party models vetted? It runs through the other three. Artefacts: an AI policy, named owners, an approval process, a vendor checklist.

Map

Establish context. What is the system for, who uses it, who could be harmed, what are the legal and ethical constraints, what are the risks and benefits? Artefacts: a use-case description, stakeholder list, a threat model (Threat Modeling), an impact assessment.

Measure

Quantify and track the risks with tests, metrics and independent review. Artefacts: the eval suites, judge calibration, red-team reports and monitoring dashboards of this course.

Manage

Prioritise and act on the measured risks: mitigate, transfer, accept or avoid, with response and recovery plans and ongoing monitoring. Artefacts: guardrails, release gates, an incident process (AI Incident Response), a decommissioning plan.

The framework also lists characteristics of trustworthy AI that the work aims at: valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy-enhanced; and fair, with harmful bias managed. Notice that "valid and reliable" is the territory of evals and "secure and resilient" is the territory of the adversarial chapters, so you have been doing Measure work for most of this course.

In July 2024 NIST published the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1), a companion that applies the four functions to generative systems. It names twelve risks that are new or made worse by generative AI: CBRN information or capabilities (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear), confabulation, dangerous, violent or hateful content, data privacy, environmental impacts, harmful bias or homogenisation, human-AI configuration, information integrity, information security, intellectual property, obscene, degrading or abusive content, and value chain and component integration. It then lists suggested actions, each tagged with the function and subcategory it belongs to (identifiers such as GV-1.1-001) and the risks it addresses. The profile is a good checklist to hold your own risk register against: for each of the twelve, either you have a control and evidence, or you have written down why it does not apply. (Read from NIST's published PDF. NIST's page also said in October 2026 that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised under the White House AI Action Plan, so check for a new version.)

ISO/IEC 42001: a management system

ISO/IEC 42001:2023, published in December 2023, is the first AI management system standard that organisations can be certified against. A management system is not a technical specification of a product. It is the set of policies, roles, processes and records by which an organisation runs some activity reliably, and it is audited: a certification body checks that you do what you say and that you improve. If your company has ever certified to ISO 27001 for information security or ISO 9001 for quality, the shape is familiar.

From secondary summaries of the standard (the text itself is paywalled and was not read for this edition), the structure is:

  • Clauses 4 to 10 hold the auditable requirements, in the common ISO management-system layout: context of the organisation, leadership, planning (including AI risk assessment and AI system impact assessment), support (resources, competence, documented information), operation, performance evaluation (monitoring, internal audit, management review) and improvement.
  • Annex A is a reference set of controls (summaries count 38, under nine objectives covering areas such as policy, resources, impact assessment, the AI system life cycle, data, information for interested parties, use, and third parties). As in ISO 27001, you choose the controls that apply and justify omissions in a Statement of Applicability.

The engineering consequence is that ISO 42001 cares less about whether a particular model is accurate than about whether you have a repeatable process that would notice if it were not: a documented risk assessment, defined roles, evaluation and monitoring that actually run, a way of handling incidents and complaints, and evidence you reviewed the results. An auditor will sample records. "Show me the last three releases and the test results that gated them" is a typical request, which is one reason to have release gates that write their own reports.

The EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the first comprehensive AI law of its kind. It applies by role and risk, and it reaches beyond Europe's borders: a provider outside the EU that places an AI system on the EU market, or whose system's output is used in the EU, can be in scope. Two roles matter most. A provider develops an AI system (or has one developed) and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name. A deployer uses an AI system under its authority in a professional capacity. If you build an assistant on a model API and ship it to customers under your brand, you are usually the provider of that AI system, and the company that supplies the model is a provider of a general-purpose model. Roles can change: a deployer that substantially modifies a high-risk system or puts its own name on it can become a provider.

Risk tiers

  • Prohibited practices (Article 5) include social scoring, certain biometric categorisation and untargeted scraping of facial images, and manipulation that causes harm. They have applied since 2 February 2025.
  • High-risk systems are those in the product-safety areas of Annex I (such as medical devices) or in the listed use areas of Annex III: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment and worker management, access to essential services such as credit and insurance, law enforcement, migration and the administration of justice. They carry the heaviest obligations.
  • Transparency duties (Article 50) apply to systems that interact with people, generate synthetic content or perform emotion recognition or biometric categorisation: tell people they are dealing with an AI, mark synthetic content, label deepfakes.
  • Minimal risk covers everything else: no AI-specific obligations, though other law (data protection, consumer, product liability) still applies.

The script below is a toy triage that encodes these tiers as lookups. It exists to make the structure concrete and to show why classification is a first-class engineering question: the tier follows from what the system is used for, not from the model inside it. The same model is minimal risk as a code assistant and high-risk as a CV screener.

# A toy first-pass triage of an AI use case against the EU AI Act's risk tiers.
# It is a teaching aid, not legal advice: real classification turns on the Act's text (Articles 5, 6, 50,
# Annexes I and III), exceptions and guidance. Dates are those in force on 14 October 2026, after the Digital Omnibus.
PROHIBITED = {"social scoring of people", "untargeted scraping of faces", "emotion recognition at work or school"}
ANNEX_III = {"cv screening", "credit scoring", "exam proctoring", "benefit eligibility", "law enforcement risk scoring"}
PRODUCT_SAFETY = {"medical device software", "machinery safety component"}      # Annex I products
TALKS_TO_PEOPLE = {"customer chatbot", "voice assistant"}
GENERATES_CONTENT = {"image generator", "marketing copy writer"}

def tier(use_case):
    if use_case in PROHIBITED:
        return "prohibited", "banned since 2 Feb 2025"
    if use_case in ANNEX_III:
        return "high-risk (Annex III)", "obligations from 2 Dec 2027"
    if use_case in PRODUCT_SAFETY:
        return "high-risk (Annex I product)", "obligations from 2 Aug 2028"
    if use_case in TALKS_TO_PEOPLE or use_case in GENERATES_CONTENT:
        return "transparency duties (Art 50)", "applies from 2 Aug 2026"
    return "minimal risk", "no AI Act obligations beyond general law"

for uc in ["customer chatbot", "cv screening", "spam filter", "credit scoring", "medical device software",
           "social scoring of people", "marketing copy writer", "internal code assistant"]:
    t, when = tier(uc)
    print(f"{uc:<26}{t:<30}{when}")
print("\nnote: a customer chatbot that also screens CVs is two use cases; the stricter tier applies to the screening part.")

What high-risk means for engineers

For high-risk systems the provider must meet requirements set out in Articles 9 to 15, run a quality management system (Article 17), complete a conformity assessment, and monitor the system after release (Article 72). Read as an engineering list:

9A continuous risk management system across the life cycleA risk register, reviewed on each release and incidentRed Teaming
10Data governance for training, validation and test data: relevance, representativeness, bias examinationData cards, dataset provenance, slice-level resultsBuilding a Domain Benchmark
11Technical documentation (Annex IV) before releaseDesign, evaluation methods and results, kept currentAssertions & Eval Harnesses
12Automatic event logging over the system's lifetime, enough to identify risk situations and support monitoringTraces with versions and decisions, retained with careTracing & Observability
13Transparency and instructions for deployersIntended purpose, limits, accuracy levels, oversight needsGrounding, Abstention & Confidence

15Appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurityDeclared metrics, adversarial testing, attack-resistance evidencePrompt Injection & Jailbreaks

Deployers of high-risk systems have their own duties in Article 26: use the system as instructed, assign competent human oversight, monitor operation, and keep the logs the system generates for a period appropriate to its purpose and at least six months unless other law says otherwise. Providers must report serious incidents to authorities (Article 73, covered in the previous chapter). Fines are tiered: up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for prohibited practices, up to EUR 15 million or 3% for breaching most other obligations, and up to EUR 7.5 million or 1% for giving incorrect information to authorities, with lower caps for small businesses (Article 99).

General-purpose AI models

Model developers have separate obligations under Articles 53 to 55, which have applied since 2 August 2025: technical documentation, information for downstream providers, a copyright policy and a public summary of training content, and, for models presumed to pose systemic risk (a presumption based on training compute above 1025 floating-point operations, among other criteria), model evaluation including adversarial testing, serious incident reporting and cybersecurity protections. If you build on someone else's model, ask for their documentation: it is an input to your own, and part of your vendor assessment.

State of play, October 2026: the Digital Omnibus changed the dates

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the "Digital Omnibus on AI", was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026 (law-firm summaries from Cooley and Mondaq, read 14 October 2026; read the Regulation itself before relying on any date). It moved the high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and for Annex I product-embedded systems to 2 August 2028. Transparency duties under Article 50 still apply from 2 August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for machine-readable marking on generative systems already on the market. General-purpose model rules were not delayed. New prohibitions on systems for generating non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material apply from 2 December 2026. The Omnibus also changed the AI literacy duty (Article 4) from a requirement on providers and deployers to ensure staff competence into a duty on the Commission and Member States to encourage it. Delay is not repeal: the requirements are the same, and customers' contracts may ask for them sooner. Dates in this area have moved before; check the Official Journal and the AI Office before you plan.

From requirements to evidence

The three documents share one practical demand: traceability from a risk to a control to evidence that the control worked, with an owner and a date. The cheapest way to satisfy it is to generate the evidence from the systems you already run, instead of writing it up by hand before an audit.

The script builds such a pack for an imaginary support assistant. Each row of the register carries the framework clause, the metric and threshold, and the latest result and date. The script marks a row as passing only if the result meets the threshold and is no more than 30 days old, and flags rows that fail, are stale or have no evidence at all. The numbers are made up for illustration; the structure is the point.

from datetime import date

TODAY = date(2026, 10, 14)
MAX_AGE_DAYS = 30                     # evidence older than this is stale and must be re-run

# One row per risk: where it lives in the frameworks, the control, and the latest evidence (if any).
# (id, risk, NIST function, EU AI Act article, owner, metric, direction, threshold, result, date of run)
REGISTER = [
    ("R1", "Invented policy answers",       "MEASURE", "Art 15", "Priya", "groundedness",       ">=", 0.95, 0.962, date(2026, 10, 2)),
    ("R2", "Injection exfiltrates data",    "MEASURE", "Art 15", "Tomas", "attack success",     "<=", 0.02, 0.031, date(2026, 9, 28)),
    ("R3", "Personal data in trace store",  "MANAGE",  "GDPR",   "Aiko",  "PII filter recall",  ">=", 0.98, 0.985, date(2026, 8, 30)),
    ("R4", "Cross-tenant retrieval",        "MAP",     "Art 15", "Tomas", "leaked documents",   "<=", 0,    0,     date(2026, 10, 10)),
    ("R5", "Large refunds without a human", "MANAGE",  "Art 14", "Priya", "gate bypass rate",   "<=", 0,    0,     date(2026, 10, 11)),
    ("R6", "User not told it is an AI",     "GOVERN",  "Art 50", "Aiko",  "disclosure shown",   ">=", 1.0,  None,  None),
]

def status(direction, threshold, result, when):
    if result is None:
        return "NO EVIDENCE"
    if (TODAY - when).days > MAX_AGE_DAYS:
        return "STALE"
    ok = result >= threshold if direction == ">=" else result <= threshold
    return "PASS" if ok else "FAIL"

print(f"Evidence pack for support-assistant, {TODAY}")
print(f"{'id':<4}{'risk':<31}{'NIST':<8}{'Law':<8}{'metric':<19}{'need':<9}{'got':<8}{'age':>4}  status")
counts = {}
for rid, risk, fn, art, owner, metric, d, thr, res, when in REGISTER:
    st = status(d, thr, res, when)
    counts[st] = counts.get(st, 0) + 1
    got = "-" if res is None else f"{res:g}"
    age = "-" if when is None else str((TODAY - when).days)
    print(f"{rid:<4}{risk:<31}{fn:<8}{art:<8}{metric:<19}{d + ' ' + format(thr, 'g'):<9}{got:<8}{age:>4}  {st} ({owner})")
print("\nsummary:", ", ".join(f"{n} {k}" for k, n in sorted(counts.items())))
blocked = [r[0] for r in REGISTER if status(r[6], r[7], r[8], r[9]) != "PASS"]
print("release blocked by:", ", ".join(blocked) if blocked else "nothing")

Three kinds of gap appear, and each is common in real audits. R2 fails: the control exists and the test shows it is not good enough, which is a release blocker. R3 is stale: the PII filter was last measured 45 days ago, and the world has moved (new prompt, new log fields), so the old result proves little. R6 has no evidence: perhaps the disclosure banner exists, but nobody wrote a test, so you cannot show it. The last one is the cheapest to fix and the most embarrassing to discover in front of an auditor. Wiring the pack into the release gate (The Improvement Flywheel, Lab 7) means it is current whenever you ship.

Analogy: the food-safety inspection

A restaurant is subject to the law (hygiene regulations), may adopt a recognised management system (a documented food-safety plan that an outside body certifies) and uses good-practice guidance (how to cool food, what to log). The inspector does not taste every dish. They read the temperature logs, check that the plan was followed and look for the records that would show a problem was caught. The cook who keeps the logs daily passes in an hour; the cook who writes them the night before is caught by the first gap.

Where it breaks down: a fridge has a known safe temperature. For an AI system, what counts as "accurate enough" or "robust enough" is a judgement you must define, justify and defend, and the hazards (a new jailbreak, a changed model) appear faster than any inspection schedule.

Common misconception

"Compliance means safe", and its twin, "we are not in the EU, so none of this applies". A certificate or a completed checklist shows that a process exists and was followed; it does not show that your system handles your users' real inputs well. The evidence is only as good as the evals behind it, which is why this chapter comes after the rest of the course. And the EU AI Act is defined by where a system is placed on the market or its output is used, not where its developer sits, while the Omnibus delayed some obligations without removing them.

Key takeaways

  • The NIST AI RMF is a voluntary risk framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage); ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable management system; the EU AI Act is binding law applied by role and risk tier.
  • The tier follows from the use, not the model: the same model can be minimal risk in one product and high-risk in another. Know whether you are a provider or a deployer.
  • For high-risk systems, Articles 9 to 15 read as an engineering list: risk register, data governance, documentation, logs, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy, robustness and security evidence.
  • The Digital Omnibus (in force 27 July 2026) moved high-risk dates to 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028; transparency duties and general-purpose model rules were not delayed. Dates have moved before, so verify against the Official Journal.
  • All three ask for traceability from risk to control to dated evidence with an owner. Generate that evidence from eval runs, red-team reports, traces and incident records, and let a release gate fail on stale or missing evidence.
  • A certificate shows a process exists, not that the system is safe, and a delay is not a repeal.

Check your understanding

A company uses one general model for two products: an internal code-review helper and a tool that ranks job applicants. Under the EU AI Act, what follows?

  1. Both are high-risk because they use the same model
  2. Neither is regulated, since the model is general-purpose
  3. Only the model's developer has obligations
  4. The applicant-ranking tool is a high-risk use (employment, Annex III) while the code helper is probably minimal risk, because the tier follows the use
The Act classifies AI systems by intended use. Employment screening is an Annex III area; an internal developer tool generally is not. The model developer has separate general-purpose model duties, but that does not remove the provider and deployer duties for the high-risk application.

Your team passes a customer's security questionnaire and an ISO/IEC 42001 audit. A colleague says "so our assistant is certified safe". What is the best correction?

  1. That is right, certification covers the product
  2. The certification shows the organisation has a working AI management system; the assistant's safety still depends on evidence from evals and monitoring of that system
  3. ISO 42001 is voluntary, so it proves nothing
  4. The certificate is valid only in the EU
ISO/IEC 42001 certifies a management system, meaning policies, processes and records, not the behaviour of a particular model. That is not nothing, and it makes good evidence easier to produce, but it does not replace measuring each system.

Which of the following maps most directly to Article 14 (human oversight) of the AI Act for a refund-approving agent?

  1. An approval gate that routes refunds above a limit to a trained reviewer who can reject them, plus a control to halt the agent
  2. A longer system prompt asking the model to be careful
  3. A dashboard of average latency
  4. A signed model card from the model vendor
Article 14 asks that people can understand outputs, notice over-reliance, override or reverse them and stop the system. An engineered gate and stop control provide that. Prompts are not oversight, latency is unrelated, and a vendor's card helps with documentation but not with your deployment's oversight.

An evidence pack shows a PII-filter recall of 0.985, measured 45 days ago, against a threshold of 0.98, and the system has had two prompt changes and a new log field since. How should the register treat it?

  1. Pass, because the number meets the threshold
  2. Fail, because it is below 1.0
  3. Stale: re-run the measurement, because the system changed and the old result no longer supports the claim
  4. Not applicable, because filters are not covered by the frameworks
Evidence is a claim about the current system. A result that predates changes is weak support, so the register should flag it for re-measurement rather than count it as a pass. It did not fail the threshold, and data protection is within scope of all three documents.

A teammate says: "The Digital Omnibus delayed the high-risk rules to December 2027, so we can ignore the AI Act until then." What is the most accurate response?

  1. Correct; nothing applies before then
  2. Partly: Annex III high-risk duties moved, but prohibitions apply already, transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026, general-purpose model rules apply, and customers may require the evidence earlier
  3. Wrong; the Omnibus cancelled the delay
  4. Wrong; the Act applies only to providers outside the EU
The delay applies to specific obligations. Prohibited practices (since February 2025), general-purpose model duties (since August 2025) and Article 50 transparency duties (from August 2026) are not delayed, and contracts and procurement often ask for the evidence ahead of the legal date.

Which framework function mostly covers the work of running the evals, judges and red-team exercises from this course?

  1. Govern
  2. Map
  3. Manage
  4. Measure
Measure covers quantifying and tracking risks with tests, metrics and independent review. Govern sets policy and accountability, Map establishes context and identifies risks, and Manage acts on what was measured through mitigations, response and monitoring (the guardrails and incident process).
