Privacy & Data Handling for AI

An AI application copies people's words into prompts, traces, indexes, evaluation sets and a vendor's systems, and each copy is personal data you are responsible for. This chapter maps where those copies appear, how to minimise what leaves your control, how to delete across stores you do not fully own, what to ask a model vendor about training and retention, and what data residency does and does not guarantee.

Why this matters

Privacy failures in AI products are rarely clever attacks. They are ordinary data-handling mistakes made easy by the technology: full conversations logged forever "for debugging", customer text pasted into an eval set, a vendor that retains prompts longer than you promised users, a deletion request that clears the database but not the vector index or the backups. The cost is regulatory (in December 2024 the Italian data protection authority fined OpenAI EUR 15 million, citing among other things training on personal data without an adequate legal basis and transparency failures) and commercial (enterprise buyers ask these questions before they sign). After this chapter you can draw the data-flow diagram for a system, decide what to redact, retain and delete, and read a vendor's terms for the four things that matter.

Not legal advice

This chapter describes engineering practice and the main ideas of European data protection law (the GDPR) as an illustration. Other jurisdictions differ in detail, and your obligations depend on your role and facts. Involve your data protection officer or counsel for decisions with legal weight.

Where personal data lives in an AI system

Personal data is, in the GDPR's wording (Article 4), any information relating to an identified or identifiable person. That includes the obvious (names, emails, phone numbers) and the less obvious (an order number that your database links to a customer, a rare medical condition plus a postcode, a voice recording). The US term PII, personally identifiable information, overlaps with it, but is narrower in most definitions. A smaller group, the GDPR's special categories (health, biometrics, religion, political opinion and others, Article 9), gets stricter treatment. The organisation that decides why and how data is processed is the controller, and one that processes it on the controller's behalf, such as a model vendor, is a processor; the person the data is about is the data subject.

The first job is to find where the data goes. A conventional application stores personal data in a few places you designed. An AI application scatters copies as a side effect of how it works.

The retrieval and agent chapters add more: an agent's memory store (Memory), tool results that contain records fetched from other systems, and uploaded documents that are chunked and embedded. An embedding is not anonymous just because it is a list of numbers; it is derived from the text and can often be matched back to it, so treat a vector index as holding the personal data of its source documents.

Minimise before the model sees it

The cheapest privacy control is to send less. Data minimisation (GDPR Article 5(1)(c)) means collecting and passing on only what the purpose needs. Practical forms: do not put the whole customer record in the prompt when the question needs the order status; retrieve only chunks the asking user is permitted to see (Filters, Metadata & Permissions); and mask direct identifiers that the model does not need before the call. Masking has two flavours. Redaction removes the value and keeps a label ("[EMAIL_1]"). Pseudonymisation replaces it with a stand-in that you can map back, so the model's reply can be restored for the user. The mapping must live with you, apart from the prompt and the logs; with it, the data is still personal data, but the vendor and the trace store hold much less.

The script is a small redactor for a support assistant, with a labelled test set of six messages and eight PII items, a Luhn check so that card-like numbers are masked only when they are plausible, and a round trip through the stand-in model from lab-support/llm.py. It reports recall in the sense of the guardrails chapter (Guardrail Classifiers & Content Policy): of the items a person labelled as PII, how many are fully removed. A piece of an item left behind counts as a leak.

import re
from llm import ScriptedModel, text_of

def luhn(digits):
    s, alt = 0, False
    for ch in reversed(digits):
        d = int(ch)
        if alt:
            d = d * 2 - 9 if d * 2 > 9 else d * 2
        s, alt = s + d, not alt
    return s % 10 == 0

PATTERNS = {
    "EMAIL": re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.-]+"),
    "PHONE": re.compile(r"(?:\+\d{1,3}[ -]?)?(?:\(?\d{2,5}\)?[ -]?)\d{3,4}[ -]?\d{3,4}\b"),
    "CARD":  re.compile(r"\b(?:\d[ -]?){13,19}\b"),
}

def redact(text, known_names=()):
    """Replace PII with numbered placeholders; return the new text and the mapping to restore it."""
    mapping, counter = {}, {}
    def put(kind, value):
        for ph, v in mapping.items():
            if v == value: return ph
        counter[kind] = counter.get(kind, 0) + 1
        ph = f"[{kind}_{counter[kind]}]"; mapping[ph] = value; return ph
    for name in known_names:                                   # names your own system already knows
        text = re.sub(re.escape(name), lambda m: put("NAME", m.group(0)), text)
    text = PATTERNS["EMAIL"].sub(lambda m: put("EMAIL", m.group(0)), text)
    text = PATTERNS["CARD"].sub(lambda m: put("CARD", m.group(0)) if luhn(re.sub(r"\D", "", m.group(0))) else m.group(0), text)
    text = PATTERNS["PHONE"].sub(lambda m: put("PHONE", m.group(0)), text)
    return text, mapping

# (text, the PII strings a human labeller marked)
CASES = [
    ("Hi, I'm Maria Gonzalez, my email is maria.g@example.com", ["Maria Gonzalez", "maria.g@example.com"]),
    ("Call me on +44 7700 900123 about order 8841", ["+44 7700 900123"]),
    ("Card 4111 1111 1111 1111 was charged twice", ["4111 1111 1111 1111"]),
    ("Please ship to 12 Elm Road, Leeds LS6 1AB", ["12 Elm Road, Leeds LS6 1AB"]),
    ("My name is Kenji Watanabe and I live in Osaka", ["Kenji Watanabe", "Osaka"]),
    ("IBAN GB82 WEST 1234 5698 7654 32 please refund", ["GB82 WEST 1234 5698 7654 32"]),
]

def leaked(item, redacted):
    """An item counts as leaked if any word-sized piece of it survives (partial masking is not removal)."""
    return any(tok in redacted for tok in re.findall(r"[\w@.+-]{3,}", item))

def recall(known):
    caught = total = 0
    for text, pii in CASES:
        red, _ = redact(text, known)
        for item in pii:
            total += 1; caught += not leaked(item, red)
    return caught, total

c, n = recall(known=())
print(f"patterns only          : {c}/{n} labelled items removed")
c, n = recall(known=("Maria Gonzalez", "Kenji Watanabe"))
print(f"plus the customer's name: {c}/{n} labelled items removed")

text, pii = CASES[0]
red, mapping = redact(text, known_names=("Maria Gonzalez",))
print("\nsent to the model :", red)
model = ScriptedModel(["Thanks [NAME_1]. I have sent a confirmation to [EMAIL_1]."])
reply = text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": red}]))
for ph, val in mapping.items(): reply = reply.replace(ph, val)
print("shown to the user :", reply)
print("\nleft in the redacted text of the other cases:")
for text, pii in CASES[3:]:
    red, _ = redact(text)
    print("  ", red)

Patterns catch three of eight: the email, the phone number and the card. They cannot catch a name, an address or a place, because those have no fixed shape. A cheap and effective addition is to use entities your own system already knows: the logged-in customer's name, the shipping address on their account. That lifts recall to five of eight, though the stranger's address and the city remain. The IBAN case is the instructive failure: the phone pattern masked a fragment of it, so the redacted text shows "[PHONE_1]" in the middle of an IBAN while the rest stays readable, which looks like protection and is not. Three practical rules follow.

  • Measure the filter on a labelled set from your own traffic, report recall per type, and treat any number as a ceiling, not a guarantee. Machine-learned entity recognisers do better on names and addresses than patterns, and they too miss things.
  • Do not redact what the task needs. If the user asks "what is the status of order 8841?", masking the order number breaks the answer. Decide per field whether the model needs it.
  • Layer it. Minimise the input, filter the output (a model can repeat PII it was given or recall some from its training data), and limit what is stored afterwards. No single layer is the privacy control.

Logs, traces and eval sets

The observability chapter (Tracing & Observability for LLM Systems) argued that content is the evidence and is sensitive. The privacy view of the same decision is purpose limitation and storage limitation (Article 5(1)(b) and (e)): data collected for one purpose should not be quietly reused for another, and should be kept no longer than that purpose needs. For a trace store this means the tiered design: metadata for everyone, redacted content for a limited group, full content only for flagged traces, short time-to-live on the most sensitive tier, and access logged.

Evaluation sets deserve separate attention because they are built to be kept. It is tempting to copy real conversations into a benchmark (Building a Domain Benchmark) or a regression suite (The Improvement Flywheel). Before you do, ask whether the purpose you told users covers it, redact or replace identifiers (substitute realistic fake values rather than placeholders, which can change model behaviour), and record the source, so that a deletion request can reach the copy. Where possible, synthesise cases that keep the structure of a real failure without the person.

Retention and deletion

The GDPR gives people a right to erasure (Article 17), subject to exceptions such as the establishment or defence of legal claims, and expects a response without undue delay and within one month of the request (Article 12(3), extendable in complex cases). Many other laws have similar rights. The engineering difficulty is the list in the diagram: the data is in places keyed in different ways, some of which you cannot search by user.

The script models an erasure request against the stores of the support assistant. Each store has a key (how rows are found), a normal retention and whether one user's rows can be deleted now. One store is under a legal hold, as when a dispute requires preserving evidence.

from datetime import date, timedelta

TODAY = date(2026, 10, 14)

# Where one user's data from a support assistant can end up.
# (store, normal retention in days or None, how the data is keyed, can we delete one user's rows now?)
STORES = [
    ("app database (account, tickets)",   None, "user id",        True),
    ("trace store: redacted messages",     180, "user id hash",   True),
    ("trace store: full-content sample",    30, "trace id only",  False),   # cannot find one user's rows cheaply
    ("vector index of past tickets",      None, "ticket id",      True),
    ("eval set built from production",    None, "copied text",    False),   # free text, no key
    ("nightly database backups",            35, "whole snapshot", False),
    ("model vendor logs (abuse review)",    30, "vendor-held",    False),
    ("analytics warehouse extract",        365, "user id",        True),
]
LEGAL_HOLD = {"trace store: full-content sample"}          # preserved for a dispute: do not delete

def plan(request_day):
    rows, last = [], request_day
    for store, ttl, key, can_delete in STORES:
        if store in LEGAL_HOLD:
            action, done = "HOLD: legal hold, restrict access, review date set", None
        elif can_delete:
            action, done = "delete now", request_day
        elif ttl is not None:
            done = request_day + timedelta(days=ttl)
            action = f"expires by TTL on {done}"
        else:
            action, done = "manual scrub: search text, remove, re-verify", None
        if done and done > last: last = done
        rows.append((store, key, action))
    return rows, last

rows, last = plan(TODAY)
print(f"erasure request received {TODAY}")
for store, key, action in rows:
    print(f"  {store:<38}{key:<16}{action}")
print(f"\nautomated paths finish by {last} ({(last - TODAY).days} days)")
print("open items:", sum(a.startswith(("HOLD", "manual")) for _, _, a in rows))

  trace store: redacted messages        user id hash    delete now
  trace store: full-content sample      trace id only   HOLD: legal hold, restrict access, review date set
  vector index of past tickets          ticket id       delete now
  eval set built from production        copied text     manual scrub: search text, remove, re-verify
  nightly database backups              whole snapshot  expires by TTL on 2026-11-18

  analytics warehouse extract           user id         delete now

The plan splits the stores into four kinds. Those keyed by a user identifier can be deleted at once. Those with a short time-to-live can be left to expire (backups in 35 days, the vendor's logs in 30), which regulators generally accept for backups when the data is kept out of use and the schedule is short, but check that with your data protection officer, and make sure restores re-apply the deletion. Those with a legal hold must not be deleted, only restricted, with a review date. And the eval set, keyed by nothing, needs a manual scrub: search the text, remove, verify. The two open items are the ones to design out. Give each store a user key (a hashed identifier in traces), expire the full-content tier quickly, and log which production items entered which eval set.

Deletion also collides with preservation. In May 2025, in the New York Times copyright case against OpenAI, a US magistrate judge ordered OpenAI to preserve output log data, including data users had deleted, regardless of its usual retention policy (secondary coverage by law firms; later developments were not verified). The practical lesson is general: a court order or legal hold can override your retention promise, and your vendor's too. Know who can place a hold, and say honestly in your privacy notice what you retain and when a hold could extend it. The same principle applies to preserved evidence after an incident (AI Incident Response). Backups and their restore tests are covered in Backups & Disaster Recovery; deletion across logs and backups in general in Data Protection & Privacy.

Training on customer data

Two different questions hide under this heading.

Will my vendor train on what I send?

Providers differ, and they differ between consumer and business products. As an example, Anthropic announced in August 2025 that consumer plans would start using chats for model training unless users opted out, with retention of up to five years for those who allowed it, while commercial products and API use were excluded (secondary reports; the primary announcement was not fetched, so read the current terms). Such terms change, so an architecture review should treat them as inputs to check, not facts to remember. Ask for the answer in writing in the contract: whether prompts and outputs are used for training or evaluation, whether human reviewers can read them, how long they are retained (including for abuse monitoring), and whether a zero-data-retention option exists for you. If your staff use consumer chat products for work, none of your enterprise terms applies to what they paste, which is a policy and tooling problem, not only a contract one.

Should I train or fine-tune on my users' data?

If you fine-tune a model on customer conversations (Supervised Fine-Tuning), the data now sits in the weights. Models can memorise and repeat rare strings from training data, and there is no reliable way to delete one person's contribution from trained weights short of retraining. So decide before training: what legal basis and notice cover the use, whether the data can be filtered and de-identified first, whether each tenant's data may be mixed with others' (usually not without agreement), and what you will do when a deletion request arrives. Options include training only on data that was consented to or synthetic, keeping tenant-specific knowledge in retrieval (deletable) instead of in weights (not), and per-tenant adapters that can be removed (LoRA, QLoRA & Parameter-Efficient Tuning). The European Data Protection Board's Opinion 28/2024 (17 December 2024) holds that a model trained on personal data cannot be assumed to be anonymous, which has to be assessed case by case, that legitimate interest as a legal basis needs a three-step test and cannot be a default, and that unlawful processing in development may affect later use of the model (summaries by law firms; the Opinion itself was not read).

State of play, October 2026

Provider data terms, regional options and zero-retention programmes change often, and this chapter's examples (Anthropic's consumer terms of August 2025, the 2025 preservation order in the New York Times case, the EDPB opinion of December 2024, the Garante's fine of December 2024) were read through secondary sources. Before relying on any of them, read the current terms, data processing addendum and sub-processor list for each vendor you use, and record the date you read them in your system's documentation. Re-check at each renewal and whenever a vendor emails you about changes.

Data residency and transfers

Data residency is the requirement, from law, contract or policy, that data be stored or processed in a given place. It sounds like one property and is at least three: where the data is stored at rest, where it is processed (the region where the model runs when it reads a prompt), and who can access it (support staff, sub-processors and administrators in other countries). A promise about one says nothing about the others.

In the GDPR, moving personal data out of the European Economic Area is a transfer that needs a lawful mechanism (Chapter V, Articles 44 to 49): an adequacy decision for the destination, standard contractual clauses with supplementary measures, or a narrow derogation. Remote access from another country counts. Practical work for an AI system: pin your storage to an approved region; choose a model endpoint that processes in a region you can defend (many providers offer regional endpoints at a price or capacity cost); list sub-processors and where they are; and, when a regional option does not exist for the model you need, either minimise what you send or accept and document the transfer.

Analogy: the photocopier in the office

Imagine an office where every document passes through a photocopier that quietly keeps a copy in its memory, forwards another to a contractor for quality review, and files a third in a cabinet nobody has the key to. When a client asks for their papers to be destroyed, destroying the original is the easy part. Privacy work in an AI system is finding every copier, cabinet and contractor, deciding which copies should exist, and writing down who can destroy each.

Where it breaks down: a paper copy is physical and can be found. An embedding or a fine-tuned weight is a transformed copy that is hard to recognise and sometimes impossible to remove individually, so the decision about whether a copy should exist at all has to be made before it is created.

Common misconception

"We redact PII before logging, so the logs are not personal data." Redaction filters have a recall below 100%, as the script above shows; names, addresses and free text slip through; and pseudonymised data is still personal data under the GDPR if it can be re-identified using other information you hold. Treat the filtered store as lower risk, not as out of scope, and keep access control, retention limits and a deletion path on it. The twin mistake is "the vendor does not train on API data, so we have no privacy exposure": retention, review access, residency and sub-processors remain.

Key takeaways

  • An AI application makes lasting copies of personal data in prompts sent to vendors, trace stores, vector indexes, eval sets, backups and vendor logs. Draw them all and give each an owner, a retention period and a deletion path.
  • Minimise first: send only what the task needs, retrieve only what the user may see, and redact or pseudonymise identifiers. Measure the filter's recall per type on labelled data and use what your system already knows, such as the customer's own name.
  • Treat traces and eval sets as data stores with purpose and storage limits. Tier content, expire it quickly, and record where production text entered an eval set so deletion can reach it.
  • Erasure works only if each store has a key. Plan the four cases: delete now, expire by TTL, hold, and manual scrub. Legal holds can override your retention promise.
  • Get a vendor's training, retention, review, region and sub-processor terms in writing and re-check them. Data in model weights is hard to delete, so decide before fine-tuning on customer data, and prefer retrieval for tenant knowledge.
  • Residency has three parts: storage, processing and access. Pinning your own stores does not decide where the model runs or who can read the vendor's logs.

Check your understanding

A deletion request arrives. You delete the user's rows from the application database. Which store is the most likely to still hold their words, unnoticed?

  1. The password hash table
  2. The load balancer's access log
  3. An evaluation set built by copying production conversations, and the vector index of past tickets
  4. The DNS cache
Derived and copied stores are keyed differently from the source: the vector index by chunk or ticket id, the eval set by nothing at all. The other options hold little or no message content. This is why each copy needs a recorded source and a deletion path.

A redactor has pattern-based recall of 3 of 8 on a labelled set. What is the best improvement for the remaining misses, which are names and addresses?

  1. Add entities your system already knows (the account holder's name and address) and a learned entity recogniser, then re-measure recall
  2. Declare the logs out of scope because redaction ran
  3. Add more regular expressions until recall reaches 100%
  4. Stop measuring, since the number will be low anyway
Names and addresses lack a fixed shape, so more regexes give diminishing returns, and no filter reaches 100%. Known entities and a trained recogniser help, and measurement shows whether they did. Redacted data may still be personal data, so scope does not shrink by declaration.

A team stores everything in an EU region and promises customers "your data never leaves the EU". The model endpoint they call runs in another region. What is the gap?

  1. None, because storage is what residency means
  2. The model reads the prompt but does not keep it, so nothing leaves
  3. The gap exists only for special categories of data
  4. Residency has storage, processing and access parts; the prompt is processed outside the EU, and vendor logs or staff may also sit elsewhere
The promise covers only the data at rest in your region. The prompt crosses to wherever inference happens, which can be a transfer under the GDPR, and vendor-side logs, review staff and sub-processors need their own answers. Regional endpoints or a narrower promise would fix it.

A product team wants to fine-tune a model on last year's support conversations. Which approach best limits the problem of later deletion requests?

  1. Fine-tune on everything, since the model will not repeat the data
  2. Keep customer-specific knowledge in a retrieval index, which can be edited, and fine-tune only on consented, de-identified or synthetic data
  3. Fine-tune and tell users that deletion is impossible
  4. Delete the conversations from the database afterwards, which removes them from the model
Retrieval stores can have records removed, while weights cannot reliably forget a person short of retraining. Deleting the source does not remove what the model memorised, and models can repeat rare training strings. Telling users deletion is impossible does not meet the legal obligation.

Your retention policy keeps full-content traces for 30 days. A litigation hold is placed on a set of traces from 20 days ago. A user in that set asks for erasure. What is the most defensible handling?

  1. Restrict access to the held traces, record the hold and its basis, tell the user what can and cannot be deleted for now, and delete when the hold ends
  2. Delete them immediately; a deletion request always wins
  3. Ignore the request until the hold is lifted without replying
  4. Move the traces to a personal drive to keep them safe
Erasure has exceptions, including the establishment or defence of legal claims, and a hold may legally require preservation. The defensible path is to restrict, document and communicate, then delete when the hold ends. Deleting may breach the hold, ignoring the user breaches the response duty, and a personal drive is a new uncontrolled copy.

Which statement about vendor terms is the most reliable guide for an architecture review?

  1. Consumer and business products from one vendor always share the same data terms
  2. If a vendor does not train on API data today, it never will
  3. Terms on training, retention, review and regions differ by product and change over time, so record the version you reviewed and the date, and re-check at renewal
  4. Data terms matter only for the largest vendors
Vendors have split consumer and business terms and have changed defaults, so a review is a dated snapshot. Assuming terms are shared across products, fixed over time or only relevant to large vendors are the common ways teams are surprised.
