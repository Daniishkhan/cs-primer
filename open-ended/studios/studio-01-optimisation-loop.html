Design an Automated Optimisation Loop

A service business wants a better rule for deciding which job a free technician takes next, and someone has suggested letting a language model evolve the rule. Design the whole loop: whether to search at all, the evaluator, the budget, the archive, the guard rails and the rollout. The evaluator turns out to be the hard part, and the search will find every hole in it.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the path of an optimisation project: clarify, decide whether to search, build the evaluator, budget, choose the search space, draw the architecture, run the loop, break the evaluator on purpose, add safety, roll out, and summarise aloud. A rubric at the end separates a competent answer from a senior one. Every number in the worked answers comes from a script on this page (a small simulator with fixed seeds, so you can rerun it) or is labelled as an assumption you should challenge. The mutation step, which a real system would hand to a language model, is a deterministic stand-in here: random edits to a weight vector. The design questions do not depend on which one you use.

📐 The brief

An operations director at a regional home-repair company says: "Our dispatcher picks the next job for each free technician with a rule an old supervisor wrote years ago. I have read that AI systems now evolve programs and beat human-written heuristics. Can we point one at our dispatch rule and let it run? Tell me what it would take, what it would cost, and how we would know it was safe to switch on."

  • About 10 technicians in the field, around 85 jobs a day, three job classes: emergency (burst pipe, no heating; due within 2 hours), priority (due within 4 hours) and standard (due the same working day). A late job costs more the higher its class.
  • The rule today is an expert rule: urgency first, travel time as a tie-breaker. Nobody has measured how good it is.
  • There is two years of job logs (arrival time, location, class, duration) but no simulator. Engineering can build one in a few weeks.
  • A bad rule is expensive in a visible way: a missed emergency is a refund and a bad review, and a rule that quietly ignores some customers is a regulatory and reputational problem.
  • Builds on LLM-guided program evolution, open evolution frameworks, evaluator and reward hacking, held-out evaluation, safety of self-modifying systems and search inside real products. The mini FunSearch in Lab 3 is the same loop at toy scale.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes. Nothing here needs more than the Python standard library.

2Should we search at all?

Before building anything, what would you measure to decide whether an evolutionary search is worth its cost? Name the baselines and what result would make you stop.

Compare with a worked answer

Chapter 18 gives the ladder: the default, an expert rule, hand tuning, random search, hill climbing, then population or LLM-guided search. Each rung is the baseline for the next. Below, all six rungs are run on the simulator of stage 3 (the file dispatch_eval.py, listed there), with a budget of 300 evaluations for each search, six repeats per method, and the cost reported on 100 days the searches never saw. Lower is better.

import random, statistics
from dispatch_eval import simulate
from loop import *
def total(p, days): return statistics.mean(simulate(p, d)["cost"] for d in days)
def random_search(budget, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed); best = None
    for _ in range(budget):
        w = [rng.uniform(-1, 6), rng.uniform(-1, 3), rng.uniform(0, 8), rng.uniform(-2, 3), rng.uniform(0, 4)]
        c = score(w, SEARCH)
        if best is None or c < best[0]: best = (c, w)
    return best
def hill_climb(budget, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed); w, c = EXPERT, score(EXPERT, SEARCH)
    for _ in range(budget - 1):
        cand = mutate(w, rng); cc = score(cand, SEARCH)
        if cc <= c: w, c = cand, cc
    return c, w

  • The incumbent is already good. The expert rule costs 22.95, against 53.62 for first come first served. Its gap to the best search average (18.14) is 4.81 units, or about 21 per cent of the incumbent's cost. That 21 per cent is the whole prize, and it is worth having only if a unit is worth enough (stage 4).
  • The searches cannot be ranked. Random search at 19.78 (runs 18.3 to 21.4), hill climbing at 18.14 (17.7 to 19.5) and the stand-in evolution at 18.51 (17.8 to 19.6) overlap. In a space of five numbers a cheap method is hard to beat. Evolution earns its place when the space is programs rather than weights (stage 5), where there is no gradient and no sensible random sampler.
  • Stop rules decided now. Stop and ship the expert rule with a hand tweak if the best search is within noise of the incumbent on the selection days (stage 7). Stop if building the evaluator costs more than a year of the expected saving. Continue if the gain interval clears the margin the business named.
  • Error analysis is still free. Read the days where the expert rule does worst before searching. If the dominant failure is "a technician drove 40 minutes past a nearby job", that is a one-line rule change, and the search would only rediscover it (error analysis).

The honest summary for the director: measure first; the search is probably worth about a fifth of the cost, and a cheap search finds most of it. The reason to build the full loop is the second and third runs, as demand shifts, and the ability to evolve rules richer than five weights.

3Build the evaluator

Design the scorer. What goes in it, how many simulated days does each evaluation need, and what are the three jobs the score has to do (chapter 16)?

Compare with a worked answer

The evaluator is a simulator of one working day. Jobs arrive during the day with a class, a location and a duration; technicians finish a job, then take the waiting job with the highest score from the policy; nobody starts a job after the shift ends, and unstarted jobs carry over to tomorrow. The cost has four parts: lateness times the class penalty, travel time, overtime, and a charge for each carried-over job. Every constant is an assumption chosen to make the trade-offs real (what one unit of cost is worth in money is a business question, taken up in stage 4).

"""The evaluator: a one-day simulator of a field-service business. Every constant is an assumption."""
import random

N_TECH, DAY_H = 10, 10.0                     # technicians; shift length (nobody starts a job after DAY_H)
CLASSES = {"emergency": (0.10, 2.0, 5.0),    # share of jobs, deadline in hours, penalty per late hour
           "priority": (0.30, 4.0, 2.0),
           "standard": (0.60, 8.0, 1.0)}
OVERTIME, TRAVEL_COST, CARRY_FLAT, CARRY_HOURS = 3.0, 0.5, 10.0, 6.0

def make_day(seed, rate=85):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    jobs = []
    for i in range(rate):
        t = rng.uniform(0, 9.0)
        c = rng.choices(list(CLASSES), [v[0] for v in CLASSES.values()])[0]
        jobs.append(dict(t=t, cls=c, x=rng.uniform(0, 20), y=rng.uniform(0, 20),
                         dur=max(0.3, rng.lognormvariate(0.0, 0.5)), due=t + CLASSES[c][1], pen=CLASSES[c][2]))
    return sorted(jobs, key=lambda j: j["t"])

def simulate(policy, seed, rate=85, charge_carried=True):
    """policy(features) -> score; a free technician takes the waiting job with the highest score."""
    jobs, techs = make_day(seed, rate), [dict(free=0.0, x=10.0, y=10.0) for _ in range(N_TECH)]
    waiting, i, cost, travel, done, late_std = [], 0, 0.0, 0.0, 0, 0
    while True:
        te = min(techs, key=lambda q: q["free"]); now = te["free"]
        if now >= DAY_H: break                                   # earliest-free technician is past shift end
        while i < len(jobs) and jobs[i]["t"] <= now:
            waiting.append(jobs[i]); i += 1
        if not waiting:
            if i >= len(jobs): break
            te["free"] = jobs[i]["t"]; continue
        best, best_s = None, -1e18
        for j in waiting:
            tr = (abs(j["x"] - te["x"]) + abs(j["y"] - te["y"])) / 30.0     # hours at 30 km/h
            s = policy(dict(wait=now - j["t"], slack=j["due"] - now - tr, travel=tr, dur=j["dur"], pen=j["pen"]))
            if s > best_s: best, best_s = (j, tr), s
        j, tr = best; waiting.remove(j)
        finish = now + tr + j["dur"]
        te.update(free=finish, x=j["x"], y=j["y"])
        late = max(0.0, finish - j["due"])
        cost += j["pen"] * late; travel += tr; done += 1
        late_std += (j["cls"] == "standard" and late > 0)
    carried = waiting + jobs[i:]
    cost += TRAVEL_COST * travel + OVERTIME * sum(max(0.0, t["free"] - DAY_H) for t in techs)
    if charge_carried:                                           # v2 evaluator: a job left for tomorrow is a late job
        cost += sum(j["pen"] * (max(0.0, DAY_H - j["due"]) + CARRY_HOURS) + CARRY_FLAT for j in carried)
    return dict(cost=cost / len(jobs) * 10, carried=len(carried), urgent_carried=sum(j["cls"] != "standard" for j in carried),
                travel=travel / max(1, done), late_std=late_std / max(1, done))

Three design decisions in that file matter more than the code around them.

  1. A carried-over job is charged. The first draft of this evaluator (called v1 in stage 8) did not charge it. The flag charge_carried keeps both versions so you can watch what the search does with each.
  2. Each day is a seed. A day is a pure function of its seed, so the same days can be replayed against any policy (paired comparison) and different seeds can be assigned to different jobs.
  3. The policy sees features, not the answer. It receives waiting time, slack to the deadline, travel hours, duration and the class penalty. It cannot see the job's identity, the customer, or the future.

How many days per evaluation? The simulated cost of a day is noisy, and the search will ask the evaluator thousands of questions. The script measures that noise for the incumbent over 200 seeded days and then the noise of a candidate-minus-incumbent difference.

import statistics, math

  • One day is not a measurement. The standard deviation of a day is 7.9 on a mean of 24.7. With the 4 search days used above, the half-width of a 95 per cent interval for one rule is 7.75 units, wider than the whole 4.8 unit gain being chased. The search loop is therefore working with a signal it can barely see; it is guided by it, not convinced by it.
  • Pairing helps a lot. Comparing a candidate with the incumbent on the same days has a standard deviation of 4.36 instead of 7.9. At 100 days the paired half-width is 0.85, small against a gain of 5. A gain of 10 per cent of the incumbent's cost (2.5 units) needs only about 12 paired days to resolve, though the 100-day figure is the one to quote.
  • Three sets, three jobs. The search set (4 days, here) guides the loop and is cheap and noisy. The selection set (20 fresh days) picks among the 5 finalists. The test set (100 days, drawn from a later period of the logs) is opened once, by a person, at the end. Section 7 runs the whole protocol.
  • Fidelity versus speed. The toy simulator runs in milliseconds. A realistic one with road networks, parts availability and no-shows might take a minute of CPU a day. The design knob is a cascade: a cheap approximate simulator screens candidates, and only the top few meet the expensive one (chapter 10 describes this staged evaluation in OpenEvolve).

Validate the simulator itself. Run the incumbent through it for last quarter and compare simulated lateness, travel and overtime with the real ones. If they differ by more than about 10 per cent, fix the simulator before searching: every score the search sees is a statement about the simulator, not the business.

4Estimate the budget

What will one run cost in compute and model calls, what does the project cost in people, and what does the gain have to be worth for this to break even?

Compare with a worked answer

A run has three parts: the model calls that propose candidates, the simulated days that score them, and the people. The people are the surprise. Every figure below is an assumption to replace with your own: 1,500 proposed candidates, a quarter of them rejected before simulation (they crash or fail a smoke test), 6,000 input and 1,500 output tokens per proposal, token prices of $3 and $15 per million, 90 core-seconds per realistic simulated day at $0.04 per core-hour, and 15 engineer-days at $600.

  • A run is cheap; the project is not. The run costs $65 and finishes in about two hours on 64 cores. The 15 engineer-days (simulator validation, evaluator hardening, reviewing the winner, the rollout) cost $9,000. Teams budget the first number and are surprised by the second.
  • The value hangs on one conversion. The test gain of stage 7 is 4.66 cost units per ten jobs. Over 25,500 jobs a year, that is $118,830 if a unit is worth $10, but only $11,883 if a unit is worth $1. Break-even sits at $0.77 per unit. The director's finance team must say what a unit is worth before the project starts; if it is near break-even, take the expert rule and a hand tweak.
  • Re-runs are part of the price. The year-one figure includes three runs (the first and two re-tunes). The people cost does not triple, because the evaluator and the review process already exist; the re-run is mostly compute and a smaller review.
  • Which lever hurts first? Model-call cost scales with candidates; simulation cost scales with candidates times days. If the real simulator were 100 times slower, simulation would be about $470 and still not the problem. The scarce resource is trustworthy evaluation, not tokens.
  • Fix the budget before the run. Candidates, dollars and wall-clock hours go in a config file, with a hard stop. A budget chosen after seeing the curve is a reporting choice, and it inflates results (chapter 16).

Cost of the model calls themselves is the subject of cost, latency and quality trade-offs.

Start with the weight vector, then widen only if it earns it. The ladder of stage 2 says a five-number space is easy to search by any method. If the best weights beat the incumbent by a margin that matters, move to a short typed program, because that is where an LLM mutation operator can add structure (a special case for jobs near the shift end, say) that no random sampler would invent. The reason to start narrow is review: the manager must be able to read every candidate that reaches the finalists.

What stays fixed, outside the evolved function:

  • The constraint wrapper: emergencies within 30 minutes of their deadline are served first, whatever the score says; any job waiting more than 12 hours is served next. The evolved function only ranks what remains. This is the hard-constraint layer of chapter 17, and it is why the search cannot buy a better score by starving anyone.
  • The feature dictionary. Adding a feature (say, customer postcode) is a human decision with a review, never something the search may add.
  • The evaluator, the seeds, the budget and the loop itself. In the vocabulary of self-improving agents, this system does not edit itself: it edits one function. That is a deliberate choice, and it shrinks the safety problem a great deal.

The mutation operator. In production, the operator is a prompt to a language model: the parent function, its score, the scores of two good and two diverse relatives, and an instruction to propose a better version as a diff (chapter 9). Here it is mutate() in loop.py (stage 7), which perturbs one or two weights with Gaussian noise, from a seeded generator. Everything else about the loop is unchanged by that swap, which is the point of keeping the operator behind one function.

6Draw the architecture

Draw the loop and its surroundings. Where is the trust boundary, what is the search allowed to touch, and what sits outside it?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Everything generated runs in the sandbox. The candidate function is untrusted code, even when a friendly model wrote it: no network, no filesystem outside a scratch directory, CPU and wall-clock limits, no credentials (container security and chapter 17).
  • The diff guard is a cheap gate before the expensive one. It parses the candidate and rejects anything that is not a pure function of the feature dictionary: imports, file or network calls, access to the evaluator's modules, reads of environment variables. This catches whole classes of cheating before a single simulated day.
  • The archive is the memory of the search. It keeps the best candidate in each region of behaviour (stage 7), not just the best overall, so that an unusual but promising rule survives long enough to be mutated.
  • The sealed test set is held by a person. It is a later period of the logs, resampled into simulated days, stored where the loop has no read access. It is opened once, after the finalists are chosen.
  • The dashed arrow from the live dispatcher is how the evaluator stays honest over time: fresh job logs refresh the simulator's arrival and duration distributions each quarter. Nothing flows the other way automatically.

The structure is the same as the batch-job pattern of a distributed job scheduler: the evaluations are independent jobs, a queue feeds them to workers, and the controller needs idempotent result writes so a retried evaluation cannot double-count.

7Run the loop: archive, selection and test

Describe the search procedure end to end: how parents are chosen, what the archive keeps, how finalists are picked, and what you report. Then run it on the simulator.

Compare with a worked answer

The loop is quality-diversity in miniature (chapter 4). Each candidate is scored on the search days and also described by two behaviours: its mean travel hours per job and how many jobs it leaves for tomorrow. The archive is a 4 × 4 grid over those two numbers and keeps the cheapest candidate in each cell. A parent is drawn uniformly from the archive's elites, not from the top few, so a rule that travels more but protects deadlines (or the reverse) keeps being mutated. This is the MAP-Elites idea (chapter 4; pyribs for a library version).

"""The search loop around the evaluator. The 'LLM mutation' is a seeded stand-in: random edits to a weight vector."""
import random, statistics
from dispatch_eval import simulate

SEARCH, SELECT, TEST = list(range(100, 104)), list(range(200, 220)), list(range(5000, 5100))
EXPERT = [0.0, 0.0, 4.0, 0.0, 1.0]            # incumbent: urgency - 4 * travel hours

def policy(w):                                 # weights: wait, penalty, travel, duration, urgency
    return lambda f: (w[0]*f["wait"] + w[1]*f["pen"] - w[2]*f["travel"] - w[3]*f["dur"]
                      + w[4]*f["pen"]/max(0.25, f["slack"] + 1.0))

def score(w, days, charge_carried=True, rate=85):
    return statistics.mean(simulate(policy(w), d, rate, charge_carried)["cost"] for d in days)

def mutate(w, rng):                            # stand-in for 'ask the model to edit the rule'
    c = list(w)
    for k in rng.sample(range(5), rng.choice([1, 2])): c[k] += rng.gauss(0, 0.8)
    return c

def evolve(charge_carried, budget, seed, mu=10):
    rng, f = random.Random(seed), lambda w: score(w, SEARCH, charge_carried)
    pop = [(f(EXPERT), EXPERT)] + [(f(m), m) for m in (mutate(EXPERT, rng) for _ in range(mu - 1))]
    for _ in range(budget - mu):
        parent = rng.choice(sorted(pop)[:5])[1]
        child = mutate(parent, rng); pop.append((f(child), child)); pop = sorted(pop)[:mu]
    return pop[0]

The protocol around it is the three-set design of stage 3: search on 4 days, choose among five finalists on 20 fresh days, test the winner once on 100 later days, and report a paired bootstrap interval for the incumbent minus the winner, in cost per ten jobs. A second test uses a demand rise of 12 per cent (95 jobs a day) that the search never saw.

    m = lambda k: statistics.mean(x[k] for x in r)
    return m("cost"), (m("travel"), m("carried"))

def cell(desc):          # behaviour: (travel hours per job, jobs carried over per day) -> cell of a 4 x 4 grid
    t, c = desc
    return (min(3, max(0, int((t - 0.20) / 0.05))), min(3, max(0, int((c - 3) / 3))))

def map_elites(budget, seed):
    rng, archive = random.Random(seed), {}
    def add(w):
        c, d = evaluate(w, SEARCH); k = cell(d)
        if k not in archive or c < archive[k][0]: archive[k] = (c, w)
    add(EXPERT)
    while budget > 1:
        parent = rng.choice(list(archive.values()))[1]       # any elite, not only the best
        add(mutate(parent, rng)); budget -= 1
    return archive

def paired_ci(a, b, days, rate=85, n_boot=2000, seed=0):    # cost(a) - cost(b) per day, bootstrap over days
    gaps = [simulate(policy(a), d, rate)["cost"] - simulate(policy(b), d, rate)["cost"] for d in days]
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    boots = sorted(statistics.mean(rng.choices(gaps, k=len(gaps))) for _ in range(n_boot))
    return statistics.mean(gaps), boots[int(0.025 * n_boot)], boots[int(0.975 * n_boot)]

Common misconception

"The search score is the improvement." The score the loop saw on its 4 search days is a noisy, selected number that the winner was picked for being good at. Assume the search will exploit any flaw in the score. Name three ways this evaluator could be gamed, then say how you would find out which one is real before it ships.

Chapter 15 lists the families: exploit the harness, exploit the data, exploit a degenerate task, exploit the metric. Candidates for this evaluator, before any experiment:

  1. The metric forgets someone. Lateness is only counted for jobs that are started. If a job left for tomorrow costs nothing, a rule that never serves hard jobs has a lovely score.
  2. Memorising the days. If the search set is four fixed seeds, a rule with enough freedom could fit those four days. With five weights this is unlikely; with free-form code it is not.
  3. Reaching the harness. A generated function that reads the simulator's job list, or changes a constant in the evaluator module, scores perfectly. The diff guard exists for this.

Number 1 is cheap to test, because the first draft of the evaluator really did have it. Chapter 15's checklist has the longer list, and Lab 6 lets you harden one.

Two points are easy to miss. First, the person who wrote the evaluator should not be the only person who approves its winner: the author knows which corner cases the evaluator ignores and is the worst placed to see the search use them. Second, change control applies to the evaluator too. A new evaluator version changes every score; keep its version in each log row and never compare numbers across versions (chapter 18). The review checklist at the end of chapter 17 is a good template for the sign-off.

This loop is much less dangerous than a self-modifying agent, because the search edits a pure function that decides a ranking, in a business where a person can override any dispatch. Matching the controls to the freedom you grant is the design principle; the more of the system the loop may edit, the more of the table above has to be built before you start.

What to monitor, in order of importance: the hard-constraint overrides (a rise means the evolved score is drifting into territory the wrapper has to rescue), emergency and priority on-time rates by depot, carried-over jobs per day (the descriptor that flagged v1), overtime, complaints by class, and the live version of the offline metric. See deployment strategies for the canary mechanics and SLOs and error budgets for turning "no worse than" into a number.

Triggers to roll back: any breach of a hard-constraint metric; emergency on-time rate down more than the agreed margin on two consecutive days; carried-over jobs above the incumbent's level by a set amount. These are automatic, not discussed. Triggers to search again: the quarterly schedule; a new depot, job type or seasonal peak; a refreshed simulator that changes the incumbent's own score materially. Re-runs start from the previous winner and the archive, with a smaller budget.

Ownership. The rule, the evaluator, the budget and the monitoring dashboard belong to a named team, not to whoever ran the search. The evaluator gets tests (does v2 charge a carried-over job?) like any production code, and the budget for the next quarter's re-run is in the plan.

  • Run it continuously. Treat it as an online learning system: the evaluator needs a live refresh, the held-out set is now a moving target (the reused test set problem of chapter 16 comes back in full), and every automatic promotion needs a guard metric and a rollback. Prefer a scheduled loop with a human gate; continuous promotion is a different, riskier product.
  • Cover routing too. The search space is bigger, the evaluator more expensive and the score noisier. Use the same ladder, expect operations-research solvers to be the strong baseline, and consider evolving only a small part (a cost function a solver uses) rather than the solver.
  • Let the model add features. This widens what the function can see, including attributes it must not use. Require a human to approve each new feature and run a fairness check (does the rule's pick change when only the customer's neighbourhood changes?) as part of the evaluator.
  • Let the loop edit its own mutation prompt or evaluator. That is self-modification in the sense of chapter 13; do not add it to a production loop without the controls of chapter 17, and never let anything the loop writes touch the evaluator.
  • The gain is small, or a unit is worth little. Ship the expert rule and the hand tweak that error analysis suggests, close the project and write down why. That is a successful project.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"Before anyone evolves anything I would measure today's rule against a few cheap ones. On a simulator, first come first served costs 54 per ten jobs, your expert rule 23, and the best search I tried about 18, so the expert rule has most of the gain and the search is worth roughly a fifth. The evaluator is the project. It is a simulator of a working day that charges lateness by class, travel, overtime and every job left for tomorrow, validated against last quarter. I found out why that last charge matters the hard way: my first version did not charge carried-over jobs, and the loop reported a cost of 3 where the real cost was 60, because it learned to skip the hard jobs. So the scorer charges everything, a second independent scorer re-checks the finalists, and tripwires flag candidates whose behaviour, such as carry-over per day, moves far from today's.

The search edits only one pure function in a sandbox, behind a diff guard and a fixed wrapper that always serves near-deadline emergencies and anyone waiting more than 12 hours. It keeps an archive of the best rule per behaviour cell, picks five finalists, chooses among them on fresh days and opens a sealed later-period test once. The result is a paired gain of about 4.7 units with an interval of 3.8 to 5.6. A manager reads the winner, we replay it offline, shadow it for two weeks, canary it in one depot against a holdback, and keep the old rule behind a switch. We re-run quarterly, with a budget fixed in advance, and the evaluator has an owner."

Notice the shape: measure the baselines → price a unit → build and harden the evaluator → narrow search space inside a sandbox → archive, selection, sealed test → staged rollout with an owner.

Key takeaways

  • Measure the incumbent and cheap baselines first. In the worked case the expert rule captured about 86 per cent of the gain available, and three searches were not clearly distinguishable.
  • The evaluator is the product. A simulator that forgot to charge for jobs left undone let the search report a cost of about 3 where the real cost was roughly 60.
  • One day of simulation is noise (standard deviation 7.9 on a mean of 24.7). Pair comparisons on shared days and report an interval from days the search never touched.
  • The money is in people and upkeep, and the value hangs on what one unit of the score is worth. Price it before building.
  • Search a narrow, readable space inside a sandbox, with hard constraints the search cannot edit, then roll out through shadow and canary with a rollback switch and a named owner.
