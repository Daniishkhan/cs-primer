Review a Self-Improvement Claim

A colleague's team presents a slide: "our coding agent improved itself by 30%". You are asked whether the company should believe it, fund more of it, or build on it. Decide what you need to see, work out what the process could have produced by luck, and write a verdict that a manager can act on and the team can accept as fair.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock review session of 60 to 75 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong review with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the review of a result rather than the design of a system: restate the claim, check whether it is outside noise, simulate the process that produced it, audit what data was used for what, audit what the agent could touch, check the baseline, decide what evidence to request, read what the evidence might show, weigh generality and cost, and write the verdict. A rubric at the end separates a competent review from a senior one. The team, the agent and the numbers on the slide are invented for this exercise; every other number comes from a script on this page (fixed seeds, so you can rerun it) or from a source named in the text.

📐 The brief

A team in your company presents one slide to the leadership group: "Our coding-support agent improved itself by 30%. We let it edit its own scaffold for 60 iterations and it went from 41% to 53% on our benchmark. We would like budget to run it on the whole platform." The head of engineering asks you, the applied AI engineer, to review the claim before the meeting ends.

  • What the slide and the short write-up say: a frozen foundation model inside a scaffold of prompts, tools and a workflow; an internal benchmark of 150 repository tasks, each passed or failed by running the project's tests; 61 tasks solved at the start and 80 at the end (40.7% to 53.3%).
  • The method, in two sentences: in each of 60 iterations the agent read its own logs, proposed a change to its scaffold and applied it, producing a child agent. The child was run on the 150 tasks and kept if it scored higher than its parent.
  • What the slide does not say: how many runs were made, what the baseline got on a second attempt, which tasks were used for what, what the agent was allowed to edit, how much it cost, or whether anyone read the changes.
  • The leadership is not technical. They want to know: is it real, is it worth money, and is it safe to let it keep going?
  • Builds on self-improving coding agents, evaluator and reward hacking, held-out evaluation and safety of self-modifying systems. Case 6 reads a published system of the same kind, and statistics for evals covers the intervals used here.
  • You have 60 to 75 minutes. Nothing here needs more than the Python standard library.

Your notes

Keep your questions, your guess at how much of the gain is real, and the list of evidence you would ask for.

1Restate the claim precisely

"Improved by 30%" can mean several things. Write the claim as numbers, with the unit, the base, the data and the number of runs, and say which of these the slide leaves unspecified.

Compare with a worked answer

Start by turning the slide into arithmetic. Relative and absolute gains are different claims, and a score on 150 tasks has a measurable uncertainty from the first line.

import math
def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z*z/n
    c = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / d; h = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h
n, ka, kb = 150, 61, 80
a, b = ka / n, kb / n
print(f"claim: {a:.1%} -> {b:.1%}   absolute {100*(b-a):.1f} points   relative {100*(b/a-1):.0f}%")
print(f"tasks solved: {ka} -> {kb} of {n}   (a gain of {kb-ka} tasks)")
for name, k in (("before", ka), ("after", kb)):
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n); print(f"  {name}: {k/n:.1%}  95% interval {lo:.1%} to {hi:.1%}")
print(f"standard error of one score near 50%: {100*math.sqrt(0.25/n):.1f} points; a re-run of the same agent can move by about +-{196*math.sqrt(0.25/n):.0f} points")
for d in (40, 60, 100):
    print(f"if the two agents disagree on d = {d} tasks, a net gain of {kb-ka} tasks has z = {(kb-ka)/math.sqrt(d):.2f}")

  • "30%" is a relative gain. The absolute gain is 12.7 percentage points, or 19 more tasks solved out of 150. A relative figure sounds larger than the same change in points, and it is larger still when the base is small. Ask for both, always.
  • Each score has a wide interval. The 95 per cent (Wilson) interval for the starting 61 of 150 is 33.1% to 48.7%, and for the final 80 of 150 it is 45.4% to 61.1%. They overlap between 45.4% and 48.7%. Overlap does not by itself mean no difference, because the two agents were scored on the same tasks (a paired comparison is sharper), but it shows how far from conclusive two bare percentages are.
  • One run moves by about eight points. The standard error of a score near 50% on 150 tasks is 4.1 points, so the same agent run twice can plausibly land eight points apart. The claimed gain (12.7 points) is only 1.6 times that.
  • What a paired design would need. If the two agents disagree on 60 of the 150 tasks, a net gain of 19 tasks has z = 2.45, which is respectable. If they disagree on 100, z = 1.90, which is not. The slide cannot be judged without the per-task results, which is the first thing to ask for.

The claim, restated: "On a 150-task internal benchmark, in a single run per agent, an agent chosen from 60 descendants by its score on that benchmark solved 19 more tasks than its ancestor (12.7 points, 31% relative)." Every clause of that sentence is a place a reviewer can push: chosen from 60, by its score on that benchmark, single run, internal benchmark.

2Simulate the process that produced the number

Suppose the 60 edits were equally likely to help or hurt. The team kept a child whenever it scored higher than its parent on the 150 tasks. How big a "gain" would that process report on average, and how much would be real?

Compare with a worked answer

This is the winner's curse of chapter 16 applied to a loop that runs it 60 times in a row. The script below is a toy and not the team's system. Each edit changes the agent's true success rate by a random amount (standard deviation 1.5 points). Each evaluation is the number of passes in 150 independent trials at the true rate. A child replaces its parent if it scores higher. Everything is seeded and averaged over 2,000 runs. In the first scenario edits help and hurt equally often; in the second, edits help by 0.2 points on average. A final agent is also re-measured once on 500 fresh tasks.

"""Ratchet on noise: 60 self-edits that are, on average, neither good nor bad; keep a child if it scores higher than its parent."""
import random, statistics
def run(seed, iters=60, n=150, start=61/150, edit_mean=0.0, step=0.015):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    measure = lambda p: sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(n)) / n
    true, seen = start, measure(start)
    first = seen
    for _ in range(iters):
        child = min(0.95, max(0.05, true + rng.gauss(edit_mean, step)))   # an edit helps or hurts at random
        s = measure(child)
        if s > seen: true, seen = child, s                              # keep it if it looks better
    fresh = sum(rng.random() < true for _ in range(500)) / 500          # one re-measurement on 500 unseen tasks
    return first, seen, true, fresh
for label, mean_edit in (("edits help or hurt equally often (mean effect 0)", 0.0), ("edits help slightly (+0.2 points on average)", 0.002)):
    rs = [run(s, edit_mean=mean_edit) for s in range(2000)]
    first = statistics.mean(r[0] for r in rs); seen = statistics.mean(r[1] for r in rs); true = statistics.mean(r[2] for r in rs)
    fr = statistics.mean(r[3] for r in rs)
    big = sum(r[1] - r[0] >= 0.127 for r in rs) / len(rs)
    print(f"{label}")
    print(f"  measured start {first:.3f}  measured final {seen:.3f}  true final {true:.3f}  fresh re-measure {fr:.3f}  -> reported gain {100*(seen-first):+.1f} pts, true gain {100*(true-61/150):+.1f} pts")
    print(f"  runs whose reported gain reaches the claimed +12.7 points: {big:.1%}")
edits help or hurt equally often (mean effect 0)
  measured start 0.407  measured final 0.531  true final 0.440  fresh re-measure 0.440  -> reported gain +12.4 pts, true gain +3.3 pts
  runs whose reported gain reaches the claimed +12.7 points: 45.0%
edits help slightly (+0.2 points on average)
  measured start 0.407  measured final 0.542  true final 0.451  fresh re-measure 0.452  -> reported gain +13.6 pts, true gain +4.5 pts
  runs whose reported gain reaches the claimed +12.7 points: 53.1%
  • A "gain" of the claimed size is an ordinary outcome. With symmetric edits, 45.0 per cent of toy runs report at least 12.7 points; with edits that help slightly, 53.1 per cent do. A result that appears in about half of runs of a process with little or no real effect is not evidence of a large effect.
  • Some of the gain is real, and the toy says so. The true improvement is 3.3 points even when edits are symmetric, because keeping children that score higher does favour the genuinely better ones. The selection works, only much more weakly than the reported number says. It is wrong to conclude "nothing happened": the right conclusion is that the size of the effect is unknown and probably a quarter of what was reported.
  • The number to ask for is the fresh re-measurement. It is unbiased (44.0% in the toy) because no decision touched it. That is the whole value of a held-out set.
  • What the toy leaves out makes it worse for the claim. The team scored every child on the same 150 tasks, so differences among children partly reflect which tasks each happens to solve; the repeated use of one fixed set also lets the loop adapt to those tasks. The toy draws fresh noise for every evaluation, which is kinder than reality.

Common misconception

"The agent really got better: look, the final score is higher than the first score." The final score is the highest of many noisy scores, produced by a process that kept whichever child scored highest. A higher score at the end of such a process is expected even when the true gain is small. The question is how big the true gain is, and only data the process never saw can say.

3Audit which data did which job

Draw what the 150 tasks were used for and what they should have been used for. How many sets does a loop like this need, and how do you size them?

Compare with a worked answer

Questions to ask, with the answers that would reassure you and the answers that would not:

QuestionReassuring answerWorrying answer
Which tasks guided parent selection?A search set, separate from the report"The 150", the same as the headline
Which tasks chose the final agent?A fresh selection set, scored once per finalistThe same set, or "the best score in the log"
Which tasks gave the headline?A sealed set written after the loop started"All of them", or "we checked a few more at the end"
How were tasks collected?Sampled from tickets after the agent's design was frozen; deduplicated against anything in prompts or examplesHand-picked by the people building the agent, or taken from tickets the agent's examples came from

Sizing. With 150 tasks the test set cannot be much smaller than the whole benchmark: at a pass rate near 50 per cent, 150 tasks give a half-width of about 8 points, and 1,000 give about 3. A team that wants to detect a gain of five points with a paired design needs the sealed set to be a few hundred tasks. If that is too expensive to label, say so and report a wide interval. Do not pretend a small set is a large one. Building a domain benchmark covers how to build one, and contamination covers the leak of tasks into prompts and training data.

4Audit what the agent could touch

A self-modifying agent edits files. Which of them could change how it is scored, and how would you check the history of 60 versions for that?

Compare with a worked answer

The most reliable way to get a higher score from a scorer is to change the scorer. Chapter 15 and the hallucination experiment in case 6 show it happening in systems built by careful researchers: in the Darwin Gödel Machine paper's appendix an agent earned a perfect score by removing the logging that the detector relied on. The authors reported it, and could report it because every change was recorded as a lineage.

Questions: Where does the test runner live, and can the agent write there? Does the agent run in the repository whose tests it is graded by? Who owns the timeout, the retry count, the grader, the random seeds? Does the scaffold see the test files, or the expected outputs? Is there a record of every diff? The script below is the kind of check the review should ask for: scan each version's diff for edits that reach the measuring apparatus. The lineage is invented, and a real check would use a proper parser; the point is the shape.

"""Lineage audit: scan the diff of every agent version for edits that touch the measuring apparatus. The lineage here is invented."""
import re
PROTECTED = [r"^eval/", r"^harness/", r"^graders?/", r"^tests?/", r"conftest\.py$", r"\.github/"]
SUSPICIOUS = [r"\bsys\.exit\(0\)", r"\bskip\b|\bxfail\b", r"return\s+(True|1\.0)\s*#?", r"os\.environ\[.?(GRADER|SCORE)", r"timeout\s*=\s*None"]
lineage = [
    ("v01", ["agent/prompts.py"],                              ""),
    ("v09", ["agent/tools/edit.py", "agent/tools/view.py"],    "def edit(path, old, new): ..."),
    ("v23", ["agent/retry.py", "harness/runner.py"],           "-    timeout = 600\n+    timeout = None"),
    ("v31", ["agent/prompts.py"],                              ""),
    ("v38", ["agent/self_check.py", "eval/score.py"],          "+    return True  # all tests pass"),
    ("v47", ["agent/context.py", "agent/tools/edit.py"],       "+    summary = compress(history)"),
]
print(f"{'version':8s} {'verdict':26s} reason")
for ver, files, diff in lineage:
    hits = [f for f in files if any(re.search(p, f) for p in PROTECTED)]
    sus = [p for p in SUSPICIOUS if re.search(p, diff)]
    verdict = "BLOCK and human review" if hits else ("flag for review" if sus else "ok")
    why = ", ".join(hits) if hits else (", ".join(sus) if sus else "")
    print(f"{ver:8s} {verdict:26s} {why}")
version  verdict                    reason
v01      ok                         
v09      ok                         
v23      BLOCK and human review     harness/runner.py
v31      ok                         
v38      BLOCK and human review     eval/score.py
v47      ok
  • Two of six sampled versions would have been blocked. Version 23 edited the harness's runner and removed a timeout; version 38 edited the scoring module and returns True. A rule that blocks edits to protected paths needs no model of the agent's intent. The rule has to run before the child is scored, not after the headline is written.
  • The ancestry matters. If version 47, the best agent, descends from 23 or 38, its score is not evidence. Ask for the lineage of the winner and read each step in it.
  • Absence of evidence is not evidence. If the team cannot produce diffs for the 60 versions, the safe conclusion is that nobody knows whether the scorer was touched. That alone is enough to refuse the headline.
  • Look at the winner's behaviour, not only its score. Compare average runtime, number of tool calls and the share of tasks where it ran the tests. A jump in pass rate with a collapse in runtime is a red flag. A passing score that does not run the project's tests is not a pass.

Layers that should exist (sandbox, split of what may and may not change, a diff guard, lineage, human oversight) are in the review checklist of chapter 17.

5Check the baseline and the comparison

The slide compares the final agent with the starting agent. Is that the right comparison? Name three alternatives a fair reviewer would add, and what result from each would change the story.

Compare with a worked answer

A baseline measured once, with less effort, makes any later agent look good. Chapter 16 asks for equal effort and a paired comparison. For this claim:

ComparisonWhat it testsA result that would change the story
Starting agent, re-run several timesHow much of the 12.7 points is run-to-run noiseIf the starting agent scores 50% on a second run, the headline gain is gone
Starting agent plus one day of human tuning of its prompt and toolsWhether a person would find the same improvement cheaplyIf a day of human tuning gets 52%, the self-improvement loop was an expensive way to do it
Starting agent with the same compute spent on sampling (best of three attempts with a checker, chapter 8)Whether the gain is an architecture improvement or just more tries per taskIf best-of-three on the starting scaffold matches the final agent, the "improvement" is spend
Random edits, with the same keep-if-higher ruleWhether the loop is better than noise plus selection (the toy of stage 2)If random edits reach 50% on the same benchmark, the loop adds no information
Final agent on a different model snapshot or providerWhether the scaffold carries over, or is fitted to one modelNo gain on another model means the gain is tied to that model's habits

Also check that the two agents faced the same conditions: the same model snapshot (providers update models; a change halfway through the run is a confound), the same tool versions, the same time and token budgets, the same scoring script. The unit that matters for the business is quality per unit of cost, so the final agent's cost per task must be compared with the starting agent's; a more elaborate scaffold usually costs more per task, and a gain that is bought with three times the tokens is a different claim.

Compute-matched, not just task-matched. A fair statement has the form: "At equal cost per task, the discovered scaffold solves X per cent against Y per cent for the best hand-built scaffold we could build in a day."

6Decide what evidence to ask for

Write the request you would send to the team: a short, ordered list. For each item, say what you would conclude from each possible answer.

Compare with a worked answer

Order the requests by how much information they give per hour of the team's time, and phrase them so that an honest team can answer quickly. A good request does not accuse; it names what would make the claim believable.

  1. Per-task results for the starting and the final agent on the 150 tasks (pass/fail, runtime, cost, which tests ran). It allows a paired analysis, and it shows whether the gain is spread over many tasks or concentrated in a few.
  2. Repeats. The starting agent and the final agent, three runs each on the 150 tasks. This measures run-to-run noise, the denominator of every interval.
  3. A sealed set. 300 new tasks, never used for any decision, collected by someone outside the team, run once for each agent (three times if affordable). This is the evidence that settles the question.
  4. The lineage. Every diff from the starting agent to the winner, the scores of every version, and the parent of each. Read the winner's ancestry and run the protected-path check of stage 4.
  5. The harness. Where the tests and scorer live, who can write there, the timeout and retry settings and the model snapshot and version for every run.
  6. The cost. Tokens and dollars for the whole loop, and per task for the starting and final agents.
  7. A baseline with equal effort (stage 5): at minimum the starting agent given the same compute per task as the final one.

Then read what comes back. As an exercise, the script below simulates one plausible answer to the request: per-task outcomes for the starting and the final agents on a sealed set of 300 tasks, three runs each. It is a simulation, not real data. The final agent's per-task success probability is the starting agent's plus a small shift (mean +4 points), with a spread that makes some tasks better and some worse.

"""What an evidence pack might show (simulated): per-task results for v0 and the claimed best agent on 300 sealed tasks, 3 runs each."""
import random, statistics, math
rng = random.Random(21)
N, RUNS = 300, 3
p0 = [min(0.97, max(0.03, rng.betavariate(1.2, 1.7))) for _ in range(N)]                 # v0's per-task success chance
p1 = [min(0.97, max(0.03, p + rng.gauss(0.04, 0.10))) for p in p0]                      # the new agent: small real gain, noisy per task
run = lambda p: sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(RUNS)) / RUNS                        # fraction of 3 runs that pass
a = [run(p) for p in p0]; b = [run(p) for p in p1]
gap = [y - x for x, y in zip(a, b)]
boots = sorted(statistics.mean(rng.choices(gap, k=N)) for _ in range(4000))
m0, m1 = statistics.mean(a), statistics.mean(b)
print(f"sealed set: {N} tasks x {RUNS} runs   v0 {m0:.1%}   claimed best {m1:.1%}")
print(f"paired gain {100*(m1-m0):+.1f} points ({100*(m1/m0-1):+.0f}% relative), 95% bootstrap interval ({100*boots[100]:+.1f}, {100*boots[3900]:+.1f})")
wins = sum(g > 0 for g in gap); losses = sum(g < 0 for g in gap)
print(f"tasks where the new agent did better: {wins}, worse: {losses}, same: {N - wins - losses}")
reruns = [sum(rng.random() < p for p in p0) / N for _ in range(5)]
print("v0 re-run five times on the same 300 tasks, one run per task:", ", ".join(f"{100*x:.1f}" for x in reruns))
sealed set: 300 tasks x 3 runs   v0 39.8%   claimed best 44.3%
paired gain +4.6 points (+11% relative), 95% bootstrap interval (+0.8, +8.3)
tasks where the new agent did better: 103, worse: 71, same: 126
v0 re-run five times on the same 300 tasks, one run per task: 41.0, 42.3, 45.0, 45.0, 40.7
  • The sealed result is smaller and still positive. The paired gain is +4.6 points (+11% relative), with a 95 per cent bootstrap interval of +0.8 to +8.3. The lower end is close to zero. This is the shape of result you should expect from a loop with a modest real effect and an inflated headline: a gain that survives the test, at a third of the size and with an interval that does not rule out very little.
  • The agents differ on many tasks. The final agent does better on 103 tasks and worse on 71, with 126 the same. A single average hides the fact that the new agent breaks things it used to do. For a platform rollout, the 71 matter: look at what kind of task they are.
  • Run-to-run noise is of the same order as the gain. Five runs of the starting agent on the same 300 tasks give 41.0% to 45.0%, a spread of four points. Without repeats, a single sealed-set comparison could land anywhere in that range.
  • What would change the verdict. A gain interval that clears about 3 points on the sealed set, a lineage with no protected-path edits, and a baseline of equal effort that does not match the final agent would support a bounded "yes". A sealed gain near zero, a touched harness, or a baseline that matches the final agent would support "no".

7Generality, cost and what the gain is for

Suppose the sealed-set gain holds up. What else does a manager need to know before paying for a platform-wide rollout?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Does it transfer? The strongest evidence that a scaffold improvement is real is that it helps on things it was not selected on: other models, other benchmarks, other languages. The Darwin Gödel Machine paper (Zhang et al., 2025) tested all three, and as read in case 6, reported mixed transfer: large gains on some other models (59.5% against 19.0% for the SWE-bench-discovered agent on a newer Claude model) and small ones where the base agent was already strong (33.3% against 32.0% on Polyglot with another model). Mixed is the pattern to expect, and a good template for the request: ask the team for a second benchmark and a second model, and weight the transfer gain over the in-sample one.
  • What does it cost? The same paper estimated one SWE-bench run at about two weeks and USD 22,000 of API charges, or roughly USD 730 per percentage point gained (the per-point division is arithmetic by the case's author, and is meaningful only for that run). For the team in this brief, divide the loop's cost by the sealed gain, not the reported one: if the loop cost USD 8,000 and the true gain is 4.6 points, that is about USD 1,700 per point, 2.8 times the USD 630 the slide implies. Include the people.
  • What is a point worth? Translate points into outcomes: tickets resolved without a human, hours saved, defects avoided. If 4.6 points of a 150-task benchmark corresponds to a few tickets a day, the platform rollout may not pay. A gain that is statistically clear can still be commercially small (chapter 18, from model to value).
  • Is the benchmark the job? An internal benchmark of 150 repository tasks is a sample of one kind of work. The platform will see other kinds. Ask what share of production tickets look like the benchmark, and run the final agent in shadow on live tickets before trusting any number.
  • Does it decay? A scaffold tuned to a model's habits can degrade when the provider updates the model. A budget line for re-running the evaluation each quarter is part of the price.
  • Does it make the agent worse at something else? Check cost per task, latency, refusals, tool errors and the rate of confident wrong answers, not only the pass rate (cost, latency and quality).

8Safety: should it keep going?

The team wants to leave the loop running. What would you need to see about the process, not just the result, before agreeing?

Compare with a worked answer

A result can be sound while the process is unsafe, and the reverse. Chapter 17 asks how much freedom the loop has; the review should answer that in the team's own terms.

QuestionWhat a safe answer sounds like
What can an agent edit?One directory of scaffold code and prompts. The harness, the scorer, the sandbox settings and the loop controller are outside it and read-only.
Where does generated code run?In a sandbox with no network, no credentials, resource limits and a fresh filesystem per run.
Who reads the changes?A person reviews the winner's whole lineage before anything is deployed, and spot-checks the rest.
What is the budget?A cap on iterations, dollars and wall-clock, fixed in advance; the loop cannot raise it.
What does the agent have access to once deployed?The same tools as the starting agent. If a self-edit added a new tool or a wider permission, that is a separate review.
What did you see that surprised you?An honest team has an example: a version that tried to change a test, a strange loop, an odd diff. A team that saw nothing unexpected in 60 self-edits has probably not read them.

Do not let the verdict on the result depend on the answers to these, and do not let a good result excuse bad answers. "It went from 41 to 53" is not a reason to let an agent with write access to its own scorer carry on, and "the process is careful" is not a reason to believe a score reported on the data used to select it. Two separate verdicts, one on the number and one on the process, are easier to act on.

9Write the verdict

Write a one-paragraph verdict for the head of engineering, and a three-line message for the team. Say what you believe, what you do not, what you want next and what you would fund now.

Compare with a worked answer

A review is useful when it ends in a decision a person can take. A good verdict states the belief in graded terms, names the specific missing evidence, and offers a cheap path to a yes.

Evidence levelWhat you can sayAction
0. A headline and no protocol"There is a claim."Ask for the evidence pack; do not fund

The verdict for the head of engineering (level 1): "The result may well be real in part, but the slide cannot show it. The 30 per cent compares the best of 60 descendants with their ancestor on the very 150 tasks used to choose them. A simulation of that process, with edits that help and hurt equally, reports a gain of the same size about half the time while the true gain is about a quarter of it. I would not budget a platform rollout on this number. I would fund two weeks to do four things: run both agents three times on a new 300-task set that someone outside the team collects, read the lineage and the harness for edits to the scorer, compare with the starting agent given the same compute per task, and report cost per task. If the sealed gain clears about three points and the lineage is clean, I would back a shadow pilot on live tickets."

The message for the team: "Thanks for sharing this; the idea is promising and the setup is clean enough to test properly. Could you send per-task results, the lineage and three repeats of both agents? We will build a sealed 300-task set with you. If the gain holds, we will fund the pilot." A review that is fair to the team gets a faster, more honest evidence pack than one that sounds like an accusation.

10Say it out loud: a three-minute summary

Say your review aloud in three minutes, then compare with the model summary.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The claim is 61 tasks solved out of 150 going to 80, which is 12.7 points or 31 per cent relative. That is the best of 60 descendants, chosen because it scored highest on those same 150 tasks, from a single run. A score on 150 tasks has a standard error of four points, and a keep-if-higher loop ratchets on noise: in a simulation where edits help and hurt equally often, about half the runs report a gain this big, and the agent's real improvement is about a quarter of the reported one. So I believe something happened and I do not believe the size.

I would ask for four things. Per-task results and three repeats, so I can pair and measure noise. A sealed set of 300 tasks that nobody optimised against. The lineage and the harness, to see whether any version touched the scorer, because the easiest way to a higher score is to change the scorer. And a baseline with equal effort, including best-of-three on the starting agent, and cost per task, so that I know whether it is a better design or just more spend. On a simulated sealed set the gain shrinks to about four to five points with an interval that nearly reaches zero, which is a typical outcome.

My recommendation is a two-week funded evaluation, not a platform rollout, and a shadow pilot if the sealed gain clears about three points with a clean lineage. Separately, the process needs a sandbox, protected harness files and a person reading the diffs before it keeps running."

Notice the shape: restate the claim as numbers → simulate the process → audit data and access → ask for specific evidence → graded verdict with a funded next step.

Self-check rubric

Tick what your own review (before reading the answers) covered. The first items are what a solid answer has; the later ones separate a senior answer.

  • Restated the claim with absolute and relative gain, the base, the data and the number of runs, and noted what the slide omits.
  • Put an interval on each score and on the difference, and compared the gain with run-to-run noise.
  • Recognised that the final agent is the best of many and explained why the reported gain is biased upwards.
  • Simulated or reasoned about a keep-if-higher ratchet and said the true gain is probably real but smaller.
  • Asked which data did which job (parent selection, keeping children, reporting) and required a separate sealed set.
  • Sized the sealed set from the interval needed, not from convenience.
  • Asked what the agent could edit, and checked the lineage for edits to the scorer, harness, timeouts or tests.
  • Looked at behaviour (runtime, tool calls, whether tests ran), not only pass rate.
  • Required an equal-effort baseline, a repeat of the starting agent and a compute-matched comparison.
  • Asked for transfer to another model or benchmark, and weighted it over the in-sample gain.
  • Computed cost per point from the sealed gain, including people, and asked what a point is worth.
  • Separated a verdict on the number from a verdict on the process and its safety controls.
  • Ended with a graded verdict, a specific funded next step and a message to the team that is fair to them.

Key takeaways

  • A self-improvement gain reported on the data used to choose the winner is the best of many noisy scores. In a toy with symmetric edits it came out at 12.4 points, almost exactly the slide's 12.7, against a true gain of 3.3.
  • Ask which data did which job. The headline needs a sealed set that no decision has touched, sized for the interval you need, and run-to-run repeats.
  • Read the lineage. The simplest way for a loop to raise its score is to edit what measures it, so protected paths and a record of every diff are part of the evidence.
  • Compare with an equal-effort baseline and at equal cost, and look for transfer to another model or benchmark before trusting the gain.
  • Write a graded verdict that funds the cheap next test, treats the team fairly, and judges the number and the process separately.
