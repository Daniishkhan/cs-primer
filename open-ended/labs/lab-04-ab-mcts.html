Lab 4: Adaptive Branching over Many Models

Chapter 8 ended its ladder of inference-time search with a decision made call by call: given the answers so far and a fixed number of model calls left, should the next call sample a new answer, refine an existing one, or hand the work to a different model? In this lab you build that decision. You start with the two fixed strategies and the closed form for what repeated sampling buys, then write the Thompson-sampling rule that picks between them from observed gains, then the rule that picks which answer to refine, then widen the choice to several models with different strengths, and finally write the scoreboard that says honestly whether any of it helped.

Why this lab matters

Most teams that use a model to solve hard problems end up with one of two loops. Sample many times and pick the best (wider), or take one answer and keep asking the model to fix it (deeper). Both are fixed strategies, and chapter 8's miniature showed that each loses badly in one kind of task: refining wins when feedback is informative and wastes the budget when it is not, and sampling is safe but never uses feedback. Adaptive branching, the idea of Inoue et al. (2025) behind AB-MCTS, is to stop choosing in advance and let the observed gains decide, and the multi-model variant adds the choice of model as one more decision of the same kind.

You cannot see why it works from the paper's headline numbers. You see it by writing the pieces and watching a policy that was never told which world it is in behave like the better fixed strategy in each. You will also see the costs, which papers underplay: learning takes calls, so a policy with six arms is slower than one with two; a parent-selection rule that helps in one world hurts in another; and an improvement that looks real in one run needs an interval around it before you believe it. Those costs are the daily reality of products that spend money on every model call (chapter 18), and the statistics are those of evaluating with error bars.

Stand-in models, a simplified algorithm, no network

No real model is called. The "models" are numeric stand-ins: each call returns the score a verifier would give the answer (0 to 1), drawn from a distribution that depends on the model, and the lab's own parameters decide how good each model is at fresh answers and at repairs. That makes every run reproducible and lets the checker know the truth. It also means the numbers say nothing about any real model; what carries over is the structure of the decision. The algorithm is a simplification of AB-MCTS: the paper decides at every node of a tree, with per-node probability models; this lab flattens that into one decision per call over a pool of answers (which action, then which parent), and uses the Gaussian Thompson draw of chapter 8's miniature rather than the paper's statistical models. Every random number comes from a seeded random.Random, a failure prints enough to replay it (python3 check.py solution 3 7 runs milestone 3 on seed 7), and the whole check takes about two seconds. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only.

What you'll build

An answer is a node with a score from a verifier, a perfect one here (chapter 15 is about what happens when it is not). A call to a model either produces a fresh answer (wider) or a child of an existing one (deeper). A search has a budget of calls, and the question is how to spend them. Five milestones:

  1. The loop and two fixed strategies: the run loop that spends a budget, computes each call's gain, and stops early at a target; always-wider and always-deeper; and the closed form for the best of n samples.
  2. Wider or deeper, learned: Thompson draws over the gains each action has delivered, so the policy tracks the better fixed strategy in each world.
  3. Which answer to refine: chapter 10's weighted parent rule, and a world where refining the leader is a trap.
  4. Many models: arms are (action, model) pairs, in a world where one model drafts well and another repairs well.
  5. An honest scoreboard: solve rate, calls to solve, and confidence intervals.

Milestone 1: the loop and the two fixed strategies

The world is given, and it helps to know its rules. Each ModelSpec describes how one stand-in model behaves: a fresh answer scores s = Ua with U uniform on [0, 1] (so a = 1 is a fair coin and a = 3 is mostly mediocre); a refined answer moves a fraction gain of the way from its parent's score to the parent's hidden ceiling, plus a little noise; each call has a cost. Four kinds of task combine these:

TaskWhat is trueUsed in
refinableFeedback helps: gains of 0.30 to 0.40 for three models (small, medium, large), the large one best at fresh answers and five times the price of the small.milestones 1, 2, 5
lotteryFeedback is useless: gain 0, so refining is another noisy draw around the same score.milestones 1, 2, 5
plateauOne model with a strong gain, but each answer has its own ceiling, between 0.4 and 1.0 of the way to the top.milestones 3, 5
splitA drafter with fresh answers whose ceiling is high but which cannot refine; an editor that refines well but whose own fresh answers start down dead ends (ceiling 0.25); a cheap small model in between.milestones 4, 5

An Env holds the answers so far, counts calls, and exposes one method, apply(action), which makes exactly one call. An action is a tuple: ("wider", m) for a fresh answer from model m, or ("deeper", p, m) for a refinement of answer p by model m. A policy is an object with choose(env, rng) and observe(action, node, gain). What you write first is the loop that connects them, run_search.

The key quantity is the gain of a call: how far the new answer lands above the best score before the call, or zero if it does not beat it. A call that produces a 0.7 when the best is 0.8 has taught the policy nothing about progress, however good the answer looks in isolation. The first call's gain is its whole score (the best before it is 0). Ties and stopping matter too: with a target, the search stops as soon as the best score reaches it, which is early stopping and the main source of savings in a real product (chapter 8: "make early stopping a feature").

The two fixed strategies are short. Always wider asks one model for a fresh answer every time. Always deeper asks for one fresh answer and then refines the best answer so far. And before simulating anything, a closed form worth deriving: if one fresh answer scores Ua, the best of n of them has mean n / (n + a).

Go deeper optional

The chance that one answer scores at most x is P(Ua ≤ x) = x1/a. The maximum of n independent scores is at most x exactly when all are, so its distribution function is xn/a on [0, 1]. For a variable on [0, 1] the mean is 1 minus the integral of the distribution function: 1 − ∫01 xn/a dx = 1 − 1 / (n/a + 1) = n / (n + a). With a = 2 and n = 30 this is 0.9375, the figure chapter 8's miniature reports as 0.940. Chapter 8's other formula, coverage(n) = 1 − (1 − p)n for a task each sample solves with probability p, is the coverage function you also write: 0.64 for p = 0.05 and n = 20.

Solution spoiler 
def coverage(p, n):
    """Chance that at least one of n independent answers is correct when each is correct with ..."""
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** n

def expected_best_of_n(n, a=2.0):
    """Mean of the best of n fresh answers when one answer scores U ** a with U uniform on [0, 1]."""
    return n / (n + a)

class WiderOnly:
    """Always sample a new answer from one model."""

    def __init__(self, model=0):
        self.model = model

    def choose(self, env, rng):
        return ("wider", self.model)

    def observe(self, action, node, gain):
        pass

class DeeperOnly:
    """Sample one answer, then refine the best answer so far, again and again, with one model."""

    def __init__(self, model=0):
        self.model = model

    def choose(self, env, rng):
        if not env.nodes:
            return ("wider", self.model)
        return ("deeper", env.best.id, self.model)

    def observe(self, action, node, gain):
        pass

def run_search(policy, env, rng, target=None):
    """Spend the budget: repeatedly ask policy.choose(env, rng) for an action, env.apply() it, compute ..."""
    log = []
    while env.calls_left > 0:
        before = env.best_score
        action = policy.choose(env, rng)
        node = env.apply(action)
        gain = max(0.0, node.score - before)
        policy.observe(action, node, gain)
        log.append(Step(env.calls, action, node.id, node.model, node.score, gain, env.best_score))
        if target is not None and env.best_score >= target:
            break
    solved = target is not None and env.best_score >= target
    return SearchResult(env.best_score, env.calls, env.spent, solved, log)

Milestone 2: wider or deeper, learned from gains

The fixed strategies each have a world where they are right. A policy that does not know which world it is in needs a way to find out while spending the budget, and the natural evidence is the gain: how much has each kind of action been improving on the best answer so far? If refining has been paying (gains of 0.1, 0.2), do more of it; if it has been paying nothing, do not. This is the bandit problem of Lab 1, with two arms, where the reward of an arm is the gain it produced.

The rule is the one in chapter 8's miniature, with three small ingredients. First, each arm keeps a list of the gains it has delivered. Second, a shrunk mean pulls the average of that list towards an optimistic prior (0.1, as if seen twice), so an arm with no data looks decent and gets tried: (0.1 × 2 + sum of gains) / (n + 2). Third, a draw: instead of comparing means, sample one number from a Gaussian centred on the shrunk mean, with a spread that shrinks as evidence arrives, 0.2 / √(n + 1), and pick the arm with the larger draw. That is Thompson sampling (Lab 1, milestone 3, did it with Beta beliefs); it explores through the width of its beliefs and has no exploration constant to tune.

Read the picture as the policy does. With no data the two arms have the same wide belief and are chosen about equally. After eight observations the arm that paid nothing has a narrow belief near 0.02 and the arm that paid 0.25 a narrow belief near 0.22; their draws barely overlap, so the better one wins almost every time. The script shows the chosen action's frequency for four histories on an environment that already has four answers (so "deeper" is possible):

Note what the arms learn from: not the score of an answer but its gain over the best so far, so an arm stops being attractive once the best answer cannot be improved any more. That is the diminishing-returns signal in the table of chapter 8 ("Go deeper, until returns shrink").

Common misconception

"Adaptive branching must beat both fixed strategies." It does not and cannot: in a world where refining is right, the adaptive policy spends its first few calls finding that out, and lands slightly below always-deeper (0.997 against 1.000 in the refinable world at 20 calls); in the lottery world it lands slightly above always-wider (0.909 against 0.906), because the occasional refinement of a high-scoring answer is one more noisy draw. The claim that matters is the one in the chart: close to the better strategy in each world, far from the worse one, with no knowledge of the world. The check asserts exactly that, with margins of 0.02 and 0.03.

def shrunk_mean(history, prior_mean=0.1, prior_weight=2):
    """Mean of the observed gains, pulled toward an optimistic prior as if the prior had been seen ..."""
    return (prior_mean * prior_weight + sum(history)) / (len(history) + prior_weight)

def thompson_draw(history, rng, prior_mean=0.1, prior_weight=2, scale=0.2):
    """One Thompson-style draw for an arm: a Gaussian around shrunk_mean(history) whose spread shrinks ..."""
    n = len(history)
    mu = shrunk_mean(history, prior_mean, prior_weight)
    return rng.gauss(mu, scale / math.sqrt(n + 1))

class TwoArmPolicy:
    """Wider or deeper, with one model, decided by Thompson draws over the gains each action has delivered."""

    def __init__(self, model=0, lam=None):
        self.model, self.lam = model, lam
        self.hist = {"wider": [], "deeper": []}

    def choose(self, env, rng):
        if not env.nodes:
            return ("wider", self.model)
        w, d = thompson_draw(self.hist["wider"], rng), thompson_draw(self.hist["deeper"], rng)
        if w >= d:
            return ("wider", self.model)
        return ("deeper", env.best.id if self.lam is None else pick_parent(env.nodes, rng, self.lam), self.model)

    def observe(self, action, node, gain):
        self.hist[action[0]].append(gain)

Milestone 3: which answer to refine

So far "deeper" meant "refine the best answer so far". That is exploitation, and it has a failure mode that wider-or-deeper accounting cannot see. Refinement does not improve an answer for ever. In many real tasks an answer embodies an approach, and repairs improve the details of the approach until the approach itself is the limit: a patch that fixes every failing test but one, in a design that cannot handle the last case. In the plateau world each fresh answer is given a hidden ceiling, and refinement closes 80% of the remaining distance to it on every step. The answer that leads at any moment is not necessarily the one with the highest ceiling.

A policy that always refines the leader polishes A, which is ahead for two refinements and then flat. Its deeper arm keeps reporting a gain of zero, so the Thompson draws push it towards "wider"; but whenever it does go deeper, it picks A again. The cure is to refine a sample of good answers and to count how many children each has already had: an answer that has produced several children without beating itself should lose priority to one that has not been tried. Chapter 10 met this idea as ShinkaEvolve's "weighted" parent rule, and its table is the check for this milestone: for each answer,

weighti ∝ sigmoid(λ (scorei − median score)) / (1 + kidsi),

normalised to sum to 1, where sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + e−x). The sigmoid rises smoothly with score around the median, so low scorers are not ruled out entirely; the divisor shrinks the weight of answers that already have children. The parameter λ is the pressure: a large λ behaves like "refine the best", a small one like "refine anything". The script reproduces chapter 10's six programs for λ = 10 and 30, then compares four parent rules in the plateau world.

Common misconception

"Spreading the refinements is the better rule." It is the better rule here. In the split world of milestone 4, where a good approach is repaired by chaining several refinements of the same answer, the same sampling rule costs solved tasks: with all three models as arms, always refining the best answer solves 73% of tasks in 40 calls, against 58% to 66% for the weighted rule at λ between 3 and 30 (I measured 300 seeds for each). There is no free parent rule; the right one depends on whether refinement chains are productive or hit ceilings. That is the motivation for AB-MCTS to keep statistics per node rather than a global rule, which this lab's flat pool cannot do. The check for this milestone therefore tests the plateau world only, and the policies default to lam=None, meaning refine the best.

Solution spoiler 
def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-x))

def parent_weights(scores, kids, lam=10.0):
    """Chapter 10's weighted parent rule, normalised to sum to 1: weight_i = sigmoid(lam * (score_i - ..."""
    w = [sigmoid(lam * (s - statistics.median(scores))) / (1 + k) for s, k in zip(scores, kids)]
    total = sum(w)
    return [x / total for x in w]

def pick_parent(nodes, rng, lam=10.0):
    """The id of the node to refine: one draw, rng.choices(range(len(nodes)), weights)[0], with weights ..."""
    weights = parent_weights([n.score for n in nodes], [n.kids for n in nodes], lam)
    return nodes[rng.choices(range(len(nodes)), weights)[0]].id

Milestone 4: many models

Real systems have more than one model on offer: a cheap fast one and a large slow one, a general model and a code specialist, models from different vendors with different habits. The multi-model variant of AB-MCTS adds the choice of model as a third decision, treated as one more bandit. The easiest way to express it here is to widen the arms. Instead of two arms (wider, deeper) there are 2×M arms: ("wider", 0), ("deeper", 0), ("wider", 1), and so on, each with its own list of gains and its own Thompson draw. One draw per arm, the largest wins, and the winning arm names both the kind of action and the model.

Why would this ever beat the best single model? Because models are good at different jobs, and in the split world that is the whole point. The three stand-ins are:

ModelFresh answers (a)Refinement gainCeiling of its fresh answersCost
drafter5.00.00 (cannot refine)0.97 (good approaches)2
editor6.00.60 (repairs well)0.25 (dead-end approaches)2
small5.00.150.601

An answer's ceiling is set by the model that drafted it, whoever refines it afterwards. So the best plan is to draft with the drafter and repair with the editor, and neither model can do that alone: the drafter alone is a sampler with a high ceiling it cannot climb towards, and the editor alone climbs fast towards a ceiling of 0.25. (This is the toy version of a real pattern: a strong model proposes an approach, a cheaper one iterates on details.) The policy is never told any of this. Common misconception

"More models can only help." Every extra arm is something to learn, and learning costs calls. In the lottery world, where feedback is useless and the best strategy is plain sampling from the largest model, the policy with all three models as arms solves 59% of tasks in 20 calls, against 73% for adaptive branching with the large model alone, and 72% for the plain sampler. That gap is the price of exploring six arms when only one matters. The benefit of choice grows with the budget and with how different the models really are; with a small budget and similar models, pick one.

Your task

The constructor, the arm list and the histories are given: self.arms is [("wider", 0), ("deeper", 0), ("wider", 1), ...] and self.hist maps each arm to its list of gains. On the first call there is nothing to refine, so draw once for each wider arm only and take the largest. Afterwards draw once for every arm, in arm order, and take the largest (ties to the earlier arm); a deeper arm refines env.best.id, or pick_parent if self.lam is set. observe files the gain under the arm (action[0], action[-1]). model_shares returns, for the steps of one kind in a log, the fraction made by each model.

class MultiModelPolicy:
    """Wider or deeper, AND which model, as one Thompson choice over arms (kind, model)."""

    def __init__(self, n_models, lam=None):
        self.lam = lam
        self.arms = [(kind, m) for m in range(n_models) for kind in ("wider", "deeper")]
        self.hist = {arm: [] for arm in self.arms}

    def choose(self, env, rng):
        if not env.nodes:
            draws = [(thompson_draw(self.hist[a], rng), -i, a) for i, a in enumerate(self.arms) if a[0] == "wider"]
            return ("wider", max(draws)[2][1])
        draws = [(thompson_draw(self.hist[a], rng), -i, a) for i, a in enumerate(self.arms)]
        kind, m = max(draws)[2]
        if kind == "wider":
            return ("wider", m)
        return ("deeper", env.best.id if self.lam is None else pick_parent(env.nodes, rng, self.lam), m)

    def observe(self, action, node, gain):
        self.hist[(action[0], action[-1])].append(gain)

def model_shares(log, kind, n_models):
    """For the steps of `kind` ("wider" or "deeper") in a search log, the fraction made by each model: ..."""
    steps = [s for s in log if s.action[0] == kind]
    if not steps:
        return [0.0] * n_models
    return [sum(1 for s in steps if s.model == m) / len(steps) for m in range(n_models)]

Milestone 5: an honest scoreboard

Everything so far compared means over a few hundred seeds and asserted that one number was bigger than another. A real decision, such as whether to ship a six-arm policy to a product that pays per call, needs more than that, and a search policy has its own natural measures. Three of them are in this milestone.

  • Solve rate: the fraction of tasks where the best score reached the target within the budget. This is the Pass@k of the AB-MCTS paper's ARC-AGI-2 experiments (any attempt among the calls made is correct); it assumes the verifier can recognise the winner, as ours can.
  • Calls to solve: the median number of calls used, with an unsolved run counted as worse than any solved one (the budget plus one, by default). It is the cost side: two policies with the same solve rate are not equal if one needs half the calls. The median, because the distribution has a heavy tail of unsolved runs.
  • A confidence interval: a mean of 300 seeds is an estimate, and the normal-approximation interval, mean ± 1.96 × sample standard deviation / √n, says how far off it could be. The sample standard deviation divides by n − 1. Two policies whose intervals do not overlap are very likely different; two whose intervals overlap may be, but this experiment cannot say (statistics for experiments and statistics for evals cover the better tools: bootstrap, paired differences).

evaluate_policy runs one policy afresh on one task for a list of seeds and collects all three. Its seed convention is part of the contract: run i uses Env(task, budget, seed) and random.Random(seed + 7919) for the policy, so that two policies on the same seed see the same stream of fresh answers, which is what makes paired comparisons fair. The script prints the whole scoreboard: four worlds, three policies each.

def solve_rate(results):
    """Fraction of results with solved == True."""
    return sum(r.solved for r in results) / len(results)

def calls_to_solve(results, penalty=None):
    """Median number of calls over the results; unsolved ones count as `penalty` calls (default: the ..."""
    if penalty is None:
        penalty = max(r.calls for r in results) + 1
    return statistics.median(r.calls if r.solved else penalty for r in results)

def mean_ci(values, z=1.96):
    """(mean, half_width) of a normal-approximation confidence interval for the mean: half_width = z * ..."""
    n = len(values)
    mean = sum(values) / n
    if n < 2:
        return mean, 0.0
    sd = math.sqrt(sum((v - mean) ** 2 for v in values) / (n - 1))
    return mean, z * sd / math.sqrt(n)

def evaluate_policy(make_policy, task, budget, seeds, target=0.95):
    """Run make_policy() afresh on Env(task, budget, seed) for every seed, with policy randomness ..."""
    runs = []
    for seed in seeds:
        env = Env(task, budget, seed)
        runs.append(run_search(make_policy(), env, random.Random(seed + 7919), target))
    return {"best": mean_ci([r.best for r in runs]), "solve_rate": solve_rate(runs),
            "calls": calls_to_solve(runs), "spend": sum(r.spend for r in runs) / len(runs), "runs": runs}

Going further

  1. A tree instead of a pool. Make the decision at each node as AB-MCTS does: every node has a "generate a new child" option next to its existing children, and a Thompson draw per option, with statistics kept per node and backed up the path.   2. Pay for cost. Give the search a spend budget instead of a call budget, and divide each arm's draw by cost ** kappa. At what kappa does the six-arm policy start preferring the small model in the refinable world, and what does it do to the solve rate at equal spend?
  3. Parallel waves. Real searches issue several calls at once. Change the loop to choose a batch of eight actions from the same state (draws are independent, so this is cheap) and observe all eight results afterwards. Systems connection

A search policy is a scheduler that spends a metered resource, and the engineering of that resource is the systems side of this lab. Wider calls are independent and run in parallel, so their latency is that of the slowest in the batch, the fan-out arithmetic of tail latency at scale; deeper calls form a chain and add up. A product that promises an answer in ten seconds is choosing, through this policy, how much of its budget can be sequential. The call budget is a load multiplier too: a feature that turns one user request into forty model calls multiplies demand on a shared pool by forty, which is where the knee of the curve in queueing theory arrives early. And per-model cost makes the policy a cost controller: the sensible production version has a spend cap per request, early stopping when the checker passes (the median-calls column above), and logs of which model did what, which model_shares is the start of. Treat the rates and prices of any real model as volatile; keep them in configuration, not in the policy.

What you learned

  • Repeated sampling has a closed form: the best of n draws of Ua has mean n/(n+a), and coverage 1 − (1 − p)n. Check a simulation against the formula before trusting either.
  • No fixed strategy wins everywhere: always-deeper is best when feedback helps (1.000 at 20 calls) and worst when it does not (0.658 against 0.906 for sampling). The gain over the best answer so far is the signal that tells the worlds apart.
  • Thompson draws over observed gains, with a prior that makes untried arms attractive and a spread that shrinks with evidence, give a policy that stays close to the better fixed strategy in each world without being told which it is. It cannot beat both, because it has to learn.
  • Which answer to refine is a separate decision. Refining the leader polishes dead ends; sampling parents by score with a penalty per child (chapter 10's rule) helps when answers have ceilings and hurts when chains of refinements pay. There was no rule that won in both worlds.
  • Models can be arms. When one model drafts and another repairs, the six-arm policy solved 73% of tasks in 40 calls against 51% for the best single model; when one model is simply best, the extra arms cost exploration (59% against 73%).
  • Report solve rate, calls to solve and an interval, not just a mean best score: in the split world the mean barely moved while the solve rate rose by twenty points.

Check your understanding

In the lottery world (refining gives no gain), always-deeper scores 0.658 after 20 calls while always-wider scores 0.906. Why is deeper so much worse?

  1. Refinement lowers the score of an answer on average
  2. The deeper strategy makes fewer calls than the wider one
  3. It spends the whole budget re-rolling the first mediocre answer it drew, so it never looks at the rest of the space
  4. Its answers are scored by a different verifier
With a gain of zero, a refinement is the parent's score plus noise, so after the first fresh answer the search is stuck near that score. Sampling spends every call on a new independent draw and the best of them keeps rising. Both strategies make the same number of calls and use the same verifier; refinement does not lower scores on average, it just does not raise them.

A policy has seen the "deeper" arm pay a gain of about 0.25 eight times and the "wider" arm pay 0.0 eight times. About how often will the next Thompson comparison pick wider?

  1. Rarely, about 2% of the time, because the two beliefs barely overlap
  2. Half the time, because Thompson sampling always keeps exploring equally
  3. Never, because Thompson sampling is greedy once it has data
  4. About a third of the time, one for each possible model
Each belief is narrow after eight observations (spread about 0.067) and they are 0.2 apart, so the wider draw beats the deeper one only in the tail. That is the 0.02 in the lab's output. Thompson sampling is not greedy (the third option): the chance is small but not zero, and it never reaches exactly zero, which is how the policy notices if the world changes. Half-and-half (second option) describes the no-evidence case.

Two answers have the same score. One has already had three children and the other none. With chapter 10's weighted rule, what are their probabilities of being chosen to refine?

  1. 0.5 and 0.5, because the scores are equal
  2. 0.25 and 0.75
  3. 0.33 and 0.67
  4. 0.2 for the one with three children and 0.8 for the other
Equal scores give equal sigmoid factors, so the weights are 1/(1+3) = 0.25 and 1/(1+0) = 1, which normalise to 0.2 and 0.8. The first option ignores the child penalty, and the other two split the weight in ratios other than 1 to 4.

In the plateau world, why does always refining the best answer do worse than sampling the parent with the weighted rule?

  1. The best answer always has the lowest ceiling
  2. Each answer stops improving at its own ceiling, and the leader at any moment is not necessarily the answer with the highest ceiling, so refinement can polish a dead end
  3. Refinement is noisy and the best answer is the noisiest
  4. The weighted rule gives refinement a larger gain per call
Refinement closes most of the distance to a hidden ceiling in a few steps. The leader after two refinements may have a low ceiling; refining it again gains nothing, while an answer that started lower may have a much higher one. The best answer does not always have the lowest ceiling (the first option is false), and the weighted rule changes which answer is refined, not the gain per call.

In the lottery world the six-arm policy solved 59% of tasks in 20 calls, the one-model adaptive policy 73%. What is the best explanation?

  1. Models in the lottery world are worse than in the other worlds
  2. Thompson sampling does not work when feedback is useless
  3. Six arms with little data each take more calls to sort out than two, and in this world only one model matters
  4. The six-arm policy is given fewer calls
Learning which of six arms pays takes more observations than learning which of two does, and in the lottery world the models differ only in how good their fresh answers are, so the exploration brings little. The budget is the same, the models are the same ones the one-model policy uses, and Thompson sampling works in the lottery world (it matches the sampler with one model).

In the split world the six-arm policy solves 0.73 of tasks and the best single model 0.51, with intervals of ±0.05 and ±0.06 over 300 seeds. What can you conclude?

  1. The intervals do not overlap, so the difference is very unlikely to be luck at this sample size; it holds for this world and these stand-in models, not for real ones
  2. The policies are tied, because the intervals are wide
  3. The six-arm policy is better in every world, because it won here
  4. Nothing, because a confidence interval cannot compare two policies
The lower end of the first interval (0.68) is above the upper end of the second (0.57), which is strong evidence for a real difference in this simulation (with paired seeds, a paired test would be sharper). It says nothing about other worlds: in the lottery world the same policy lost. Overlapping intervals would be inconclusive, not a tie, and non-overlap is a legitimate, if conservative, comparison.
