Lab 2: Novelty Search & MAP-Elites

Chapter 3 showed a small maze in which the search that follows the score fails and the search that ignores the score succeeds. Chapter 4 showed an algorithm that keeps the best solution for every kind of behaviour instead of one champion. In this lab you build all three on the same maze: a genetic algorithm that chases the distance to the goal, the same algorithm with its selection rule swapped for novelty, and MAP-Elites, which fills a map of the maze cell by cell. Then you read the map, find the stepping stones the score would have thrown away, and see why an archive is more than a better way to find one answer.

Why this lab matters

Almost every self-improvement loop in this course has the shape "score a candidate, keep the best, repeat": the prompt optimisers of chapter 11, the program search of chapter 9, the agent designs of chapter 12. When such a loop gets stuck it does so quietly: the score stops improving, the run looks converged, and nobody sees the better answers it never visited. A maze is the smallest honest place to watch that happen, because you can draw every cell the search ever occupied and count them. By the end of this lab you will have seen the failure, built the two standard escapes with your own code, and measured what each one costs.

You will also have written the data structure that FunSearch, AlphaEvolve and their open-source cousins keep at their heart (chapter 10): a table with one slot per kind of behaviour and a rule for who holds the slot. The rule is five lines long. Where it goes wrong, it goes wrong in ways the milestones are designed to expose: replacing an elite on a tie, choosing parents from the best cells only, describing behaviour with the wrong coordinates.

Deterministic, standard library only

No network, no clock, no model. Every random number comes from a seeded random.Random, and the check prints enough to replay a failure: python3 check.py solution 4 7 runs milestone 4 on seed 7 only. Python 3.9 or newer; nothing to install. The check runs the experiments of milestones 2, 3 and 5 on 30 seeds of 12,000 evaluations each, shares the results between milestones, and finishes in about eleven seconds for the reference solution. The mutation operator here is a random replacement of moves (it is given, in mutate). In later labs that operator is a language model proposing a change to a program or a prompt; there, a deterministic stand-in takes its place, and the loop around it is the loop you write here.

What you'll build

Five milestones:

  1. The walker: follow a genome of 45 moves through the maze, find where it ends, how far that is from the goal and how quickly it got there.
  2. A genetic algorithm that follows the objective: tournament selection and mutation, with the scoring rule passed in as a function. Watch it get trapped at the wall.
  3. Novelty search: the same loop, scored by how unlike its neighbours each walker's final cell is, with an archive of places already seen.
  4. MAP-Elites: an archive with one elite per final cell, where a child competes only against the occupant of its own cell.
  5. Reading the map: coverage, QD-score, the elite that solves the maze, and the stepping stones along its route; then all three methods side by side.

Milestone 1: the walker

A genome here is a list of 45 moves, each 0 (up), 1 (down), 2 (left) or 3 (right). A walker starts on the start cell and follows them in order; a move into a wall, or off the edge of the grid, does nothing. The walker's behaviour is the cell where it ends up. Everything else in the lab (the objective, novelty, the archive) is a function of that one cell, which is why the maze is such a clean test bed: the whole behaviour space is the 85 open cells, small enough to draw, and the genome space, 445 sequences, is far too big to enumerate.

The maze is the one from chapter 3. The goal is closest to the start in a straight line (7.07 cells), but a wall with a single gap on the far left stands between them. A problem like this is deceptive: following the score leads away from the answer. Here is what the score does along the route a person would draw (nine moves left, seven up through the gap, eight right, 24 moves in all):

The distance to the goal climbs from 7.07 to 10.63 and does not come back below the start's value until step 17 of the 24. A search that keeps whichever walkers are closest to the goal discards precisely the walkers that are on their way.

The fifth function in this milestone, arrival_step, is a measure of efficiency that milestone 4 will use as quality: how many moves it took to reach the final cell for good, meaning the index of the last move that changed the position. A walker that stops moving at move 12 and spends the remaining 33 moves bumping into a wall has arrival step 12. A walker that wanders back and forth and ends one step from where it was, has the arrival step of its last change, not of the first time it visited the cell.

def path(maze, genome):
    """The cells visited, as a list of length len(genome) + 1: the start cell, then the cell after each move.
    A move into a wall (or off the grid) leaves the walker where it is."""
    r, c = maze.start
    cells = [(r, c)]
    for m in genome:
        dr, dc = MOVES[m]
        if maze.is_open(r + dr, c + dc):
            r, c = r + dr, c + dc
        cells.append((r, c))
    return cells

def walk(maze, genome):
    """The behaviour of a genome: the cell where the walker ends up."""
    return path(maze, genome)[-1]

def goal_distance(maze, cell):
    """Straight-line (Euclidean) distance from `cell` to the goal."""
    return math.dist(cell, maze.goal)

def arrival_step(cells):
    """How many moves it took to reach the final cell for good: the index of the last move that changed
    the position (0 if the walker never moved). `cells` is a path as returned by `path`."""
    last = 0
    for i in range(1, len(cells)):
        if cells[i] != cells[i - 1]:
            last = i
    return last

Milestone 2: a genetic algorithm that follows the objective

A genetic algorithm keeps a population of genomes, evaluates them, lets the better ones produce the next generation, and repeats (chapter 2). Two ingredients are enough here. Tournament selection picks k individuals at random and takes the best of them as a parent, so better walkers are chosen more often but even a mediocre one has a chance. Mutation (given) copies the parent and replaces each move by a random one with probability 0.05, so on average 2.25 of the 45 moves are replaced. There is no crossover, as in chapter 3's maze.

The design decision of this milestone is what evolve takes as its argument. Instead of hard-wiring the objective, it takes a scorer, a function scorer(ends, state) that receives the final cell of every walker in the population and returns one score per walker (higher is better). The loop never looks at the distance to the goal for selection, only at what the scorer says. That is the single change milestone 3 will make: a different scorer, the same loop. The state argument is a dictionary that lives for the whole run so a scorer can remember things between generations; the novelty scorer will keep its archive there.

The loop is the same one chapter 3 ran, with an evaluation budget of 60 walkers for 200 generations (12,000 evaluations). Each generation: walk every genome; record the cells visited and the closest approach to the goal; if the goal was reached, stop and report; otherwise score and breed. The result is a Result with solved, evals (generations used times the population size, up to and including the generation that solved it), closest (the smallest distance to the goal seen) and visited (every final cell any walker ended on).

def tournament(scores, rng, k=3):
    """Pick k random individuals (without replacement) and return the index of the one with the highest score."""
    return max(rng.sample(range(len(scores)), k), key=lambda i: scores[i])

def objective_scorer(maze):
    """A scorer for `evolve`: higher is better, so the score is minus the distance to the goal."""
    def scorer(ends, state):
        return [-goal_distance(maze, e) for e in ends]
    return scorer

def evolve(maze, scorer, seed, pop_size=60, gens=200):
    """A generational genetic algorithm with tournament selection and mutation (no crossover).

    Every generation: walk each genome to get its final cell; update `visited` and `closest`; if the goal
    has been reached stop and return Result(True, (gen + 1) * pop_size, 0.0, visited); otherwise ask
    `scorer(ends, state)` for one score per genome (`state` is a dict the scorer may use to remember
    things between generations) and build the next population with `tournament` and `mutate`.
    If the budget runs out return Result(False, gens * pop_size, closest, visited).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pop = [random_genome(rng) for _ in range(pop_size)]
    state, visited, closest = {}, set(), math.inf
    for gen in range(gens):
        ends = [walk(maze, g) for g in pop]
        visited.update(ends)
        closest = min(closest, min(goal_distance(maze, e) for e in ends))
        if closest == 0:
            return Result(True, (gen + 1) * pop_size, 0.0, visited)
        scores = scorer(ends, state)
        pop = [mutate(pop[tournament(scores, rng)], rng) for _ in range(pop_size)]
    return Result(False, gens * pop_size, closest, visited)

Milestone 3: novelty search

Novelty search (Lehman and Stanley, 2008 and 2011; chapter 3) throws away the objective as the selection rule. Instead each walker is judged by how different its behaviour is from everything else seen. Three pieces:

Behaviour characterisation
A short description of what a candidate does, chosen by the designer. Here it is the final cell, as a pair (row, column). Distance in this space is Euclidean distance between cells.
Novelty score
The mean distance from a walker's final cell to its k nearest neighbours among the other walkers of this generation and an archive of past behaviours. A cell in a crowded region scores low; one far from everything scores high.
Archive
A growing list of final cells that were novel when first found. Without it, a population could circle back to places it visited twenty generations ago, which look novel only because everybody else has moved on. The archive makes "new" mean new to the whole run.

You write two functions. novelty(cell, others, k) is the score for one cell. novelty_scorer(k, threshold) returns a scorer to plug into your evolve: for each final cell in the generation it computes the novelty against the other cells of that generation plus the archive stored in state["archive"]; afterwards, every cell of the generation whose novelty exceeds threshold is appended to the archive. Note the order: scores are computed from the archive as it was before this generation, and the archive grows afterwards. That ordering is what the check tests with a hand example.

Solution spoiler 
def novelty(cell, others, k=10):
    """Mean distance from `cell` to its k nearest neighbours among `others` (a list of cells).
    With fewer than k others, use all of them; with none at all the novelty is 0."""
    d = sorted(math.dist(cell, o) for o in others)[:k]
    return sum(d) / len(d) if d else 0.0

def novelty_scorer(k=10, threshold=1.5):
    """A scorer for `evolve` that ignores the goal. Each final cell is scored by its novelty against the
    other cells of this generation plus `state["archive"]`; afterwards every cell of the generation whose
    novelty exceeds `threshold` is appended to the archive."""
    def scorer(ends, state):
        archive = state.setdefault("archive", [])
        scores = [novelty(e, ends[:i] + ends[i + 1:] + archive, k) for i, e in enumerate(ends)]
        archive.extend(e for e, s in zip(ends, scores) if s > threshold)
        return scores
    return scorer

Common misconception

"Novelty search is random search with extra steps." It is directed: selection pushes the population towards unexplored behaviour, which is why it reaches the goal in 21 of 30 runs here. Drawing random genomes with the same budget does much worse (the script below). But novelty search is not a better optimiser either. It ignores the goal when choosing parents, so on a friendly problem it is slower than the objective; in chapter 3's control on a smooth landscape it needed about nine times as many evaluations. Novelty is a better answer to a deceptive problem and a worse one to an easy problem.

Milestone 4: MAP-Elites

Novelty search escapes the trap but then forgets quality: it keeps no record of the best way to reach anywhere, and its selection pressure is unrelated to how good anything is. Quality-diversity algorithms want both: many different solutions, each as good as it can be. MAP-Elites (Mouret and Clune, 2015; chapter 4) is the simplest. The behaviour space is divided into cells, and an archive holds at most one solution per cell, the best one found so far, called the elite. The loop is:

  1. Seed: evaluate some random genomes and put each in the cell its behaviour points at.
  2. Pick a parent by choosing a filled cell uniformly at random and taking its elite. Not the fittest: any cell.
  3. Mutate it to make a child, and evaluate the child: its cell and its quality.
  4. Compete locally: if the child's cell is empty, store it; if it is occupied and the child is strictly better, replace the occupant; otherwise discard the child.

Two details carry the algorithm. Uniform parent choice spends as much effort on a poor elite on the edge of the map as on the champion in the middle, which pushes the frontier outward. And competition is local, so a mediocre solution that does something nobody else does is safe: it has a cell to itself. In this lab the descriptor is the final cell, optionally coarsened by integer division (bins=2 merges 2 by 2 blocks of cells), and the quality is 1 - arrival_step / 45: the fewer moves a genome needs to get where it ends up, the better. So the elite in each cell is the quickest known way to arrive there, and the elite in the goal cell is a solution to the maze.

The archive is a dictionary {key: (quality, genome)}. The first init genomes are random; after that each genome is the mutation of the elite of a uniformly random filled cell (choose from sorted(archive) so that runs are repeatable). def descriptor(cell, bins=1):
    """The archive cell a final cell belongs to: (row // bins, col // bins)."""
    return (cell[0] // bins, cell[1] // bins)

def quality(maze, genome):
    """1 - arrival_step / len(genome): the fewer moves a genome needs to get where it ends up, the better.
    A genome that never leaves the start scores 1.0."""
    return 1.0 - arrival_step(path(maze, genome)) / len(genome)

def try_insert(archive, key, q, genome):
    """Local competition. Store (q, genome) under `key` if the cell is empty or q is STRICTLY better than
    the occupant's quality. Return True if the archive changed."""
    if key not in archive or q > archive[key][0]:
        archive[key] = (q, genome)
        return True
    return False

def map_elites(maze, evals, seed, bins=1, init=300):
    """MAP-Elites. The first `init` genomes are random; after that each genome is `mutate` applied to the elite
    of a uniformly random filled cell (rng.choice over the archive's cells, in sorted order so runs
    are repeatable). Every genome is evaluated once: its cell is descriptor(walk(maze, genome), bins) and its
    quality is `quality`. Returns the archive: {cell: (quality, genome)}."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    archive = {}
    for e in range(evals):
        if e < init or not archive:
            genome = random_genome(rng)
        else:
            parent = archive[rng.choice(sorted(archive))][1]
            genome = mutate(parent, rng)
        try_insert(archive, descriptor(walk(maze, genome), bins), quality(maze, genome), genome)
    return archive

Milestone 5: reading the map

An archive is only useful if you can read it. Four small functions turn it into answers: coverage (what fraction of the reachable cells are filled), qd_score (the sum of the qualities), solution_path (the path of the elite in the goal's cell, or None) and stepping_stones (the cells on that path that are farther from the goal than the start is: the part of the route where the score is wrong). They are short, but the last one is the point of the lab. Take the elite that solves the maze and ask which of its cells the objective would have kept. The answer is: not these.

Coverage uses the breadth-first maze.reachable() given in the starter, mapped through descriptor, so the denominator is correct for any bins (you cannot fill a cell of the archive that contains no open maze cell). Remember that QD-score depends on the resolution of the archive, so compare scores only between runs that use the same descriptors and the same bins.

def coverage(archive, maze, bins=1):
    """Fraction of the reachable archive cells that hold an elite. (Reachable: the cells of
    maze.reachable(), mapped through `descriptor`.)"""
    reachable = {descriptor(c, bins) for c in maze.reachable()}
    return len(set(archive) & reachable) / len(reachable)

def qd_score(archive):
    """The sum of the elites' qualities."""
    return sum(q for q, _ in archive.values())

def solution_path(archive, maze, bins=1):
    """The elite genome's path (list of cells) for the cell that contains the goal, or None if that cell is empty."""
    key = descriptor(maze.goal, bins)
    if key not in archive:
        return None
    return path(maze, archive[key][1])

def stepping_stones(archive, maze, bins=1):
    """Cells of the solution path that are NOT closer to the goal than the start is: the part of the route where
    the objective gets worse before it gets better. Returns the sorted list of distinct cells on the path whose
    distance to the goal is greater than the start's, or None if the maze is unsolved."""
    p = solution_path(archive, maze, bins)
    if p is None:
        return None
    d0 = goal_distance(maze, maze.start)
    return sorted({c for c in p if goal_distance(maze, c) > d0})

The picture is what the algorithm was for. It is not one answer; it is a statement about the whole maze: here is the quickest way I found to end up in each cell. The solution is a by-product, the elite in the goal cell. The stepping stones are in the same table, with their elites: the gap cell (4, 3) holds a genome that the objective would have ranked below the start (distance 8.54 against 7.07), and MAP-Elites kept it because it was the only genome in that cell.

Going further

  1. Make a harder maze. Maze takes any list of strings. Build a U-shaped trap (a pocket directly between the start and the goal) and a longer detour, and compare the three methods at the same budget. When does MAP-Elites stop reaching the goal? What helps more, a larger budget or a lower mutation rate?
  2. Coarser maps. Run map_elites with bins=2 and bins=3. The archive has fewer, larger cells; how does the number of runs that solve the maze change? Remember that QD-scores are not comparable across resolutions.
  3. A real objective as quality. Change quality to minus the distance of the walker's path to the goal and keep the final cell as the descriptor. Now the elites are the most goal-seeking genome for each cell. Chapter 4 calls the coarse versions of this local competition; is the archive better or worse at reaching the goal?
  4. Minimal criterion. Add a rule to the novelty scorer that gives a walker no score unless it moved at least 10 cells from the start (Lehman and Stanley's minimal criterion novelty search). Does it help or hurt in this maze?
  5. A better mutation operator. Replace mutate with one that inserts, deletes or swaps moves instead of overwriting them. This is the slot where a language model goes in later labs: the loop does not change, only the proposal distribution does. Which of your three searches benefits most?
  6. Where this goes next. Lab 3 (A Mini FunSearch) keeps an archive of programs rather than walkers. Case study 2 is the human version of this lab: Picbreeder, where people chose by interest and no objective, and stepping stones appeared that objective search could not later reach.

Systems connection

Deception is the local-maximum problem of metric-driven iteration. A team that A/B tests its way up one ranking formula (from model to value) is a hill-climber on a metric, and the design that would need three worse releases before it paid off never gets built. The industrial cousin of novelty search is coverage-guided fuzzing: a fuzzer keeps any input that exercises code it has not exercised before, regardless of whether the input crashes anything, because that input is a stepping stone to deeper code paths. And the archive itself is a keyed store with an admission rule, like a cache with a rule for what may replace an entry: the discipline you applied in try_insert (strictly better, only within a cell) is the same kind of invariant a cache needs to stay explainable.

What you learned

  • On a deceptive maze, a genetic algorithm that follows the distance to the goal solved 7 of 30 runs and piled up against the wall; the cells it needed to cross scored worse than the start.
  • Swapping the scorer for novelty (distance to nearest neighbours plus an archive) kept the loop and raised the solved runs to 21 of 30, with the population spread over nearly the whole maze. It depends on a behaviour description: with only the final row, it solved 3.
  • MAP-Elites keeps one elite per behaviour cell, picks parents uniformly from the filled cells, and lets a child compete only in its own cell. It solved 26 of 30 and returns a map, not a point.
  • The archive's invariants are cheap to state and powerful to test: more evaluations never lose a cell and never make a cell worse, so the QD-score never falls.
  • The solution of the maze is the elite in the goal cell, and the stepping stones along its route are cells the objective would have discarded. That is what stepping stones look like in data.
  • Coverage and QD-score depend on the archive's resolution; compare them only between runs with the same descriptor and bins.

Check your understanding

On the maze, why does a genetic algorithm that selects walkers by distance to the goal usually fail?

  1. Its mutation rate of 0.05 is too low to find the gap
  2. The cells on the way through the gap are farther from the goal than the cells pressed against the wall, so selection discards the walkers that are making progress
  3. Tournament selection with k = 3 is too strong; k = 1 would solve it
  4. A population of 60 cannot cover a maze of 85 cells
The route to the goal passes through cells that score worse than the start (the distance rises from 7.07 to 10.63 before it falls), so selection removes them. A lower or higher mutation rate or a different tournament size changes the speed of the climb, not where it ends. The population size is not the limit: the novelty search that solves the maze uses the same 60 walkers.

In novelty_scorer, why is each walker's novelty computed against the other walkers of the generation plus the archive, and not against all of them including itself?

  1. Including itself would make the scorer slower
  2. The archive has to be consulted last
  3. A walker is always at distance 0 from itself, so every score would shrink and the nearest neighbour of everything would be itself
  4. The archive may not contain the walker's own cell
A walker's own cell is at distance 0, so with itself among the neighbours every walker would have a free neighbour at distance 0 dragging its score down. Leaving it out compares each walker with the rest of the world. Speed is unaffected, and the archive can contain the walker's own cell (that is the case where the cell is no longer novel).

In MAP-Elites, why is the parent chosen uniformly from the filled cells instead of choosing the elite of highest quality?

  1. Choosing the best would spend most of the effort in the crowded middle of the map; uniform choice spends as much on the edge, which is how the frontier of filled cells moves outward
  2. Uniform choice guarantees that every elite is improved in every generation
  3. The highest quality elite is always in the goal cell, so there is nothing to learn from it
  4. Uniform choice is required for the QD-score to be monotone
The edge of the map is where new cells come from, and the cells at the edge are usually the ones with poor elites. The QD-score is monotone for any parent rule, because only try_insert changes the archive. Uniform choice does not improve every elite each generation (only one parent is mutated per evaluation), and the best elite is not in the goal cell at the start.

A MAP-Elites run with 3,000 evaluations has a QD-score of 4.05 and one with 12,000 evaluations has 10.7. A colleague says the 3,000-evaluation run 'found worse solutions'. What is the best response?

  1. Agree: a lower QD-score means the best solution was worse
  2. Disagree: QD-score ignores quality and counts only filled cells
  3. Agree, but only if the two runs used different seeds
  4. Partly disagree: the QD-score sums the qualities of all elites, so it mixes how many cells are filled with how good each is, and says nothing about the best single solution
The QD-score adds up every elite's quality, so a run with more cells filled and better elites scores higher, and it rises with the budget even if the best single genome was found early. It does depend on quality (so it is not simply a cell count), and seeds change the numbers a little, not the reading.

Novelty search with the final row as its behaviour description solved 3 of 30 runs, against 21 with the final cell. What does this show?

  1. Novelty search is unreliable and should not be used
  2. Novelty search explores only along the directions that the behaviour description separates, so the description decides what it can find
  3. The final row is a more compact description and should have worked better
  4. The archive threshold was set too high for rows
With rows only, a walker at (3, 5) and one at (3, 11) are the same behaviour, so nothing pushes the population to the goal's column after it crosses the wall; it still crossed in most runs (75 cells occupied on average). The description is the designer's contribution, and a poor one is not rescued by the algorithm.

The elite in the goal cell of a 12,000-evaluation MAP-Elites run needs 41 moves, although a 24-move route exists. What would most reliably shorten it?

  1. Lowering the archive's resolution to bins=3
  2. Replacing try_insert's strict comparison with a tie-replacing one
  3. A larger evaluation budget: every cell's elite is replaced whenever a quicker route turns up, and the QD-score was still rising at 36,000 evaluations
  4. Choosing parents from the cell with the best quality only
Quality improves with more mutation of existing elites, and the table of milestone 4 shows the QD-score still climbing after all cells are filled. A coarser archive has fewer, larger cells and so keeps fewer elites; ties do not improve quality; and always mutating the best elite gives up the diversity that got the search to the goal.
