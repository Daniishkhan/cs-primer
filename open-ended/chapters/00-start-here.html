Start Here

Most of AI engineering is about getting a model to answer a question. This course is about systems that do something different. They search, keep what they find, and use it to search better. You will learn the classical ideas underneath: trees, populations and novelty. You will see how language models changed what such systems can do. You will also meet the integrity problems that make them dangerous to trust without care.

Why this matters

This is where the builder spine of the AI path was heading. Some of the most striking recent AI results did not come from asking a model once. They came from a loop that asks thousands of times, keeps the best answers and builds on them. One such loop is reported to have found a faster way to multiply small matrices. Another was a coding agent that rewrote its own tools.

All of these loops share one skeleton, which is easy to learn. They also share one hazard, which is easy to miss. A loop that pushes a score up will find every gap between the score and what you actually wanted. By the end of this course you will be able to write such loops yourself in plain Python. You will run one on a real problem, packing items into bins. You will make the loop improve its own search. You will also check whether an improvement is real or a trick played on your test.

Two ways to get a good answer

There are two broad ways to get a good result out of a computer. The first is to compute it: the problem is understood well enough that a procedure produces the answer directly, such as sorting a list or fitting a line. The second is to search for it: generate candidates, test them against something that tells you how good they are, and use the results to choose the next candidates. You use search when two things hold. The space of possible answers is too large to enumerate, and you can check an answer far more cheaply than you can derive one. A pharmacist's timetable, a faster sorting routine, a better prompt and a safer wiring plan are all easier to evaluate than to construct.

Most search in classical computing has a fixed target. There is an objective, a number to push up, and the algorithm stops when it plateaus. This is optimisation. A different tradition, open-ended search, asks what happens when you do not stop. Such a system keeps producing new, increasingly interesting things, as biological evolution does, rather than converging on one answer. The two traditions disagree about how to search. Optimisation says: follow the score. Open-endedness says: the score can be a trap, and collecting diverse stepping stones sometimes gets you further than aiming straight. Chapter 3 makes that disagreement concrete, with code.

Analogy: a cook perfecting a recipe

A cook does not derive the perfect soup from chemistry. They make a batch, taste it, change one thing, and taste again. They keep a notebook of what worked, and they sometimes go back to an older version and branch from there. Search is that habit made mechanical: propose a change, score it, remember the good ones, repeat. Optimisation is the cook who only ever chases the best-tasting spoonful. Open-ended search is the cook who also keeps odd, different recipes in the notebook, because one of them may lead somewhere the best soup never would.

Where it breaks down: a cook's tongue is a trustworthy judge. In these loops the judge is code, and a loop that pushes its score up will find every gap between that code and what you really wanted. The later chapters on evaluator hacking are about exactly that.

One loop, many systems

Nearly every system in this course is the same four-step loop around an archive of everything tried so far. Only the contents of the boxes change.

To make that skeleton concrete, here is the whole loop in 25 lines. The task is deliberately silly: find a hidden 18-character phrase. The evaluator counts correct characters, the proposer changes one character at random, and the selector picks among the five best entries in the archive. There is nothing else.

import random, string

TARGET = "SEARCH NEVER STOPS"
ALPHABET = string.ascii_uppercase + " "

def score(s):                                   # the evaluator: how many characters are right
    return sum(a == b for a, b in zip(s, TARGET))

def propose(parent, rng):                       # the proposer: change one character at random
    i = rng.randrange(len(parent))              # (later chapters replace this with a language model)
    return parent[:i] + rng.choice(ALPHABET) + parent[i + 1:]

def search(budget, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    first = "".join(rng.choice(ALPHABET) for _ in TARGET)
    archive = [(score(first), first)]           # everything we have tried and what it scored
    for step in range(1, budget + 1):
        parents = sorted(archive, reverse=True)[:5]       # the selector: pick among the best five
        child = propose(rng.choice(parents)[1], rng)
        archive.append((score(child), child))
        if archive[-1][0] == len(TARGET):
            return step, child
    return budget, max(archive)[1]

for seed in range(3):
    steps, found = search(5000, seed)
    print(f"seed {seed}: '{found}' after {steps} proposals")
seed 0: 'SEARCH NEVER STOPS' after 2247 proposals
seed 1: 'SEARCH NEVER STOPS' after 1590 proposals
seed 2: 'SEARCH NEVER STOPS' after 2347 proposals

Blind guessing would need about 2718 tries (roughly 6 × 1025). The loop needs around two thousand, because the archive remembers what worked. The proposer here is random. In chapter 9 it becomes a language model that writes plausible program edits, and in chapter 12 the thing being edited is an AI agent. In this course, anywhere a language model would be the proposer, we run a deterministic stand-in instead, and say so. Every example then runs offline, for free, and gives the same answer each time.

How to study this course

  • Depth tags. Each chapter is labelled Deep dive, Core or Quick intro. Deep chapters derive the mechanism and include a step-through or interactive widget; read them with the code open. Core chapters give the working picture. Quick intros, like this one, orient you.
  • Run the code. Every listing is pure Python from the standard library with seeded randomness, and the output shown is what we got by running it. Change one thing, predict what happens, then run it.
  • Labs. Seven labs build the key mechanisms: a bandit and MCTS, novelty search and MAP-Elites, a mini FunSearch, adaptive branching, a mini meta-agent search, a gameable evaluator, and an evolver that improves its own search. Each has a starter.py and a check.py; you run python3 check.py and it reports which milestones pass. No network, no key and no cost.
  • Stand-in models. Wherever a language model would normally propose changes, our code uses a deterministic stand-in function. Each lab page shows how to point it at a real model if you choose to. The stand-ins make results reproducible. They also mean a lab result tells you the mechanism works, not how well a particular model would do.
  • Case studies and studios. Seven cases read primary sources on AlphaZero, Picbreeder, FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, Voyager, the Darwin Gödel Machine and The AI Scientist. Two studios ask you to design an optimisation loop and to review a claim of self-improvement before reading the model answer. The third is the capstone of the builder spine: build and audit your own self-improving loop.

State of play, October 2026

This field moves quickly. Systems named in this course (AlphaEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, the Darwin Gödel Machine, The AI Scientist and others) are snapshots from 2023 to 2026, and some will be superseded or revised. The chapters keep the durable ideas in the main text: the loop, the trade-offs and the failure modes. Dated claims go in boxes like this one, with sources. Where a result matters to an argument, we cite the paper and say what it reports rather than repeating a headline.

Common misconception

"Self-improving AI means a model rewriting its own weights until it becomes superhuman." The systems in this course are far more modest and more concrete. They edit prompts, programs, tool code and agent scaffolds, and measure the result with an evaluator. Whether the improvements are real, whether they generalise beyond the evaluator, and whether the loop is safe to run are engineering questions. They have engineering answers, and they are most of what Part 5 is about.

Key takeaways

  • Search is the right tool when candidates are cheap to check and hard to derive. It is a loop: select a parent, propose a change, evaluate, store.
  • Optimisation follows a score; open-ended search keeps generating new things and treats the score as only one signal. They disagree most when the score is a trap.
  • The same skeleton underlies tree search, evolution, program evolution with LLMs and self-improving agents. Learn the skeleton once.
  • The evaluator is the part of the loop that most often fails; Part 5 is about trusting results.
  • Everything is runnable offline in pure Python with seeded randomness; model calls use clearly labelled stand-ins.

Check your understanding

Which situation is best suited to search rather than direct computation?

  1. Sorting a list of 10,000 numbers
  2. Computing the mean of a column
  3. Finding a faster variant of a routine when you can benchmark any candidate but cannot derive the best one
  4. Looking up a customer's address by ID
Search pays off when candidates are cheap to check and hard to derive. The other three have direct procedures that produce the answer without trial and error.

In the four-step loop of this chapter, which step most often causes a search to produce misleading results?

  1. The evaluator, because the loop will exploit any gap between its score and what you want
  2. The archive, because it stores too many candidates
  3. The selector, because it picks parents randomly
  4. The proposer, because mutation is random
Selection and proposal can be inefficient but they cannot make a result false; a flawed evaluator can. That is why Part 5 of the course is devoted to evaluators.

The toy phrase search found an 18-character target in about two thousand proposals, while blind guessing would need about 6 × 1025. What accounts for the difference?

  1. The random proposer is much better than a random guess
  2. The evaluator knows the target
  3. Python's random number generator is biased
  4. The archive lets the loop build on partial successes, each proposal being a small change to something that already scored well
The evaluator does see the target through its score (option 2 describes a true fact but not what makes the difference), but the score only helps because candidates are kept and improved incrementally. Without the archive and parent selection, each proposal would be a fresh random guess.

Why does this course use deterministic stand-in functions where a language model would propose changes?

  1. Language models cannot be used for search
  2. So every example runs offline, free and reproducibly, at the cost of telling you about mechanism rather than the quality of any particular model
  3. Because stand-ins are more accurate than real models
  4. Because the algorithms do not depend on the proposer
The goal is reproducible learning, and each lab shows how to connect a real model. The proposer does matter for results (it determines how often candidates are sensible), so the labs prove the mechanism, not the model's skill.
