Open-Ended Algorithms

Every search so far had a fixed problem. This chapter drops that assumption: what if the search also invents the problems? It introduces an algorithm that grows a population of environments together with the agents that solve them. It then uses that algorithm to ask what it would mean to be open-ended at all.

Why this matters

Part 1 of this course made search better at one task: find the best answer to a question someone wrote down. Self-improving agents face a different situation. Their next task is not written down. It has to be chosen, generated or discovered. The quality of that choice decides whether the system keeps improving or stalls.

After this chapter you can explain why training directly on a hard problem often fails where a path of easier problems succeeds. You can describe how the algorithm builds that path automatically. You can say what “open-ended” means in a testable way, and recognise when a proposed system only sounds open-ended. The next two chapters replace its hand-written environment generator with foundation models (Models of Interestingness) and apply the same loop to a game-playing agent (Lifelong Agents).

Searching over problems, not only solutions

Imagine teaching someone to climb. You could take a beginner to a sheer cliff and say “get to the top”. They would fall, learn nothing from falling, and give up. The task is so far beyond them that no attempt gets closer in a way they can notice. A good coach instead picks a route slightly harder than the last one, and the learner gradually acquires skills that no single route would have taught. The problem is shaped to the learner, and the learner is shaped by the problem.

In reinforcement-learning terms this is a curriculum: an ordering of tasks from easy to hard. Hand-designed curricula work when you know what the final task is and what skills lead to it. They fail in three cases. The end goal may be unknown. There may be many possible goals. Or the route to a hard task may pass through tasks that no designer would think to include. Chapter 3 gave the general reason: the stepping stones to a good solution rarely look like it.

The Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer (POET) automates the coach. It was introduced by Wang, Lehman, Clune and Stanley (2019, “Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer (POET): Endlessly Generating Increasingly Complex and Diverse Learning Environments and Their Solutions”, Uber AI). It maintains a population of environment–agent pairs. Environments are generated by mutating existing ones. Agents are trained to solve their own environment. Agents are also swapped between environments, so a skill acquired for one challenge can unlock another. Nobody specifies the final challenge. The set of challenges grows as the agents become able to meet them.

What POET does, step by step

In the original experiments the environments were two-dimensional obstacle courses for a bipedal walker, adapted from OpenAI Gym's BipedalWalker. Each course is described by a few numbers: how rough the ground is, and the range of widths of gaps and heights of stumps. The agent is a neural-network policy trained with evolution strategies (ES). This method, from the evolutionary algorithms chapter, estimates a gradient by trying random perturbations of the parameters. It then moves toward the ones that scored higher. The same machinery can sit on any optimiser: reinforcement learning or a black-box search would do.

The algorithm starts with one trivial environment (flat ground) and a randomly initialised agent, and then repeats four activities.

  1. Optimise. Each agent takes ES steps on its own paired environment only. This is ordinary training, in parallel.
  2. Generate. Environments whose agent has reached a score threshold are allowed to reproduce: each is copied and slightly mutated into a candidate child. The child starts with a copy of its parent's agent. Only environments that have been “mastered” reproduce, so compute is not spent mutating problems that are currently hopeless.
  3. Filter and rank. Candidates must pass a minimal criterion. The inherited agent must score inside a band set by lower and upper bounds. Too low means the task is impossible and gives no learning signal. Too high means it is trivial and has nothing to teach. Survivors are ranked by novelty: the distance of the new environment from all environments seen so far. This is the same idea as the novelty archive. The most novel are admitted. When the population is full, the oldest environments are retired.
  4. Transfer. Periodically, every agent is tried on every other environment. Suppose an agent from environment B does better on environment A than A's own agent does, either directly or after one ES step. Then it replaces A's agent. This is how skills learned for one challenge spread to another.

Analogy: a gym that builds its own equipment

A gym owner watches what members can already lift. The owner buys the next machine a little harder than any of them can manage. If some member surprises everyone on one machine, the owner tries that member on the others. Over a few years the gym has a wide range of equipment, and no one planned the range. Environments are the machines and agents are the members. The minimal criterion is “not so heavy that nobody can move it, not so light that it is pointless”, and transfer is moving a member across machines.

Where it breaks down: a gym owner knows what “stronger” means. POET only knows that environments should be different from the ones it already has. It has no independent notion of whether a new environment is worth solving. That gap is the subject of chapter 6.

A miniature POET you can run

The original runs on large clusters, but the ideas fit in 70 lines. In this toy an environment is a point e in four dimensions, and the agent is another point w; an agent solves an environment when it is close to it. The reward is a needle: 100 × max(0, 1 − distance / 1.5), which is exactly zero when the agent is more than 1.5 away. A blank agent at the origin therefore gets no signal at all from a faraway environment. That is what makes hard environments hard. ES sees four zeros however it perturbs, so it does not move. (This is a deliberately minimal stand-in for sparse-reward tasks. It has none of a walker's physics, and it makes no claim about speed or scale.)

The script compares five ways to spend an identical budget of 60,000 evaluations. The first is training directly on ten environments at distance 8. The second is the full POET loop. The other three are POET with transfer, novelty ranking or the minimal criterion removed. The score it reports is the number of 2-unit regions of environment space in which at least one environment was solved. This rewards covering different problems rather than solving one many times. Environments mutate by small steps (standard deviation 0.6) 80% of the time and by wild jumps (2.0) 20% of the time, so that some children are unsolvable.

import math, random

K, R = 4, 1.5                                    # env/agent dimensions; reward radius (needle width)
SOLVED, MC_LO, MC_HI = 80, 10, 90                # solved score; minimal-criterion band for a new env
def score(w, e):                                 # reward is zero unless the agent is within R of the skill the env needs
    return 100 * max(0.0, 1 - math.dist(w, e) / R)

def es_step(w, e, rng, n=4, sigma=0.3, lr=0.5):  # one evolution-strategies step: nudge w toward better perturbations
    evals, upd = 0, [0.0] * K
    for _ in range(n):
        eps = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(K)]
        sp = score([a + sigma * b for a, b in zip(w, eps)], e)
        sm = score([a - sigma * b for a, b in zip(w, eps)], e)
        evals += 2
        upd = [u + (sp - sm) * b for u, b in zip(upd, eps)]
    scale = lr * sigma / (n * 100)
    return [a + scale * u * 10 for a, u in zip(w, upd)], evals

def novelty(e, others, k=5):
    d = sorted(math.dist(e, o) for o in others)[:k]
    return sum(d) / len(d)

def poet(seed, budget, transfer=True, use_novelty=True, use_mc=True):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pairs = [([0.0] * K, [0.0] * K)]             # (env, agent): start with a flat env and a blank agent
    archive, solved_envs, cellset, used, it = [[0.0] * K], set(), set(), 0, 0
    curve = []
    while used < budget:
        it += 1
        for i, (e, w) in enumerate(pairs):       # 1. every agent improves on its own environment
            w, c = es_step(w, e, rng); used += c; pairs[i] = (e, w)
            if score(w, e) >= SOLVED: solved_envs.add(tuple(round(x, 3) for x in e)); cellset.add(tuple(int(x // 2) for x in e))
        if it % 5 == 0:                          # 2. solved environments spawn mutated children
            cands = []
            for e, w in pairs:
                if score(w, e) >= SOLVED:
                    step = 0.6 if rng.random() < 0.8 else 2.0   # mostly small edits, sometimes a wild one
                    child = [x + rng.gauss(0, step) for x in e]
                    s = score(w, child); used += 1
                    if (MC_LO <= s <= MC_HI) or not use_mc:   #    minimal criterion: not impossible, not trivial
                        cands.append((novelty(child, archive + [p[0] for p in pairs]) if use_novelty else rng.random(), child, w))
            for _, child, w in sorted(cands, key=lambda c: -c[0])[:2]:   # keep the most novel
                pairs.append((child, list(w))); archive.append(child)
                if len(pairs) > 10: pairs.pop(0)
        if transfer and it % 10 == 0:            # 3. try every agent on every other environment
            for i, (e, w) in enumerate(pairs):
                best = max((p[1] for p in pairs), key=lambda cand: score(cand, e))
                used += len(pairs)
                if score(best, e) > score(w, e): pairs[i] = (e, list(best))
        curve.append((used, len(cellset)))
    far = max((math.dist(e, [0.0] * K) for e in solved_envs), default=0.0)
    return len(cellset), far, curve               # cells = 2-unit boxes of environment space holding a solved env

def direct(seed, budget, n_envs=10):             # no curriculum: train on hard envs from the start
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    envs = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(K)] for _ in range(n_envs)]
    envs = [[x / math.dist(e, [0.0] * K) * 8 for x in e] for e in envs]   # all at distance 8
    agents, used = [[0.0] * K for _ in envs], 0
    while used < budget:
        for i, e in enumerate(envs):
            agents[i], c = es_step(agents[i], e, rng); used += c
    ok = [e for a, e in zip(agents, envs) if score(a, e) >= SOLVED]
    return len({tuple(int(x // 2) for x in e) for e in ok}), max((math.dist(e, [0.0] * K) for e in ok), default=0.0)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    BUDGET, SEEDS = 60_000, 10
    print(f"budget {BUDGET:,} evaluations, {SEEDS} seeds; solved = score >= {SOLVED}/100; a blank agent scores 0 beyond distance {R}")
    print(f"{'method':30s} {'regions solved':>14s} {'farthest solved':>16s}")
    for name, f in (("direct training on hard envs", lambda s: direct(s, BUDGET)),
                    ("POET", lambda s: poet(s, BUDGET)[:2]),
                    ("  without transfer", lambda s: poet(s, BUDGET, transfer=False)[:2]),
                    ("  without novelty (random pick)", lambda s: poet(s, BUDGET, use_novelty=False)[:2]),
                    ("  without minimal criterion", lambda s: poet(s, BUDGET, use_mc=False)[:2])):
        r = [f(s) for s in range(SEEDS)]
        print(f"{name:30s} {sum(x[0] for x in r)/SEEDS:14.1f} {sum(x[1] for x in r)/SEEDS:16.1f}")
budget 60,000 evaluations, 10 seeds; solved = score >= 80/100; a blank agent scores 0 beyond distance 1.5
method                         regions solved  farthest solved
direct training on hard envs              0.0              0.0
POET                                    136.9             14.3
  without transfer                      155.5             16.0
  without novelty (random pick)           67.4              8.3
  without minimal criterion              99.5             15.9

Four things stand out, and one of them is a surprise worth taking seriously.

  • Direct training solves nothing. At distance 8 the reward is zero everywhere the optimiser looks. This is the sparse-reward version of the deceptive landscapes from chapter 3: no gradient to follow, so no learning. POET gets to distance 14 to 16 by never asking the agent to jump more than a step.
  • Novelty ranking matters most here. Without it, children are picked at random. The population drifts and revisits the same neighbourhood, and coverage halves (about 67 regions against 137). Ranking by novelty is what sends the frontier outward.
  • The minimal criterion matters too. Without it, some unsolvable children (those produced by the wild jumps) are admitted. They have no learning signal and never become solved. They occupy places in a population of only ten, so coverage falls from 137 to about 100.
  • Transfer did not help in this toy; it cost a little. Without it, the run reached about 155 regions against 137. The reason is structural. Every environment here is a smooth needle that its parent's agent already approaches. There are no skills to carry across, so the all-pairs evaluations only spend budget. Do not read this as a verdict on transfer in general. The authors of POET reported transfer as essential in their domain. There an agent learned to jump over stumps in one environment, and that skill later improved performance in the environment it came from. They give one example: a parent environment's agent scored 349 at iteration 2,300 with transfer against 309 without. A toy with no deceptive structure cannot reproduce that, and an honest chapter says so.

Common misconception

“POET is a curriculum learner, so a fixed hand-made curriculum should do as well.” In the POET paper, the authors compared against a direct-path curriculum that increases difficulty along a hand-designed route. They report that it could not reproduce many of the environments POET solved. The range of environments it could solve was much narrower. The point is not that curricula are bad. It is that the best path to a hard task may not be the one a person would draw. POET explores many paths at once and lets agents cross between them.

What does “open-ended” mean?

POET is described as open-ended, and the word is used loosely enough to need pinning down. A process is open-ended if it keeps producing artefacts that are both novel and learnable, relative to some observer, indefinitely. Hughes, Dennis, Parker-Holder, Behbahani, Mavalankar, Shi, Schaul and Rocktaschel (2024, “Open-Endedness is Essential for Artificial Superhuman Intelligence”) define it in the same way. Each word does work.

  • Novel means the observer, with a model built from the past, finds the new artefact increasingly hard to predict. A system that cycles through the same ten outputs is not novel after the first cycle.
  • Learnable means that an observer who studies more history can predict the new artefacts better. Pure randomness is endlessly novel and never learnable; it is not interesting.
  • Relative to an observer means the same process can be open-ended for a person and dull to a different observer. This makes the definition operational: you pick an observer (a model, a panel of people), then measure.

This is more than a definition: it can be run as a measurement. The next script builds three artefact streams and two observers. One observer stops learning after 400 tokens (“frozen”). The other keeps updating from recent history. Each observer is a bigram model that predicts each token from the one before. A stream is novel if the frozen observer is surprised by late output (more than one bit per token). It is learnable if the up-to-date observer does at least one bit better. The three streams are a cycle of five symbols and uniform random noise over eight symbols. The third is a stream in which a new stage adds four unseen symbols every 400 tokens. Each stage's symbols are always followed by their own fixed partner. The one-bit thresholds are arbitrary and chosen for this demonstration.

import math, random
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

def cycle(n, rng):   return [i % 5 for i in range(n)]                    # repeats forever
def noise(n, rng):   return [rng.randrange(8) for _ in range(n)]         # random, nothing to learn
def growing(n, rng):                                                     # new "words" keep arriving, each with a rule
    out, vocab = [], 4
    while len(out) < n:
        if len(out) % 400 == 0: vocab += 4                               # a new stage adds 4 unseen symbols
        a = rng.randrange(vocab - 4, vocab)                              # a recent symbol...
        out += [a, (a * 7 + 3) % 1000 + 100 * (vocab // 4)]              # ...always followed by its own partner
    return out[:n]

def train(tokens):                               # a bigram model: how often does y follow x?
    nxt, tot = defaultdict(Counter), Counter()
    for x, y in zip(tokens, tokens[1:]): nxt[x][y] += 1; tot[x] += 1
    return nxt, tot

def loss(model, tokens, vocab=2000):             # mean surprise in bits, with light smoothing
    nxt, tot = model
    return sum(-math.log2((nxt[x][y] + 0.01) / (tot[x] + 0.01 * vocab)) for x, y in zip(tokens, tokens[1:])) / (len(tokens) - 1)

def run(name, gen, n=3000, early=400, window=100):
    stream = gen(n, random.Random(0))
    frozen = train(stream[:early])                       # an observer who stopped learning early
    late = stream[-window:]                              # what the process produces much later
    fresh = train(stream[-3 * window:-window])           # an observer who kept up (recent history only)
    f, u = loss(frozen, late), loss(fresh, late)
    novel, learn = f > 1.0, (f - u) > 1.0
    verdict = "open-ended" if novel and learn else "repeats" if not novel else "noise" if not learn else "?"
    print(f"{name:10s} frozen-observer loss {f:5.2f} bits   up-to-date loss {u:5.2f} bits   novel={novel!s:5}  learnable={learn!s:5}  -> {verdict}")

for name, g in (("cycle", cycle), ("noise", noise), ("growing", growing)): run(name, g)
cycle      frozen-observer loss  0.32 bits   up-to-date loss  0.59 bits   novel=False  learnable=False  -> repeats
noise      frozen-observer loss  3.63 bits   up-to-date loss  4.11 bits   novel=True   learnable=False  -> noise
growing    frozen-observer loss 10.97 bits   up-to-date loss  2.64 bits   novel=True   learnable=True   -> open-ended

The cycle is not novel (0.32 bits: the frozen model predicted it fine). The noise is novel, but staying up to date makes no difference (3.63 against 4.11 bits, a little worse because the fresh model has fewer examples). There is nothing to learn. The growing stream passes both tests. The frozen observer is lost (10.97 bits) while the up-to-date one copes (2.64 bits). Each new stage has rules that can be picked up quickly once the new symbols appear. That is the signature of open-endedness in this vocabulary. Note the limits too. A bigram model is a very weak observer. With this definition, whether a system is open-ended depends on the observer you choose. The measure tells you how to ask the question, not who is right.

Measuring innovation in POET

POET's authors needed a way to say that their system was making progress rather than merely running. The follow-up paper is Enhanced POET. It comes from Wang, Lehman, Rawal, Zhi, Li, Clune and Stanley (2020, “Enhanced POET: Open-Ended Reinforcement Learning through Unbounded Invention of Learning Challenges and their Solutions”). It introduced a generic measure of how much a system keeps producing open-ended innovation. It is the accumulated number of novel environments created and solved (ANNECS). Count only environments that passed the minimal criterion, that were novel when created, and that some agent later solved. A flat ANNECS curve means the system has stopped; a steadily rising one is evidence, not proof, of open-endedness. The same paper listed what the original lacked. These were a domain-general novelty measure, a better rule for when to attempt transfers, a more flexible way to encode environments, and that measure itself. The toy's “regions solved” plays the same role as ANNECS in miniature.

Where the idea goes next

POET sits in a family of ideas about letting the learning process shape its own tasks.

  • AI-generating algorithms. Jeff Clune's 2019 paper introduced AI-GAs (AI-generating algorithms). Its full title is “AI-GAs: AI-generating algorithms, an alternate paradigm for producing general artificial intelligence”. It argues for three pillars: meta-learn the architectures, meta-learn the learning algorithms, and automatically generate effective learning environments. POET is an early instance of the third.
  • Unsupervised environment design. Other research programmes, such as PAIRED (Dennis et al., 2020) and Prioritised Level Replay (Jiang et al., 2021), use different signals (regret, learning potential) to choose environments for a single learner. They differ from POET in what they optimise. The question they ask is the same.
  • Foundation models as designers. POET's hand-written generator can only mutate the numbers it was given. A language model can write the code of an entirely new environment. That is OMNI-EPIC (Open-endedness via Models of human Notions of Interestingness, with Environments Programmed in Code), the subject of the next chapter.
  • Open-endedness as a route to capability. Hughes et al. (2024) argue that systems capable of open-ended discovery are necessary for artificial superhuman intelligence, and that foundation models now provide the components. This is a position paper, not an experimental result; weigh it as an argument.

Limits, honestly

  • The space of problems is still designed. POET's environments are drawn from a family the authors defined (bounded obstacle parameters). It can be endlessly inventive inside the family and not at all outside it. Enhanced POET's more flexible encoding relaxes this but does not remove it.
  • “Novel” is relative to a distance you chose. A distance on obstacle parameters counts a bumpier course as new even when it teaches the same skill; a distance on behaviour might count nothing as new. The novelty measure is a stand-in for “interesting”, and it can be wrong in both directions.
  • The minimal criterion is a hand-set band. Choose it too narrow and the population stalls; too wide and unlearnable problems clog it, as the ablation showed.
  • Evidence of open-endedness is partial. Rising curves over a finite run cannot show a process would continue forever, and published runs are finite. Treat “open-ended” as a direction of travel and a measurement protocol, not a certified property.
  • Safety. A system that invents its own tasks can invent tasks you did not intend. The hazards, and how to bound them, return in chapter 17.

Systems connection

POET is a distributed system as much as an algorithm. Step 1 is embarrassingly parallel. Each pair trains independently, one worker per pair, with results gathered through a queue. Step 4 is not. Testing every agent on every environment is quadratic in the population. That is why the toy above pays visibly for it, and why POET attempts transfer only periodically (every 10 iterations in the toy). If you run such a job on GPUs, the scheduling and cost questions are those of parallelism and GPUs. The long-running, restartable nature of the job is the checkpointing problem of long training runs.

State of play, October 2026

POET (2019) and Enhanced POET (2020) are research systems from Uber AI. I did not check for a maintained library. This chapter's results come from the toy above, not from re-running the originals. The line they started continues in work that uses foundation models to propose tasks, for example OMNI-EPIC (2024). It also continues in position papers arguing for open-endedness as a goal for AI, such as Hughes et al. (2024). Benchmarks and metrics for open-endedness are not settled. Any claim that a particular system “is” open-ended should be read against its own definition and observer.

Key takeaways

  • When a task is too hard to give any learning signal, generate easier relatives of it and climb. POET does this automatically by evolving environments and agents together.
  • POET's loop is optimise, generate from solved environments, filter by a minimal criterion and rank by novelty, then transfer agents between environments.
  • In the toy, direct training solved 0 regions. Novelty ranking and the minimal criterion each mattered. Transfer did not pay for itself, because the toy has no deceptive structure to exploit. The original paper reports transfer as essential in its walker domain.
  • A process is open-ended if it keeps producing outputs that are novel and learnable to an observer. Noise is novel but unlearnable; a converged optimiser is learnable but not novel.
  • Open-endedness is observer-relative and shown only over finite runs; progress is tracked with measures such as ANNECS.
  • POET's weakness is its hand-designed environment space and distance; replacing both with a model of what is interesting is the next step.

Check your understanding

In the toy POET, training directly on environments at distance 8 solved none, while POET reached much farther. What is the best explanation?

  1. Direct training used a smaller budget than POET.
  2. The agents in direct training were too small to represent the solution.
  3. The reward is exactly zero away from the target, so the optimiser has no signal to follow until it is close; POET reaches far environments through a chain of near ones.
  4. POET uses a better optimiser than the ES used for direct training.
Both used the same budget and the same ES step. The agent can represent any point; the problem is a flat, zero reward away from the needle. POET gives the optimiser a nearby target at every stage, so there is always a gradient. The optimiser itself is the same (option 4 is wrong).

What does the minimal criterion in POET protect against?

  1. Admitting environments the inherited agent finds either impossible (no learning signal) or trivial (nothing to learn).
  2. Admitting environments that are too similar to existing ones.
  3. Agents overfitting to their own environment.
  4. Transfers that make an agent worse than before.
The criterion is a lower and an upper bound on the inherited agent's score in the child environment. Similarity to existing environments (option 2) is handled by the novelty ranking, a separate step. Option 4 is handled by the replace-only-if-better rule in transfer.

In the ablation, removing transfer slightly increased the toy's coverage. What should you conclude?

  1. Transfer is useless and POET should drop it.
  2. The experiment is invalid and should be discarded.
  3. Transfer is only needed with a larger budget.
  4. In this toy there is nothing to transfer, so transfer only costs evaluations; the result says little about domains with real stepping stones.
Every toy environment is a smooth needle that its parent's agent already reaches, so swapping agents gains nothing and costs budget. The original authors report transfer as essential in their walker domain, so option 1 over-generalises. Option 3 is untested speculation, and the experiment is valid for what it shows (option 2).

A text generator produces uniformly random characters forever. By the novelty-and-learnability definition, is it open-ended?

  1. Yes, because it never repeats itself.
  2. No, because although each output is surprising, more history never helps an observer predict it, so it is not learnable.
  3. Yes, because the observer is always surprised.
  4. No, because open-ended processes must be deterministic.
Noise is the top-left cell of the grid: novel and unlearnable. Surprise alone is not enough; the observer must be able to do better with more history. Determinism is irrelevant (option 4); the growing stream in the chapter used a seeded random generator and still passed.

A team claims their self-improving system is open-ended because its score has risen every week for six months. What is the strongest objection?

  1. Six months is too short for any system to be called open-ended.
  2. Scores can never measure progress.
  3. A rising score on a fixed objective shows optimisation, not that the system keeps producing artefacts that are novel and learnable to an observer.
  4. Open-ended systems must stop improving eventually.
A converged-looking optimiser can still creep upwards on a fixed score; that is the repetition cell. The claim needs a statement of what is new and who is learning it (for instance, ANNECS-style counts of new, solved problems). Duration alone (option 1) is not the issue, and option 4 gets the idea backwards.

In POET, only environments whose agent has reached a score threshold are allowed to reproduce. Why?

  1. To avoid spending compute mutating problems that are currently too hard for any agent, and to keep new environments near what the population can already do.
  2. To make sure every environment is eventually solved perfectly.
  3. To keep the population size constant.
  4. To prevent two agents from ever being tested on the same environment.
Reproducing from solved environments yields children close to the frontier of current ability, which is where learning happens. Population size is controlled separately (retiring the oldest), and nothing guarantees perfect solutions (option 2).
