Evolutionary Algorithms

Evolution is a way to search for good answers. It needs no gradients, no model of the problem and no understanding of why a solution works. It keeps a group of candidates and scores them. The better ones have offspring, and the offspring are changed a little. This chapter builds that loop from its parts and shows what happens when selection is too strong. It then follows the loop into evolution strategies, which are now used to train neural networks.

Why this matters

Evolutionary search is the engine inside most of this course. Later chapters use it to evolve programs (chapter 9), prompts (chapter 11) and agent designs (chapter 12). They also use it in a self-improving coding agent called the Darwin Gödel Machine (chapter 13). All of these are populations, scores, selection and variation, with a language model supplying the variation.

Suppose you understand why a population collapses, why mutation rate matters and what an evaluation budget buys. Then those systems stop being mysterious. You also get a practical tool. An evolutionary method is often the shortest route to a decent answer in three cases. The problem may not be differentiable. Evaluations may be the only thing you can afford to query. Or the search space may be awkward, as with programs, schedules and prompts.

Evolution as a search procedure

Charles Darwin's argument needs only three ingredients, and each is easy to state without any biology. There is variation: individuals differ. There is selection: some differences make an individual more likely to leave offspring. And there is inheritance: offspring resemble their parents. If all three hold, a population drifts toward whatever the environment rewards, without anything in the system understanding the environment.

An evolutionary algorithm (EA) copies this recipe to optimise things. You choose a representation: a way to write down a candidate solution as a data structure called the genome. It might be a bit string, a list of numbers, a tree or a program. You define a fitness function that scores a genome, higher being better. Then you repeat a loop on a population of genomes. The names come from biology, but the mapping is concrete:

Evolutionary methods have a long history. Holland's Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems (1975) founded the genetic algorithm. Rechenberg (1973) and Schwefel developed evolution strategies in Germany for engineering design. Koza extended the idea to evolving programs in the early 1990s.

A famous engineering result came from Hornby, Lohn and Linden ("Computer-automated evolution of an X-band antenna for NASA's Space Technology 5 mission", Evolutionary Computation, 2011). Space Technology 5 (ST5) was a spacecraft mission of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). The authors describe evolved antennas for the ST5 spacecraft, launched in March 2006. In their tests these outperformed the contractor's hand-designed antenna. They are described as the first computer-evolved hardware in space. Nobody designed the odd, bent-wire shape; selection found it.

A first genetic algorithm

A genetic algorithm (GA) is an EA whose genome is a fixed-length string, usually of bits. We apply one to a classic problem: the 0/1 knapsack. There are 40 items with weights and values (here random, seeded), and a weight limit equal to 40% of the total weight. Choose items to maximise total value without exceeding the limit. The exact answer is computable by dynamic programming, so we can check how close evolution gets. In your own work, finding the optimum exactly is usually impossible; here it lets us measure.

The program below uses four operators.

  • Tournament selection: draw k individuals at random, and the fittest becomes a parent.
  • One-point crossover: pick a cut position, then take the head from one parent and the tail from the other.
  • Bit-flip mutation: flip each bit with probability 1/40, so on average one flip per child.
  • Elitism: copy the best two individuals unchanged into the next generation, so progress is never lost.
Step through

One generation of a genetic algorithm

  1. Evaluate. Four genomes of eight bits. To keep the arithmetic visible, fitness is just the number of 1 bits: 4, 5, 2 and 6. Average 4.25. Filled squares are 1s.
  2. Select. Four tournaments of size 2 pick parents. P3 loses to P1 (2 against 4), and P2 loses to P4 (5 against 6). P2 beats P3 (5 against 2), and P4 beats P1 (6 against 4). The parents are P1, P4, P2, P4. Notice that P4 was picked twice and P3 never. The weakest genome gets no offspring.
  3. Pair and cut. Parents pair up in order. Pair one is cut after bit 3, pair two after bit 5, and each pair swaps tails.
  4. Children. From P1 and P4: 01111011 and 10101001. From P2 and P4: 11100011 and 10111110. Each child mixes the ancestors' good halves, but also their bad halves.
  5. Mutate. A bit flip hits child 2 (position 4) and child 3 (position 5), both turning a 0 into a 1. Mutation here helped, but equally often a flip would hurt; it is the source of new variation that crossover cannot create from existing bits.
  6. New generation. The new population scores 6, 5, 6 and 6: average 5.75, up from 4.25. In a real run, elitism would also copy the best parents across so the best score can never fall.

Here is the full program. It is about 60 lines of standard-library Python. The final lines report the average best fitness over 20 seeded runs, at several generations. They also compare against random search given exactly the same number of fitness evaluations.

import random

# A 40-item knapsack: pick items to maximise value within a weight limit.
rng = random.Random(7)
N = 40
W = [rng.randint(5, 40) for _ in range(N)]
V = [rng.randint(10, 100) for _ in range(N)]
CAP = sum(W) * 2 // 5

PENALTY = 25                                             # per unit of overweight

def fitness(g):
    w = sum(W[i] for i in range(N) if g[i]); v = sum(V[i] for i in range(N) if g[i])
    return v if w <= CAP else v - PENALTY * (w - CAP)          # penalty for overweight

def optimum():                                           # dynamic programming, exact
    best = [0] * (CAP + 1)
    for w, v in zip(W, V):
        for c in range(CAP, w - 1, -1):
            best[c] = max(best[c], best[c - w] + v)
    return best[CAP]

def tournament(pop, fits, k, rng):
    return max(rng.sample(range(len(pop)), k), key=lambda i: fits[i])

def evolve(seed, pop_size=60, gens=100, k=3, p_cross=0.8, p_mut=1 / N, elite=2):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pop = [[int(rng.random() < 0.15) for _ in range(N)] for _ in range(pop_size)]
    history = []
    for gen in range(gens + 1):
        fits = [fitness(g) for g in pop]
        history.append(max(fits))
        order = sorted(range(pop_size), key=lambda i: -fits[i])
        nxt = [pop[i][:] for i in order[:elite]]         # elitism: the best survive untouched
        while len(nxt) < pop_size:
            a, b = pop[tournament(pop, fits, k, rng)], pop[tournament(pop, fits, k, rng)]
            if rng.random() < p_cross:                    # one-point crossover
                cut = rng.randrange(1, N); a = a[:cut] + b[cut:]
            else:
                a = a[:]
            nxt.append([1 - x if rng.random() < p_mut else x for x in a])   # bit-flip mutation
        pop = nxt
    evolve.last_pop = pop
    return history

def random_search(seed, evals):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return max(fitness([int(rng.random() < 0.15) for _ in range(N)]) for _ in range(evals))

if __name__ == "__main__":
    opt = optimum()
    print(f"exact optimum (dynamic programming): {opt}")
    runs = [evolve(s) for s in range(20)]
    for gen in (0, 10, 30, 100):
        mean = sum(r[gen] for r in runs) / len(runs)
        print(f"GA generation {gen:3d}: best fitness {mean:6.1f}  ({mean / opt:5.1%} of optimum)")
    evals = 60 * 101
    rs = sum(random_search(s, evals) for s in range(20)) / 20
    def feasible_gap():
        # how good is a solution that the weak penalty (4 per unit) would score at 1.0 or more?
        global PENALTY
        PENALTY = 4
        h = [evolve(s)[-1] for s in range(20)]
        PENALTY = 25
        return sum(h) / len(h)
    print(f"random search, same {evals} evaluations: {rs:6.1f}  ({rs / opt:5.1%} of optimum)")
    print(f"with a weak penalty of 4 per unit, the GA's 'best' averages {feasible_gap():.0f}, above the true optimum: it learned to cheat")

GA generation   0: best fitness  676.7  (43.0% of optimum)
GA generation  10: best fitness 1318.2  (83.9% of optimum)
GA generation  30: best fitness 1533.4  (97.5% of optimum)
GA generation 100: best fitness 1568.1  (99.8% of optimum)
random search, same 6060 evaluations:  921.0  (58.6% of optimum)

After 100 generations the GA is at 99.8% of the true optimum, using 6,060 evaluations out of a space of 240 (about 1012) genomes. Random search with the same budget reaches 58.6%. Most of the progress is early: 84% by generation 10, 97.5% by generation 30. The last 2% costs as much as the first 84%. You will see this pattern in every search in this course.

The last line of output deserves attention. With a weak overweight penalty (4 points per unit), the GA's "best" fitness averages 1575, above the true optimum of 1572. That is impossible for a feasible plan. The population found plans that break the weight limit but still score well. For the highest-ratio items, each extra unit of weight is worth more than 4 points of value. The GA did what it was told, which was to maximise the number the function returns. A search loop will find every gap between what you scored and what you meant. Chapter 15 is devoted to this, and it starts here, in a 40-item toy.

Selection pressure and diversity

Selection pressure is how strongly the algorithm favours the fittest. In tournament selection it is set by the tournament size k. With k = 1 there is no pressure at all: parents are chosen at random and the population drifts. With k = 2 the better of two wins; with k = 20 in a population of 60 almost only the top few ever reproduce.

Pressure trades speed against diversity, the amount of genetic difference within the population. High pressure converges quickly, because good genomes spread fast. But then everyone descends from a few ancestors. The population has no variety left to explore alternatives, and it can settle on a mediocre solution and stay there. This failure is called premature convergence. Mutation is the only thing that adds variety back, which is why the two settings interact.

The experiment below sweeps tournament size with mutation on and off, over 30 seeded runs each. It measures diversity as the average Hamming distance between random pairs of genomes, as a fraction of genome length. The Hamming distance is the number of bits that differ.

import random
from ga import evolve, optimum, N

def diversity(pop):
    """Average Hamming distance between 200 random pairs, as a fraction of the genome length."""
    r = random.Random(1)
    d = 0
    for _ in range(200):
        a, b = r.sample(pop, 2)
        d += sum(x != y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    return d / 200 / N

opt = optimum()
print("tournament size k, mutation rate -> best fitness after 40 generations, final diversity (mean of 30 runs)")
for p_mut, label in ((1 / N, "mutation 1/N"), (0.0, "no mutation")):
    for k in (1, 2, 3, 7, 20):
        best = div = 0
        for s in range(30):
            h = evolve(s, gens=40, k=k, p_mut=p_mut)
            best += h[-1] / 30; div += diversity(evolve.last_pop) / 30
        print(f"  {label:13s} k={k:2d}: best {best / opt:6.1%} of optimum, diversity {div:5.1%}")

Three observations from the numbers.

  • Mutation rescues the search. At k = 3 it takes the mean best from 75% of the optimum to 99%.
  • The best results come at moderate pressure (k = 2 to 7). With k = 20 the population loses diversity and a little quality. With k = 1 the search wastes its evaluations on random wandering (84%).
  • Even a mutation rate as low as one flip per child keeps about 5% diversity alive at k = 20.

Real systems add mechanisms to protect diversity. These include niching, population islands that rarely exchange members, and explicit novelty bonuses. The last is the subject of chapter 3.

Thread: bin packing

Here the genome is a small priority function, and its fitness is the number of bins it uses over a set of instances (lower is better). Start a population from first fit, best fit and their random variants. If selection is strong, every survivor soon becomes a small edit of best fit, and the population has lost its variety. Mutation is what lets a child try a different scoring rule. Score every candidate on the same instances, or fitness differences will be partly luck. Common misconception

"Evolution finds the global optimum if you run it long enough." There is no such guarantee for any realistic setting. A GA is a heuristic, which means it concentrates effort where earlier samples did well. If the best region is narrow and surrounded by poor ones, the population will usually be somewhere else. The standard practices are three. Run several independent restarts. Keep mutation high enough to leave local hills. Measure results over many seeds, not one lucky run.

Variation: mutation and crossover

Mutation

Mutation supplies new genetic material. How big a step it takes is the key design decision. If it is too small, the population explores slowly or stalls on a local hill. If it is too large, children are no better than random genomes, so inheritance stops mattering. A common rule of thumb for bit strings is about one flip per genome (rate 1/length), as above. For real-valued genomes, mutation adds Gaussian noise. Its standard deviation is the step size, and later in this chapter we let the algorithm adapt it.

The right mutation is as much a representation question as a rate question. For a timetable, swapping two events keeps the timetable valid. Flipping a bit in a naive encoding usually breaks it. For a program, a sensible mutation changes one expression, not one character. For a prompt, it rewrites a sentence. In chapter 9 a language model plays this role, proposing mutations that stay inside the space of plausible programs. That is the main reason LLMs and evolution combine so well. Evolution needs a mutation operator that usually produces something sensible, and a language model is one.

Crossover

Crossover combines two parents. Its attraction is that two individuals which each solved a different part of the problem can pass both solutions on to one child. This is the building block hypothesis (Holland 1975, popularised by Goldberg 1989): good genomes are assembled from short, high-quality fragments, and crossover mixes fragments. It holds well for problems whose parts are separable, such as choosing independent projects or tuning independent knobs.

The hypothesis has been disputed for decades, and the honest status is "sometimes". Take genomes whose parts interact strongly. A fragment that is excellent in one parent's context can be harmful in another's. The first half of a good aircraft wing paired with the second half of another is rarely a wing. For permutations such as routes and schedules, naive one-point crossover produces invalid children and needs special operators.

Many successful systems use mutation only. Rechenberg's evolution strategies began that way. The open-source program evolvers in chapter 10 mostly rely on mutation by a language model, with occasional recombination of ideas. The rule of thumb is to try mutation-only first. Add crossover if the problem has separable pieces, and test whether it helps by removing it (an ablation).

Analogy: the seed-saving gardener

A gardener grows 60 tomato plants, saves seeds from the three tastiest, plants those next year, and repeats. Seeds from two good plants may carry a good trait from each (crossover). A random seed will sometimes differ from its parents (mutation). After a few seasons the tomatoes are better, although nobody calculated anything about tomato genetics. Now suppose the gardener saves seed from only the single best plant for ten years. The crop becomes uniform, and one blight can wipe it out. That is selection pressure without diversity.

Where it breaks down: the gardener gets variation and heredity from biology. In software you have to design both. A poor representation or mutation operator gives children that are unrelated to their parents, so selection has nothing to act on. Or it gives children that are all invalid, so nothing survives. The gardener's tomatoes never fail to be tomatoes.

Evolution strategies: evolving real numbers

Evolution strategies (ES) are the real-valued branch of the family. The genome is a vector of numbers, such as the parameters of a controller or a neural network. The main operator is Gaussian mutation.

Two classic forms are written (μ + λ) and (μ, λ). Both keep μ parents and produce λ children. In the plus form, parents compete with the children. In the comma form, the next parents are chosen only from the children. The simplest is (1 + 1)-ES, with one parent and one child. The child replaces the parent if it is better.

Adapting the step size

The weak spot is the step size σ. Rechenberg's 1/5 success rule adapts it from experience. If more than one fifth of recent mutations were improvements, steps are too timid, so grow σ. If fewer, they are too bold, so shrink it. The intuition is geometric. Near an optimum most large random steps overshoot, so a high success rate means you are far from it and could afford to move faster.

Later methods adapt not only the size but the whole shape of the sampling distribution. CMA-ES learns which directions are worth stepping in. Its name comes from covariance matrix adaptation (CMA) evolution strategy (Hansen and Ostermeier, 2001). It remains a strong default for problems with up to a few hundred real parameters. Chapter 20 builds it from scratch.

ES as a gradient estimator

There is a second, neater view. Imagine perturbing the parameters θ by Gaussian noise σε and averaging the resulting fitness. This smoothed fitness is differentiable even if the original function is not, and its gradient has a simple sample estimate:

Plug in numbers. One parameter, a fitness that peaks at 3, and five hand-picked noise draws, then more draws.

The idea

  1. theta = 0, sigma = 0.5, F(x) = −(x − 3)², noise draws eps_i
  2. per draw: point ← theta + sigma × eps; f ← F(point); vote ← f × eps
  3. estimate ← (sum of votes) ÷ (n × sigma)
  4. repeat with random draws, n = 5, 100, 10000: true slope is +6
"""The evolution-strategies gradient estimate by hand, in one dimension.
Fitness F(theta) = -(theta - 3)^2 peaks at 3, so at theta = 0 the true
slope of the smoothed fitness is +6 (uphill is to the right)."""
import random

# ── 1. The problem and the noise ─────────────────────────────────────────
THETA = 0.0                  # current parameter
SIGMA = 0.5                  # size of the perturbations
EPS = [-1.2, 0.4, 0.9, -0.3, 1.5]    # five hand-picked draws from N(0, 1)


def F(theta):
    """Fitness: higher is better, best at theta = 3."""
    return -(theta - 3) ** 2


# ── 2. Perturb, score, and weight each score by its own noise ────────────
print("  eps  theta+sigma*eps    F    F*eps")
total = 0.0
for e in EPS:
    f = F(THETA + SIGMA * e)
    total += f * e
    print(f"{e:5.1f} {THETA + SIGMA * e:12.2f} {f:8.2f} {f * e:8.2f}")

# ── 3. Average over the n draws and divide by sigma ──────────────────────
n = len(EPS)
print(f"sum of F*eps = {total:.2f}")
print(f"estimate = {total:.2f} / ({n} * {SIGMA}) = {total / (n * SIGMA):.2f}")

# ── 4. More draws: the noisy estimate settles near the true slope of 6 ───
rng = random.Random(0)
for n in (5, 100, 10000):
    draws = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(n)]
    est = sum(F(THETA + SIGMA * e) * e for e in draws) / (n * SIGMA)
    print(f"n = {n:5d}: estimate = {est:6.2f}")

In words: draw n random perturbations, score each, and move θ toward the perturbations that scored well, away from those that scored badly. That is the natural-ES update used by Salimans et al. ("Evolution Strategies as a Scalable Alternative to Reinforcement Learning", OpenAI, 2017). Workers need only exchange scalar scores when the random seeds are shared. Their abstract reports that the method therefore scaled to over a thousand parallel workers. It solved 3D humanoid walking in 10 minutes, with Atari results competitive after an hour of training.

Such et al. ("Deep Neuroevolution", Uber AI, 2017) showed that even a plain genetic algorithm could train networks with over four million parameters on Atari. Neither result beat gradient-based reinforcement learning across the board. The point was that gradient-free search was far more competitive than expected, and that it is easy to parallelise.

How good is this estimate? The script below runs a (1+1)-ES with the 1/5 rule on a 20-dimensional bowl. It then checks the gradient estimator against the true gradient of a 100-dimensional bowl, for increasing population sizes.

import numpy as np

def sphere(x): return float(np.sum(x ** 2))             # minimise; optimum is 0 at the origin

# 1. (1+1)-ES with Rechenberg's 1/5 success rule
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=20) * 3
fx, sigma, evals, successes = sphere(x), 1.0, 0, 0
for step in range(1, 4001):
    child = x + sigma * rng.normal(size=20)
    fc = sphere(child); evals += 1
    if fc < fx:
        x, fx = child, fc; successes += 1
    if step % 20 == 0:                                   # every 20 steps, adapt the step size
        sigma *= 1.5 if successes / 20 > 0.2 else 0.82
        successes = 0
    if step in (1, 200, 1000, 4000):
        print(f"(1+1)-ES step {step:4d}: f = {fx:10.3e}, sigma = {sigma:8.2e}")

# 2. Evolution strategies as a gradient estimator: grad ~ 1/(n*sigma) * sum f(x + sigma*eps) * eps
d, sigma = 100, 0.1
theta = np.ones(d)
true_grad = 2 * theta
print("\ncosine between the ES gradient estimate and the true gradient (d = 100)")
for n in (10, 100, 1000, 10000):
    cos = []
    for trial in range(20):
        eps = rng.normal(size=(n, d))
        scores = np.array([sphere(theta + sigma * e) for e in eps])
        scores = scores - scores.mean()                  # baseline: subtract the mean score
        est = (scores[:, None] * eps).sum(axis=0) / (n * sigma)
        cos.append(est @ true_grad / (np.linalg.norm(est) * np.linalg.norm(true_grad)))
    print(f"  population {n:6d}: cosine {np.mean(cos):.3f}   (theory ~ {np.sqrt(n / (n + d)):.3f})")

The step-size rule works as intended. σ fell from 1.0 to about 10−12 as the search closed in on the optimum. The fitness reached 10−22 after 4,000 evaluations.

The gradient estimator is the more instructive result. In 100 dimensions the cosine between the estimate and the true gradient is only 0.27 with 10 samples, 0.70 with 100 and 0.96 with 1,000. It closely follows the rule of thumb √(n / (n + d)). The consequence is general: the number of evaluations needed to point in a useful direction grows with the number of parameters.

Backpropagation gets the exact gradient of all parameters from one backward pass (A0 chapter 9). ES pays hundreds or thousands of forward evaluations for a noisy one. That is why you do not use ES for a model you can differentiate. You use it in three cases. You cannot differentiate, as with a simulator, a game score, a human rating or a test-suite result. Or the reward arrives only after a very long episode. Or you have a huge number of cheap workers.

There is also a "no free lunch" caution. Wolpert and Macready (1997) proved that, averaged over all possible problems, no search algorithm beats any other. That does not make evolution useless on your problem. It means an EA is good exactly to the extent that its assumptions match the problem. Those assumptions are that similar genomes have similar fitness and that good fragments recombine. Choose the representation and operators so that they do.

State of play, October 2026

Evolutionary methods appear in modern AI in three places. First, as black-box optimisers for hyperparameters and architectures: Real et al. (2019) used a form of tournament evolution ("regularized evolution") to find image classifier architectures that matched or beat hand-designed ones at the time. Second, in evolutionary model merging, which recombines the weights of existing language models and is covered in A3 chapter 16. Third, as the outer loop of LLM-guided program and prompt search, the topic of Part 3 of this course. Tools and benchmark results in this area change monthly, so treat any specific system named in a later chapter as a snapshot.

Systems connection

Scoring a population is embarrassingly parallel. Each genome's evaluation is independent, so you can fan them out to workers, as in any parallel workload. The catch is the tail. A generation finishes only when its slowest evaluation does, so one stuck simulator stalls the whole population. This is the same fan-out amplification known from tail latency.

Practical evolvers use three remedies. They set timeouts. They use asynchronous steady-state replacement, where a child replaces an individual as soon as it is scored, with no generation barrier. And they use seeded random streams so a run can be replayed exactly (A0 chapter 4).

What goes wrong

  • Premature convergence. Pressure too high, mutation too low, population too small. Symptoms: diversity near zero, fitness flat. Fixes: bigger population, lower k, higher mutation, restarts, islands.
  • Noisy fitness. If the same genome can score differently on different evaluations, a lucky evaluation can crown a poor genome. Re-evaluate the elites, average several evaluations, or compare with enough samples. The statistics are in chapter 16.
  • Exploited fitness function. The weak-penalty knapsack above. Anything the scorer does not penalise, the population will eventually use.
  • Bloat and drift. In variable-size genomes (programs) selection often favours growth with no benefit. Add a size penalty or a size limit.
  • Evaluation cost. 6,000 evaluations is nothing for a knapsack and everything for a training run that takes an hour. Budget first; choose the method second.
  • Deception. Sometimes following fitness uphill leads away from the best solution. That is the next chapter.

Key takeaways

  • An evolutionary algorithm needs a genome, a fitness function, selection and variation. The loop is the same for bit strings, vectors, programs and prompts.
  • On a 40-item knapsack the GA reached 99.8% of the true optimum with 6,060 evaluations; random search with the same budget reached 58.6%.
  • Selection pressure speeds convergence and destroys diversity. Without mutation, diversity fell to about zero by generation 40 for every k of 2 or more.
  • Moderate pressure, adequate mutation and several seeds beat any single tuned run. Crossover helps when parts are separable; test it, do not assume it.
  • Evolution strategies evolve real vectors and adapt their step size. They can be read as noisy gradient estimators, whose quality grows with the square root of population over parameters.
  • A search loop exploits any gap in the fitness function. A weak penalty let the GA beat the true optimum by cheating.

Check your understanding

Which of these is NOT something an evolutionary algorithm needs from the problem?

  1. A way to represent a candidate as a genome
  2. A fitness function that scores a candidate
  3. The gradient of the fitness function
  4. A way to make small changes to a candidate
Evolution is useful precisely because it needs only scores, not gradients. The other three (representation, fitness, variation) are the minimum ingredients.

In the selection experiment, a GA with no mutation and tournament size 7 ended with diversity of 0.0% and about 71% of the optimum. What is the best explanation?

  1. Selection copied the same few genomes until nothing differed, and without mutation nothing new could appear
  2. Tournament size 7 selects at random
  3. Crossover produces invalid children
  4. The population was too large to converge
With no mutation, the only variation is what exists in the initial population; selection and crossover can only shuffle it, and the population collapses to copies. Size 7 is strong, not random (random selection is k = 1).

A GA on a scheduling problem with a weak penalty for rule violations reports a "better than optimal" schedule. What most likely happened?

  1. The optimum was computed wrongly
  2. The random seed was lucky
  3. Crossover repaired the schedule
  4. The population found schedules that violate rules but score well under the penalty
Exceeding the true optimum is impossible for a feasible solution, so the answers must be infeasible ones that the penalty undercharges. This is the knapsack episode in the chapter. Raise the penalty above the best gain per unit of violation, or make infeasible genomes unrepresentable.

In the (1+1)-ES with the 1/5 success rule, a run of mutations where about half succeed suggests what?

  1. The step size is too large, so shrink it
  2. The step size is too small, so grow it
  3. The population is too diverse
  4. Crossover should be switched on
A high success rate means steps are timid: you are far from an optimum and could move faster. A very low rate means steps overshoot. There is no population or crossover in a (1+1)-ES.

An evolution strategy gradient estimate in 100 dimensions has cosine 0.27 with the true gradient at population 10. Roughly how large a population gives a cosine near 0.7?

  1. About 20
  2. About 50
  3. About 100
  4. About 10,000
The measured cosine at 100 was 0.70, matching the rule of thumb √(n/(n+d)) = √(100/200) = 0.71. Ten thousand gives 0.995, far more than needed; 20 or 50 would give about 0.41 and 0.58.

When is an evolutionary or ES method more attractive than gradient descent for tuning a system?

  1. The score comes from a black-box simulator or test run and cannot be differentiated
  2. You have a differentiable loss and a small dataset
  3. You need the exact gradient for a mathematical proof
  4. You have one worker and an expensive evaluation
Black-box scoring is the natural home of evolution. With a differentiable loss, backpropagation is far cheaper per useful direction. One worker with expensive evaluations is the worst case for a population method, because it needs many evaluations per generation.
