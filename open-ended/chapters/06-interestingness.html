Models of Interestingness

The previous chapter's algorithm could tell that a new problem was different, but not that it was worth solving. A foundation model, trained on a vast amount of human writing, can make a rough judgement of what people find interesting. This chapter shows how one system uses that judgement to choose what an agent learns next. It shows how a second system lets a model also write the environments. It also shows where the idea breaks.

Why this matters

Any system that picks its own next task has to answer “what is worth learning?”. Get it wrong in one direction and the agent practises thousands of trivial variants of something it already knows. Get it wrong in the other and it bangs against tasks that are too hard to give any signal. Hand-written rules cover a narrow domain and then run out.

After this chapter you can explain why learnable is not the same as worth learning. You can describe how a judge model and a learning-progress signal combine into a curriculum, and reproduce the effect in a small deterministic simulation. You can also name the failure modes that you must test for: the judge being gamed, the judge being wrong, and the generated tasks being broken. The same question returns whenever a language model is asked to rank ideas, as in Voyager's curriculum and the idea-ranking steps of automated science.

The problem: learnable but boring

The previous chapter's curricula had two filters: a problem must be solvable by the current agent but not trivially so (the minimal criterion). It must also be unlike earlier problems (a distance in some parameter space). Both are mechanical. Neither knows what the problem is about.

Consider an agent in a crafting game. It has learned to “collect 1 wood”. A task generator now offers “collect 2 wood”, “collect 3 wood”, and so on up to 90. Each of these is learnable. Each is different from the previous one by any parameter distance. Each is easy at first, so each shows quick improvement. Measured by learning progress (how fast the agent's success rate on a task is changing), they look excellent. An agent steered by learning progress alone will happily spend its budget there. A person would call them one task, not ninety. What they would call interesting is “make a table”, a task of a different kind that opens new possibilities.

That judgement is the gap. In the paper that introduced OMNI (Zhang, Lehman, Stanley and Clune, 2023, “OMNI: Open-endedness via Models of human Notions of Interestingness”) the problem is stated as follows. Many learnable tasks are uninteresting, such as minor variations of tasks already mastered. The proposed remedy is to ask a model.

A model of interestingness

The central claim is that a foundation model (a large pretrained language model; see The Language-Modelling Objective) has absorbed human judgements about what is interesting. Humans wrote the text it was trained on. It reflects what they bothered to describe, what they called a breakthrough, what they dismissed as routine. So the model can be used as a model of interestingness (MoI). It is shown what the agent has learned and failed so far, plus a list of candidate tasks, and is asked which candidates are interesting. OMNI combines this with learnability to choose tasks.

  • Learnability is measured from experience. For every task, OMNI's curriculum tracks two exponential moving averages of the agent's success rate. One tracks recent attempts and one tracks the longer run. It samples tasks whose success is changing the most. The paper describes this as an extension of the learning-progress curriculum of Kanitscheider et al. (2021), with success rates normalised against a random-action policy. A task that is flat at zero (too hard) or flat at one (mastered) has no learning progress and is not chosen.
  • Interestingness is judged by the foundation model. It is prompted with a few examples in two of the environments (few-shot) and with none in the third (zero-shot). Given the agent's current successes and failures, it labels tasks as interesting or not. Boring tasks have their sampling weight multiplied by 0.001, which pushes them to the bottom without deleting them.

Analogy: an editor with taste

A magazine commissions articles. One filter is practical: can this writer deliver this piece by the deadline? Another is taste: is this worth printing, given what we ran last month? A magazine with only the first filter fills itself with competent repetition. One with only the second commissions brilliant pieces nobody can write. An editor with both is a model of interestingness plus a learnability check. Foundation models are a bit like an editor who has read most of what has been published. Their taste is the average of what was published.

Where it breaks down: an editor is accountable to readers, who vote with their attention. The model has no readers in the loop, and its taste can drift or be exploited without anyone noticing.

What OMNI found

The authors tested OMNI in three environments of increasing openness. In Crafter (a 2D game modelled on Minecraft, with a tech tree) they removed survival, leaving 15 genuinely interesting tasks. They diluted these with 90 boring tasks (numerical repeats) and 1,023 extremely challenging ones. In BabyAI (a grid world for grounded language instructions) they extended instructions to five steps, giving 1,364 tasks. In AI2-THOR (a 3D kitchen from the Allen Institute for AI (AI2)) the task space is effectively infinite. So GPT-4, a Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) model, also proposed new tasks and wrote code to check whether each had been completed. The paper reports the number of tasks the agent learned by the end of training, with ranges across seeds.

These are tasks learned, as reported in the paper (OMNI, arXiv 2306.01711). I read the table from the paper's text, not from the figures. In Crafter, OMNI learned 14 of the 15 interesting tasks. In Crafter and BabyAI the authors report it was nearly indistinguishable from an oracle that labels interesting tasks perfectly. The foundation model was GPT-3 (Davinci) in Crafter and GPT-4 in BabyAI and AI2-THOR. Note what the table does not say. It does not say that the agent became generally capable, or that the model's notion of interesting matched any particular person's. It says only this: with this judge, in these three environments, the curriculum learned more tasks of the kind a person would care about. The authors themselves list the limits. The first version estimates learning progress statistically rather than by model judgement. The boring tasks in the main experiments are synthetic numerical variants. The approach needs substantial compute.

The effect, in a small simulation

The simulation below reproduces the shape of the Crafter experiment without any model call. The pool has 405 tasks, of three kinds. First, 15 interesting ones are arranged in a tech tree. Collecting wood unlocks a table, which unlocks a pickaxe, and so on. A task can only be practised successfully once its prerequisites are learned. Second, there are 90 boring variants (“collect 2 wood” to “collect 91 wood”), unlocked by the first task and slightly easier to learn. Third, there are 300 impossible tasks that never improve.

Three sampling rules choose what to practise in each of 8,000 steps. The first is uniform. The second is learning progress only: the gap between a fast and a slow moving average of success. The third is learning progress with the judge, in which tasks the judge calls boring have their weight cut by a factor of 1,000.

The judge is a stand-in. A rule, not a model. It calls a task boring if it is only a numeric variant (“collect # wood”) of a different task the agent has already learned. A real run would send the learned and failed tasks and the candidates to a language model and parse its answer. This rule is deterministic so that the numbers reproduce.

import random, re
from collections import Counter

# task pool: 15 interesting tasks in a tech tree, 90 boring numeric variants, 300 impossible ones
TREE = [("collect wood", []), ("place table", [0]), ("make wood pickaxe", [1]), ("collect stone", [2]),
        ("place furnace", [3]), ("make stone pickaxe", [2, 3]), ("collect coal", [2]), ("collect iron", [5]),
        ("make iron pickaxe", [4, 6, 7]), ("collect diamond", [8]), ("eat plant", [0]), ("make wood sword", [1]),
        ("make stone sword", [3, 11]), ("defeat zombie", [11]), ("sleep in shelter", [3])]
TASKS = ([dict(text=t, pre=p, rate=0.10, kind="interesting") for t, p in TREE]
         + [dict(text=f"collect {n} wood", pre=[0], rate=0.15, kind="boring") for n in range(2, 92)]
         + [dict(text=f"build monument {n}", pre=[], rate=0.0, kind="impossible") for n in range(300)])
shape = lambda text: re.sub(r"\d+", "#", text)               # "collect 7 wood" -> "collect # wood"
SHAPE = [shape(t["text"]) for t in TASKS]

def judge_interesting(i, learned, seen):
    """Deterministic stand-in for a foundation model's judgement. A real model would be shown the
    tasks the agent has learned and failed and asked which candidates are interesting. This rule
    calls a task boring if it is only a numeric variant of a different task already learned."""
    return seen[SHAPE[i]] - (i in learned) == 0               # no other learned task has the same shape

def run(method, seed, steps=8_000):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n = len(TASKS)
    p = [0.0] * n                                            # true success probability of each task
    fast, slow = [0.0] * n, [0.0] * n                        # two moving averages of observed success
    for _ in range(steps):
        if method == "uniform":
            w = [1.0] * n
        else:
            w = [abs(fast[i] - slow[i]) + 0.002 for i in range(n)]            # learning progress
            if method == "LP + interestingness":
                learned = {j for j in range(n) if p[j] >= 0.5}
                seen = Counter(SHAPE[j] for j in learned)
                w = [x if judge_interesting(i, learned, seen) else x * 0.001 for i, x in enumerate(w)]
        i = rng.choices(range(n), weights=w)[0]
        if all(p[j] >= 0.5 for j in TASKS[i]["pre"]):                       # practice only helps if prerequisites are met
            p[i] += TASKS[i]["rate"] * (1 - p[i])
        success = rng.random() < p[i]
        fast[i] += 0.3 * (success - fast[i]); slow[i] += 0.05 * (success - slow[i])
    inter = sum(p[i] >= 0.8 for i in range(n) if TASKS[i]["kind"] == "interesting")
    return inter, sum(x >= 0.8 for x in p)

print(f"{len(TASKS)} tasks: 15 interesting, 90 boring variants, 300 impossible; 8,000 practice steps, mean of 5 seeds")
print(f"{'sampling rule':24s} {'interesting learned (of 15)':>28s} {'all tasks learned':>18s}")
for m in ("uniform", "learning progress only", "LP + interestingness"):
    r = [run(m, s) for s in range(5)]
    print(f"{m:24s} {sum(x[0] for x in r)/5:28.1f} {sum(x[1] for x in r)/5:18.1f}")
405 tasks: 15 interesting, 90 boring variants, 300 impossible; 8,000 practice steps, mean of 5 seeds
sampling rule             interesting learned (of 15)  all tasks learned
uniform                                           0.8               72.8
learning progress only                            3.2               65.4
LP + interestingness                              9.6               10.6

Read the two panels against each other. Uniform and learning-progress sampling learn about 73 and 65 tasks in total, almost all of them “collect n wood”, and only 0.8 and 3.2 of the 15 interesting ones. With the judge, the agent learns only 10.6 tasks in total but 9.6 of the interesting 15. A headline of “tasks learned” would rank the methods backwards. The quantity that matters is the count of tasks the designer values. That is exactly the argument of the OMNI paper. It is why choosing the metric is part of the method.

One more number keeps this honest. In this run, 78% of all practice steps under the judged curriculum still went to the 300 impossible tasks, through the small exploration floor added to every weight. The boring tasks received only 6% (against 68% under learning progress alone). The simulation shows the judge solving the boring-variant problem. It does not show an efficient curriculum. A better learnability signal (one that stops probing hopeless tasks) is the obvious improvement.

Common misconception

“A model of interestingness decides what is true or valuable.” It decides what a language model, trained on human text, rates as interesting given a prompt. That is a proxy for human taste, with the biases of the training text and of the prompt. The authors note the Goodhart's-law risk. If the agent or generator is optimised against the model's judgements, the judgements may stop being reliable. They propose adding human feedback. Treat the model as a ranking heuristic that you validate, not as an oracle.

OMNI-EPIC: the model writes the environments too

OMNI picked among tasks that already existed, or (in AI2-THOR) tasks a model described. The next step removes the pre-built environment. It is OMNI-EPIC (Faldor, Zhang, Cully and Clune, 2024, “OMNI-EPIC: Open-endedness via Models of human Notions of Interestingness with Environments Programmed in Code”). Foundation models write the code of new environments and the reward functions that define success. EPIC stands for Environments Programmed in Code, and code is the key. A program can describe anything a simulator can do, so the space of possible tasks is no longer a short list of parameters.

The components, as the paper describes them:

  • Task archive. An unbounded store of tasks, learned and failed, each saved as a natural-language description plus environment code. It starts from three seed descriptions.
  • Task generator. A language model proposes the next task, conditioned on archive tasks retrieved as similar to a randomly chosen one.
  • Environment generator. A language model writes PyBullet-based Python code (reset, step, reward and termination functions in the style of Gymnasium). Compile errors are fed back for up to five repairs.
  • Post-generation model of interestingness. Compares a new task with its most similar archive neighbours and discards it if it is not sufficiently novel or worthwhile.
  • Reinforcement learning. DreamerV3 trains one specialist agent per task, warm-started from the closest successfully learned task. The robot is a simulated R2D2 with six discrete actions and 64 by 64 colour images.
  • Success detector. The environment generator also writes a success function. The authors tried vision-language models as detectors and found them not yet accurate enough, so they used generated code. In a study with 50 participants the success detector agreed with human judgement 72.7% of the time.

What did it produce? Over a 200-iteration run with no learning, generated tasks spread across distinct clusters (ball-kicking, moving obstacles, object delivery, navigation) well beyond the three seeds. In short runs with learning, the highlighted run learned 16 tasks and failed 6, with one judged uninteresting. Failed tasks led to easier follow-ups, and successes were combined into harder ones. There were two controls: no archive, and no interestingness model. Against both, OMNI-EPIC reached significantly higher cell coverage (3 runs). It also reached a higher progress score (5 runs). That score is ANNECS (accumulated number of novel environments created and solved) in its OMNI variant. Both differences had p < 0.05 by Mann-Whitney U. The authors state plainly what remains undone. It is not yet “Darwin complete”, since it works only in one simulator and mainly one robot. The agents are specialists whose generalisation was not evaluated. Success detection relies on generated code with the 72.7% agreement above. Runs were short for compute reasons. They also could not rule out that some generated environments resemble training data.

A gate you can run

The “post-generation” check is the part of OMNI-EPIC most easily copied into your own pipelines. Before spending effort on a proposed task, compare it with what is already in the archive and discard near-duplicates. The script below does that with a stand-in embedding (a hashed bag of words with common words removed) and cosine similarity, threshold 0.75. The proposer is a RuleModel from the course's shared llm.py. It is a scripted stand-in for a language model and returns a fixed next task given the previous one. A real run would call a model and use a real embedding model (see Dense Retrieval).

import math, re, zlib
from llm import RuleModel

STOP = {"the", "a", "an", "to", "and", "of", "it", "on", "in", "with"}
def embed(text, dim=512):                         # stand-in for a text embedding: hashed bag of words
    v = [0.0] * dim
    for w in re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower()):
        if w not in STOP: v[zlib.crc32(w.encode()) % dim] += 1.0
    return v

def cosine(a, b):
    na, nb = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a)), math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in b))
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / (na * nb)

# A scripted stand-in for the LLM that proposes the next task, given the previous one.
proposer = RuleModel([
    (r"red ball", "navigate to the blue ball"),
    (r"blue ball\.", "pick up the red key and open the red door"),
    (r"red door", "open the door with the red key"),
    (r"door with the red key", "carry the ball through the door and drop it in the box"),
    (r"blue ball through", "stack three boxes and climb on top to reach the high shelf"),
    (r"in the box\.", "carry the blue ball through the door and drop it in the box"),
    (r"high shelf\.", "stack three boxes and climb on top to reach the higher shelf"),
    (r"higher shelf", "build a ramp from boxes to roll the ball up to the shelf"),
], default="navigate to the green ball")

archive = ["navigate to the red ball", "pick up the key", "open the door with the key"]
last, THRESHOLD = archive[0], 0.75
print(f"gate: reject a proposal whose cosine similarity to an archived task is >= {THRESHOLD}")
for step in range(1, 9):
    proposal = proposer.complete([{"role": "user", "content": f"Last task: {last}. Propose the next task."}]).content[0]["text"]
    sims = [(cosine(embed(proposal), embed(t)), t) for t in archive]
    best, nearest = max(sims)
    keep = best < THRESHOLD
    print(f"{step}. {proposal:62s} nearest {best:.2f} -> {'ADD' if keep else 'reject'}")
    if keep: archive.append(proposal)
    last = proposal + "."
print(f"archive now holds {len(archive)} tasks")

The gate works on the obvious cases. Proposal 5 (the same delivery task with the blue ball) and proposal 7 (“higher shelf” for “high shelf”) are rejected at 0.94 and 0.87. Proposals 4, 6 and 8 are accepted as new. It also shows what a threshold costs. Proposal 1 (“navigate to the blue ball”) is a thin variation of the seed task and got in at 0.67. Proposal 3 (“open the door with the red key”) added a new idea only to the extent of a colour and was rejected at 0.87. A bag of words cannot tell a colour change from a change of plan. This is why real systems use learned embeddings, and often a second language-model judgement on the borderline cases. Where you put the threshold is a choice between a cluttered archive and a missed idea.

Neighbours and successors

The pattern, “use a foundation model to judge what is new and promising”, has spread beyond curricula.

  • Intelligent Go-Explore (Lu, Hu and Clune, 2024). Go-Explore archives discovered states and returns to promising ones. The original relied on hand-designed heuristics for which states are promising. Intelligent Go-Explore replaces those heuristics with a foundation model's judgement of how interesting a state is. The authors report that it strongly exceeds classic reinforcement-learning and graph-search baselines on language- and vision-based exploration tasks, including some where a prior foundation-model agent (Reflexion) completely fails.
  • Quality-diversity through AI feedback (chapter 4). Here the model judges the descriptor and the quality of a text, so the archive has axes such as genre or sentiment.
  • Voyager (next chapter). Its automatic curriculum asks GPT-4 for the next task, with the instruction to discover as many diverse things as possible: a model of interestingness inside an agent loop.
  • Human-in-the-loop versions. Picbreeder used people clicking on the pictures they found interesting. Models of interestingness are an attempt to approximate that human signal cheaply.

What goes wrong

  • Judging the judge. You need ground truth. Have people label a sample of tasks, then measure agreement between the model and the labels, as you would for any LLM judge. The Crafter experiment could use an oracle only because the authors built the task pool with known labels.
  • Gaming the judge. A task generator rewarded for tasks the judge likes will learn the judge's quirks. This is the over-optimisation problem of reward models, one level up.
  • Broken generated tasks. Generated environments can be impossible, trivially solvable by a bug, or rewarding something other than what the description says. Agreement of 72.7% between a generated success test and human judgement means roughly one task in four is misjudged. Run a sample past people, and keep tasks whose success tests were verified by an independent check.
  • Training-data echo. A model asked for “new” tasks may reproduce familiar ones. The OMNI-EPIC authors could not rule out resemblance to training data, and say so. See Contamination, Saturation & Decay.
  • Narrow taste. “Interesting” as the model sees it is averaged over its training text. A curriculum shaped by it may under-explore ideas that text rarely praises.
  • Cost. Each step has a model call or several, plus reinforcement learning on each task. The paper notes the approach needs substantial compute.

Systems connection

The gate in this chapter is an approximate nearest-neighbour lookup: embed, search the archive, compare with a threshold. At a few hundred tasks, a linear scan is fine. At a million, you need an approximate nearest-neighbour index (see Dense Retrieval in A4), and the same trade-offs between recall and latency apply (a missed near-duplicate is a false accept). Note also that every judgement is an LLM call. The loop therefore inherits the failure behaviour of any network dependency: retries, timeouts and idempotent writes to the archive.

State of play, October 2026

OMNI (2023) and OMNI-EPIC (2024) are preprints from Jeff Clune's group and collaborators. The arXiv listings I read show no peer-reviewed venue. The idea of using a language model as a judge of novelty or promise is now common in agent and program-search systems. Results depend heavily on the chosen model, prompt and environment. I did not verify claims about newer systems that cite this work. Compare any new claim against its own evaluation: which judge, validated against which human labels.

Key takeaways

  • Learnable is not the same as worth learning. Learning progress rewards easy variations of what is known. A curriculum steered by it alone wastes its budget on them.
  • A model of interestingness uses a foundation model, which carries human judgements from its training text, to rank candidate tasks. OMNI multiplies the weight of boring tasks by 0.001 and combines the judgement with learning progress.
  • OMNI reported more tasks learned than uniform or learning-progress-only sampling in Crafter (82 against 4 and 55), BabyAI (8 against 1 and 4) and AI2-THOR (13 against 0 and 2).
  • In the simulation, the judged curriculum learned the fewest tasks in total and the most of the interesting ones: choose the metric the designer values, not the count.
  • OMNI-EPIC has language models write the environment and its success test as code, gated by an archive-based novelty check. Its authors report a 72.7% agreement of the success detector with humans and list the missing pieces.
  • Validate the judge against human labels, guard against gaming, test generated tasks, and expect narrow taste and real cost.

Check your understanding

A curriculum steered only by learning progress spends most of its budget on “collect 2 wood”, “collect 3 wood”, and so on. Why does this happen?

  1. Learning progress is broken and always favours easy tasks.
  2. Each variant is learnable and starts easy, so each shows fast improvement; learning progress measures how fast success is changing, not whether the task is worth learning.
  3. The tasks are too hard and the optimiser is stuck.
  4. The agent forgets the earlier variants and has to relearn them.
Learning progress works as designed: it finds tasks where success is rising quickly, and many near-identical easy tasks all qualify. It needs a second signal about what is worth learning. Option 3 is the opposite problem: impossible tasks show no progress and are skipped.

In the simulation, the judged curriculum learned about 11 tasks in total while uniform sampling learned about 73. Which conclusion is right?

  1. Uniform sampling is much better, since it learned the most tasks.
  2. The judge is broken, because it prevented learning.
  3. The result is noise from using only 5 seeds.
  4. Total tasks learned is the wrong metric: the judged curriculum learned about 10 of the 15 interesting tasks, while uniform learned fewer than one.
Almost all of uniform's 73 are the boring numeric variants. The designer's objective is the interesting tasks, and by that measure the judged curriculum wins by a wide margin. The gap (0.8 against 9.6) is far larger than seed noise would explain, though 5 seeds is a small sample.

OMNI multiplies the sampling weight of a task judged boring by 0.001 instead of removing it. What is the most likely reason?

  1. The judge can be wrong, and a tiny non-zero weight lets a wrongly dismissed task still be tried occasionally and recover.
  2. Removing tasks would break the learning-progress moving averages.
  3. A weight of exactly zero is not allowed by random number generators.
  4. Boring tasks are needed as prerequisites for the interesting ones.
A soft penalty is a hedge against judge errors. Generators accept zero weights (option 3 is wrong), and prerequisites are a property of the task structure, not of the penalty (option 4). Option 2 has nothing to do with the choice.

In OMNI-EPIC, a human study found that the generated success detector agreed with people 72.7% of the time. What does this imply for using the generated tasks?

  1. Nothing, since 72.7% is above the 50% chance level.
  2. The detector is ready for production use as it stands.
  3. A material fraction of tasks may be misjudged as solved or unsolved, so results should be spot-checked by people or by an independent check before they are trusted.
  4. The detector should be replaced by a random guesser.
Roughly one judgement in four disagrees with humans, which is large when thousands of tasks are generated. Beating chance (option 1) is not the bar for a signal that decides what the agent has learned. The authors themselves list this as a limitation.

You raise the novelty gate's similarity threshold from 0.75 to 0.95. What is the most likely effect on the task archive?

  1. It admits fewer tasks, because the bar is higher.
  2. It admits more tasks, including near-duplicates, because only proposals almost identical to an archived task are now rejected.
  3. It makes no difference, as the gate only matters for the first task.
  4. It makes the gate reject everything.
The gate rejects a proposal if its similarity to an archived task is at or above the threshold. Raising the threshold makes rejection rarer, so the archive gets bigger and more repetitive. Lowering it would do the opposite and risk dropping genuinely new ideas that share words.

A colleague proposes training the task generator to maximise the model of interestingness's score. What is the main risk?

  1. The generator will run out of tasks.
  2. The judge will refuse to score its own tasks.
  3. The tasks will be too easy to learn.
  4. The generator will find the judge's quirks and produce tasks that score well without being interesting to people, which is Goodhart's law.
Once the judge's score is the target, it stops being a reliable measure of the underlying thing. The OMNI authors flag this and suggest human feedback. Whether the tasks end up too easy (option 3) depends on the learnability filter, not on this risk.
