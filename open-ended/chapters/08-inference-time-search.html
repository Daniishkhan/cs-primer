Inference-Time Search

A model that answers once is a lottery ticket; a model that answers many times, and has some way to tell the good answers from the bad, is a search procedure. This chapter covers the whole ladder of inference-time search: repeated sampling, voting, verifiers, search over reasoning steps and adaptive branching that decides, call by call, whether to try something new or improve what it has.

Why this matters

The first half of this course searched with programs: tree search, evolution, archives. From here on the thing being mutated or expanded is produced by a language model, and the first place that shows up is the most common one: at answer time. A coding assistant that samples twenty patches and runs the tests, a maths tutor that votes over ten solutions, a research agent that tries three plans and keeps the best are all doing inference-time search, usually without calling it that. If you do not understand why these tricks work, you will either pay for samples that cannot help (because you have no way to pick the good one) or stop too early (because the first answer looked fine). After this chapter you can estimate what extra samples will buy, pick a selector that fits your task, and read papers such as AB-MCTS without mystery. The next chapter takes the same idea one level up: search not over answers but over programs that produce answers.

Spending compute when the question arrives

For most of the history of deep learning, the compute budget lived in training. You paid a large bill once, then each query cost a single forward pass (or one pass per generated token, as in the KV cache chapter). Quality was a property of the weights. Inference-time search (also called test-time compute or test-time scaling) changes the bargain: keep the weights fixed, and spend more computation on this particular question to get a better answer to it.

There are only two ways to spend extra computation on one question, and every method in this chapter is a combination of them:

  • Go wider. Produce more independent attempts at the whole thing, then choose among them.
  • Go deeper. Build on an attempt: continue it further, check it, repair it, or branch from its middle.

Both need a way to tell a better attempt from a worse one. That judgement, which this chapter calls the selector, is where inference-time search succeeds or fails. A model that can produce a correct answer one time in twenty, together with a perfect check, is a reliable solver. The same model with no check is a coin you cannot read.

Repeated sampling and coverage

Start with the plainest method. Ask the model the same question n times at a non-zero temperature (the decoding strategies chapter explains why temperature makes samples differ), and collect n answers. The fraction of problems for which at least one of the n answers is correct is called coverage, or, in the code-generation literature, pass@k with k equal to n.

If one sample is correct with probability p, and samples are independent, coverage after n samples is

coverage(n) = 1 − (1 − p)n

Worked example. A problem the model solves 5% of the time (p = 0.05) is solved by at least one of 20 samples with probability 1 − 0.9520 = 0.64, and by one of 45 samples with probability 1 − 0.9545 = 0.90. The same model, one sample, solves it 5% of the time. Nothing about the model changed; only the number of tickets did.

Two facts make this more than arithmetic. First, problems differ wildly in p. Averaging over a test set hides it: some questions have p near 1 and are solved by the first sample, many have p near 0.02 and need dozens of attempts, and some have p of exactly zero because the model cannot do them at all. The coverage curve of a whole benchmark is a blend of many such curves. Brown et al. (2024), "Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute with Repeated Sampling", measured coverage over four orders of magnitude of sample counts and found it growing roughly log-linearly, well described by an exponentiated power law. On SWE-bench Lite (real GitHub issues, checked by the repository's tests) they report DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct rising from 15.9% solved with one sample to 56% with 250 samples, above the 43% single-sample state of the art they cite for that benchmark at the time. Chen et al. (2021), the Codex paper, had already shown the same effect on HumanEval: 28.8% with one sample and 70.2% with 100.

Second, samples are not perfectly independent. They come from one model with one set of misconceptions. If a model has a systematic blind spot (a wrong formula it always reaches for), the 100th sample is no more likely to avoid it than the first. Real coverage curves flatten earlier than the independent formula predicts, and they flatten at the fraction of problems for which p is truly above zero.

Go deeper optional: estimating pass@k without sampling k times

Suppose you drew n samples for a problem and c were correct. The chance that a random subset of k of them contains at least one correct sample is 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k), where C is the binomial coefficient (Chen et al., 2021). This is the unbiased estimator of pass@k, and it is better than the naive 1 − (1 − c/n)k, which is biased high when n is small. With n = 20 samples and c = 3 correct it gives pass@1 = 0.15, pass@5 = 0.60 and pass@10 = 0.895 (the last line of the listing below). Always sample more than k and use the estimator: it also lets you draw the whole curve from one batch of samples.

Choosing among the samples

Coverage is a statement about an oracle that knows which answer is right. In production nobody does. You hold n candidates and must return one. There are three families of selector, in decreasing order of how much you can trust them.

Exact checkers

When the task has a mechanical test, use it. Code can be run against unit tests; a proof can be checked by a proof assistant; a SQL query can be executed and compared with a known result; a configuration can be validated against a schema. An exact checker (a verifier in the strict sense) turns coverage directly into accuracy: if any sample passes, you return it, so you get the whole coverage curve for the price of running the tests. This is why coding and mathematics lead the inference-time-search results, and why the Brown et al. SWE-bench numbers are so large. It is also why the evaluation chapters keep insisting on deterministic checks first (assertions and eval harnesses).

Voting

When there is no checker but answers can be compared (a number, a multiple-choice letter, a short string), count them and return the most common. This is self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022, "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models"). The paper's intuition: there are many different valid reasoning paths to a correct answer but, for a given problem, errors tend to scatter, so the correct answer is the one that different paths agree on. The authors report gains from replacing greedy decoding with sampling-and-voting of +17.9 points on GSM8K (grade-school maths word problems), +11.0 on SVAMP, +12.2 on AQuA, +6.4 on StrategyQA and +3.9 on ARC-challenge, for the models in that paper.

Voting has a built-in assumption that is easy to forget: wrong answers are scattered, right answers are concentrated. When the model has a favourite wrong answer, the vote converges on it, with more and more confidence as n grows. Voting also only works when answers canonicalise: you cannot count votes over free-form essays or patches without first deciding when two of them "say the same thing".

Learned verifiers and judges

Otherwise train or prompt a model to score candidates. Cobbe et al. (2021), "Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems", introduced GSM8K (8.5 thousand grade-school problems) and showed that sampling many solutions and returning the one a trained verifier ranks highest scales better with data than fine-tuning the generator alone. A verifier can score only the final answer (an outcome reward model) or each step of the reasoning (a process reward model, PRM). Lightman et al. (2023), "Let's Verify Step by Step", compared the two on the MATH benchmark, released PRM800K (800,000 step-level human labels), and found process supervision clearly better; their best process-supervised model solved 78% of a representative subset of the MATH test set. An LLM-as-judge is the same idea with a general model and a rubric instead of a trained scorer.

A learned verifier is itself a model, so it makes errors, and those errors interact with search in a nasty way that the next section shows.

A simulation you can run

The listing below builds the smallest honest laboratory for these ideas. A stand-in model faces 400 problems. Each problem has its own per-sample success probability p, drawn from a Beta(0.7, 1.3) distribution (many hard problems, some easy ones; mean 0.34). Wrong samples fall on one of four wrong answers, and one of them, the "trap", is five times as popular as each of the others. A verifier gives every sample a noisy score: correct samples centre on 1.0, wrong ones on 0.0, both with standard deviation 1.0. There is no real language model here. The model is a random-number generator with the properties named, which makes the output exactly reproducible and keeps attention on the selection logic.

import random
from math import comb

random.seed(7)
N_PROBLEMS, N_MAX = 400, 64
# Stand-in model: each problem has its own chance p that one sample is right.
# Wrong samples pick one of 4 wrong answers; one of them ("trap") is far more popular.
def make_problem():
    """Chance that one sample is right."""
    return random.betavariate(0.7, 1.3)            # many hard problems, some easy ones

def sample(p):
    """One sample: (answer, verifier_score). The verifier is noisy but informative."""
    if random.random() < p:
        return "right", random.gauss(1.0, 1.0)
    return random.choices(["trap", "w1", "w2", "w3"], weights=[5, 1, 1, 1])[0], random.gauss(0.0, 1.0)

def trial(p, n):
    s = [sample(p) for _ in range(n)]
    oracle = any(a == "right" for a, _ in s)                       # coverage / pass@n
    votes = {}
    for a, _ in s: votes[a] = votes.get(a, 0) + 1
    vote = max(votes, key=votes.get) == "right"                    # majority vote
    best = max(s, key=lambda t: t[1])[0] == "right"                # best-of-n by verifier
    return oracle, vote, best

problems = [make_problem() for _ in range(N_PROBLEMS)]
print(f"mean single-sample accuracy: {sum(problems)/len(problems):.3f}")
print(f"{'n':>3} {'coverage':>9} {'majority':>9} {'verifier':>9}")
for n in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64):
    tot = [0, 0, 0]
    for p in problems:
        for _ in range(10):                                        # 10 repeats per problem
            for k, r in enumerate(trial(p, n)): tot[k] += r
    d = N_PROBLEMS * 10
    print(f"{n:>3} {tot[0]/d:>9.3f} {tot[1]/d:>9.3f} {tot[2]/d:>9.3f}")

# pass@k unbiased estimator (Chen et al., 2021): from c correct out of n drawn, chance that k picks contain one.
def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    return 1.0 if n - c < k else 1.0 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)
print("\npass@k estimate from n=20 samples with c=3 correct:", [round(pass_at_k(20, 3, k), 3) for k in (1, 5, 10)])

Why a better-looking curve can hide a worse answer

The verifier line never turns down in the table. In real systems it sometimes does. A verifier is trained on examples from a finite distribution of answers. Pick the highest-scoring of a handful of samples and you are mostly selecting good answers. Pick the highest of thousands and you are increasingly selecting the answers that exploit the verifier's blind spots: confident-sounding, formatted the way the verifier liked in training, wrong. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022), "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization", measured exactly this with best-of-n sampling against a proxy reward model: the true (gold) reward first rises with optimisation pressure, then falls. This is Goodhart's law in reward models, and it returns in force in chapter 15. For now: the larger the search, the more the selector is the thing being attacked.

Common misconception

"If the model is right most of the time, voting over more samples always gets closer to the truth." Voting only helps when the correct answer is the modal answer. For any problem where the model's single-sample accuracy is below its probability of its favourite wrong answer, more samples make the wrong answer win more reliably, not less. In the table above, voting over 64 samples is no better than voting over 8, because it has saturated on exactly the problems where it was going to be right. The way out is a selector that has information the generator does not: a test, a calculation, a retrieval, or a separately trained verifier.

Searching over steps, not just answers

Repeated sampling restarts from scratch every time. If a solution has six steps and each is right 60% of the time, the chance that a whole chain is right is 0.66, about 4.7%, and sampling whole chains wastes most of its effort on chains that went wrong in step two and then kept writing confidently for four more steps. If something could tell you after step two that the chain had gone wrong, you could stop it there and spend the saved effort elsewhere.

That is search over reasoning steps. Treat a solution as a path through a tree: each node is a partial solution, each edge is one more step proposed by the model. Yao et al. (2023), "Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models", framed prompting this way, with the model proposing next "thoughts", evaluating partial states, and a classical search procedure (breadth-first, depth-first, beam) deciding which to extend. On the Game of 24 (combine four numbers with arithmetic to reach 24), the paper reports GPT-4 with ordinary chain-of-thought prompting solving 4% of tasks and Tree of Thoughts solving 74%. Whatever the exact figures on other models, the structure is the lesson: step-level evaluation lets the search discard bad prefixes early. If you read the tree search chapter you will recognise the machinery; the new part is that the "simulator" and the "value function" are language models, and both are fallible.

The listing below puts numbers on this with the six-step example. It compares best-of-n on whole chains against a beam search that keeps two partial chains and consults a step verifier whose accuracy we vary. Cost is counted in step-calls (one call asks the model for one step), which is the honest unit when steps are the thing you pay for.

import random

STEPS, P_GOOD, WIDTH = 6, 0.6, 2   # 6 steps; each proposed step is good with chance 0.6; keep 2 partial chains

def best_of_n(rng, n):
    """n complete chains, 6 calls each. A chain is right only if every step is good."""
    ok = any(all(rng.random() < P_GOOD for _ in range(STEPS)) for _ in range(n))
    return ok, n * STEPS

def beam(rng, acc):
    """Step-level search. A process verifier of accuracy `acc` labels each partial chain
    'good' or 'bad'; chains labelled bad are dropped. Each kept chain proposes 2 next steps."""
    calls, beams = 0, [True]        # True = the chain is genuinely good so far
    for _ in range(STEPS):
        cand = []
        for good in beams:
            for _ in range(2):
                calls += 1
                ok = good and rng.random() < P_GOOD
                label = ok if rng.random() < acc else not ok
                cand.append((label, ok))
        kept = [c for c in cand if c[0]] or cand[:1]    # never drop everything
        beams = [ok for _, ok in kept[:WIDTH]]
    return any(beams), calls

rng, T = random.Random(11), 5000
print(f"{'method':<28} {'calls':>6} {'solved':>7}")
for n in (1, 2, 4, 8):
    r = [best_of_n(rng, n) for _ in range(T)]
    print(f"{'best-of-' + str(n) + ' full chains':<28} {r[0][1]:>6} {sum(x for x, _ in r)/T:>7.3f}")
for acc in (0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.99):
    r = [beam(rng, acc) for _ in range(T)]
    print(f"{'beam, step verifier ' + str(acc):<28} {sum(c for _, c in r)/T:>6.1f} {sum(x for x, _ in r)/T:>7.3f}")

Three results are worth a pause. A step verifier at 99% accuracy solves 56% of problems with about 16 calls; best-of-n needs 48 calls to reach 30%. A verifier at 75% still beats best-of-4 (25.5% for 15 calls against 17.7% for 24). And a verifier at 50% accuracy, which is a coin flip, solves 5.8%, no better than a single full chain (4.8%) while costing 16 calls rather than 6. A step verifier near chance is worse than no search, because you have paid for the machinery and it is pruning at random.

State of play, October 2026

Much of what this section describes as an external search procedure is now partly absorbed into the model. "Reasoning models" trained with reinforcement learning on checkable rewards (see RL with verifiable rewards) emit long chains of thought that backtrack, re-check and try alternatives inside a single generation, which is a learned form of depth-first search. External search around them (parallel samples plus a checker) still adds value in the same way, and combining the two is common practice. Which is cheaper for a given task changes with model prices and sizes, so measure on your own task rather than trusting a general rule. Check current vendor documentation for the model families you use.

Giving each question the compute it deserves

A fixed n for every question is wasteful in both directions. An easy question (p near 1) needs one sample; a hopeless one (p near 0) is a bottomless pit. Snell, Lee, Xu and Kumar (2024), "Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters", studied exactly this allocation problem and report two headline results: a "compute-optimal" strategy that adapts to each prompt's difficulty improves the efficiency of test-time compute by more than 4 times compared with a plain best-of-n baseline, and in a FLOPs-matched comparison, test-time compute can outperform a 14 times larger model, but only on problems where the smaller model already has a non-trivial success rate. The second clause matters as much as the first. Search amplifies a model's competence; it does not create competence from nothing.

Their further observation is that the best method depends on difficulty. Roughly, for easy and medium problems, sequential revision (take an answer and ask for improvements, "going deeper") tends to pay; for hard problems, spreading effort over independent samples or a tree ("going wider") tends to pay. The rule is not exact, and the paper's own strategies estimate difficulty from the model's behaviour. The practical message is simple to state and awkward to implement: you need a way to decide, while you search, where the next call should go.

Adaptive branching: wider or deeper, decided by the data

The table above is a policy, and a policy that reacts to what the search has seen so far is exactly what the bandit machinery of the tree search chapter provides. Inoue, Misaki, Imajuku, Kuroki, Nakamura and Akiba (2025), "Wider or Deeper? Scaling LLM Inference-Time Compute with Adaptive Branching Tree Search" (a NeurIPS 2025 spotlight, from Sakana AI), propose AB-MCTS, adaptive-branching Monte Carlo tree search. In ordinary MCTS the number of children each node can have is fixed in advance, so "how wide" is a setting. In AB-MCTS, at every node the algorithm chooses between two actions: generate a new candidate answer (go wider) or refine an existing one using external feedback such as test results (go deeper). To choose, it keeps a probability model of how good the result of each action is likely to be, and samples from those models, the Thompson sampling idea from the bandit chapter: an action that has often paid off is drawn more often, but an uncertain action still has a chance to win the draw.

The paper also proposes a multi-model variant that adds a third choice, which language model to call, treated as one more bandit. The Sakana AI write-up of the work reports ARC-AGI-2 experiments (120 public evaluation tasks, a budget of 250 LLM calls per task, success measured as Pass@k, meaning any attempt among those made is correct): repeated sampling with o4-mini solved 23% of tasks, AB-MCTS with the same model 27.5%, and the multi-model version over 30% using o4-mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek R1 together. The algorithms are released as TreeQuest, an Apache-2.0 library from Sakana AI (the repository offers ABMCTS-A, which aggregates node statistics, and ABMCTS-M, which uses mixed models fitted with PyMC).

State of play, October 2026

The ARC-AGI-2 figures above come from Sakana AI's July 2025 write-up of its own experiments with models then current, and they are Pass@k scores, which assume the right answer can be recognised among those produced. The same write-up reports a stricter Pass@2 setting with a simple rule-based selection of two answers, where the multi-model system scored 19.2%. Model names, benchmark versions and leaderboard scores change quickly; read the arXiv paper (2503.04412) and the repository for the current method and results before relying on any number.

A miniature you can read in one sitting

The paper's algorithm manages a tree of nodes with per-node statistics. To see the essential idea without that bookkeeping, the listing below strips it to a single decision repeated 30 times: each step either draws a fresh answer or refines the best one so far, and the choice is made by Thompson sampling over the improvement over the best-so-far that each action has delivered. This is a deliberately simplified stand-in, not AB-MCTS itself: it has no tree and no per-node models, and the "model" is a pair of formulas. A fresh answer scores a random number between 0 and 1, squared (so mostly mediocre); a refinement moves a score a fraction gain of the way to 1, plus noise. We test two worlds. In "refinable" tasks feedback helps (gain 0.35). In "lottery" tasks it does not (gain 0), so refining is wasted effort.

import random

# Stand-in "model + feedback". A fresh answer scores ~ U(0,1) squared-ish (usually mediocre).
# A refinement of an answer with score s moves it toward 1 by a fraction `gain` (plus noise).
# Two kinds of task: "refinable" (feedback helps, gain 0.35) and "lottery" (feedback useless, gain 0).
def fresh(rng):            return rng.random() ** 2
def refine(rng, s, gain):  return min(1.0, max(0.0, s + gain * (1 - s) + rng.gauss(0, 0.05)))

def run(strategy, gain, budget, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pool = [fresh(rng)]                         # scores of all answers so far
    wide, deep = [], []                         # gains over the best-so-far from past "wider" / "deeper" actions
    for _ in range(budget - 1):
        if strategy == "wide":   act = "wide"
        elif strategy == "deep": act = "deep"
        else:                                    # adaptive: Thompson sampling over the two actions
            def draw(hist):
                n = len(hist)
                mu = (0.1 * 2 + sum(hist)) / (n + 2)                 # shrink toward an optimistic prior of 0.1
                return rng.gauss(mu, 0.2 / (n + 1) ** 0.5)           # more data, less spread
            act = "wide" if draw(wide) > draw(deep) else "deep"
        best = max(pool)
        if act == "wide":
            s = fresh(rng); wide.append(max(0.0, s - best))
        else:
            parent = max(range(len(pool)), key=lambda i: pool[i] + rng.gauss(0, 0.05))   # refine a good one
            s = refine(rng, pool[parent], gain); deep.append(max(0.0, s - best))
        pool.append(s)
    return max(pool)

for name, gain in (("refinable", 0.35), ("lottery", 0.0)):

No fixed strategy wins in both worlds. Always going deeper is best on refinable tasks (1.000) and disastrous on lottery tasks (0.670), where it polishes the first mediocre answer for thirty calls. Always going wider is safe (0.940 in both) but never exploits useful feedback. The adaptive version matches the better fixed strategy in each world (1.000 and 0.944). It does that without being told which world it is in, because its estimates of "gain from deeper" and "gain from wider" are learned during the search. The price is a little exploration: on one seed's lottery task, the first dozen calls mix both actions before it settles almost entirely on going wider.

Step through

Adaptive branching: one search, decision by decision

  1. No answers yet. The first action is forced: generate.
  2. Go wider. A fresh answer arrives with score 0.40.
  3. Go wider again. A second answer, B at 0.55, becomes the best so far.
  4. Go deeper. Refining B with feedback yields B1, a gain of 0.15. Deeper looks productive.
  5. Deeper again, smaller gain. B2 improves by only 0.04: returns are diminishing.
  6. Wider again. The balance of estimates tips back to fresh answers. The cycle continues until the budget is spent. (Scores and draws here are illustrative, not from a run.)

Analogy: a prospector with a limited number of digs

A prospector can dig a new hole somewhere (wider) or keep digging in a hole that has shown a vein (deeper). A good prospector does not decide in advance how many holes to open. She digs a few, follows the vein that looks richest, and when it thins out, moves on. How she splits her effort depends on what the ground turns out to be like, which is what the adaptive search estimates as it goes.

Where it breaks down: a prospector sees a vein directly. The search sees only a score from the selector, and that score can be wrong. A "vein" the verifier mistakes for gold sends all the digging to the wrong hole, which is the verifier-exploitation problem again.

What it costs, and where it runs

Search spends tokens, and tokens cost money and time. Three engineering consequences follow.

  • Budget is the first-class parameter. A search with no cap on calls is a liability. Set a maximum number of model calls and a maximum spend per request, make early stopping a feature (stop when the checker passes), and record calls used so cost per solved task can be reported. The cost-versus-quality trade-off itself is the subject of cost, latency and quality, and the price arithmetic of the economics of a token applies directly: n samples cost roughly n times as much, less any prompt-prefix caching you can use.
  • Parallel samples are a fan-out. Sampling 64 candidates concurrently takes as long as the slowest of them, not the average, so the tail-latency arithmetic applies: see tail latency at scale. The remedies are the same too: set a deadline and use whatever has finished, or stop the rest as soon as a candidate passes the checker. Wider search parallelises; deeper search is sequential, because each refinement needs the last one's output. That difference decides what latency a product can promise.
  • Checkers must be safe to run. If the selector is "run the candidate code against tests", then you are executing text produced by a model, many times, automatically. Treat it as untrusted input: a sandbox, resource limits and no ambient credentials (sandboxes and permissions). Chapter 9 shows this pattern in code.

Systems connection

Inference-time search is a hedged request in disguise. Tail latency at scale shows that sending the same request to several replicas and taking the first good reply cuts the tail; best-of-n with early stopping does the same for answer quality instead of speed. The queueing and capacity chapters (queueing theory, load testing and capacity) matter here too: an app that multiplies each user request by 16 model calls multiplies its load on a GPU pool by 16, and a sudden spike will hit the latency knee sooner.

When to use which

  1. Is there an exact checker? Use it, sample as wide as the budget allows, stop at the first pass. This is the strongest position to be in. If there is not, consider building one: a partial checker (format valid, tests that exist, units consistent) is still better than none.
  2. Can answers be compared and are errors scattered? Majority vote, with deliberate tie-breaking. Check on a sample of your data that the modal answer is usually right; if the model has a favourite wrong answer, voting hurts.
  3. Only a learned verifier or judge? Use it, but calibrate it against human labels on your own task (LLM-as-judge) and cap n: the benefit of larger n shrinks while the risk of verifier exploitation grows.
  4. Does the task have intermediate structure a step verifier can use? Search over steps. Test the step verifier's accuracy first; below about 70% to 75% in the toy above, it has stopped paying.
  5. Is the difficulty mixed, and the feedback informative? Allocate adaptively: stop on easy items, repair when the feedback is specific, sample anew when it is not.

Key takeaways

  • Inference-time search spends extra computation on one question. The two moves are wider (more independent attempts) and deeper (build on an attempt); everything else is a way to combine them.
  • Coverage grows as 1 − (1 − p)n for independent samples and varies hugely across problems; real curves flatten where the model has blind spots.
  • The selector decides how much coverage you can use. Exact checkers are best, voting works when the right answer is the modal one, and learned verifiers are the weakest link and the likeliest to be exploited as n grows.
  • Step-level search saves compute by pruning bad prefixes, but a step verifier near chance accuracy makes things worse than plain sampling.
  • Adaptive branching (AB-MCTS) chooses between a new answer and a refinement using Thompson sampling over observed gains, which lets one algorithm cope with tasks where feedback helps and tasks where it does not.
  • Search is an engineering cost: cap the budget, stop early, expect tail latency from fan-out, and run model-written code in a sandbox.

Check your understanding

A problem is solved by a single sample with probability 0.05, and samples are independent. About how many samples give a 90% chance that at least one is correct?

  1. About 10
  2. About 20
  3. About 45
  4. About 200
Solve 1 − 0.95n = 0.9, so 0.95n = 0.1 and n = ln 0.1 / ln 0.95, about 45. Twenty samples give only 64%, which is the tempting answer if you think "20 tries at 5% is about 100%". Two hundred would be right only if samples were far from independent.

In the simulation, majority voting stays near 39% from n = 8 to n = 64 while coverage rises from 75% to 93%. What is the best explanation?

  1. On hard problems one wrong answer is more popular than the right one, so more samples confirm the wrong answer
  2. Voting needs at least 100 samples to start working
  3. The verifier noise is too high for voting
  4. Coverage is measured on different problems from voting
Voting returns the modal answer. When the trap answer is more common than the correct one on a problem, extra samples only strengthen the trap. The verifier does not take part in voting, so its noise is irrelevant, and both columns use the same problems.

Best-of-n against a learned verifier improves from n = 4 to n = 64, but a gap to the oracle coverage remains and may eventually widen in a real system. What causes the gap?

  1. The generator cannot produce correct answers any more
  2. Larger n makes the samples more correlated
  3. Ties are broken at random
  4. The verifier misranks some candidates, and a larger pool contains more wrong candidates that happen to score highly
Coverage counts problems where a correct sample exists; the verifier must still rank it above every wrong sample. With more candidates there are more chances for a wrong one to outscore the right one, and in real systems the highest-scoring wrong answers are often those that exploit the verifier's weaknesses (Gao et al., 2022).

In the six-step example, a step verifier with 50% accuracy solved 5.8% of problems using about 16 calls. Whole-chain sampling with one chain solved 4.8% using 6 calls. What should you conclude?

  1. Step verification at 50% is a bargain because it nearly doubles the accuracy
  2. A verifier at chance accuracy prunes at random, so the search buys almost nothing for its extra cost
  3. Beam search always costs about 16 calls
  4. The result shows that process supervision is worse than outcome supervision
With a coin-flip verifier the beam keeps arbitrary prefixes, which is close to sampling at random. The small difference between 5.8% and 4.8% is not worth nearly three times the calls. The experiment says nothing about process versus outcome supervision, which is about how a verifier is trained, not about its accuracy at chance.

On a "lottery" task, where refining an answer never improves it, why does the adaptive-branching miniature still score about as well as always going wider?

  1. It is told in advance that the task is a lottery
  2. It always starts by going wider and never goes deeper
  3. The observed gains from refining stay near zero, so the sampled estimate for "deeper" keeps losing the draw
  4. Refinement is disabled whenever the score is below 0.5
Thompson sampling updates its estimate of each action from experience. When refinements deliver no improvement over the best-so-far, "deeper" stops winning draws except for occasional exploration. It is not told the task type (option 0), and it does go deeper early on to find out (option 1).

A product samples 32 candidate answers in parallel and returns the first one that passes a validator. Compared with waiting for all 32 and voting, which is the most accurate statement?

  1. Latency now depends on how quickly one valid sample arrives, which trims the slow tail of the fan-out, but only works if a validator can recognise a good answer individually
  2. Latency is unchanged because all 32 calls must complete before any decision
  3. Accuracy must fall, because the first valid answer is never the best one
  4. Cost falls to that of a single call
Early stopping avoids waiting for the slowest sample, the same idea as a hedged request. If the validator is exact, the first passing answer is as good as any. Calls already in flight still cost money unless cancelled, so cost does not fall to one call, and the approach cannot be used for voting, which needs the full set.
