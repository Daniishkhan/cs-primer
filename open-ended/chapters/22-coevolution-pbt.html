Co-evolution & Population-Based Training

Most search loops score a candidate against a fixed test. This chapter covers what changes when the test is alive: other evolving populations, problems that grow with their solvers, or a training run whose settings are evolved while it trains. It ends with why these ideas matter to a loop that improves its own harness.

Why this matters

This is reading for the literacy pack, not a step of any artifact. It helps most alongside Artifact 4, where an outer agent rewrites an inner one and the thing doing the scoring may change with it. After this chapter you can tell competitive from cooperative co-evolution. You can explain what population-based training (PBT) does that a grid search cannot. And you can say why a flat or rising score in a co-evolving system is hard to read.

When fitness depends on other learners

In the evolutionary algorithms of chapter 2 a candidate's fitness is a fixed function: the same program gets the same score every time. Co-evolution breaks that assumption. The fitness of a candidate depends on other candidates that are evolving too. Two forms matter in practice.

Competitive

Populations are rivals. Hillis (1990, "Co-evolving parasites improve simulated evolution as an optimization procedure") evolved sorting networks against a population of parasites, test cases chosen to break them. A host's fitness came from the parasite it faced, so hard cases were produced as the hosts improved and the search was less likely to stall on a local optimum.

Cooperative

Populations are parts of one solution. Potter and De Jong (1994, "A Cooperative Coevolutionary Approach to Function Optimization") gave each species one parameter of the function, and a member is scored by how well it works together with representatives of the other species.

A competitive loop is a source of difficulty that no designer had to write: the opponent gets harder as you get better. It is also the cause of the Red Queen effect, the situation where both sides keep adapting and neither pulls ahead. Chapter 19 ran a host-parasite arms race and showed that the hosts beat parasites from the past and lose to parasites from the future while scoring no better than chance against the present. I will not repeat it. Keep one consequence: in a co-evolving system, a flat score is not a stalled search and a rising score is not progress, because the yardstick moves.

The cooperative form has the opposite difficulty, called credit assignment. If a team of species scores well, which member deserves the credit? Potter and De Jong's answer was to evaluate each member inside the best-known team. That is cheap, but it can lock the species into a particular partnership.

Co-evolving problems and solutions: POET

The Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer (POET; Wang, Lehman, Clune and Stanley, 2019) is the best-known case where the second population is the curriculum. It keeps pairs of an environment and an agent, mutates environments into new ones, trains each agent on its own environment, and moves agents between environments when the transfer helps. The abstract reports that it solved challenges that direct optimisation, or a direct-path curriculum, could not, and that transfer between environments was essential. Chapter 5 covers POET in full, including its minimal criterion and novelty filter, so this chapter goes no further. The point to carry is the shape: problems and solvers advance together, and neither is fixed.

Population-based training

The third idea is more practical. Training a network involves hyperparameters (the learning rate, the strength of regularisation) that people normally set before the run, often by running a grid or random search of many separate runs. Jaderberg et al. (2017, "Population Based Training of Neural Networks") instead train a population of models in parallel and let the hyperparameters change during the run. Their abstract says the method uses a fixed compute budget to optimise models and hyperparameters jointly, and so discovers a schedule of hyperparameter settings rather than a single configuration. They report faster convergence and better final performance on deep reinforcement learning, machine translation and generative adversarial networks (GANs).

The mechanism has two moves, applied periodically to each worker.

  1. Exploit. If a worker is doing badly compared with the rest, replace its weights and hyperparameters with a copy of a better worker's.
  2. Explore. Perturb the copied hyperparameters, for example multiply the learning rate by 0.8 or 1.25, and carry on training.

A population-based training run you can read

The script below is a miniature, using only the standard library. The task is to drive a single number to zero when every gradient is noisy. A large learning rate approaches the target quickly but jitters around it, and a small one is slow but steady, so the best rate falls over the run. Eight workers share a compute budget. The baseline is a fair one: the same eight workers, with fixed learning rates from 0.5 down to 0.04 and no copying, and the best worker chosen at the end. Both methods pick by a noisy evaluation, and both are reported on the true loss. Twenty seeds were too few to separate them, so the script uses 100.

"""Population-based training in miniature (standard library only).

Task: drive theta to 0 on f(theta) = theta^2 / 2 when each gradient is
noisy. A big learning rate is fast but jittery; a small one is slow but
precise. The best rate therefore changes during training: a schedule.
"""
import random
import statistics

POP, ROUNDS, STEPS = 8, 30, 10                   # workers, rounds, steps/round
NOISE = 3.0                                      # std of gradient noise

def train(theta, lr, rng):
    """Some SGD steps; return the new theta."""
    for _ in range(STEPS):
        theta -= lr * (theta + rng.gauss(0, NOISE))
    return theta

def score(theta, rng):
    """Noisy evaluation of f(theta): used only to choose and to copy."""
    return statistics.fmean((theta + rng.gauss(0, 1.0)) ** 2 / 2
                            for _ in range(5))

# ── 1. Baseline: a grid of fixed learning rates, same compute ──────────
LRS = [0.5 * 0.7 ** i for i in range(POP)]       # 0.5 down to 0.04

def fixed_grid(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    thetas = [10.0] * POP
    for _ in range(ROUNDS):
        thetas = [train(t, lr, rng) for t, lr in zip(thetas, LRS)]
    best = min(thetas, key=lambda t: score(t, rng))   # pick by noisy score
    return best ** 2 / 2                         # report the true loss

# ── 2. PBT: exploit (copy a top worker) and explore (perturb its lr) ───
def pbt(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pop = [{"theta": 10.0, "lr": lr} for lr in LRS]
    history = []
    for _ in range(ROUNDS):
        for w in pop:
            w["theta"] = train(w["theta"], w["lr"], rng)
            w["score"] = score(w["theta"], rng)
        pop.sort(key=lambda w: w["score"])
        history.append(pop[0]["lr"])
        for loser in pop[-2:]:                   # bottom quarter
            winner = rng.choice(pop[:2])         # exploit: copy weights + lr
            loser["theta"] = winner["theta"]
            loser["lr"] = winner["lr"] * rng.choice([0.8, 1.25])  # explore
    best = min(pop, key=lambda w: score(w["theta"], rng))
    return best["theta"] ** 2 / 2, history

# ── 3. A hundred seeds, paired: same seed, same compute, same evaluation ─────
seeds = range(100)
base = [fixed_grid(s) for s in seeds]
runs = [pbt(s) for s in seeds]
res = [r[0] for r in runs]
diff = [b - p for b, p in zip(base, res)]
half = 1.96 * statistics.stdev(diff) / len(diff) ** 0.5
print(f"fixed-lr grid  mean loss {statistics.fmean(base):.3f}")
print(f"PBT            mean loss {statistics.fmean(res):.3f}")
print(f"paired gain {statistics.fmean(diff):.3f} +/- {half:.3f} (95% CI), "
      f"PBT better in {sum(d > 0 for d in diff)}/100 seeds")
print("best worker's lr by round, seed 0:",
      " ".join(f"{x:.2f}" for x in runs[0][1][::4]))
fixed-lr grid  mean loss 0.119
PBT            mean loss 0.060
paired gain 0.059 +/- 0.038 (95% CI), PBT better in 56/100 seeds
best worker's lr by round, seed 0: 0.35 0.14 0.12 0.29 0.12 0.12 0.12 0.08

The mean loss falls from 0.119 to 0.060, and the paired difference is 0.059 ± 0.038 (95% confidence), so the gain is real at this size but not large relative to its uncertainty. Note also that PBT was better in only 56 of 100 seeds. Most of the gain in the mean comes from fewer bad endings, because a fixed worker with a high learning rate can finish on an unlucky jitter. A copied worker with a lowered rate plausibly avoids that, though I did not test this explanation. The trace of the best worker's learning rate in seed 0 begins at 0.35 and settles near 0.12 before dipping to 0.08, so the population did drift downward, as the task rewards. It is a single trace from one seed; do not read it as a discovered law.

Common misconception

"Population-based training is a faster random search." Random search runs independent trials and discards the losers. PBT lets the winners' weights be inherited, so a worker benefits from training already done, and its hyperparameters can change mid-run. The price is that the population is no longer independent: selection on a noisy evaluation can copy a lucky worker's weights into every slot, and the whole population then shares one flaw. A final test on data that selection never saw (chapter 16) matters even more here than in an ordinary search.

Why this matters for loops that improve their own harness

Three connections to the artifacts. First, the harness is a population of settings. An agent harness has prompts, tool descriptions and retry budgets, much like a training run has a learning rate. A loop that holds several harness variants, copies the one doing well and perturbs it is PBT, with a harness in place of a network. The same warnings apply: use a fair baseline with the same budget, and score on tasks the loop never saw.

Second, the evaluator can be one of the co-evolving populations. Iacob et al. (2026, "The Red Queen Gödel Machine: Co-Evolving Agents and Their Evaluators") argue that self-improving agent methods assume a fixed evaluation criterion, and propose a framework in which learned evaluators improve alongside the agents they guide. The abstract reports, among other results, that at low reasoning effort the co-evolved coder passes 82.1% of held-out tasks against 75.0% for a fixed-evaluator baseline. These are the authors' numbers from a paper they describe as a preliminary preprint (v3, 8 October 2026), and I have not seen the code or reproduced anything. Treat it as a pointer to the idea, not as a settled result.

Third, co-evolution is where evaluator hacking becomes an arms race. In chapter 15 a fixed scorer is exploited by search. If the scorer is also being improved, the exploiter and the defender both move, the Red Queen effect appears, and a rising score no longer means what it used to. This is the reason Artifact 4's standards demand a fixed budget and scores on private splits that neither side can see.

Analogy: sparring partners

Two boxers who train only against each other get better together, and the gym's scoreboard shows nothing: each wins about half their rounds. A boxer who trains against a fixed bag gets a clean score but stops improving once the bag is easy. Co-evolution gives you the sparring partner, and takes away the scoreboard.

Where it breaks down: boxers can be measured against the rest of the world by entering a tournament. A co-evolving population needs the same, an outside test that neither side trained against, and building it is the hard part.

Systems connection

Exploit-and-explore is the logic of a fleet manager that replaces its worst replicas with copies of its healthiest ones and then perturbs each copy's configuration slightly. Competitive co-evolution is a red team and a defender who both ship updates every week, so incident counts can stay flat while both sides improve.

Key takeaways

  • Co-evolution makes fitness depend on other evolving populations: competitive (rivals, as in Hillis) or cooperative (parts of one solution, as in Potter and De Jong).
  • In a co-evolving system the yardstick moves, so a flat score is not a stall and a rising score is not progress; use an outside test.
  • POET co-evolves problems and solvers (see chapter 5); population-based training evolves weights and hyperparameters together by exploit and explore.
  • In the miniature, PBT beat a fair fixed-rate baseline by 0.059 ± 0.038 over 100 paired seeds, and was better in only 56 of them.
  • A harness is a population of settings and an evaluator can be a co-evolving population, so these ideas apply to Artifact 4 with the same standards: fair baseline, fixed budget, unseen scores.

Check your understanding

Two competitive populations co-evolve for a long time and each one's score against the current opponent stays near 50%. What do you conclude?

  1. The search has stalled and should be restarted
  2. Both populations have converged to the optimum
  3. The opponent has a bug
  4. Nothing yet: the Red Queen effect makes a flat score compatible with large progress, so test against an outside or archived opponent
Each side's difficulty rises as the other improves, so the pairwise score hides progress. Chapter 19's host-parasite run showed hosts beating past parasites but not present ones. A flat score cannot separate stalled from healthy.

What does population-based training do that a grid search of independent runs cannot?

  1. It lets a weak worker inherit a strong worker's weights and then change hyperparameters mid-run, so settings follow a schedule
  2. It guarantees the best final model
  3. It needs fewer workers than a grid search
  4. It removes the need for a validation set
Inheritance of weights (exploit) and perturbation of hyperparameters (explore) are the new moves. There is no guarantee of optimality, the budget is the same, and selection on noisy scores makes a held-out test more necessary, not less.

In the miniature, PBT's mean loss was half the baseline's, yet PBT won in only 56 of 100 seeds. What is the best explanation?

  1. The seeds were different for the two methods
  2. The loss is measured without noise
  3. The mean is pulled by a few bad endings in the baseline, so a large average gain can come from a small edge in most seeds
  4. PBT lost in the other 44 seeds because it failed to converge
The two methods used the same seeds (the comparison is paired), and the evaluation noise affected both. Losses are skewed: a few unlucky endings dominate a mean. This is why the script also reports a confidence interval and a win count.

A team builds a loop that keeps eight variants of an agent harness, copies the best into the worst slots and perturbs the prompt. Which check best protects against a lucky result?

  1. Report the best variant's score on the tasks used for selection
  2. Score the final variant on tasks selection never saw, against a baseline given the same budget
  3. Run more rounds until the score stops changing
  4. Remove the perturbation step
Selection on noisy scores picks luck as well as skill, so only unseen tasks and a fair same-budget baseline show a real gain. The selection score is exactly the biased number, more rounds give selection more chances to overfit, and removing exploration only removes the search.
