Tree Search, Bandits & MCTS

Some problems cannot be solved by computing an answer; they have to be searched for, one costly trial at a time. This chapter builds the oldest idea in that family from the bottom: how to split a limited budget between trying new things and repeating what works, how that idea grows into Monte Carlo tree search, and how AlphaZero replaced its weakest part with a learned network.

Why this matters

Every system in the rest of this course is a search loop with a budget: propose something, score it, decide what to try next. The "decide what to try next" step is always a version of the problem in this chapter. When an agent samples several answers and picks one (chapter 8), when an evolutionary system chooses which program to mutate (chapter 9), when a product team A/B tests variants, someone is trading exploration against exploitation. After this chapter you can state that trade-off precisely, implement the standard solutions, explain why tree search works on games, and read the AlphaZero paper without being lost.

You met bandits briefly in A3 chapter 6, where the reward came from a human or a verifier and the question was how a language model should be trained. Here the same mathematics is used for a different job: deciding where to spend compute while searching. We rebuild what we need from scratch, so you can read this chapter on its own.

Search as spending a budget

A search problem has a set of possible solutions or action sequences (the search space), a way to score a candidate, and a limit on how many candidates you can afford to score. Three examples from three domains: a hospital scheduling surgeries (the space is every possible timetable, the score is overrun minutes), a lawyer's tool exploring clause rewrites (the space is edits, the score is a checker that flags conflicts), a chess engine (the space is move sequences, the score is the final result). In every case the space is far too large to enumerate. Chess has somewhere between 1043 and 1045 legal positions by published estimates, Go has far more, and a timetable with 30 surgeries and 8 theatres already has more arrangements than you could ever try.

So search is never "look at everything". It is deciding, repeatedly, which candidate to evaluate next, using what the earlier evaluations told you. A tree is the natural shape when the candidate is built from a sequence of choices: the root is the starting state, each edge is a choice, and each leaf is a complete candidate. The branching factor is how many choices there are at each node, and the depth is how many choices a complete candidate needs. A game with 30 choices per turn and 60 turns has 3060 leaves, about 1088. Even evaluating a billion leaves a second would not scratch it.

Classical game programs coped with this by pruning (alpha-beta) and by a hand-written evaluation function that guesses how good a position is without playing it out. That works when an expert can write the function, as in chess, and fails when nobody can, as in Go. Monte Carlo tree search, the subject of this chapter, needs no hand-written evaluation. It estimates a position's value by sampling, and spends its samples where they matter. To see how, we start with the smallest search problem there is.

Exploration versus exploitation: the bandit

A multi-armed bandit is a row of slot machines ("one-armed bandits"). Each arm pays out with some fixed probability that you do not know. You get a fixed number of pulls. The goal is to collect as much reward as possible over the whole run. Nothing about the state changes between pulls, so there is no tree, only the pure dilemma:

  • Exploitation: pull the arm that looks best so far. It earns the most in expectation if your estimate is right.
  • Exploration: pull an arm that looks worse, or that you have barely tried, to learn whether the estimate is wrong.

Neither extreme works. An agent that never explores can lock onto an arm that was lucky early. An agent that always explores wastes pulls on arms it already knows are poor. The dilemma appears whenever the information you collect depends on the choices you make: a recommender choosing which article to show, a clinical trial allocating patients between treatments, an engineer choosing which of 20 prompt variants to evaluate on a precious human-labelled set, a search over programs choosing which parent to mutate.

Regret: measuring the cost of not knowing

To compare strategies we need a number. Regret is the reward you gave up compared with an all-knowing player who pulled the best arm every time. If the best arm pays 0.50 and you pull an arm that pays 0.30, you lose 0.20 in expectation on that pull. Total regret after T pulls is the sum of those losses:

R(T) = T · μ* − ∑t=1..T μat

where μ* is the best arm's true mean and μat the true mean of the arm chosen at step t. You never see regret while running (it needs the true means), but in a simulation you can compute it exactly, which is why we use simulations to study these methods. How regret grows is the real question. If it grows in proportion to T ("linear regret"), the strategy keeps making a constant fraction of mistakes forever. If it grows like log T, mistakes become rarer and rarer, which is the best any strategy can do in general.

Three standard strategies

Greedy always pulls the arm with the highest average so far. It has linear regret in the worst case, because an unlucky first few pulls on the best arm can banish it for good.

Epsilon-greedy pulls a uniformly random arm with probability ε (say 0.1) and the greedy arm otherwise. It fixes the lock-in problem but pays a permanent tax: even when it has identified the best arm, 10% of its pulls are random, so its regret is still linear in T, just with a small slope.

UCB1 (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer, "Finite-time analysis of the multiarmed bandit problem", 2002) is the one to understand deeply, because tree search is built on it. It picks the arm maximising an upper confidence bound:

  2. bonus ← c × sqrt(ln t ÷ n_i)
  3. UCB ← mean + bonus; pull the arm with the highest UCB
  4. later, t = 100 with pulls 40, 50, 10: the gap between arms narrows
"""UCB1 by hand on the chapter's three arms, at t = 20 pulls so far."""
import math

# ── 1. Three arms: average reward so far and pulls so far ────────────────
ARMS = {"A": (0.40, 10), "B": (0.50, 8), "C": (0.30, 2)}   # (mean, n_i)
T = 20                      # total pulls so far, the t in the formula
C = 0.5                     # exploration constant


# ── 2. The bonus, then mean plus bonus ───────────────────────────────────
def bonus(n_i, t=T, c=C):
    """c * sqrt(ln t / n_i): big when the arm is under-tried."""
    return c * math.sqrt(math.log(t) / n_i)

Here x̄i is the average reward of arm i, ni how many times it has been pulled, t the total pulls so far, and c an exploration constant. Read the second term as "how much more optimistic should I be about this arm?" It is large when ni is small (little evidence) and shrinks as the arm is tried. The ln t in the numerator grows slowly, so an arm that is neglected for a long time creeps back up the ranking until it is tried again. This principle is called optimism in the face of uncertainty: act as if each arm is as good as it plausibly could be, and let the evidence correct you.

Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933, in Biometrika, long before computers) takes a Bayesian route. Keep a belief about each arm's payout rate, draw one random sample from each belief, and pull the arm whose sample is highest. For a win-or-lose arm the belief is a Beta distribution with parameters (1 + wins, 1 + losses). Arms you know little about have wide beliefs, so they occasionally produce a high draw and get tried; arms you know well produce draws near their mean. Exploration emerges from randomness in the beliefs, with no constant to tune.

Go deeper where the UCB1 bonus comes from

Hoeffding's inequality says that for n independent rewards in [0, 1], the sample mean x̄ is within ε of the true mean μ except with probability at most exp(−2nε2). We want a bonus ε such that the true mean is almost surely below x̄ + ε, so that the bound really is an upper bound, and we want the failure probability to shrink as t grows, so that we stop being fooled. Choose the failure probability t−4. Setting exp(−2nε2) = t−4 gives 2nε2 = 4 ln t, so ε = √(2 ln t / n). That is UCB1 with c = √2 ≈ 1.41, the value in the original paper. Auer et al. then prove that the expected number of pulls of any arm with gap Δi below the best is at most about 8 ln T / Δi2 plus a constant, which gives logarithmic regret.

The proof uses the worst case over all reward distributions, so c = √2 is conservative. In practice a smaller c is often better, as the next experiment shows. This is a recurring pattern: theory fixes the shape of the bonus; practice tunes its size.

Running them

Here are the strategies on a three-armed bandit with win rates 0.30, 0.45 and 0.50. The gap between the best two arms is only 0.05, so telling them apart takes hundreds of pulls. Each strategy is run for 2,000 pulls, repeated over 200 random seeds, and we average the regret.

import math, random

ARMS = [0.30, 0.45, 0.50]          # true win rates, hidden from the learner
def pull(arm, rng):                # one noisy reward: 1 with prob ARMS[arm]
    return 1 if rng.random() < ARMS[arm] else 0

def run(policy, steps=2000, seed=0, c=math.sqrt(2)):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n = [0] * len(ARMS); total = [0.0] * len(ARMS); reward = 0
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        if 0 in n:                         # play every arm once first
            a = n.index(0)
        elif policy == "greedy":
            a = max(range(3), key=lambda i: total[i] / n[i])
        elif policy == "eps":
            a = rng.randrange(3) if rng.random() < 0.1 else max(range(3), key=lambda i: total[i] / n[i])
        else:                              # UCB1
            a = max(range(3), key=lambda i: total[i] / n[i] + c * math.sqrt(math.log(t) / n[i]))
        r = pull(a, rng); n[a] += 1; total[a] += r; reward += r
    regret = steps * max(ARMS) - sum(n[i] * ARMS[i] for i in range(3))
    return reward, n, regret

    regs, counts = [], [0, 0, 0]
    for seed in range(200):
        _, n, g = run(policy, seed=seed, c=c)
        regs.append(g); counts = [c + x for c, x in zip(counts, n)]

Read these numbers carefully, because they contradict a common summary. Greedy is worst: with 36% of pulls on the best arm it is close to picking at random after its early luck. Epsilon-greedy with ε = 0.1 beats UCB1 with the textbook constant c = 1.41 here, because on this problem the textbook constant over-explores: the arms are close, the bonus is wide relative to the gaps, and 2,000 pulls is not very long. But UCB1 with c = 0.5 beats everything (regret 23, 82% of pulls on the best arm), while c = 0.2 is too greedy and its regret climbs back to 49. The exploration constant matters more than the choice of family. The algorithm names are less important than knowing which knob sets the exploration, and tuning it on a simulation of your own problem.

Common misconception

"UCB is provably optimal, so the default constant is the right one." The guarantee is about the shape of the regret curve in the worst case, not about the best constant on your problem. Two arms that differ by 0.05 and two that differ by 0.4 want very different amounts of exploration. Treat c as a hyperparameter and tune it on a simulator of your task, exactly as you would a learning rate.

Systems connection

Bandits are how a platform runs experiments that adapt as evidence arrives. Fixed A/B tests, covered in A1 chapter 21, send equal traffic to every variant until a sample-size target is hit; a bandit shifts traffic toward the winner during the test, which reduces the cost of testing a bad variant but makes the final statistical comparison harder to interpret. Progressive rollouts are the safety-first cousin of the same idea: expose a new version to a little traffic, watch, and decide whether to widen it.

From one decision to a tree

A bandit has one decision. A game or a planning problem has a sequence of decisions, each of which opens a new set of choices. Now suppose we treat the choice at every node of the tree as its own small bandit: the arms are the moves, the reward is eventually winning. Then UCB tells us how to descend the tree, always taking the move that is best-or-most-uncertain. This is the key step, made by Kocsis and Szepesvári ("Bandit based Monte-Carlo planning", 2006), who called the resulting algorithm UCT (UCB applied to trees). The name Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) was coined in the same year by Rémi Coulom in a paper on a Go program; Browne et al.'s 2012 survey is the standard overview.

The word "Monte Carlo" means the value of a position is estimated by random sampling: play the game out with random moves many times and count how often you win. A position that wins 70% of random playouts is probably better than one that wins 40%, even though random play is terrible play. The estimate is noisy but needs no knowledge of the game beyond its rules.

The four phases

MCTS builds a tree gradually, one iteration at a time. Each iteration does four things, and the loop can stop whenever the time or compute budget runs out (an "anytime" algorithm):

  1. Selection. Starting at the root, walk down the tree. At each node that has all its moves tried already, pick the child with the highest UCB score. Stop at a node that still has an untried move.
  2. Expansion. Add one new child node for an untried move.
  3. Simulation (also called a rollout). From the new node, play random moves to the end of the game and record who won.
  4. Backpropagation. Walk back up the path from the new node to the root, adding one visit to every node and adding the result to its win count. The result is flipped at each level, because what is a win for one player is a loss for the other.

After many iterations the tree is lopsided. It is deep where moves keep looking good and shallow where they do not. When time is up, the engine plays the root's child with the most visits, not the highest win rate. Visit counts are robust: a child with a 100% win rate from one lucky visit has been barely looked at, while a child with 1,940 visits and a 95% win rate has been tested repeatedly and survived.

The step-through below uses a tiny tree so you can see the numbers. Each node shows wins / visits from the point of view of whoever moved into it. The exploration constant is c = 1.

Step through

One MCTS iteration on a small tree

  1. The tree so far. The root has been visited 4 times: once for B (a loss) and three times for A. A has one explored child, A1, and one untried move. B also has an untried move (dashed). Each node's record is wins over visits for whoever moved into it.
  2. Selection. At the root both children have been tried, so choose by UCB with c = 1. A scores 2/3 + √(ln 4 / 3) = 0.67 + 0.68 = 1.35. B scores 0 + √(ln 4 / 1) = 1.18. A wins: it has a good record and is not neglected. A still has an untried move, so selection stops there.
  3. Expansion. Add a node for A's untried move, A2. It starts with no wins and no visits.
  4. Simulation. From A2, both sides play random moves to the end of the game. In this iteration we happen to win. The tree is not changed during a rollout; its only output is the result.
  5. Backpropagation. The result flows up the path. A2 gets a visit (and no win, since the player who moved into A2 is our opponent, who lost). A, which our side moved into, gets a visit and a win: 3 wins / 4 visits. The root's count becomes 5. Repeat thousands of times, then play the most-visited child.

A complete implementation

Time to run it. The game is Nim: a pile of stones, players alternate taking 1, 2 or 3, and whoever takes the last stone wins. It is a good test because it has a known answer: leave your opponent a multiple of 4 and you win. From a pile of 10 the right move is to take 2, leaving 8. The code below is a complete UCT search with random rollouts, in about forty lines. Notice that nothing in mcts knows the multiple-of-4 rule; it learns what to do only from simulated games.

import math, random

# Nim: a pile of stones, players alternate taking 1, 2 or 3; taking the last stone wins.
def moves(pile): return [m for m in (1, 2, 3) if m <= pile]

class Node:
    def __init__(self, pile, parent=None, move=None):
        self.pile, self.parent, self.move = pile, parent, move
        self.children, self.untried = [], moves(pile)
        self.visits, self.wins = 0, 0.0      # wins for the player who just moved INTO this node

def rollout(pile, rng):
    """Random play to the end. Returns True if the player to move at `pile` wins."""
    to_move_wins = True
    while pile > 0:
        pile -= rng.choice(moves(pile))
        if pile == 0: return to_move_wins
        to_move_wins = not to_move_wins
    return False                              # pile already 0: the player to move has lost

def mcts(pile, iters, rng, c=1.0):
    root = Node(pile)
    for _ in range(iters):
        node = root
        while not node.untried and node.children:                # 1. selection
            node = max(node.children, key=lambda k: k.wins / k.visits +
                       c * math.sqrt(math.log(node.visits) / k.visits))
        if node.untried:                                          # 2. expansion
            m = node.untried.pop(rng.randrange(len(node.untried)))
            child = Node(node.pile - m, node, m); node.children.append(child); node = child
        mover_wins = not rollout(node.pile, rng)                  # 3. simulation (mover = who moved into node)
        while node:                                               # 4. backpropagation
            node.visits += 1; node.wins += mover_wins
            mover_wins = not mover_wins; node = node.parent
    return root

rng = random.Random(0)
root = mcts(10, 2000, rng)
print("pile=10, 2000 iterations. Best move is 2 (leaves 8, a multiple of 4).")
for k in sorted(root.children, key=lambda k: k.move):
    print(f"  take {k.move}: visits {k.visits:5d}  win rate {k.wins / k.visits:.2f}")

print("\nshare of winnable piles where the most-visited move is optimal (5 seeds per pile)")
print("  iterations   piles 1-12   piles 13-24   piles 25-40")
for iters in (10, 50, 200, 1000, 5000):
    row = []
    for lo, hi in ((1, 12), (13, 24), (25, 40)):
        ok = tot = 0
        for pile in range(lo, hi + 1):
            if pile % 4 == 0: continue
            for rep in range(5):
                r = mcts(pile, iters, random.Random(pile * 100 + rep))
                best = max(r.children, key=lambda k: k.visits).move
                ok += (pile - best) % 4 == 0; tot += 1
        row.append(f"{ok / tot:7.1%}")
    print(f"  {iters:9d}   " + "      ".join(row))

Why random rollouts hit a wall

The weakness is the rollout. Nim is deep: from a pile of 37 the game lasts about 18 moves. A random player makes a mistake almost every turn, so after a few random moves the outcome is close to a coin flip regardless of which first move was good. The signal "this move leads to a position that wins by force" is drowned by noise from the dozens of random moves after it. Only when the tree grows deep enough that the random part is short does the signal come back, and growing a tree that deep takes exponentially many iterations.

The same problem hits any domain where random continuations are far worse than competent ones: a random sequence of edits to a contract, random merges in a schedule, random tokens from a language model. Three fixes were used before neural networks: hand-written rollout policies (play "sensible" moves instead of random), hand-written evaluation functions that cut the rollout short, and domain tricks such as RAVE, which shares statistics between related moves. All three need an expert. AlphaGo's contribution was to learn them from data.

The AlphaZero idea

AlphaGo (Silver et al., "Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search", Nature, 2016) combined MCTS with two neural networks: a policy network that proposes which moves look promising, and a value network that estimates who is winning from a position. It beat the European champion Fan Hui 5 to 0 and then the world-class professional Lee Sedol 4 to 1 in 2016. The first version learned partly from human expert games. AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., Nature, 2017) removed that, learning from self-play alone, and AlphaZero (Silver et al., arXiv 1712.01815, 2017; later published in Science, 2018) used one algorithm for chess, shogi and Go. The authors report that starting from random play and knowing only the rules, it reached superhuman strength in all three within 24 hours of training. The detailed story is the subject of case study 1; here is the mechanism.

One network, two heads

A single network takes a board position and produces two outputs. The policy head p gives a probability for every legal move: a prior belief about which moves are worth searching. The value head v gives a number in [−1, 1] (or [0, 1] in our code) predicting the final result for the player to move. Search uses both:

  • The value v replaces the random rollout. When a new leaf is added, you do not play to the end; you ask the network. The result is one network evaluation instead of a long, noisy simulation.
  • The prior p replaces uniform exploration. Instead of trying every move once before preferring any, the search starts by exploring the moves the network thinks are good.

The selection rule changes to match. AlphaGo Zero's version, usually called PUCT (predictor plus UCT), picks the move maximising

score(s, a) = Q(s, a) + cpuct · P(s, a) · √( ∑b N(s, b) ) / ( 1 + N(s, a) )

Q is the average value found so far for that move, P the network's prior for it, N the visit counts, and cpuct a constant. Compare with UCB1: the bonus is still "large when this move has few visits compared with the parent", but it is now multiplied by the prior, so moves the network dislikes get a small bonus and are explored only after the good ones have been checked. The "1 +" in the denominator lets unvisited moves have a finite score, so no special case for untried moves is needed.

Search as a policy improvement operator

The training targets are the elegant part. After running, say, 800 simulations from a position, the visit counts at the root form a probability distribution over moves. This distribution is better than the network's raw prior p, because it incorporates lookahead and corrections. So the network is trained to move p toward the visit distribution, and v toward the actual game result. The AlphaGo Zero paper describes the search as a "policy improvement operator" and the game result as the "policy evaluation operator". In plain terms: search is a way of making the policy better than it was, using computation; training is a way of storing that improvement in the weights so the next search starts from a better place. Repeat. The loop needs no examples of good play, only the rules and a way to detect a win.

The 2017 paper reports 800 simulations per move during training (and compares search speed: tens of thousands of positions per second for AlphaZero against tens of millions for the traditional engine it beat, the point being that a good network lets you search far less). We cannot train a network here, but we can show the effect of replacing the rollout with a value estimate and the uniform exploration with a prior. The stand-ins below are hand-written, not learned: the prior favours moves that leave a multiple of 4, and the "value network" knows who is winning but is deliberately wrong 20% of the time, as a real network would be. Errors are fixed at each node, not re-sampled, so they are not averaged away.

import math, random

def moves(pile): return [m for m in (1, 2, 3) if m <= pile]

# Stand-ins for a trained network. NOT learned: hand-built, and deliberately wrong 20% of the time.
def net(pile, rng, informed=True):
    """Return (priors over moves, value for the player to move in [0,1])."""
    winning = pile % 4 != 0
    value = 0.9 if winning else 0.1
    if rng.random() < 0.2: value = 1 - value                  # a 20% error rate
    raw = {m: (3.0 if informed and (pile - m) % 4 == 0 else 1.0) for m in moves(pile)}
    z = sum(raw.values())
    return {m: v / z for m, v in raw.items()}, value

class Node:
    def __init__(self, pile, prior):
        self.pile, self.prior = pile, prior
        self.children, self.visits, self.value_sum = {}, 0, 0.0   # value_sum: from the view of the player to move HERE
    def q(self): return self.value_sum / self.visits if self.visits else 0.0

def puct_search(pile, iters, rng, c_puct=1.5, informed=True):
    root = Node(pile, 1.0)
    for _ in range(iters):
        node, path = root, [root]
        while node.children and node.pile > 0:
            sqrt_n = math.sqrt(node.visits)
            def score(m):                                       # Q (negated: child is the opponent) + U
                ch = node.children[m]
                return -ch.q() + c_puct * ch.prior * sqrt_n / (1 + ch.visits)
            m = max(node.children, key=score)
            node = node.children[m]; path.append(node)
        if node.pile == 0: value = 0.0                          # player to move has lost
        else:
            priors, value = net(node.pile, rng, informed)
            node.children = {m: Node(node.pile - m, p) for m, p in priors.items()}
        for n in reversed(path):                                # backup, flipping perspective each level
            n.visits += 1; n.value_sum += value; value = 1 - value
    return root

Compare with the random-rollout table. With no useful prior (uniform), a value estimate that is right 80% of the time already gets 58 to 73% correct on piles of 25 to 40 at 5 to 500 iterations, where rollouts managed 25 to 30%. The value estimates do not make the search perfect, because a wrong estimate at a node stays wrong; without a prior, more iterations do not reliably repair this. Add an informative prior and the picture changes: 93 to 100% correct with only five simulations and 100% by twenty. A good prior makes the search narrow, so the value errors have fewer places to hurt. This is the whole AlphaZero bargain in miniature: pay once for a learned model, then search a thousand times less.

Analogy: the chess coach and the analyst

An experienced player glances at a position and some moves simply "look right" (the policy), and has a feel for who is better (the value). That intuition is fast but sometimes wrong. When the position is critical they stop and calculate: this move, then that reply, then... (the search). Calculation corrects intuition, and over years of games the corrections retrain the intuition. AlphaZero is that cycle, run at machine speed from a standing start.

Where it breaks down: a person's intuition comes from a lifetime of varied experience; the network is trained only on positions that its own search generated, so it can develop blind spots that no opponent in its training ever exploited. Adversarial work on superhuman Go programs later found simple strategies that fooled them, which is a reminder that self-play coverage is not the same as total coverage.

State of play, October 2026

The AlphaZero line continued as MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2019, arXiv 1911.08265), which learns its own model of the game's dynamics instead of being given the rules, and as the planning component in other DeepMind systems. Tree search guided by a learned model is also an active idea for language models: sampling several reasoning paths and searching over them is covered in chapter 8, including Sakana AI's adaptive-branching AB-MCTS (arXiv 2503.04412, March 2025). The details of specific models and benchmark results change quickly; the selection rule and the loop above are the durable part.

Making it work in practice

Choosing the final move

Pick by visit count, as above, or by a "robust child" rule that requires both a high visit count and a high value. Do not pick by raw win rate. When your budget is spent, prefer the answer that has survived the most scrutiny.

Rollout and evaluation costs

The cost of an iteration is dominated by the evaluation step: a rollout in a cheap simulator costs microseconds; a neural network evaluation costs a GPU call; asking a language model to judge a candidate costs a network round trip and a fraction of a cent. That changes the design. With cheap rollouts you run millions of iterations and the tree barely matters; with a 2-second evaluation you can afford a few hundred, so the quality of selection (and any prior) matters enormously. Every extension in the later chapters is about getting more value from each expensive evaluation.

Parallel search

MCTS is easy to start in parallel and surprisingly awkward to do well. If 64 workers all descend the same tree at once, they all pick the same promising leaf. The standard fix is virtual loss: when a worker passes through a node on its way down, temporarily count a visit with a loss, so the next worker is steered elsewhere; the virtual loss is replaced by the real result when the evaluation finishes. Batching many leaf evaluations into one GPU call is the same batching trade-off as in any GPU workload: more throughput, at the price of staler statistics.

When tree search is the wrong tool

  • The state is not a sequence of choices. If a candidate is a whole object (a prompt, a program, a schedule) that you mutate, trees fit poorly and evolutionary methods (next chapter) are a better match.
  • Evaluation is noisy and costly. Each node's statistics are averages of noisy results; when each sample costs real money, plain best-of-n with a good verifier may beat a tree that spends samples on bookkeeping.
  • Values are not comparable across a tree. UCB assumes rewards in a fixed range. Unbounded scores, such as raw speedup factors, need normalising or a rank-based reward.
  • The environment is adversarial and the model of the opponent is wrong. Search against a modelled opponent can be exploited by a different one, as the Go example above shows.

Key takeaways

  • Search is deciding which candidate to evaluate next under a budget. The core trade-off is exploration versus exploitation, measured by regret.
  • UCB adds an optimism bonus c√(ln t / n) that is large for rarely tried options. The constant c sets how much you explore and should be tuned, not copied.
  • MCTS runs select, expand, simulate, back up, repeatedly; it is anytime, and you play the most-visited child.
  • Random rollouts give a weak signal when games are deep; in our Nim experiment accuracy fell from 100% to about 25 to 30% as piles grew.
  • AlphaZero replaces rollouts with a learned value and uniform exploration with a learned prior (PUCT), then trains the network on the search's own visit counts and the game results.
  • A good prior narrows the search: a few simulations with an informative prior beat hundreds without one.

Check your understanding

A greedy bandit strategy pulls the arm with the best average so far. What is its main weakness?

  1. It needs to know the true payout rates in advance
  2. Early bad luck on the truly best arm can make it ignore that arm permanently
  3. It explores too much when the arms are close in value
  4. It cannot compute averages when rewards are 0 or 1
Greedy never deliberately tries an arm that looks worse, so a few unlucky pulls on the best arm can hide it for good. It does not need the true rates (option 1), and it explores too little, not too much (option 3).

In the bandit experiment, UCB1 with the textbook constant c = 1.41 had higher regret than epsilon-greedy over 2,000 pulls, but c = 0.5 beat both. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The UCB1 proof must be wrong
  2. Epsilon-greedy is generally better than UCB for short runs
  3. The proven constant is a worst-case choice; on a given problem the exploration constant should be tuned
  4. Lower c is always better
The regret guarantee is about the shape of growth over all possible problems, not about the best constant on yours. Lower is not always better: c = 0.2 was too greedy and its regret climbed back to 49.

After running MCTS for its whole budget, which child of the root should the engine play?

  1. The most-visited child
  2. The child with the highest win rate, even if it has been visited once
  3. The child with the highest UCB score
  4. The child chosen last during the search
Visit count reflects both a good record and repeated testing. A high win rate from one visit is mostly luck, and the UCB score deliberately includes an optimism bonus, which is for exploring, not for the final decision.

In the Nim experiment, plain MCTS with random rollouts was 100% accurate on piles up to 12 but only about 25 to 30% on piles of 25 to 40. Why?

  1. The exploration constant was too small
  2. Python is too slow for large piles
  3. Large piles have more branches per turn
  4. Random play for many moves washes out the signal about which first move was good
The branching factor is 3 at every pile size (option 3 is false). The difference is depth: a long random continuation makes most positions look like coin flips, so the rollout result carries little information about the first move.

What are the two things a policy-and-value network lets AlphaZero-style search do better than plain MCTS?

  1. Skip selection and backpropagation entirely
  2. Replace the random rollout with a value estimate, and focus exploration with a learned prior over moves
  3. Store every possible position in a table
  4. Remove the need to know the rules of the game
The tree, selection and backup are still there. The value head replaces the rollout and the policy head weights the exploration bonus. AlphaZero is given the rules; the later MuZero learns a model of them, which is a separate step.

What is the training target for the policy head in AlphaZero?

  1. The move a human expert played
  2. One-hot vector of the winning move at the end of the game
  3. The distribution of visit counts at the root after search
  4. The network's own previous output
Visit counts after search are a better policy than the raw network output, so training the network toward them improves the network. A human move would be supervised learning (used in the first AlphaGo, not in the Zero versions), and the one-hot winning move ignores what search learned about alternatives.
