Genetic Programming, Neuroevolution & CMA-ES

Some answers are programs. Some are small networks whose shape is not known in advance. Some are lists of real numbers that interact in awkward ways. This chapter covers three evolutionary methods, one for each kind of answer, and none of them needs a gradient.

Why this matters

Many things worth improving are not smooth numbers. A scoring rule, a small network and a set of twenty knobs are all examples. Gradient descent, the usual way to improve a model, cannot always be used on them. Older evolutionary methods can, and they are the base that newer language-model-driven systems build on. After this chapter you can grow and cross programs stored as trees. You can line up two networks of different shapes so that they can breed. You can also tune a dozen interacting numbers far faster than by plain random search. And you can say what a language model adds over each of these older tools.

Three kinds of search space

Chapter 2 built an evolutionary algorithm on bit strings and then on vectors of real numbers. Real problems are less tidy. The thing you want to improve may be a program, a network whose layout is not fixed, or a set of knobs that affect one another. Each of these needs its own kind of genome, the data structure that encodes one candidate.

Chapter 2 gave the loop: score a population, select parents, vary them, repeat. That loop stays the same here. What changes is the genome and the variation operators. This chapter gives one classic method for each kind of genome.

The three names expand as follows. Genetic programming (GP) evolves programs. NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (NEAT) evolves neural networks, adding structure as it goes. The covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) is an evolution strategy (ES), the real-number branch of the family from chapter 2, that learns the shape of its own steps.

Genetic programming

The genome is a program stored as a tree. Variation cuts and grafts branches. Use it when the answer is a formula or a rule.

NEAT

The genome is a small network: its connections and its weights. Variation adds nodes and links. Use it when the right shape is unknown.

CMA-ES

The genome is a vector of real numbers. The search learns which directions are worth stepping in. Use it for tuning knobs.

You will need two earlier ideas. Python's ast module shows how code is stored as a tree (programs as data). The words genotype, phenotype and neutral change come from evolution for builders. Both are explained again where they appear.

Genetic programming

John Koza set out genetic programming in his 1992 book Genetic Programming: On the Programming of Computers by Means of Natural Selection. His key move is simple to state. A candidate is not a string of characters. It is a tree, and trees can be cut and joined without breaking them.

Programs as expression trees

Take the best-fit rule for bin packing. It scores a bin by item - rem, where item is the size of the object to place and rem is the space left in the bin. As a tree, the root is the minus sign. Its two children are the leaves item and rem. A bigger rule, such as max(rem * 2, item - 1), is a bigger tree with the same ingredients.

Two lists define the whole search space. The function set holds the operations that may appear at internal nodes. In this chapter it is +, -, *, max and min, each taking two inputs. The terminal set holds the leaves: the inputs item and rem, and the constants 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20 and 50. That is nine terminals and five functions.

How big is that space? Count trees of depth at most 2 by hand. With no operations there are 9 leaves. Trees of depth at most 1 are those 9 leaves plus 5 × 9 × 9 = 405 one-operation trees, so 414 in all. Trees of depth at most 2 are the 9 leaves plus 5 × 414 × 414 = 856,980 trees, which makes 856,989. Each extra level squares the count, so the space explodes. This is why we search it rather than list it.

Every tree built from these two sets is a valid program. Koza called this the closure property: any function can accept the output of any other function or terminal without error. Our values are all numbers, so closure holds. If you add division you must protect it, for instance by returning 1 when the divisor is zero. Otherwise some trees would crash and closure would fail.

Python's ast module stores code in the same shape: a node for an operation, with its operands as children. Our nested tuples are a tiny syntax tree that we control. We evaluate a tree by walking it, so no evolved text is ever passed to exec. When your trees are real Python, the sandboxing rules in running candidates apply.

Making the first generation

A GP run starts from random trees, so we need a way to build them. There are two basic methods. The full method makes every branch reach the depth limit. A full tree of depth 2 with two-input functions has 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 nodes. The grow method picks a function or a terminal at random at each node, so branches stop at different depths and the shapes vary.

Koza combined them as ramped half-and-half. Half of the starting trees use full and half use grow, and the depth limit steps through a range such as 2, 3, 4. The point is variety. If every starting tree had one shape and size, crossover would have little to recombine. The function ramped in the script below does this with a counter: the depth is 2 + i % 3 and even indices use full.

Subtree crossover and subtree mutation

In chapter 2, one-point crossover cut two bit strings at a position and swapped the tails. The tree version cuts a branch. Subtree crossover picks a random node in each parent. The child is parent A with the subtree at its chosen node replaced by the subtree at B's chosen node. Subtree mutation is the same move with a donor that is a small fresh random tree.

Step through

Subtree crossover on two priority functions

  1. Two parents. A scores bins by (item + 3) - rem. B scores them by max(rem * 2, item - 1). Both are valid trees.
  2. Choose a cut in A. A random node is picked, here the plus node. Its whole subtree, (item + 3), is the part that will be replaced.
  3. Choose a donor in B. A random node in B is picked, here the minus node. Its subtree, (item - 1), is the donor.
  4. Graft. The child is A with the donor in the cut. It is (item - 1) - rem, a valid tree, and it was built without checking anything.

The child in the figure has a nice property. It ranks bins exactly as item - rem does. Within one placement decision item is the same number for every bin, so subtracting 1 from it shifts every score equally. The order of the bins does not change. Biologists call a change like this neutral: the genotype changed and the behaviour did not. Neutral changes are common in GP, and they matter for the bloat problem below.

One safety rule is worth copying. Our crossover and mutate return the unchanged parent if the child would be deeper than MAX_DEPTH. Without a cap, repeated grafting can make trees that are slow to evaluate and hard to read.

Analogy: swapping steps between two recipes

Imagine two recipe cards for a sauce. You pick one step on each card, say "simmer the tomatoes" on the first and "fry the onions" on the second, and swap them. The new card still reads like a recipe. A cook can follow it without any error. Whether the sauce tastes good is a separate question that only a taste test can answer. That is how subtree crossover works: the swap is always legal, and the score decides if it was wise.

Where it breaks down: in a real recipe, a step depends on the steps around it. "Add the stock" only makes sense after "fry the onions". A subtree has the same dependence on its position, but the tree swap pays no attention to it.

A complete run

The script below is a full GP system for the bin-packing task. Items are 20 to 60 units and bins hold 100. A tree is a priority(item, rem) function, and the item goes into the feasible bin with the highest score. The score of a tree is the mean number of bins used on six fixed instances of 100 items, plus a small charge per node. Lower is better, so selection uses min. The charge is the parsimony pressure explained in the next section.

The idea

  1. run(tree): leaves return inputs; nodes combine their children
  2. random_tree: grow may stop early, full never does; ramp the depth
  3. crossover: swap a subtree of A for one of B; mutate: regrow a subtree
  4. cost ← mean bins on 6 instances + PARSIMONY × tree size
  5. repeat: rank, keep the elite, breed by tournament and crossover
"""Genetic programming for online bin packing: evolve priority(item, rem).

A program is an expression tree, stored as nested tuples (op, left, right).
A leaf is the string 'item' or 'rem', or an integer constant.
"""
import random

CAP = 100                    # bin capacity
OPS = ("+", "-", "*", "max", "min")   # function set
CONSTS = (1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50)      # constant terminals
MAX_DEPTH = 6                # hard cap, so crossover cannot grow trees forever
POP, GENS, TOURN = 12, 12, 3
MUT_RATE = 0.2             # chance a child also gets subtree mutation
PARSIMONY = 0.02             # bins "charged" per node: penalises bloat


# ── 1. Evaluate a tree ───────────────────────────────────────────────
def run(tree, item, rem):
    if tree == "item":
        return item
    if tree == "rem":
        return rem
    if isinstance(tree, int):
        return tree
    op, left, right = tree
    a = run(left, item, rem)
    b = run(right, item, rem)
    if op == "+":
        return a + b
    if op == "-":
        return a - b
    if op == "*":
        return a * b
    if op == "max":
        return max(a, b)
    return min(a, b)


def size(tree):
    if isinstance(tree, tuple):
        return 1 + size(tree[1]) + size(tree[2])
    return 1


def show(tree):
    if isinstance(tree, tuple):
        op, left, right = tree
        if op in ("max", "min"):
            return f"{op}({show(left)}, {show(right)})"
        return f"({show(left)} {op} {show(right)})"
    return str(tree)


# ── 2. Random trees: grow, full, ramped half-and-half ────────────────
def leaf(rng):
    return rng.choice(["item", "rem", rng.choice(CONSTS)])


def random_tree(rng, depth, full):
    stop = depth == 0 or (not full and rng.random() < 0.3)
    if stop:
        return leaf(rng)
    return (rng.choice(OPS), random_tree(rng, depth - 1, full),
            random_tree(rng, depth - 1, full))


def ramped(rng, n):
    return [random_tree(rng, 2 + i % 3, i % 2 == 0) for i in range(n)]


# ── 3. Subtree crossover and subtree mutation ────────────────────────
def paths(tree, here=()):
    yield here
    if isinstance(tree, tuple):
        yield from paths(tree[1], here + (1,))
        yield from paths(tree[2], here + (2,))


def get(tree, path):
    for step in path:
        tree = tree[step]
    return tree


def put(tree, path, new):
    if not path:
        return new
    parts = list(tree)
    parts[path[0]] = put(tree[path[0]], path[1:], new)
    return tuple(parts)


def depth(tree):
    if isinstance(tree, tuple):
        return 1 + max(depth(tree[1]), depth(tree[2]))
    return 0


def crossover(rng, a, b):
    point = rng.choice(list(paths(a)))
    donor = get(b, rng.choice(list(paths(b))))
    child = put(a, point, donor)
    return child if depth(child) <= MAX_DEPTH else a


def mutate(rng, tree):
    point = rng.choice(list(paths(tree)))
    child = put(tree, point, random_tree(rng, 2, False))
    return child if depth(child) <= MAX_DEPTH else tree


# ── 4. Fitness: mean bins over fixed instances (lower is better) ─────
def instance(seed, n=100):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [rng.randint(20, 60) for _ in range(n)]


def bins_used(tree, items):
    rems = []
    for item in items:
        best, best_score = None, None
        for i, rem in enumerate(rems):
            if rem >= item:
                score = run(tree, item, rem)
                if best is None or score > best_score:
                    best, best_score = i, score
        if best is None:
            rems.append(CAP - item)
        else:
            rems[best] -= item
    return len(rems)


TRAIN = [instance(s) for s in range(6)]
TEST = [instance(s) for s in range(100, 120)]


def mean_bins(tree, data):
    return sum(bins_used(tree, d) for d in data) / len(data)


CACHE = {}   # trees are tuples, so identical trees are scored only once


def cost(tree, parsimony):
    if tree not in CACHE:
        CACHE[tree] = mean_bins(tree, TRAIN)
    return CACHE[tree] + parsimony * size(tree)


# ── 5. The generational loop ─────────────────────────────────────────
def tournament(rng, scored):
    return min(rng.sample(scored, TOURN), key=lambda s: s[0])[1]


def evolve(seed, parsimony=PARSIMONY, verbose=True):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pop = ramped(rng, POP)
    sizes = []
    shown = None
    for gen in range(GENS + 1):
        scored = sorted(((cost(t, parsimony), t) for t in pop),
                        key=lambda s: s[0])
        best_cost, best = scored[0]
        sizes.append(sum(size(t) for t in pop) / POP)
        if verbose and best != shown:
            shown = best
            print(f"gen {gen:2d} cost {best_cost:6.2f} "
                  f"size {size(best):2d}  {show(best)}")
        nxt = [best]
        while len(nxt) < POP:
            a = tournament(rng, scored)
            b = tournament(rng, scored)
            child = crossover(rng, a, b)
            if rng.random() < MUT_RATE:
                child = mutate(rng, child)
            nxt.append(child)
        pop = nxt
    return best, sizes


if __name__ == "__main__":
    best, _ = evolve(seed=2)
    print("test bins, evolved:   ", mean_bins(best, TEST))
    print("test bins, first fit: ", mean_bins(0, TEST))
    print("test bins, best fit:  ", mean_bins(("-", "item", "rem"), TEST))
gen  0 cost  44.69 size  1  item
gen  1 cost  44.59 size 13  (max(min((item * item), (1 + item)), 2) * (item - rem))
gen  2 cost  44.47 size  7  ((item + item) + (item - rem))
gen  3 cost  44.39 size  3  (item - rem)
test bins, evolved:    44.6
test bins, first fit:  44.7
test bins, best fit:   44.6

Read the output from the top. Generation 0 starts from the single leaf item. It ignores rem, so every feasible bin ties and the first one wins. That is first fit. Its cost, 44.69, is the mean 44.67 bins plus 0.02 for one node.

In generation 1 a 13-node tree appears that contains item - rem inside a larger expression. Generation 2 has a 7-node tree. By generation 3 the elite is the 3-node tree item - rem, which is exactly best fit. The cost 44.39 is 44.33 bins plus 0.06. The last three lines score the winner on 20 fresh instances: 44.6 bins for the evolved rule and for best fit, 44.7 for first fit. The lower bound for these instances is about 41.0 bins, so every online rule here leaves room.

Be honest about what happened. GP rediscovered a rule that humans already knew. It did not beat it. The tree space is small and closed, and the best rule inside it is best fit. Other seeds end on 10 - rem or 3 - rem, which rank bins identically to best fit for the same neutral reason as before.

Bloat and parsimony pressure

GP has a well-known failure called bloat: trees grow larger over the generations without getting any better. A tree that is 40 nodes long may compute exactly what a 3-node tree computes. The extra nodes are called introns, a word borrowed from genetics for stretches that do nothing.

The usual explanation is protective. Crossover often lands in the middle of a good tree and breaks it. A tree padded with useless nodes has a better chance that the cut falls in the padding, so its children survive. Selection never asked for padding, but padded trees leave more working offspring. The research literature, such as Poli, Langdon and McPhee's A Field Guide to Genetic Programming, discusses several competing theories. Treat the one above as the common story, not as settled.

The simplest cure is parsimony pressure: charge for size. Our cost adds PARSIMONY × size to the bin count. A rule with a cost of 44.33 + 0.02 × 3 beats an equal-scoring rule with 13 nodes. The next script runs 30 generations over ten seeds, with no charge and with a charge of 0.05 bins per node.

The idea

  1. settings: ten seeds, 30 generations, four generations to report
  2. for each parsimony value, run all seeds and average the tree sizes
"""Bloat: mean tree size per generation, with and without parsimony."""
import gp_binpack as gp

# ── 1. Settings ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SEEDS = range(10)       # average over ten runs to smooth the noise
gp.GENS = 30            # a longer run than the default 12
SHOW = (0, 10, 20, 30)  # generations to print

# ── 2. Run with and without a charge per node ────────────────────────
for parsimony in (0.0, 0.05):
    rows = [gp.evolve(s, parsimony, verbose=False) for s in SEEDS]
    mean_size = [sum(r[1][g] for r in rows) / len(rows) for g in SHOW]
    test = sum(gp.mean_bins(r[0], gp.TEST) for r in rows) / len(rows)
    sizes = " ".join(f"{m:6.1f}" for m in mean_size)
    print(f"parsimony {parsimony:4.2f}: mean size at gens {SHOW} = {sizes}"
          f"  | test bins {test:.2f}")
parsimony 0.00: mean size at gens (0, 10, 20, 30) =   12.7   18.8   19.1   18.3  | test bins 44.60
parsimony 0.05: mean size at gens (0, 10, 20, 30) =   12.7    3.8    4.0    4.0  | test bins 44.60

Without the charge, mean tree size drifts from 12.7 nodes to about 19 and stays there. With the charge it falls to 4. The held-out score is the same, 44.60 bins, in both settings. So the extra 15 nodes bought nothing. The effect is mild here because the population is small, the runs are short and MAX_DEPTH caps growth. In long runs with big populations it is much stronger.

Parsimony pressure has its own risk. A charge set too high rewards tiny programs even when a larger one is truly better. Here 0.05 bins per node is small next to the gaps between rules, so it breaks ties without overruling real improvements.

Common misconception

"Crossover combines the best parts of two parents." It only swaps subtrees. A subtree gets its meaning from where it sits, so a part that was good in B can be useless or harmful in A. In the stepper above, the graft changed nothing at all, because the new subtree landed where a constant shift does not matter. Crossover works as a source of new combinations that selection then judges. The credit for the good ones goes to selection.

What a language model's prior buys

Chapter 9 uses a language model as the mutation operator. Now that you have seen the older operator, the comparison is concrete. A prior here means what the operator knows before it sees your problem. Subtree mutation knows only the function and terminal sets. A code-trained language model knows how millions of programs look.

Subtree GPLanguage model edit
What changesA branch of a treeAny part of the source text
Always valid?Yes, by closureNo: may not parse, may loop forever

The trade is about where the cost lands. GP wastes many cheap evaluations on blind edits. The language model wastes few evaluations but pays for each proposal. If one bin-packing evaluation takes a millisecond, blind search is fine. If one evaluation trains a model for an hour, you want every proposal to count. Both still depend on the evaluator to separate good from bad, and the language model adds the sandboxing work that closure gives GP for free.

Neuroevolution

Neuroevolution uses evolution to build neural networks. A network has two parts that can be searched: its weights, the numbers on the connections, and its topology, the list of which units connect to which. Neuroevolution searches one or both with fitness scores instead of gradients.

Evolving weights on a fixed shape

The easy case fixes the topology and evolves the weights. The genome is the list of weights, and everything from chapter 2 applies. Salimans et al. (2017) did this with an evolution strategy and spread it over a thousand workers. Such et al. (2017), in "Deep Neuroevolution", showed that even a plain genetic algorithm with mutation and no crossover could train networks with over four million weights on Atari games. Their compact encoding stores each network as the short list of random seeds that built it, which makes the genome tiny to ship between machines.

This route is simple and it parallelises well, because every candidate is scored independently. Its limit is that a person chose the shape. Evolution cannot discover that the problem needs one more hidden unit, or fewer.

Why evolving the shape is hard

Letting the topology evolve sounds natural, but three problems get in the way, and Stanley and Miikkulainen named them in their 2002 paper "Evolving Neural Networks through Augmenting Topologies".

The first is competing conventions. Two networks can compute the same function with their hidden units in a different order. Suppose network A has hidden unit h1 detecting "x is large" and h2 detecting "y is large". Network B has the same two detectors in the opposite slots. Crossing them position by position takes A's first detector and B's first detector. That child ends up with two copies of the "y" detector and none for "x". Both parents were good and the child is broken.

The second is that new structure starts out weak. A fresh hidden node arrives with random weights and usually makes the network worse. It needs several generations of weight tuning before it pays off. If it competes against well-tuned networks right away, it is removed before it has a chance.

The third is size. If you start with large random networks, the search must tune many weights at once. Most of the work is wasted on structure that did not need to be there.

NEAT's three answers

NEAT answers each problem with one idea.

  1. Historical markings. Every time a structural change creates a new connection, it gets a global innovation number, a counter that only goes up. If the same change appears elsewhere in the population, it gets the same number. Genes are aligned by number, so two genomes can be compared and crossed without guessing which parts correspond. This fixes competing conventions.
  2. Speciation. Genomes are grouped into species by how different their structures are. Individuals mostly compete inside their own species, and each one's fitness is divided by the size of its species. A young structure then competes against its close relatives, not against the whole population. This gives new structure time to tune its weights.
  3. Start minimal. The first generation has no hidden nodes: inputs are wired straight to outputs. Structure is added by mutation and kept only if it helps. The search begins in a small space and grows only where growth pays.

There are two structural mutations. Adding a connection links two nodes that were not linked. Adding a node splits an existing connection: the old connection is disabled, a connection of weight 1.0 goes into the new node, and one with the old weight comes out of it. The network behaves almost as before, so the new node does little harm when it arrives.

Aligning genomes by innovation number

Take two genomes and list their genes by innovation number. Genes with the same number in both are matching. Genes that appear in only one genome are disjoint if their number falls inside the range of the other genome, and excess if it lies beyond the other genome's highest number.

The paper turns this into a single number, the compatibility distance. It adds up how many genes are excess, how many are disjoint, and how far the shared weights differ on average.

δ = c1E / N + c2D / N + c3 · W̄

Here E is the number of excess genes and D the number of disjoint genes. N is the gene count of the larger genome, and the paper allows N = 1 for small genomes. W̄ is the mean absolute weight difference over the matching genes. The constants c1, c2, c3 set how much each term counts. Two genomes are in the same species when δ is below a threshold.

Check it by hand on the figure. Genome A has 5 genes and B has 6, so N = 6. There are 4 matching genes, 1 disjoint gene (number 5) and 2 excess genes (numbers 6 and 7). The matching weights are identical, so the weight term is 0. With c1 = c2 = 1: δ = 2/6 + 1/6 = 0.5. The script confirms it.

The idea

  1. minimal: two input links; add_node splits a link; ledger numbers genes
  2. align: matching = both; the rest is disjoint or excess by edge
  3. crossover: matching genes from either parent, others from the fitter
  4. build A, B, D; print genes, alignment, distances and species
"""NEAT bookkeeping: innovation numbers, gene alignment, crossover, species.

A genome is a dict: innovation number -> (src, dst, weight, enabled).
Nodes 0, 1 are inputs, node 2 is the output; hidden nodes start at 3.
"""
import random

C_EXCESS, C_DISJOINT, C_WEIGHT = 1.0, 1.0, 0.4   # distance coefficients
THRESHOLD = 0.52   # tiny genomes need a tiny threshold; tune per task

ledger = {}      # (src, dst) -> innovation number, shared by the population


def innovation(src, dst):
    """The same structural change anywhere gets the same number."""
    if (src, dst) not in ledger:
        ledger[(src, dst)] = len(ledger) + 1
    return ledger[(src, dst)]


# ── 1. Genomes: start minimal, grow by structural mutation ────────────
def minimal(rng):
    genome = {}
    for src in (0, 1):
        genome[innovation(src, 2)] = (src, 2, rng.uniform(-1, 1), True)
    return genome


def add_connection(genome, src, dst, weight):
    genome[innovation(src, dst)] = (src, dst, weight, True)


def add_node(genome, inn, new_node):
    """Split connection inn: disable it, add src->new (1.0), new->dst."""
    src, dst, w, _ = genome[inn]
    genome[inn] = (src, dst, w, False)
    add_connection(genome, src, new_node, 1.0)
    add_connection(genome, new_node, dst, w)


# ── 2. Align two genomes by innovation number ─────────────────────────
def align(a, b):
    edge = min(max(a), max(b))
    matching = sorted(set(a) & set(b))
    only = sorted(set(a) ^ set(b))
    disjoint = [i for i in only if i <= edge]
    excess = [i for i in only if i > edge]
    return matching, disjoint, excess


def distance(a, b):
    matching, disjoint, excess = align(a, b)
    n = max(len(a), len(b), 1)
    diffs = [abs(a[i][2] - b[i][2]) for i in matching]
    w_bar = sum(diffs) / max(len(diffs), 1)
    return (C_EXCESS * len(excess) / n + C_DISJOINT * len(disjoint) / n
            + C_WEIGHT * w_bar)


# ── 3. Crossover: matching genes from either parent, rest from fitter ─
def crossover(rng, fitter, other):
    child = {}
    for inn, gene in fitter.items():
        if inn in other and rng.random() < 0.5:
            gene = other[inn]
        child[inn] = gene
    return child


# ── 4. Build genomes and compare them ───────────────────────────
rng = random.Random(7)
base = minimal(rng)
parent_a = dict(base)
parent_b = dict(base)
add_node(parent_a, 1, 3)               # A splits the connection 0 -> 2
add_connection(parent_a, 1, 3, 0.5)    # ...then wires input 1 to the new node
add_node(parent_b, 2, 4)               # B also splits 1 -> 2, with node 4
add_node(parent_b, 1, 3)               # B finds the same split as A

for name, genome in (("A", parent_a), ("B", parent_b)):
    print(f"genome {name}:")
    for inn in sorted(genome):
        src, dst, w, on = genome[inn]
        flag = "on " if on else "off"
        print(f"  innovation {inn}: {src}->{dst}  w={w:+.2f}  {flag}")

matching, disjoint, excess = align(parent_a, parent_b)
print("matching:", matching, "disjoint:", disjoint, "excess:", excess)
print(f"distance(A, B) = {distance(parent_a, parent_b):.2f}")
print(f"distance(A, A') = {distance(parent_a, dict(parent_a)):.2f}")

child = crossover(rng, parent_a, parent_b)
print("child genes:", sorted(child), "(A is fitter, so gene 5 is kept)")

heavy = dict(parent_a)                 # D: many more structural changes
add_node(heavy, 2, 4)
add_node(heavy, 4, 5)
add_connection(heavy, 0, 5, 0.9)
add_connection(heavy, 1, 4, -0.8)
add_connection(heavy, 3, 5, 0.2)
add_connection(heavy, 0, 4, -0.6)
print(f"distance(A, D) = {distance(parent_a, heavy):.2f}")

species = {}
for name, genome in (("A", parent_a), ("B", parent_b), ("D", heavy)):
    for sid, rep in species.items():
        if distance(genome, rep[1]) < THRESHOLD:

            break

Both A and B split the connection from node 0 to the output. The ledger gives the two splits the same numbers, 3 and 4, so they are recognised as one shared innovation. B then splits a different connection and gets numbers 6 and 7. A instead adds a link from node 1 into its new node, gene 5.

The distance between A and B is 0.50, matching the hand count. Genome D carries several more structural changes and sits at 0.55 from A. With a threshold of 0.52, A and B share a species and D starts its own. The threshold is tiny only because these genomes have a handful of genes. Real runs have much larger genomes, and the threshold is tuned to the task.

Crossover keeps the matching genes (taking each at random from either parent) and the disjoint and excess genes of the fitter parent, here A. The child has genes 1 to 5. Every gene has an innovation number, so no position needs to be guessed.

This is not a full NEAT. It leaves out network evaluation, weight mutation, fitness sharing, species bookkeeping across generations, and the rule that a gene disabled in either parent has a fixed chance of staying disabled in the child. Those pieces are in the paper. The three mechanisms that make NEAT what it is are here: innovation numbers, the distance, and the crossover rule.

Where NEAT fits

NEAT suits networks of tens to hundreds of connections, such as controllers for small control tasks, where the right layout is unknown. For large networks, most practitioners fix an architecture and train it with gradients. Speciation also anticipates a theme of chapter 3 and chapter 4: protect different solutions from each other so that early weakness does not kill them. I give no benchmark numbers for NEAT here because I have not checked a current comparison.

CMA-ES

The third method is for real-valued vectors, the territory of chapter 2's evolution strategies. It starts from the simplest one and adds three ideas.

The simplest strategy and its weakness

The (1+1)-ES keeps one parent. Each step adds Gaussian noise of size σ to make a child. If the child is no worse, it replaces the parent. Rechenberg's 1/5 success rule adjusts σ from experience: grow it when more than one step in five succeeds, shrink it when fewer do. Our code does that by multiplying σ by 1.5 after a success and by 1.5−1/4, about 0.90, after a failure. These two changes cancel when successes are one in five. If the success rate is p, the log-change per step is p − (1 − p)/4 in units of ln 1.5, and that is zero at p = 0.2.

The rule tunes one number, the length of a step. It says nothing about direction. That is a problem in a valley, a region where the score drops steeply in some directions and slowly in others. Here is a concrete one. Let f be a sum of two squares. The first uses the distance u along the valley. The second uses 1000 times the distance v across it:

f = u² + (1000 v)²

A step of 0.01 along u raises f by 0.0001. A step of 0.01 along v raises it by 100. The valley is a thousand times longer than it is wide. The ratio of the steepest to the gentlest curvature, here 106, is the condition number. A round cloud of samples must have σ near the valley's width to avoid hitting the walls. Then each step moves only about 0.001 along the floor, and crossing a distance of 1 takes on the order of a thousand accepted steps. The valley is also tilted. If it lay along a coordinate axis you could give each coordinate its own step size. Because it is rotated, no per-coordinate setting fits, and the search needs a step shape that is rotated too.

Sampling from a Gaussian with a shape

A cloud with a shape is a Gaussian distribution with a covariance matrix C. For two parameters, C is a 2 × 2 table of numbers. The diagonal entries are the variances along each axis. The off-diagonal entry says whether the two parameters tend to move together. The cloud's contours are ellipses. The ellipse axes point along the eigenvectors of C, and their lengths are the square roots of its eigenvalues.

Take C with 1 on the diagonal and 0.9 off it. Its eigenvalues are 1.9 and 0.1. The first axis points along (1, 1), with a spread of √1.9 = 1.38. The second points along (1, −1), with a spread of √0.1 = 0.32. Samples form a cigar along the diagonal, about 4.4 times longer than wide.

xk ~ m + σ N(0, C), k = 1, …, λ

In words: each of λ children is the mean m plus a random step. The step has overall size σ and a shape given by C. CMA-ES then has three jobs each generation: move m, adjust σ and reshape C. It does this from the ranking of the children, with no gradient.

We use the settings from Hansen's tutorial for two parameters. The population is λ = 4 + ⌊3 ln 2⌋ = 6 children and μ = 3 parents. The parents get weights proportional to ln(3.5) − ln i for rank i, normalised to sum to 1: 0.637, 0.285 and 0.078. The best child counts most.

Job 1: move the mean

The new mean is the weighted average of the three best children. With the tutorial's default learning rate of 1, that is m ← ∑ wi xi:λ, where xi:λ is the ith best child. The mean walks toward where the good children were. The weights sum to 1, so the mean stays a proper average.

Job 2: adapt the step size

The 1/5 rule looks at single steps. CMA-ES uses a smoother signal called an evolution path: a running sum of recent mean movements, with older ones fading out. Two moves in the same direction add up and the path grows long. Two moves in opposite directions cancel and it stays short.

Compare the path length to the length of a path from pure noise. If the mean moves consistently, the path is longer than noise would give, so steps are too small and σ grows. If the path is shorter, the mean is zig-zagging, so steps are too big and σ shrinks.

σ ← σ · exp( (cσ / dσ) ( ‖pσ‖ / E‖N(0, I)‖ − 1 ) )

Here pσ is the evolution path (rescaled so that pure noise gives a standard Gaussian), cσ sets how fast old moves fade, and dσ is a damping factor that keeps changes gentle. The denominator, the typical length of a standard Gaussian vector, is about √n(1 − 1/(4n) + 1/(21n²)), which is 1.254 for n = 2. When the ratio is 1 the path looks like noise and σ stays put. This is formula (44) in Hansen's tutorial.

Job 3: reshape the covariance

This is the idea that gives the method its name. After each generation, CMA-ES asks which steps led to good children, and makes steps like those more likely next time. Write yi = (xi − m)/σ for the step that made selected child i, scaled by the step size. The outer product yi yi⊤ is a matrix that carries the variance of that one step, pointing along it. Averaging these matrices with the selection weights gives the covariance of the successful steps. Blending that into C stretches the cloud along directions that worked.

A second source of evidence is the path of mean movement. A direction that is slightly favourable in every generation is too faint to show in one generation's three best children. It shows up clearly in the path. That path, pc, feeds the second term below, called the rank-one update.

This is Hansen's covariance update (47) with two pieces removed, which I list below. Plug in numbers with one update. The old matrix is round. The three selected steps lean up and to the right. The path is (0.3, 0.2).

The idea

  1. decay: scale the old C by 1 - c1 - cmu
  2. rank-mu: add cmu × (weighted sum of y yT)
  3. rank-one: add c1 × (p pT) for the drift direction
"""One covariance update by hand: C <- (1-c1-cmu) C + c1 pp^T + cmu sum w yy^T.

Two dimensions, rounded constants from the n = 2 run of cmaes.py.
"""
import math

C1, CMU = 0.155, 0.058            # learning rates: rank-one, rank-mu
WEIGHTS = (0.637, 0.285, 0.078)   # for the 3 best of 6 samples
# Selected steps y_i = (x_i - mean) / sigma, best first. They lean "up-right".
STEPS = ((1.2, 0.9), (0.8, 0.7), (-0.3, -0.1))
PATH = (0.3, 0.2)                 # evolution path p_c: recent mean movement
C = [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]      # start from the round cloud


def outer(u, v):
    return [[u[i] * v[j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]


def add(A, B, k=1.0):
    return [[A[i][j] + k * B[i][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]


def scale(A, k):
    return [[k * A[i][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]


def describe(name, M):
    a, b, c = M[0][0], M[0][1], M[1][1]
    mean = (a + c) / 2
    gap = math.hypot((a - c) / 2, b)
    angle = math.degrees(0.5 * math.atan2(2 * b, a - c))
    print(f"{name:19s} C=[{a:.3f} {b:.3f}; {b:.3f} {c:.3f}]  "
          f"eig {mean + gap:.3f} {mean - gap:.3f}  axis {angle:.1f} deg")


# ── 1. Decay the old matrix to make room for the new evidence ────────
stage1 = scale(C, 1 - C1 - CMU)
describe("1. decayed old C", stage1)

# ── 2. Rank-mu: the weighted average of outer products y y^T ─────────
rank_mu = [[0.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.0]]
for w, y in zip(WEIGHTS, STEPS):
    rank_mu = add(rank_mu, outer(y, y), w)
stage2 = add(stage1, rank_mu, CMU)
describe("2. + rank-mu term", stage2)

# ── 3. Rank-one: the direction the mean has been drifting in ─────────
stage3 = add(stage2, outer(PATH, PATH), C1)
describe("3. + rank-one term", stage3)

After the decay, the matrix is a smaller round cloud: both eigenvalues 0.787. The rank-μ term adds variance along the up-right diagonal, so the eigenvalues split to 0.889 and 0.787, and the long axis now points 37.6 degrees from the horizontal. The path adds a little more. The change from one update is small. That is deliberate. One generation holds three good children, which is thin evidence, and a big step in the matrix would chase noise.

The whole algorithm, run on a hard valley

Now put the three jobs together and test them. The script compares three methods on the valley from before, rotated by 0.6 radians (about 34.4 degrees) with an axis ratio of 1000. Each starts at (1, 1) with σ = 0.5 and counts function evaluations until f drops below 10−8. The budget is 20,000.

The three are the (1+1)-ES with the 1/5 rule; an isotropic strategy that uses the same weighted mean and step-size adaptation but keeps C as the identity; and CMA-ES. The second differs from the third in one way only: it never learns the shape.

The idea

  1. f(x): rotate by ANGLE, then stretch one axis by RATIO, then square
  2. (1+1)-ES: child = parent + sigma × noise; sigma × 1.5 or × 0.9
  3. constants for n = 2: lambda, mu, weights, c_sigma, c_c, c_1, c_mu
  4. 2×2 eigen-decomposition of C, in closed form, for sampling
  5. per generation: sample, rank, move mean, update sigma, update C
  6. run 11 seeds per method; print the median evaluations to target
"""Three evolution strategies on a rotated, squashed 2-D valley.

(1+1)-ES with the 1/5 rule, an isotropic weighted ES, and a simplified
CMA-ES. All three count evaluations until f(x) < TARGET.
"""
import math
import random

ANGLE = 0.6          # radians: the valley is not aligned with the axes
RATIO = 1000.0       # long axis / short axis, so conditioning is 10**6
START, SIGMA0 = (1.0, 1.0), 0.5
TARGET = 1e-8        # stop when f falls below this
BUDGET = 20_000      # give up after this many evaluations
SEEDS = range(11)


# ── 1. The objective: a rotated ellipsoid ────────────────────────────
def f(x):
    c, s = math.cos(ANGLE), math.sin(ANGLE)
    u = c * x[0] + s * x[1]
    v = -s * x[0] + c * x[1]
    return u * u + (RATIO * v) ** 2


class Counter:
    """Wraps f and counts calls, so every method pays the same way."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.calls = 0
        self.hit = None
        self.best = float("inf")

    def __call__(self, x):
        self.calls += 1
        value = f(x)
        self.best = min(self.best, value)
        if value < TARGET and self.hit is None:
            self.hit = self.calls
        return value


# ── 2. (1+1)-ES: one parent, one child, Rechenberg's 1/5 rule ────────
def one_plus_one(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    cost = Counter()
    x, sigma = list(START), SIGMA0
    fx = cost(x)
    while cost.hit is None and cost.calls < BUDGET:
        y = [x[i] + sigma * rng.gauss(0, 1) for i in range(2)]
        fy = cost(y)
        if fy <= fx:
            x, fx = y, fy
            sigma *= 1.5               # success: be bolder
        else:
            sigma *= 1.5 ** -0.25      # failure: 1 in 5 balances these
    return cost


# ── 3. Weights and constants from Hansen's tutorial (n = 2) ──────────
N = 2
LAM = 4 + int(3 * math.log(N))                   # offspring per generation
MU = LAM // 2                                    # parents kept
RAW = [math.log((LAM + 1) / 2) - math.log(i + 1) for i in range(MU)]
W = [r / sum(RAW) for r in RAW]                  # recombination weights
MU_EFF = 1 / sum(w * w for w in W)               # "effective" parents
C_SIGMA = (MU_EFF + 2) / (N + MU_EFF + 5)        # step-size path memory
D_SIGMA = 1 + C_SIGMA                            # damping (the max term is 0)
C_C = (4 + MU_EFF / N) / (N + 4 + 2 * MU_EFF / N)
C_1 = 2 / ((N + 1.3) ** 2 + MU_EFF)              # rank-one learning rate
C_MU = min(1 - C_1, 2 * (MU_EFF - 2 + 1 / MU_EFF) / ((N + 2) ** 2 + MU_EFF))
CHI_N = math.sqrt(N) * (1 - 1 / (4 * N) + 1 / (21 * N * N))


# ── 4. 2x2 linear algebra: eigen-decomposition of the covariance ─────
def eig2(C):
    a, b, c = C[0][0], C[0][1], C[1][1]
    t = 0.5 * math.atan2(2 * b, a - c)
    cs, sn = math.cos(t), math.sin(t)
    l1 = a * cs * cs + 2 * b * cs * sn + c * sn * sn
    l2 = a * sn * sn - 2 * b * cs * sn + c * cs * cs
    return (max(l1, 1e-300), max(l2, 1e-300)), ((cs, sn), (-sn, cs))


def mat_vec(B, d, v, power):
    """B diag(d**power) B^T v: power 0.5 samples, -0.5 whitens."""
    coef = [sum(B[k][i] * v[i] for i in range(2)) * d[k] ** power
            for k in range(2)]
    return [sum(coef[k] * B[k][i] for k in range(2)) for i in range(2)]


# ── 5. The generation loop: with or without covariance learning ──────
def es(seed, learn_shape, trace=None):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    cost = Counter()
    m, sigma = list(START), SIGMA0
    C = [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]
    p_sigma, p_c = [0.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.0]
    gen = 0
    while cost.hit is None and cost.calls < BUDGET:
        d, B = eig2(C)
        m0, s0, f0 = list(m), sigma, f(m)   # what this generation samples from
        pop = []
        for _ in range(LAM):
            z = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(2)]
            y = mat_vec(B, d, z, 0.5)
            x = [m[i] + sigma * y[i] for i in range(2)]
            pop.append((cost(x), y, x))
        pop.sort(key=lambda p: p[0])
        y_w = [sum(W[k] * pop[k][1][i] for k in range(MU)) for i in range(2)]
        m = [m[i] + sigma * y_w[i] for i in range(2)]
        # cumulative step-size adaptation
        inv = mat_vec(B, d, y_w, -0.5)
        k_s = math.sqrt(C_SIGMA * (2 - C_SIGMA) * MU_EFF)
        p_sigma = [(1 - C_SIGMA) * p_sigma[i] + k_s * inv[i] for i in range(2)]
        norm = math.hypot(*p_sigma)
        sigma *= math.exp(C_SIGMA / D_SIGMA * (norm / CHI_N - 1))
        if learn_shape:
            k_c = math.sqrt(C_C * (2 - C_C) * MU_EFF)
            p_c = [(1 - C_C) * p_c[i] + k_c * y_w[i] for i in range(2)]
            for i in range(2):
                for j in range(2):
                    rank_mu = sum(W[k] * pop[k][1][i] * pop[k][1][j]
                                  for k in range(MU))
                    C[i][j] = ((1 - C_1 - C_MU) * C[i][j]
                               + C_1 * p_c[i] * p_c[j] + C_MU * rank_mu)
        gen += 1
        if trace is not None:
            trace.append((gen, cost.calls, f0, s0, d, B, m0, pop))
    return cost


# ── 6. Compare: evaluations until f < TARGET, 11 seeds each ──────────
def report(name, runs):
    done = sorted(r.hit for r in runs if r.hit is not None)
    best = sorted(r.best for r in runs)[len(runs) // 2]
    if len(done) > len(runs) // 2:
        shown = f"median {done[len(done) // 2]:,} evals"
    else:
        shown = f"median best f {best:.1e} at {BUDGET:,} evals"
    print(f"{name:30s} reached {len(done):2d}/{len(runs)}  {shown}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"lambda={LAM} mu={MU} mu_eff={MU_EFF:.3f} "
          f"weights={[round(w, 3) for w in W]}")
    print(f"c_sigma={C_SIGMA:.3f} c_c={C_C:.3f} c_1={C_1:.3f} "
          f"c_mu={C_MU:.3f}")
    report("(1+1)-ES, 1/5 rule", [one_plus_one(s) for s in SEEDS])
    report("isotropic ES, step size only", [es(s, False) for s in SEEDS])
    report("CMA-ES (simplified)", [es(s, True) for s in SEEDS])
    trace = []
    es(0, True, trace)
    print("\nCMA-ES, seed 0: the 1-sigma ellipse (long x short, angle)")
    for g, evals, fm, sg, d, B, _, _ in trace:

What I simplified

This is a teaching version, not a library. Compared with Hansen's tutorial (arXiv 1604.00772) it differs in these ways.

  • It omits the stall indicator hσ in the evolution path update. That term pauses the path when σ is growing fast.
  • It uses only positive weights for the μ best children. The tutorial's 2016 defaults also give small negative weights to the worst children, which speeds up learning of C.
  • It decomposes the 2 × 2 matrix in closed form every generation. Real code caches an eigen-decomposition for n × n.
  • It has a fixed population size and no restarts, bounds or termination tests.
  • Because dσ = 1 + cσ for small n, I wrote that special case directly. The tutorial's formula has an extra term that is zero here.

I took the formulas for the weights, learning rates, path updates and covariance update from the tutorial. I checked them against the tutorial's text. I also tested only one objective, with a rotation and a ratio that I chose. The result shows the effect clearly, but it is one function, not a benchmark.

Analogy: walking a narrow canyon in thick fog

You must get along a canyon floor in fog. You cannot see the walls, but you can toss a pebble in several directions and hear how far each falls. At first you throw in a circle. You learn that pebbles thrown sideways fall far and those thrown along the floor do not. So you throw fewer sideways and more lengthwise, and you take longer strides along the canyon, because your last few strides all went the same way. The canyon's shape is in your throwing pattern.

Where it breaks down: a real walker would feel the slope underfoot. CMA-ES only sees how each sample ranks against the others, and it pays for each throw with an evaluation.

Common misconception

"CMA-ES scales to any number of parameters." It does not. The matrix C has n × n entries, so 1,000 parameters means a million entries, about 8 MB, and a decomposition costing around 109 operations. A million parameters would need 1012 entries, about 8 TB. That is why CMA-ES is the default for up to a few hundred parameters, and why the sampled-gradient style of ES in chapter 2 is used for networks. Cheaper variants keep only the diagonal or a low-rank part. I do not cover them here.

A link to chapter 2's gradient estimator

The weighted mean in Job 1 and the ES gradient of chapter 2 both move the mean toward good samples. Hansen's tutorial points to a derivation of CMA-ES from the natural gradient, a way to take the steepest step when distances are measured in the distribution's own shape. I cite it only as a pointer and have not worked through that derivation.

The three methods are tools for different jobs. Pick by the kind of thing you are searching, not by which sounds newest.

CMA-ES is a quiet workhorse. Optuna, a widely used hyperparameter tuning library, ships a CMA-ES sampler. Chapter 16 of the large-model course, model merging and evolutionary merging, describes evolutionary search for merge recipes without naming the algorithm. Akiba et al. (2024), "Evolutionary Optimization of Model Merging Recipes", state that they used the CMA-ES implementation in Optuna for the parameter-space search and CMA-ES in EvoJAX for the data-flow search. So the algorithm you just built is the engine inside that case.

The same warning applies. CMA-ES will fit its scoring set as hard as it can. Report the final result on a different set, as chapter 16 explains. A tuner that finds, for example, a recipe scoring 91% on the 60 examples it saw and 85% on new ones has overfitted, whatever its algorithm.

Systems connection

Scoring candidates is the expensive part, and the scores are independent of each other. That makes every method here easy to spread across machines, the same shape of work as in parallelism, SIMD and GPUs. GP candidates that are real source code are untrusted programs. Run them as running candidates describes: with a time limit and no access to files.

Key takeaways

  • Genetic programming stores programs as trees built from a function set and a terminal set. Closure makes every subtree swap valid.
  • Subtree crossover only swaps branches. Selection decides whether the swap was good, and many swaps are neutral.
  • Bloat is growth without improvement. Parsimony pressure, a charge per node, removes it at little cost.
  • NEAT gives every new gene an innovation number. That aligns genomes for crossover, supports speciation, and lets the search start minimal.
  • Compatibility distance counts excess and disjoint genes plus the mean weight gap. Genomes below a threshold share a species.
  • CMA-ES samples from a Gaussian with a learned covariance matrix. It moves the mean, adapts the step size from an evolution path, and reshapes the cloud from successful steps.
  • Learning the shape is what solves ill-conditioned, tilted valleys. In our test CMA-ES needed a median of 578 evaluations where simpler strategies had not finished after 20,000.
  • Use GP or language model edits for discrete structure, CMA-ES for up to a few hundred real numbers, NEAT for small networks, and sampled-gradient ES beyond that.

Check your understanding

A GP run on bin packing produces the tree (item - 5) - rem. Compared with item - rem, how will it pack?

  1. Worse, because the constant 5 shifts every score down
  2. Identically, because item is the same for every bin in one decision, so the order of bins is unchanged
  3. Better, because a constant adds a safety margin
  4. It cannot be evaluated, because the constant is not in the input
Within a single placement the item size is one number, so subtracting 5 lowers every bin's score by the same amount. The highest-scoring bin stays the highest. This is a neutral change. The tempting first option confuses the size of the scores with their order, and only the order matters here.

Your GP trees grow from an average of 13 nodes to 40 over 50 generations, while the best score stops improving. What is the most direct fix?

  1. Raise the mutation rate
  2. Use a larger population only
  3. Switch to one-point crossover on the flattened text
  4. Charge a small cost per node, or cap the depth, so extra nodes do not come free
This is bloat. Parsimony pressure makes padding cost something, and a depth cap limits growth. More mutation or a larger population does not remove the advantage of padding. Crossing flattened text would break closure and produce invalid programs.

Genome A has genes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and genome B has genes 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9. Which statement is right?

  1. Genes 1 to 3 match; 4 and 5 are disjoint; 6 to 9 are excess
  2. Genes 1 to 3 match; 4 to 9 are all excess
  3. Genes 1 to 5 match; 6 to 9 are excess
  4. Only gene 1 matches, because the rest differ in weight
Matching means the same innovation number, whatever the weight. Genome A ends at gene 5. Genes 4 and 5 appear only in A and fall inside the range of B, so they are disjoint. Genes 6 to 9 appear only in B and lie beyond A's highest number, so they are excess.

Why does NEAT divide each genome's fitness by the size of its species?

  1. To make fitness values smaller and easier to compare
  2. To stop the population from ever growing
  3. So that a young species with new structure is not crowded out by a large, well-tuned one
  4. To make crossover between species more likely
New structure starts weak and needs time to tune its weights. Sharing fitness inside a species means a small species is not outcompeted by a big one, which protects innovation. The division does not limit growth overall, and NEAT breeds mostly within species, not across them.

On a tilted, narrow valley, a (1+1)-ES with the 1/5 rule creeps while CMA-ES races ahead. What does CMA-ES have that the 1/5 rule lacks?

  1. A larger population, which alone explains the gap
  2. A covariance matrix, so its steps are long along the valley and short across it
  3. A gradient estimate computed from the samples
  4. A different objective function
The 1/5 rule tunes one length for all directions, so it must stay as small as the valley is wide. CMA-ES learns direction as well as size. In our test the isotropic weighted strategy, which has the same population and weighted mean but no covariance learning, also failed to finish, so population size is not the cause.

You need to tune 3 million weights of a fixed network with no gradient available. Which choice fits best?

  1. CMA-ES with a full covariance matrix
  2. NEAT starting from the minimal network
  3. A sampled-gradient evolution strategy or a genetic algorithm that mutates weights
  4. Genetic programming over expression trees
A full covariance matrix for 3 million parameters would have about 9 trillion entries. NEAT is for small networks of unknown shape, and the shape here is fixed. GP searches programs, not weight vectors. Evolution strategies and genetic algorithms only need to store and perturb the weight vector.
