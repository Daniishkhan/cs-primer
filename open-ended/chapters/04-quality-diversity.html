Quality-Diversity & MAP-Elites

Most search algorithms return one answer. Quality-diversity algorithms return a map instead: the best solution they could find for every kind of behaviour you care about. This chapter builds the simplest such algorithm from scratch, then shows how to read the map it produces. Keeping a large, varied archive of good solutions is one of the most useful ideas in modern search, including search driven by language models.

Why this matters

The last chapter ended on a tension. Objective-driven search converges fast but falls into traps. Novelty search escapes traps but ignores how good anything is. Quality-diversity (QD) resolves the tension by asking for both: many different solutions, each as good as it can be. If you skip this idea, you will build self-improvement loops that keep one champion and lose every stepping stone on the way to it.

After this chapter you can build an archive of the best solution per kind of behaviour. You can choose the descriptors that define "kind of behaviour", and read the two numbers that judge a map, which are how much of it is filled and how much total quality it holds. You can also recognise the same structure inside systems that evolve programs with language models, their open-source frameworks and agent-design search. It is also the archive that open-ended algorithms generalise.

From one answer to a map

Suppose a company wants a delivery robot with a good walking gait. A normal optimiser is told "maximise speed" and returns one gait. If the robot later loses a leg, that one gait is useless, and the optimiser has nothing else to offer.

Now suppose the search had instead kept the fastest gait for every pattern of foot contact it could produce. One gait mostly uses the front legs, one mostly uses the back legs, and one hops. After damage, the robot can try the stored gaits one by one and pick whichever still works.

This is not a thought experiment. Cully, Clune, Tarapore and Mouret (2015, "Robots that can adapt like animals", Nature) used exactly such a precomputed map. It let a legged robot recover from damage in under two minutes. The robot tested a small number of stored behaviours rather than diagnosing the fault.

That is the shift in mindset. A classic optimiser treats search as finding a point. A quality-diversity algorithm treats search as illuminating a space. It sheds light on how performance varies across the kinds of behaviour a solution can have, so a human or a downstream system can choose. The name comes from the goal. The collection should be diverse, so the solutions behave differently. It should also be of high quality, so each is as good as possible for what it does.

Analogy: the best cafe in every neighbourhood

A guidebook that lists the single best cafe in the city is a classic optimiser's output. A guidebook that lists the best cafe in each neighbourhood is a quality-diversity output. The second one is more useful when you are not sure what you want. Say you are in the north of the city, it is raining, and the overall winner is a bus ride away. It also helps the editors. The best cafe in a neighbourhood that has only three cafes may be mediocre, which tells them where to look for new talent.

Where it breaks down: neighbourhoods are drawn on a map by someone else. In QD you must decide the "neighbourhoods" yourself, using the behaviour descriptors described below. A poor choice of axes produces a map that looks tidy but means nothing.

The ingredients

Every quality-diversity algorithm, and MAP-Elites in particular, is built from the same four pieces. You met two of them in the previous chapters; the new ones are the descriptor space and the archive that is organised by it.

Quality (fitness)
A number saying how good a solution is at the job, exactly as in the evolutionary algorithms chapter. Higher is better. Quality is only compared between solutions that behave alike.
Behaviour descriptor
A short vector describing what a solution does, chosen by the designer. Examples are where a robot's hand ends up, how long a generated text is, how many unit tests a program passes, and how much memory it uses. It is also called a feature vector or behaviour characterisation. It is the "neighbourhood" coordinate in the analogy.
Behaviour space
The space of all possible descriptor values. Two solutions that land close together in it count as doing nearly the same thing.
Archive
The output data structure. The behaviour space is divided into cells, and each cell holds at most one solution: the best seen so far for that region. The solution in a cell is called an elite.

Notice what the archive changes. In a normal evolutionary algorithm, solutions compete globally for a place in a limited population. A strange but promising candidate is wiped out by the many ordinary, slightly better ones. In an archive, competition is local. A candidate only competes against the current elite of the one cell it lands in. A mediocre solution that does something nobody else does is safe, because it has a cell to itself. This is the same insight as "local competition" from the novelty chapter, made structural.

The MAP-Elites algorithm

MAP-Elites (Multi-dimensional Archive of Phenotypic Elites; Mouret and Clune, 2015, "Illuminating search spaces by mapping elites") is the simplest quality-diversity algorithm, which is why it is the one to learn first. "Phenotypic" comes from biology. The phenotype is what a candidate does or looks like. Its genotype is the encoding that mutation edits (chapter 19). MAP-Elites keeps the best candidate for each kind of behaviour, so its archive is organised by phenotype. The whole algorithm fits in a paragraph:

  1. Seed. Create some random solutions. Evaluate each one to get its quality and its descriptor. Put each into the archive cell its descriptor points at, if the cell is empty.
  2. Pick a parent. Choose one filled cell uniformly at random and take its elite. Do not prefer the fittest.
  3. Vary it. Mutate the parent (or recombine it with another elite) to make a child.
  4. Evaluate the child. Get its quality and descriptor.
  5. Compete locally. Find the cell the child's descriptor falls in. If the cell is empty, store the child. If it is occupied and the child has higher quality, replace the occupant. Otherwise discard the child.
  6. Repeat from step 2 for as many evaluations as the budget allows.

Two design choices carry the algorithm. Uniform parent choice spends as much effort on a poor-but-unusual cell at the edge of the map as on the champion in the middle. This pushes the frontier of filled cells outward. And cell-wise replacement means every cell only ever improves, so quality never goes backwards anywhere on the map.

Step through

Six iterations on a 4 by 4 archive

  1. The archive. Six cells are filled; each holds the best solution found for its region of behaviour space.
  2. Choose a parent. Selection is uniform over filled cells, so weak and strong elites get equal attention.
  3. Make a child. A mutated copy is evaluated and its descriptor says where it belongs.
  4. Fill a new niche. The target cell is empty, so the child enters even though it is worse than its parent.
  5. Lose a local contest. A weaker child in an occupied cell is thrown away.
  6. Win a local contest. A stronger child replaces the elite, and the cell improves.

MAP-Elites in code

Here is the standard demonstration, small enough to read in one sitting. The solution is a planar robot arm with eight joints, each link 1/8 of a unit long. A genome is eight numbers in [0, 1], decoded as joint angles between −π and π. The behaviour descriptor is where the hand ends up, binned on a 20 by 20 grid. Quality is a preference for smooth, comfortable arms: 1 / (1 + 5 × variance of the joint angles). This is 1 for an arm whose joints all bend by the same amount, and it shrinks as the joints disagree. The task is "reach every place you can, as smoothly as possible", not "reach one place".

The listing compares three searches with the same 50,000-evaluation budget.

  • MAP-Elites.
  • Random sampling into the same archive. This is a control that shows what the archive alone achieves.
  • An ordinary genetic algorithm that keeps the highest-quality half of its population.

All numbers are means over five seeds.

import math, random

N_JOINTS = 8
def decode(g):                                   # genome in [0,1]^8 -> joint angles in [-pi, pi]
    return [(x - 0.5) * 2 * math.pi for x in g]

def hand(g):                                     # behaviour: where the end of the arm ends up
    a, x, y = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for t in decode(g):
        a += t
        x += math.cos(a) / N_JOINTS
        y += math.sin(a) / N_JOINTS
    return x, y                                  # both in [-1, 1]

def quality(g):                                  # prefer smooth arms: low variance of the angles
    t = decode(g)
    m = sum(t) / len(t)
    var = sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in t) / len(t)
    return 1 / (1 + 5 * var)

def cell(g, res):
    x, y = hand(g)
    i = min(res - 1, int((x + 1) / 2 * res))
    j = min(res - 1, int((y + 1) / 2 * res))
    return i, j

def mutate(g, rng, sigma=0.1):
    return [min(1.0, max(0.0, v + rng.gauss(0, sigma))) if rng.random() < 0.3 else v for v in g]

def map_elites(seed, evals, res=20, init=500):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    archive = {}                                 # cell -> (quality, genome)
    for e in range(evals):
        if e < init or not archive:
            g = [rng.random() for _ in range(N_JOINTS)]
        else:
            g = mutate(rng.choice(list(archive.values()))[1], rng)
        c, q = cell(g, res), quality(g)
        if c not in archive or q > archive[c][0]:
            archive[c] = (q, g)
    return archive

def plain_ga(seed, evals, pop_size=100):         # keeps the best by quality only
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pop = [[rng.random() for _ in range(N_JOINTS)] for _ in range(pop_size)]
    for _ in range(evals // pop_size):
        pop.sort(key=quality, reverse=True)
        pop = pop[:pop_size // 2] + [mutate(rng.choice(pop[:pop_size // 2]), rng) for _ in range(pop_size // 2)]
    return pop[0], len({cell(g, 20) for g in pop})   # best genome, cells occupied by the final population

def random_archive(seed, evals, res=20):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    archive = {}
    for _ in range(evals):
        g = [rng.random() for _ in range(N_JOINTS)]
        c, q = cell(g, res), quality(g)
        if c not in archive or q > archive[c][0]: archive[c] = (q, g)
    return archive

def stats(archive):
    qs = [q for q, _ in archive.values()]
    return len(archive), sum(qs), max(qs)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    EVALS, RES, RUNS = 50_000, 20, 5
    print(f"{N_JOINTS}-joint arm, behaviour = hand position on a {RES}x{RES} grid ({RES*RES} cells), {EVALS:,} evaluations, {RUNS} seeds")
    print(f"{'method':22s} {'cells filled':>12s} {'QD-score':>9s} {'best quality':>13s}")
    rows = {"MAP-Elites": lambda s: stats(map_elites(s, EVALS, RES)),
            "random + archive": lambda s: stats(random_archive(s, EVALS, RES))}
    for name, f in rows.items():
        r = [f(s) for s in range(RUNS)]
        print(f"{name:22s} {sum(x[0] for x in r)/RUNS:12.1f} {sum(x[1] for x in r)/RUNS:9.1f} {sum(x[2] for x in r)/RUNS:13.3f}")
    r = [plain_ga(s, EVALS) for s in range(RUNS)]
    print(f"{'quality-only GA':22s} {sum(x[1] for x in r)/RUNS:12.1f} {'n/a':>9s} {sum(quality(x[0]) for x in r)/RUNS:13.3f}   (cells = cells its final population covers)")
8-joint arm, behaviour = hand position on a 20x20 grid (400 cells), 50,000 evaluations, 5 seeds
method                 cells filled  QD-score  best quality
MAP-Elites                    336.2     269.7         0.995
random + archive              259.0      67.1         0.710
quality-only GA                21.0       n/a         1.000   (cells = cells its final population covers)

Read the three rows carefully, because they teach the three metrics used to judge any QD run.

  • Coverage (cells filled): MAP-Elites filled about 336 of 400 cells. The hand can never leave the unit circle, which covers about 314 cells' worth of area in a 2 by 2 box. Cells on the rim that are partly inside the circle can be filled too, so 336 is close to the ceiling. Random sampling filled 259. It rarely produces arms that stretch out or curl in tightly, so the rim and the centre stay empty.
  • QD-score (the sum of the elites' qualities): 269.7 for MAP-Elites against 67.1 for random sampling. This single number rewards filling more cells and improving each one, which is why it is the headline metric. Random sampling fills many cells but with sloppy arms (mean quality about 0.26 per cell), whereas MAP-Elites' elites average about 0.80.
  • Best quality: all three methods find a nearly perfect arm (0.995 to 1.000). A normal optimiser is not worse at finding the best single arm. What it cannot do is tell you anything about the other regions. Its final population of 100 arms sits in only 21 cells.

This is the practical message. If you only want the single best answer, a quality-diversity algorithm gives up nothing. If you want options, it gives you hundreds of them from a single run.

Common misconception

"MAP-Elites is just a grid search with a fancy name." A grid search decides in advance which points to evaluate. MAP-Elites does not know which cells are reachable, which are easy, or where the good solutions are. It discovers them by mutating what it already has. The grid is only the bookkeeping. The search runs in genome space, and the archive tells it where it has been. Notice also that random sampling into the same grid is the closest thing to a grid search here. It scored a QD-score four times lower.

Why the QD-score never goes down

Go deeper optional

Write the archive as a function that sends each cell c to the quality of its occupant, q(c). Set q(c) = 0 for an empty cell. Every real quality is at least 0, and in our example it lies in (0, 1]. The QD-score is the sum over all cells.

QD = ∑c q(c)

An update touches exactly one cell. If it is empty, q(c) rises from 0 to the child's positive quality. If it is occupied, q(c) changes only if the child is strictly better. In every case q(c) does not decrease, so QD does not decrease. Two consequences follow.

First, a plot of QD-score against evaluations is a monotone staircase. This makes it easy to compare algorithms and to decide when to stop.

Second, the proof assumes deterministic evaluation. If quality is noisy, a lucky evaluation can put an inflated number in a cell that no later honest evaluation can beat. The monotone staircase is then partly an illusion. The usual remedies are to re-evaluate elites several times, or to keep a running average of their qualities. This matters a great deal when the evaluator is a language-model judge (see LLM-as-Judge), because those scores are noisy by nature.

QD-score depends on the archive's resolution, as the next section shows. Only compare QD-scores between runs that use the same descriptors and the same cells.

Choosing the behaviour descriptor

The descriptor is the most consequential decision in a quality-diversity run, and the one that people get wrong most often. Nothing in the algorithm tells you what is worth being diverse about; you have to supply it. Good descriptors share four properties.

  • Measurable from a single evaluation. You must be able to compute the descriptor from one run of the solution. "How would a human rate its style?" is only usable if you can automate that rating, for instance with a model (see below).
  • Related to what users care about. A hand position matters to someone who wants to reach things. A code-generation system might use "length of program" and "number of loops". A customer-service prompt might use "tone" and "length of reply". A trading strategy might use "average holding time" and "maximum exposure".
  • Not a copy of the quality. If the descriptor is nearly a function of the quality score, the archive simply sorts solutions by score, and the diversity is fake. Cells fill in order along a line.
  • Few dimensions, each one meaningful. Two to six dimensions is typical. More is possible (see the CVT section) but harder to read and slower to fill.

Descriptor choice changes what you discover, not only how it looks. If the arm's descriptor were the angle of the first joint rather than the hand position, the same code would illuminate a different space. A different set of elites would survive. A good habit is to run with two plausible descriptor sets on a small budget. Then see which gives a map that tells you something you did not already know.

Resolution is a dial, not a detail

How finely you cut the descriptor space controls how much the algorithm cares about variety. A coarse archive has few cells, each covering a wide region, so it behaves a little like a normal optimiser with a handful of niches. A fine archive has thousands of cells and needs a bigger budget to fill. The next script runs the same search at five resolutions with an identical budget.

from mapelites import *
EVALS = 50_000
print("same 50,000 evaluations, different archive resolutions (mean of 5 seeds)")
print(f"{'grid':>7s} {'cells':>6s} {'filled':>7s} {'filled %':>9s} {'QD-score':>9s} {'mean quality of filled':>23s}")

    r = [stats(map_elites(s, EVALS, res)) for s in range(5)]
    f = sum(x[0] for x in r) / 5; qd = sum(x[1] for x in r) / 5
    print(f"{res:>3d}x{res:<3d} {res*res:6d} {f:7.1f} {100*f/res/res:8.1f}% {qd:9.1f} {qd/f:23.3f}")

As the grid gets finer, the fraction of cells filled drops from 100% to 74%. The mean quality of each elite falls from 0.92 to 0.45, because the same 50,000 evaluations are spread over 6,400 cells instead of 25. QD-score still rises (22.9 to 2,115.9) simply because there are more cells. That is why QD-scores from different resolutions must never be compared. The practical rule is to choose the finest grid your budget can reasonably fill, aiming for coverage above half. Report the grid next to every number.

Why diversity helps even if you only want the best

The robotics examples might leave the impression that QD is only for people who want a catalogue. It also finds better single answers on hard problems. The reason is one this course keeps returning to: the best solutions are often reached through intermediate ones that do not look good. The previous chapter showed a 20-bit trap in which the score falls as you add 1 bits, except that all ones is worth 40. Objective-driven search never found it in 30 runs. Novelty search found it in 17 of 30.

MAP-Elites can be pointed at the same trap. The behaviour descriptor is the number of 1 bits (21 possible cells, 0 to 20). The quality is the trap's score. There is no novelty archive and no distance calculation; the cells themselves record which behaviours have been seen.

import random

N = 20
def ones(g): return sum(g)
def fitness(g):                                  # the deceptive trap from the last chapter
    u = ones(g)
    return 2 * N if u == N else (N - 1) - u

def mutate(g, rng):
    return [1 - x if rng.random() < 1 / N else x for x in g]

def map_elites(seed, budget=10_000):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    archive = {}                                 # behaviour (number of 1 bits) -> (fitness, genome)
    for evals in range(1, budget + 1):
        if evals <= 40 or not archive:
            g = [rng.randint(0, 1) for _ in range(N)]
        else:
            g = mutate(rng.choice(list(archive.values()))[1], rng)   # pick any elite, not the fittest
        b, f = ones(g), fitness(g)
        if b not in archive or f > archive[b][0]:
            archive[b] = (f, g)
        if b == N:
            return evals, len(archive)
    return None, len(archive)

runs = [map_elites(s) for s in range(30)]
won = sorted(e for e, _ in runs if e)
print(f"MAP-Elites on the trap: found all-ones in {len(won)}/30 runs, median evaluations {won[len(won)//2]:,}")
print(f"cells in the archive when it finished: {sorted(c for _, c in runs)[15]} of {N + 1} (median)")
MAP-Elites on the trap: found all-ones in 30/30 runs, median evaluations 1,606
cells in the archive when it finished: 21 of 21 (median)

MAP-Elites solved the trap in all 30 runs, with a median of 1,606 evaluations. Novelty search in the previous chapter solved 17 of 30, with a median of 5,800. The two scripts count evaluations the same way, but they use different population machinery. Treat the comparison as a demonstration, not a benchmark.

The mechanism is the stepping stone. The cell for "19 ones" holds a genome whose quality is a dismal 0, yet that genome is stored and kept as a parent. Choosing parents uniformly means it is picked often, and one bit flip from it reaches the prize. An objective-driven algorithm would have discarded it at generation one.

Be honest about what this shows

The trap result depends on a descriptor ("count of 1 bits") that happens to line up with the route to the answer, exactly as in the novelty chapter. If the descriptor were unrelated to how the prize is reached, the archive would add nothing. QD does not remove the need for domain knowledge. It moves it from "design a perfect score" to "choose axes along which variety is useful".

How QD relates to novelty search

QD is easier to place if you compare it with the method from the previous chapter. Novelty search selects on behavioural distance from an archive of past behaviours; it never looks at quality. Novelty search with local competition (Lehman and Stanley, 2011) adds a second objective: among the nearest behavioural neighbours, prefer the fitter one. MAP-Elites reaches a similar place by a simpler route: a fixed partition of behaviour space plus a "best per cell" rule. Pugh, Soros and Stanley (2016, "Quality diversity: a new frontier for evolutionary computation") named the family and compared both on mazes.

Scaling the archive: CVT-MAP-Elites and CMA-ME

A grid with k bins on each of d descriptor axes has kd cells. Ten bins on four axes is 10,000 cells; on eight axes it is 100 million. Most of those cells will never be reachable, and the filled ones are so thinly spread that a normal budget cannot fill them. This is the curse of dimensionality from the earlier courses (see nearest neighbours), reappearing in the archive.

Vassiliades, Chatzilygeroudis and Mouret (2017, "Using centroidal Voronoi tessellations to scale up the multi-dimensional archive of phenotypic elites algorithm") fixed the number of cells in advance, whatever the dimension. They pick, say, 100 well-spread centroids in the descriptor space, by running k-means on random samples. Each solution goes into the cell of its nearest centroid. The cells are Voronoi regions instead of a regular grid, and the budget chooses their number, not the geometry. The method is called centroidal Voronoi tessellation (CVT) MAP-Elites.

The next script compares three archives for a four-dimensional descriptor (the elbow's position and the hand's position): a coarse grid, a fine grid, and 100 CVT cells.

import math, random
import numpy as np
from mapelites import N_JOINTS, decode, quality, mutate

def descriptor(g):                               # 4-D behaviour: elbow (x, y) and hand (x, y)
    a, x, y, out = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, []
    for k, t in enumerate(decode(g)):
        a += t; x += math.cos(a) / N_JOINTS; y += math.sin(a) / N_JOINTS
        if k == 3: out += [x, y]
    return out + [x, y]

def kmeans(points, k, rng, iters=10):            # Lloyd's algorithm: the centroids tile the space evenly
    cen = points[rng.choice(len(points), k, replace=False)]
    for _ in range(iters):
        lab = np.argmin(((points[:, None, :] - cen[None]) ** 2).sum(-1), axis=1)
        for c in range(k):
            if (lab == c).any(): cen[c] = points[lab == c].mean(0)
    return cen

def run(seed, evals, locate):
    rng = random.Random(seed); arch = {}
    for e in range(evals):
        g = [rng.random() for _ in range(N_JOINTS)] if e < 500 or not arch else mutate(rng.choice(list(arch.values()))[1], rng)
        c, q = locate(descriptor(g)), quality(g)
        if c not in arch or q > arch[c][0]: arch[c] = (q, g)
    return arch

EVALS = 30_000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sample = np.array([descriptor([random.Random(i).random() for _ in range(8)]) for i in range(5000)])
cen = kmeans(sample.copy(), 100, rng)
def cvt_cell(d): return int(np.argmin(((cen - np.array(d)) ** 2).sum(1)))
def grid_cell(res):
    return lambda d: tuple(min(res - 1, int((v + 1) / 2 * res)) for v in d)
print(f"4-D behaviour (elbow x,y and hand x,y), {EVALS:,} evaluations, mean of 3 seeds")
print(f"{'archive':22s} {'cells':>7s} {'filled':>7s} {'QD-score':>9s}")
for name, total, loc in (("grid 3 per axis", 3**4, grid_cell(3)), ("grid 10 per axis", 10**4, grid_cell(10)), ("CVT, 100 centroids", 100, cvt_cell)):
    r = [run(s, EVALS, loc) for s in range(3)]
    print(f"{name:22s} {total:7d} {sum(len(a) for a in r)/3:7.1f} {sum(sum(q for q,_ in a.values()) for a in r)/3:9.1f}")
4-D behaviour (elbow x,y and hand x,y), 30,000 evaluations, mean of 3 seeds
archive                  cells  filled  QD-score
grid 3 per axis             81    49.0      35.9
grid 10 per axis         10000  1098.3     532.4
CVT, 100 centroids         100   100.0      96.6

With 30,000 evaluations, the fine 10-per-axis grid fills about 1,100 of 10,000 cells. Its QD-score is dominated by cells that are barely better than the first thing that landed in them. The coarse grid wastes its 81 cells on huge regions and fills 49. The CVT archive fills all 100 and has the best average quality per cell. The cell count is a dial you set to match your budget, which is the right way round.

A second direction attacks how children are generated. MAP-Elites mutates blindly. Fontaine, Togelius, Nikolaidis and Hoover (2020, "Covariance matrix adaptation for the rapid illumination of behavior space") combined it with CMA-ES. That is covariance matrix adaptation (CMA) evolution strategy (ES), an optimiser that learns a good mutation distribution. Their method, CMA-ME, pairs it with MAP-Elites (ME) and reported more than doubling MAP-Elites' performance on standard QD metrics.

Another line, Cully and Demiris (2018), set out a modular framework. In it the container (the archive), selection and variation are interchangeable parts. For the rest of this course the plain algorithm is enough, because the archive idea matters more than the optimiser inside it.

Systems connection

An archive is a key-value store with one unusual write rule: put if the cell is empty or the new value is better. That is a compare-and-set. If several workers evaluate children in parallel and write to a shared archive, the write must be atomic. Otherwise two workers can overwrite each other's improvement, a failure known as a lost update. The remedy is a compare-and-swap. Real frameworks often sidestep it by letting one process own the archive and having workers send results through a queue. Evaluations are usually the slow part, whether a simulation, a unit test run or a model call, so QD parallelises well.

Quality-diversity with foundation models

The hard part of QD used to be the descriptor. "How sarcastic is this story?" cannot be computed with a formula. Language models changed that. Bradley, Dai, Teufel, Zhang, Oostermeijer, Bellagente, Clune, Stanley, Schott and Lehman (2023, "Quality-Diversity through AI Feedback", QDAIF) used a language model for both halves of the loop.

The model writes variations of a candidate text, which is the mutation operator. It also judges each text for quality and for diversity along axes described in natural language, such as the genre of a story or the sentiment of a poem. The authors report that QDAIF covered more of the specified search space with high-quality samples than non-QD baselines. They also report that human raters showed reasonable agreement with the model's judgements.

Two things are worth carrying out of that paper. First, the archive structure is unchanged. It is still a map of elites, and the only difference is that "evaluate" is now a model call. Second, the mutation and the judgement are both noisy. This brings back the problem flagged in the "Go deeper" box: an LLM judge may score the same text differently on two calls.

This course's labs use a deterministic stand-in for the LLM. It is a function that maps a parent to a child and a text to a score, so that runs are reproducible. A real run replaces the stand-in with a model call and averages several judgements.

The same structure sits underneath the program-evolution systems of Part 3. AlphaEvolve-style loops keep a database of programs organised for diversity. The open-source OpenEvolve uses a MAP-Elites grid over program features, together with several island populations (chapter 10). Meta-agent search keeps an archive of discovered agent designs (chapter 12). When you meet those systems, ask the question from this chapter. What are the descriptors, and what decides who wins a cell?

State of play, October 2026

MAP-Elites (2015) and its relatives are mature, with open-source libraries and a long literature. The core algorithm in this chapter has not changed. What is moving quickly is the use of language and vision-language models as mutation operators and as judges of descriptors, as in QDAIF (2023) and in evolutionary coding frameworks. Which framework or library is most used changes monthly. Reported gains from LLM-driven QD depend heavily on the evaluator, so read each claim against its own evaluation. A reasonable plan is to learn the algorithm here, then check the current repositories for the details.

What goes wrong

  • A descriptor that does not matter. The map fills, the QD-score climbs, and nobody can use the results because the axes describe nothing a user cares about. Test the descriptor early by looking at pairs of elites from far-apart cells: are they actually different in a way you can see?
  • A descriptor that is gameable. If the descriptor is something the solution can fake, the search will fill the cells with fakes. An example is a text that mentions the word "formal" many times to score as formal. This is the evaluator-gaming problem developed in chapter 15.
  • Noisy evaluation inflating elites. Described above. Re-evaluate before you trust a cell.
  • Mistaking coverage for usefulness. A map with 100% coverage whose cells are all poor is not a success. Look at the distribution of qualities, not only the count of filled cells.
  • Premature resolution. Starting with a very fine grid yields a sparse, patchy map. Start coarse and refine.
  • Reading the map as a result about the world. The map shows what this search found, not what exists. An empty cell means "not found", not "impossible".

Key takeaways

  • Quality-diversity algorithms return a map of the best solution for each kind of behaviour rather than one winner. You define the kinds with a behaviour descriptor.
  • MAP-Elites keeps one elite per cell, chooses parents uniformly from filled cells, and lets a child compete only against the occupant of the cell it lands in. Weak but unusual solutions survive and become stepping stones.
  • Judge a run with coverage, QD-score and best quality, and always report the grid. QD-scores from different resolutions are not comparable.
  • On the arm task, MAP-Elites matched an ordinary optimiser on best quality while filling 336 cells. On the 20-bit trap it solved 30 of 30 runs, where objective search solved none.
  • The descriptor is the main design decision. It must be computable, meaningful, not a copy of the quality, and hard to fake.
  • High-dimensional descriptors need a fixed-size archive (CVT-MAP-Elites). Smarter variation (CMA-ME) speeds illumination. Language models now supply mutations and descriptors (QDAIF) but add noise you must average out.

Check your understanding

In MAP-Elites, how is the parent for the next child chosen, and why?

  1. The fittest elite in the archive, because good parents make good children.
  2. A filled cell chosen uniformly at random, so unusual and weak elites get as much attention as strong ones.
  3. The elite whose descriptor is farthest from all others, as in novelty search.
  4. The most recently added elite, to keep the search moving.
Uniform choice over filled cells is what pushes the frontier of the map outward. Choosing the fittest (option 1) would collapse the search onto the champion, as in a normal GA. Option 3 describes novelty search, which MAP-Elites does not need because the grid already separates behaviours.

A child has quality 0.40 and its descriptor lands in an empty cell, while its parent has quality 0.90. What does MAP-Elites do with the child?

  1. Discards it, because it is worse than its parent.
  2. Discards it unless it beats the archive's median quality.
  3. Replaces its parent, since the child is newer.
  4. Stores it in the empty cell, because competition is only within a cell.
An empty cell has no occupant to beat, so the child enters, and may later be a stepping stone. Option 1 is how global selection works; the archive deliberately avoids it. Option 3 confuses the parent's cell with the child's: the parent stays where it is.

You run two MAP-Elites experiments with the same budget: one on a 10 by 10 grid, one on a 40 by 40 grid. The second reports a QD-score nearly twenty times higher. What can you conclude?

  1. Nothing about which is better: QD-score grows with the number of cells, so the two are not comparable.
  2. The 40 by 40 search is twenty times better at finding good solutions.
  3. The 10 by 10 search has a bug, since its score is so low.
  4. The finer grid must have a higher best quality as well.
QD-score is a sum over cells, so more cells give a larger total even if each elite is worse. In the sweep in this chapter the QD-score rose from 22.9 to 2,115.9 while mean quality per cell fell from 0.92 to 0.45. Compare coverage fraction and mean quality at a fixed grid instead.

Your descriptor for an archive of generated product descriptions is "the model's own confidence in its answer", which is almost perfectly correlated with the quality score. What is likely to happen?

  1. The archive fills more evenly, because the descriptor is informative.
  2. The search converges faster, since the two signals reinforce each other.
  3. The cells fill along a single line, so the archive is a sorted list of scores, not a map of different behaviours.
  4. MAP-Elites refuses to run, because descriptors must be independent of quality.
When the descriptor is a copy of the quality, the "kinds of behaviour" axis is just the score axis and diversity is illusory. The algorithm runs fine (option 4 is wrong), but it adds nothing over a sorted list. Option 1 mistakes correlation for informativeness.

You want a MAP-Elites archive over six descriptor dimensions with a budget of 100,000 evaluations. Which approach is most sensible?

  1. A regular grid with 10 bins per axis, one million cells, and hope that it fills.
  2. A CVT archive with a few hundred centroids, so the number of cells matches the budget.
  3. A single cell, which reduces to an ordinary optimiser.
  4. A regular grid with 2 bins per axis, so exactly 12 cells.
A 10-per-axis grid in six dimensions has 106 cells, ten times the budget, so most would stay empty. A CVT archive lets you choose the number of cells independently of the dimension. Two bins per axis gives 26 = 64 cells (not 12) and is too coarse to be a useful map. A single cell throws away the diversity that justifies the method.

A team uses an LLM judge to score both quality and a "formality" descriptor, and sees the QD-score rise steadily over a run. Which is the most important check before trusting it?

  1. Confirm the QD-score is rising at least linearly.
  2. Increase the grid resolution until the score is larger.
  3. Remove the quality function so only coverage is measured.
  4. Re-evaluate the elites several times and see whether their scores hold up, then check that cells really differ in formality.
With a noisy judge, the cell-wise "keep the best" rule selects lucky scores, so a rising QD-score can partly measure noise. Re-evaluating tests that, and reading elites from different cells tests that the descriptor is real. A bigger grid (option 2) raises the number without improving the evidence.
