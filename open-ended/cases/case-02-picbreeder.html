Picbreeder & Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned

Picbreeder was a website where strangers bred pictures by clicking the ones they liked. Nobody had a goal, yet the site produced skulls, butterflies and faces, and when researchers later told an evolutionary algorithm to find those same pictures on purpose, it mostly could not. The episode became the evidence for a claim by Kenneth Stanley and Joel Lehman: for some problems the best way to reach a goal is not to aim at it.

I fetched and read in full: Woolley and Stanley, "On the Deleterious Effects of A Priori Objectives on Evolution and Representation" (GECCO 2011); Kumar, Clune, Lehman and Stanley, "Questioning Representational Optimism in Deep Learning: The Fractured Entangled Representation Hypothesis" (arXiv, May 2025); and the arXiv paper "Exploring Promising Stepping Stones by Combining Novelty Search with Interactive Evolution", which summarises the first result. I did not obtain the full text of the Picbreeder journal paper (Secretan et al., Evolutionary Computation, 2011) or the 2008 CHI paper: I saw their bibliographic records and abstracts through a search tool, and I take the Picbreeder mechanics from the three papers I did read. The pattern in one sentence

Interesting things are often reachable only through intermediate steps that do not look like them, so a search that rewards resemblance to a target can fail where a search that follows what is interesting succeeds.

Context: how to breed a picture

An interactive evolutionary computation system lets a person act as the fitness function: the program shows candidates, the person picks, and the program breeds the picks. Picbreeder (Secretan et al., 2008, extended in 2011) added the collaboration. Per the papers I read, each generation showed 15 candidate images, a user selected one or more as parents, and the system produced the next 15. When a user was satisfied, they could publish an image to the site, and anyone else could take it and keep evolving it, which the site called branching. The result was a growing tree of images, each descended from someone else's.

The images are not stored as pixels. Each is the output of a CPPN (compositional pattern-producing network), a small network that takes a pixel's coordinates, x, y and the distance d from the centre, and returns that pixel's colour. A CPPN uses unusual activation functions such as sine, cosine and tanh, which is why symmetry and repetition fall out of it easily. The networks are evolved with NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies, Stanley and Miikkulainen, 2002), which starts from tiny networks and adds nodes and connections only occasionally. So a new picture begins as a very simple function and gains structure step by step. Chapter 2 covers the evolutionary machinery; the mutation and selection steps here are the same, except that the selector is a human.

Stepping stone
An intermediate discovery that is needed to reach a later one but does not necessarily resemble it.
Objective
A target the search is scored against, such as similarity to a given image.
Deception
A search landscape in which improving the score leads away from the goal; see chapter 3.
Phylogeny
The family tree of images: which image each was bred from.
Re-evolution
Taking an image that was found without a goal and giving it to the same algorithm as a goal (the Woolley and Stanley test).

What the site produced

Woolley and Stanley report that novice users encouraged to evolve patterns without an expectation of what they would get frequently found appealing images within 10 to 30 generations. The 2025 FER paper says Picbreeder users discovered "thousands" of interesting images, although the vast majority of the space of CPPN images is uninterpretable blobs. The 2011 paper gives a few specific cases with their cumulative generation counts, summed along the branch from the first ancestor: a Skull in 74 generations, a Butterfly in 90, and, with only 15 images per generation, an Eye and a Crescent in 12 each, a Warp pattern in 5 and a plain image in 1. That is small next to the thousands of generations, with ten times the population, that an automated run needed for the images it did reach.

The authors stress that the users were not usually after a particular image. As the 2025 paper puts it, if mutating an "egg" led to a "teapot", users could pivot to pursue the teapot. This goal switching is what Stanley and Lehman later argued for in general terms in their book: at each step, follow what looks interesting, because you cannot tell in advance which interesting thing is the stepping stone to something remarkable.

The test: give the algorithm the goal

Woolley and Stanley asked a pointed question. The Skull was found in 74 generations by a population of 15. If it is so easy for NEAT and CPPNs to find, then NEAT told to find the Skull should do at least as well. They tried exactly that. Their setup, as reported:

  • The same NEAT software and CPPN representation, with the population raised from 15 to 150 and each run stopped at 30,000 generations.
  • A fitness function that scores each candidate image by its distance to the target image, using a feature-based comparison. A run counts as a success if fitness exceeds 0.75 of a 1.0 maximum, which the paper says corresponds to an average error of 5.75% between feature sets and was checked by eye.
  • 20 runs per target, six targets taken from Picbreeder: Simple, Crescent, Eye, Warp, Butterfly and Skull.
  • A validation run: with a random CPPN of five connections and one hidden node as the target, 19 of 20 runs succeeded in 353 generations on average (median 135), which the authors take as evidence that the fitness function is a reasonable way to compare images.

For the two simplest targets, success took far longer than the human route: the 14 successful Simple runs needed 3,774 generations on average (median 965) against 1 for the original, and the 11 Crescent runs needed 3,500 (median 2,320) against 12. For the Eye, one of 20 runs succeeded, at generation 4,840 against 12 originally. For Warp, Butterfly and Skull, none of the 20 runs reached the target in 30,000 generations. The networks that did succeed were also bloated: for Simple, the objective-driven solutions averaged 19.5 functions and 23.4 connections against 6 functions and 5 connections in the original discovery.

Why? The paper's explanation concerns stepping stones. In the original Eye discovery, the early ancestors in the lineage did not look like an eye at all; resemblance to an eye is not the right heuristic for identifying them, and "no image comparison, no matter how good, can really reward stepping stone images". An objective that rewards looking like the target therefore pulls the population towards images that resemble it superficially while the structure needed to build it, such as symmetry, appears much later or never. The authors take care to note that the same algorithm had already found each of these images, so the failure cannot be blamed on NEAT or CPPNs being unsuited to the task.

A trap you can run

The toy below is my own, and a loose analogy: the stand-in for "follow what is interesting" is an algorithmic novelty score, not a person. The genome has 20 bits in 4 blocks of 5. The score is a deceptive trap: each block scores 5 if all five bits are 1, and otherwise 4 minus the number of ones, so the score rises as ones are removed. The target is all ones, which is also the maximum score, but every neighbouring step towards it lowers the score.

Three searches get 6,000 steps each, over 10 seeds. Random search samples genomes. The objective hill-climber flips one random bit and keeps the change if the trap score does not drop. Novelty search (chapter 3) keeps an archive of how many ones each block has, picks eight archived parents, mutates each, and keeps the child whose block profile is furthest from its nearest neighbours in a random sample of the archive. It never looks at the trap score; it only checks whether the target has appeared. Because it tries eight candidates per step, I also gave the hill-climber eight times the steps.

"""Toy: a target that is easy to reach only if you do not aim at it.
Genome: 20 bits = 4 blocks of 5. Objective: deceptive 'trap' per block
(5 if all ones, else 4 - number of ones), so climbing the score walks AWAY from all-ones.
Compare an objective hill-climber with a no-objective novelty search over
block-wise behaviour. Standard library only; seeded."""
import random
B, K = 4, 5
def blocks(g): return tuple(sum(g[i*K:(i+1)*K]) for i in range(B))
def trap(g): return sum(K if u == K else K - 1 - u for u in blocks(g))
def solved(g): return all(u == K for u in blocks(g))
def mutate(g, rng):
    h = g[:]; i = rng.randrange(B*K); h[i] ^= 1; return h

def objective(seed, budget):
    rng = random.Random(seed); g = [rng.randint(0, 1) for _ in range(B*K)]
    for t in range(budget):
        h = mutate(g, rng)
        if trap(h) >= trap(g): g = h
        if solved(g): return t
    return None

def novelty(seed, budget, k=5):
    rng = random.Random(seed); g = [rng.randint(0, 1) for _ in range(B*K)]
    archive = [(blocks(g), g)]
    for t in range(budget):
        cands = rng.sample(archive, min(len(archive), 8))      # branch from stored 'stepping stones'
        best = None
        for _, p in cands:
            h = mutate(p, rng); b = blocks(h)
            d = sorted(sum(abs(x - y) for x, y in zip(b, a)) for a, _ in rng.sample(archive, min(len(archive), 100)))[:k]
            score = sum(d) / len(d)
            if best is None or score > best[0]: best = (score, b, h)
        if best[0] > 0: archive.append((best[1], best[2]))     # keep only behaviourally new things
        if solved(best[2]): return t
    return None

BUDGET, RUNS = 6000, 10
def rand_search(seed, budget):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    for t in range(budget):
        if solved([rng.randint(0, 1) for _ in range(B*K)]): return t
    return None

for name, fn in (("random search", rand_search), ("objective hill-climber", objective), ("novelty search", novelty)):
    res = [fn(s, BUDGET) for s in range(RUNS)]
    hit = [r for r in res if r is not None]
    med = sorted(hit)[len(hit)//2] if hit else None
    print("%-22s solved %2d of %d runs in %d steps; median steps when solved: %s" % (name, len(hit), RUNS, BUDGET, med))
res = [objective(s, 8 * BUDGET) for s in range(RUNS)]
print("objective hill-climber, given 8x the steps (matching novelty's 8 candidates per step): solved %d of %d" % (sum(r is not None for r in res), RUNS))
random search          solved  0 of 10 runs in 6000 steps; median steps when solved: None
objective hill-climber solved  0 of 10 runs in 6000 steps; median steps when solved: None
novelty search         solved  8 of 10 runs in 6000 steps; median steps when solved: 3228
objective hill-climber, given 8x the steps (matching novelty's 8 candidates per step): solved 0 of 10

Random search has about a 0.6% chance per run of hitting the one target among 1,048,576 genomes in 6,000 samples, and found it in none of the 10 runs. The hill-climber never finds it: it walks to the all-zero blocks and stays there, even with eight times the steps. Novelty search found the target in 8 of 10 runs, with a median of 3,228 steps, because the archive keeps lineages that have a few ones in a block, which are exactly the stepping stones the score punishes. Caveats, mine: this is a toy in which "novel" happens to coincide with "on the way", and a different deceptive landscape could defeat novelty search too (chapter 3 shows how). It is evidence for the mechanism, not for the book's thesis.

Try it yourself

First make the score honest: replace the body of trap with return sum(blocks(g)), so the score is the number of ones. When I ran this, the hill-climber solved 10 of 10 runs (median 53 steps) and novelty search 8 of 10 (median 3,228): with an informative objective, aiming wins by a wide margin. Then restore the trap and change B, K = 4, 5 to B, K = 6, 5, which makes the target about a thousand times rarer. At 6,000 steps all methods failed in my run, so raise BUDGET and see whether novelty search catches up, remembering that the run takes longer. What does the pair of experiments say about when to trust a score?

A second look: what is inside the network

In May 2025 Kumar, Clune, Lehman and Stanley revisited Picbreeder from a different angle. They took the Picbreeder skull CPPN and trained a network of the same architecture with ordinary stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to output the identical image. The outputs match, but the authors argue the insides do not: by visualising every hidden neuron as an image, they report that the Picbreeder network builds the skull from organised parts, with symmetry computed early and reused, while the SGD network builds it as an untidy patchwork of overlapping pieces. They call the first a unified factored representation and the second a fractured entangled representation (FER). In weight sweeps, individual weights in the Picbreeder skull change one meaningful property such as mouth width, while sweeping weights in the SGD network gives, in their words, "meaningless distortions". They speculate that FER may degrade generalisation, creativity and continual learning in large models trained by SGD.

Treat this with the caution the authors apply themselves. It is a position paper, and its central comparison is mainly one image, the skull, with others in an appendix; the authors note that they chose sweeps to present each method in its best light while keeping them representative, and they release the full database of sweeps. They also say it is not yet understood whether NEAT or the open-ended search process is the cause, and they point to the 2011 re-evolution result (bloated networks when the same images are evolved as objectives) as a hint that the open-ended process may matter more. Whether the effect carries over to language models is, in their own words, speculative.

State of play, October 2026

The 2025 paper refers to Picbreeder in the past tense ("was a website"). A 2026 paper, "In Search of the Ingredients of Open-Endedness: Replicating Picbreeder with Large Vision-Language Models" (arXiv 2605.23908, prepared for GECCO 2026), replaces the human users with vision-language model agents and compares the result with about 9,758 images published by humans on the original site. I read only a summary page. As summarised there, with some exploratory noise the model-driven archives approach the human baseline for diversity, but the model trees are less balanced than the human ones, and the human users take larger semantic leaps; a full run matching the human collection's size is said to have cost around $10,000. These findings are fresh, from a preprint-stage source I did not read in full, and may be revised.

What the critics and cautious readers would say

  • Survivorship. The targets were chosen because users found them remarkable. We see the images that worked and not the thousands of branches that went nowhere. That does not undermine the re-evolution result, whose claim is only that an algorithm which found these images once cannot find them by aiming, but it does mean the numbers say little about how often a no-objective search wins overall.
  • The fitness function was one choice. Resemblance in a feature space is a natural objective but not the only one. A fitness that rewarded the right intermediate structure could in principle do better; the paper's argument is that no resemblance measure can reward stepping stones that do not resemble the target, and the authors admit that the fitness function will be scrutinised. I did not see a test of an alternative objective.
  • From images to greatness. The leap from evolving pictures to "greatness cannot be planned" in science, art and careers is the book's argument. The papers I read support a narrower claim: in this domain, with this representation, aiming defeats the algorithm.
  • Humans are in the loop. The discoveries rely on people's taste, which is hard to replicate and to measure. Later work in this course tries to replace the person with a learned notion of interestingness: see chapter 6.

Lessons for AI engineers

  • A benchmark failure is not an algorithm failure. If a search method cannot reach a goal you set, it may be that the score is the problem. Check whether your metric rewards partial progress that does not look like the answer.
  • Keep a diverse archive. Quality-diversity methods (chapter 4) store the best member of each niche so that unusual lineages survive long enough to become stepping stones. The same instinct shapes the islands in FunSearch (the FunSearch case) and the database in AlphaEvolve (the last case).
  • Objectives are still essential when they are informative. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve work because the evaluator gives a rich, graded score on a structured problem. The lesson is not "drop the objective" but "know whether your landscape is deceptive". Chapter 5 shows what to do when it is.
  • Two systems can behave identically and differ inside. An evaluator that only looks at outputs cannot tell a factored solution from a patchwork. This is one reason held-out and behavioural tests matter (chapter 16), and why Lab 2 compares search with and without an objective.

Common misconception

"Stanley and Lehman say objectives are bad." The claim is narrower: ambitious objectives can be deceptive, so that the way to reach them is not always to measure distance to them. Picbreeder users did have goals at every step, for example "make this look more like a face"; what they did not have was a single fixed target for the whole search.

Key takeaways

  • Picbreeder found images in tens of generations by following human interest, with no fixed goal.
  • Woolley and Stanley (2011) gave the same algorithm those images as targets: 0 of 20 runs reached the Skull, Butterfly or Warp in 30,000 generations.
  • The stated reason is that stepping stones need not resemble the goal, so resemblance is a poor fitness signal.
  • A deceptive trap plus novelty search reproduces the effect in 40 lines, as an analogy rather than a proof.
  • The 2025 FER paper adds a representational angle, but it is a position paper about a few networks, and its link to large models is a hypothesis.

Check your understanding

In Woolley and Stanley's experiment, why is the failure to re-evolve the Skull hard to blame on NEAT or the CPPN representation?

  1. The runs were stopped after only a few hundred generations
  2. The same algorithm and representation had already discovered the Skull on Picbreeder, in 74 generations
  3. The target was too large for the network to represent
  4. The population in the automated runs was smaller than on Picbreeder
The paper's point is that the targets were already produced by this algorithm, so representation or algorithm limits are excluded as the explanation. Runs lasted up to 30,000 generations and the automated population was larger (150 against 15), so A and D are wrong.

In the toy, why does the hill-climber never solve it even with eight times the steps?

  1. It has too small a mutation rate
  2. It does not evaluate the target
  3. Random bit flips cannot change a block
  4. Each step towards the all-ones block lowers the score, so the climber keeps moving to the all-zero local optimum
The trap scores 4 for no ones and 5 only for all ones, with lower scores in between. More steps do not help a search that rejects the intermediate states it needs.

Which statement does the evidence in these papers best support?

  1. For image-evolving CPPNs, searching by resemblance to a target can fail to find images that undirected search found quickly
  2. Objectives should be removed from all search algorithms
  3. Large language models have fractured entangled representations
  4. Human taste is necessary for any discovery
The papers show the first claim. Option C is a hypothesis the FER authors raise, not something they establish; B and D go far beyond what a few image experiments show.

What is one limitation of the FER comparison the authors themselves state?

  1. It uses a different image for every network
  2. The two networks produce different outputs
  3. It is not known whether NEAT or the open-ended search process is responsible for the organised representation
  4. SGD could not reproduce the skull at all
SGD did reproduce the skull exactly; the claim is about the inside. The authors note that the relative roles of NEAT and open-ended search are not well understood, and the paper is a position paper that focuses mainly on the skull.
