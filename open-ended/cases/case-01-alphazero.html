AlphaGo to AlphaZero

In 2015 and 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat a professional and then a world champion at Go by pairing Monte Carlo tree search with two neural networks trained on human games. Within two years the same group had removed the human games, the hand-built features, the rollouts and finally the game-specific design, leaving one loop in which search makes the network better and the network makes search better. This case reads the three papers in order and asks what each deletion taught us.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the full text of all three papers: Silver et al., "Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search" (Nature, 2016), Silver et al., "Mastering the game of Go without human knowledge" (Nature, 2017), and the arXiv preprint of Silver et al., "Mastering Chess and Shogi by Self-Play with a General Reinforcement Learning Algorithm" (December 2017). I also read the DeepMind pages on AlphaGo and AlphaGo Zero, the abstract of Wu's KataGo paper and the abstract of the MuZero paper. I did not read the later journal version of the AlphaZero paper, the contemporary commentary on the Stockfish match, or any independent re-run of the experiments, so every number below is a number the DeepMind authors report about their own systems. Where I add a point of my own it is marked as mine.

The pattern in one sentence

Use a learned network to tell tree search where to look and how good a position is, then use the results of that search as the training signal for the network, so the two improve each other without any outside teacher.

Context: a search problem too wide for brute force

Chess programs of the 1990s and 2000s, including Deep Blue, which beat the human world champion in 1997, searched many millions of positions with alpha-beta search (a way of pruning a game tree so that lines that cannot change the best move are never explored) and scored leaves with an evaluation function written by experts. Go resisted this recipe. The AlphaGo paper gives the reasons in its introduction: the number of legal moves at each turn is large, and nobody had written a good evaluation function for a Go position. Chapter 1 covers the alternative that worked best before 2015, Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), which estimates how good a move is by playing many random games from it. Random playouts are noisy, and a position that looks balanced to a random player may be lost to a strong one.

The question AlphaGo asked was whether a neural network could supply the two things MCTS lacked: a short list of promising moves at each node, and a good estimate of who is winning without playing the game out. Everything after that, up to AlphaZero, is a series of answers to a second question: where should the network's training data come from?

Policy network
A network that takes a board position and outputs a probability for every legal move, used to decide which branches of the tree to explore first.
Value network
A network that takes a board position and outputs a single number estimating how likely the player to move is to win.
Rollout
A fast, simple playout from a position to the end of the game, used by earlier MCTS programs (and the first AlphaGo) to estimate a position's value.
Self-play
Generating training games by having the current program play against itself, so no human games are needed.
Tabula rasa
"Blank slate": starting from random weights with no knowledge except the rules of the game (the term the authors use).
PUCT
The tree-search selection rule that adds a bonus proportional to the network's move probability and shrinking with the visit count, so promising moves are tried first.

AlphaGo, 2016: networks learned from people, wrapped in search

The first AlphaGo used three learned or semi-learned components. A 13-layer supervised-learning (SL) policy network was trained on 30 million positions from the KGS Go Server to predict the move a strong human played. The paper reports 57.0% accuracy on held-out positions using all its input features and 55.7% from raw board position and move history alone, against 44.4% for the best earlier result from other groups. A copy was then improved by reinforcement learning from games against earlier versions of itself, and a value network was trained on positions from those self-play games to predict the winner. Finally, a small rollout policy built on hand-designed features played fast playouts to the end of the game.

During search, each leaf position was evaluated by mixing the value network's estimate with the outcome of a rollout. The paper reports that a variant using only the value network (no rollouts) already exceeded every other Go program it tested, so the learned evaluation was doing the heavy lifting even then. Against other computer programs the final single-machine version won 494 of 495 games (99.8%); with four free handicap stones given to the opponent it won 77%, 86% and 99% against Crazy Stone, Zen and Pachi. The distributed version, using 1,202 CPUs and 176 GPUs, won 77% of games against the single-machine version. In October 2015 it beat the European champion Fan Hui 5 to 0 in a formal match, and in March 2016 it beat Lee Sedol 4 to 1 in Seoul (DeepMind's AlphaGo page).

This was a pipeline with many stages and many human fingerprints: human games to start, hand-built features for the rollouts, separate networks for policy and value, and a search that mixed learned and hand-made evaluation.

AlphaGo Zero, 2017: delete the teacher

The 2017 paper removes the human data and keeps the search. According to the paper and DeepMind's page, AlphaGo Zero starts from random play and learns only from games against itself, takes only the black and white stones as input (no hand-engineered features), uses no rollouts, and merges policy and value into one network with two outputs. The training described in the paper's empirical section ran for approximately 3 days, generated 4.9 million self-play games with 1,600 simulations per move (about 0.4 seconds of thinking), and made 700,000 parameter updates on mini-batches of 2,048 positions, with a network of 20 residual blocks. After 72 hours it beat the exact version of AlphaGo that had defeated Lee Sedol by 100 games to 0 under two-hour time controls. It ran on a single machine with 4 TPUs, whereas the older program was distributed over many machines using 48 TPUs.

A longer run of 40 days with a 40-block network produced the version the paper compares with AlphaGo Master. In a 100-game match with two-hour time controls, AlphaGo Zero won 89 to 11. On the Elo scale the authors use, they report 5,185 for AlphaGo Zero, 4,858 for Master, 3,739 for AlphaGo Lee and 3,144 for the earlier AlphaGo Fan. The raw network with no search at all, playing its highest-probability move, was rated 3,055.

The paper also isolates two architecture choices using a fixed dataset of self-play games. Switching from a plain convolutional network to a residual network improved the playing strength of AlphaGo by over 600 Elo, and combining policy and value into one network boosted it by around another 600, partly because the shared representation reduced the value error even though move-prediction accuracy dropped slightly. This is an easy result to skim past, and it is one of the cleanest in the series: it tells you that the network design mattered as much as the data source.

The loop, in AlphaZero's terms

The key to both Zero papers is what the network is trained towards. The network takes a position s and outputs (p, v) = fθ(s): move probabilities p and a value v. At every move of a self-play game the program runs an MCTS guided by that network and then plays a move sampled from the visit counts of the search, π. When the game ends, the outcome z is +1 for a win, 0 for a draw and −1 for a loss from the viewpoint of each position's player. The AlphaZero paper gives the loss as

l = (z − v)2 − πT log p + c‖θ‖2

a squared error pulling the value towards the real result, a cross-entropy pulling the policy towards the search's improved move distribution, and a weight penalty. The search is thus a policy-improvement operator (my phrase, not the paper's): with the same network, it almost always picks better moves than the raw policy, and the network is then trained to predict those better moves. It is not the policy-gradient method of LLM training chapter 7: there is no sampled-reward gradient on the policy, only supervised targets produced by search.

How the network steers the search

The AlphaGo Zero paper describes the selection step of each simulation: from the root, repeatedly choose the move that maximises Q(s, a) + U(s, a), where Q is the average value of simulations that took that move and U is a bonus proportional to the network's probability P(s, a) and shrinking as 1 + N(s, a) grows with the visit count. When a simulation reaches a leaf, the network evaluates it and returns v, which is backed up the path: there is no playout to the end of the game. A move with a high prior but poor results stops being explored as its Q falls; a move with a low prior is not explored at all unless the values underneath it justify it. At the root, Dirichlet noise is added to the prior so that the search keeps trying some moves the network currently dislikes, which matters because otherwise the network can only ever learn from what it already believes (the AlphaZero paper sets its scale to α = 0.3, 0.15 and 0.03 for chess, shogi and Go, in inverse proportion to the typical number of legal moves).

The same exploration-versus-exploitation trade-off from the bandit section of chapter 1 is at work, with the network replacing the uniform assumption about untried arms. Lab 1 builds the plain version.

AlphaZero: the same loop on three games

The December 2017 AlphaZero preprint applies the loop to chess and shogi as well as Go, "without any additional domain knowledge except the rules of the game". It also lists the ways in which it simplified AlphaGo Zero, which is a useful inventory of what was never essential:

The results, and the surprising search budget

The paper reports that AlphaZero overtook the 2016 world-champion chess program Stockfish after 4 hours (300,000 steps), the 2017 shogi champion Elmo after under 2 hours (110,000 steps), and AlphaGo Lee after 8 hours (165,000 steps). It then played 100-game matches at one minute per move against Stockfish 8, Elmo and the previous 3-day AlphaGo Zero.

The paper itself notes that the prevalence of draws in high-level chess compresses the Elo scale, so a 28-0-72 result is a smaller Elo gap than the same share of wins would imply in shogi or Go.

The most interesting number is about search, not strength. The paper reports that AlphaZero examines about 80 thousand positions per second in chess and 40 thousand in shogi, compared with 70 million for Stockfish and 35 million for Elmo. In the paper's words, it "compensates for the lower number of evaluations by using its deep neural network to focus much more selectively on the most promising variations". The authors also report that AlphaZero's MCTS scaled better with thinking time than either alpha-beta engine, which they say calls into question the widely held belief that alpha-beta is inherently superior in these games.

State of play, October 2026

The training details in this case are those reported in 2017. Since then, other groups have reimplemented the loop and made it cheaper: David Wu's KataGo paper (2019) reports "a 50x reduction in computation over comparable methods" for Go, reaching the strength of ELF OpenGo's final model in about 19 days on fewer than 30 GPUs, where the paper says AlphaZero needed "thousands of TPUs over several days" and ELF OpenGo "thousands of GPUs over two weeks". That is the author's own comparison. DeepMind's MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2019) goes further and removes the rules: it plans with a learned model of the environment and matched AlphaZero in Go, chess and shogi without being given the rules, as well as setting a new state of the art on 57 Atari games. I read both abstracts only.

A tiny AlphaZero you can run

The toy below is my own miniature of the loop, not DeepMind code. The game is Nim: a pile of 21 stones, players alternate taking 1, 2 or 3, and whoever takes the last stone wins. The "network" is two lookup tables, a value per pile size and a move distribution per pile size, both starting uniform. Self-play games are played by a PUCT-style MCTS with 30 simulations that uses the tables instead of rollouts; after each game the tables are nudged towards the result z and the search distribution π, exactly the targets in the loss above. The program is given the rules and nothing else. The known solution (the player to move loses when the pile is a multiple of 4) is used only to check what was learned.

"""Toy AlphaZero loop on Nim: pile of N stones, take 1 to 3, taking the last stone wins.
A tabular 'network' (P, V) is learned from self-play only; no strategy is given.
Standard library only; seeded."""
import math, random
N, MOVES = 21, (1, 2, 3)
def playout(n, rng):                      # +1 if the player to move at n wins a random game
    turn = 0
    while n > 0:
        n -= rng.choice([a for a in MOVES if a <= n]); turn ^= 1
    return 1.0 if turn == 1 else -1.0     # turn==1: original mover took the last stone

def mcts(n, sims, P, V, rng, use_net, c=1.5, noise=False):
    Nv, W = {}, {}                        # visits and total value per (pile, action), root-relative tree
    def search(n):                        # returns value for the player to move at n
        if n == 0: return -1.0            # previous player took the last stone: mover has lost
        acts = [a for a in MOVES if a <= n]
        tot = sum(Nv.get((n, a), 0) for a in acts)
        best, ba = -9, None
        for a in acts:
            q = W.get((n, a), 0) / Nv[(n, a)] if Nv.get((n, a)) else 0.0
            pri = P[n][a] if use_net else 1 / len(acts)
            u = q + c * pri * math.sqrt(tot + 1) / (1 + Nv.get((n, a), 0))
            if u > best: best, ba = u, a
        k = (n, ba)
        if k not in Nv:                   # leaf: evaluate, then back up
            Nv[k] = 0; W[k] = 0.0
            m = n - ba
            if m == 0: v = 1.0            # we took the last stone: we win
            else: v = -(V[m] if use_net else playout(m, rng))
        else:
            v = -search(n - ba)
        Nv[k] += 1; W[k] += v
        return v
    for _ in range(sims): search(n)
    return {a: Nv.get((n, a), 0) for a in MOVES if a <= n}

def train(games, sims, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    P = {n: {a: 1 / min(3, n) for a in MOVES if a <= n} for n in range(1, N + 1)}
    V = {n: 0.0 for n in range(N + 1)}
    log = []
    for g in range(1, games + 1):
        n, hist = rng.randint(N - 6, N), []
        while n > 0:
            visits = mcts(n, sims, P, V, rng, True)
            tot = sum(visits.values()); pi = {a: v / tot for a, v in visits.items()}
            hist.append((n, pi))
            a = rng.choices(list(pi), weights=list(pi.values()))[0]; n -= a
        z = 1.0                           # last mover wins; walk back, alternating sign
        for n_, pi in reversed(hist):
            V[n_] += 0.1 * (z - V[n_])
            for a in P[n_]: P[n_][a] += 0.1 * (pi.get(a, 0) - P[n_][a])
            z = -z
        if g in (10, 50, 200, games):
            truth = [(V[m] < 0) == (m % 4 == 0) for m in range(1, N + 1)]
            log.append((g, sum(truth)))
    return P, V, log

def match(sims, P, V, seed, games=200, start=N):
    rng = random.Random(seed); wins = 0
    for g in range(games):
        n, turn = start - rng.randint(0, 2), g % 2          # alternate who moves first
        while n > 0:
            net = (turn == 0)
            v = mcts(n, sims, P, V, rng, net)
            a = max(v, key=v.get); n -= a
            if n == 0: wins += (turn == 0)
            turn ^= 1
    return wins / games

P, V, log = train(games=400, sims=30, seed=0)
for g, ok in log: print("after %3d self-play games: value table has the correct sign at %2d of 21 piles" % (g, ok))
print("piles with V<0 (learned 'mover loses'):", [n for n in range(1, N + 1) if V[n] < -0.2])
print("true losing piles (n % 4 == 0):        ", [n for n in range(1, N + 1) if n % 4 == 0])
print("net-guided MCTS vs random-playout MCTS, 30 simulations each:", match(30, P, V, 1), "win rate")
print("same match with 6 simulations each:", match(6, P, V, 2), "win rate")
after  10 self-play games: value table has the correct sign at 14 of 21 piles
after  50 self-play games: value table has the correct sign at 21 of 21 piles
after 200 self-play games: value table has the correct sign at 21 of 21 piles
after 400 self-play games: value table has the correct sign at 21 of 21 piles
piles with V<0 (learned 'mover loses'): [4, 8, 12, 16, 20]
true losing piles (n % 4 == 0):         [4, 8, 12, 16, 20]
net-guided MCTS vs random-playout MCTS, 30 simulations each: 1.0 win rate
same match with 6 simulations each: 1.0 win rate

After 10 games the table already has the right sign at 14 of 21 pile sizes; after 50 it has all 21, and it identifies exactly the piles 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 as lost for the player to move. Net-guided search then beat the random-playout search in all 200 games at both 30 and 6 simulations. Two cautions, both mine. A 21-entry table is a toy of the "network" idea: nothing has to generalise, so this shows the loop converging but not the hard part of AlphaZero, which is learning a function that transfers to positions it has never seen. And the opponent here is a weak baseline, so the 1.0 win rate says the learned value is useful, not that it is strong.

Try it yourself

Change sims=30 in the train(...) call to 5, then to 2, and watch the count of correct signs. When I ran this, 5 simulations still reached 21 of 21 by game 200, while 2 simulations ended at 9 of 21 after 400 games: the search must be deep enough to improve on the network, or the targets teach it nothing new. Then restore 30 and change the final True in the mcts(...) call inside train to False, so self-play uses random-playout search instead of the table. I got 17 of 21 after 400 games. What the critics and later work said

I did not fetch public commentary on the matches, so this section sticks to limitations visible in the papers themselves and to the later work I read.

  • Go was a friendly domain, and the authors say so. The AlphaZero paper notes that Go's rules are translation-invariant, defined through adjacency, and symmetric under rotation and reflection, which suits convolutional networks, and that its binary outcomes help training. Chess and shogi are, it says, "arguably, less innately suited". The loop still worked, but the authors needed to drop the symmetry tricks.
  • The method needs a perfect simulator and a clear outcome. Search runs by playing out the rules, and training needs z. Games with known rules and a win condition supply both for free; most real tasks supply neither. MuZero removes the first requirement, not the second.
  • Compute is part of the result. Self-play used 5,000 first-generation TPUs, while the matches were played on a single machine with 4 TPUs. The comparison with Stockfish (64 threads, 1 GB hash, one minute per move, 100 games) is a comparison of systems under particular settings, and the authors report only that configuration. Wu's later paper is a reminder that the original recipe was not the cheapest way to reach its strength.
  • Strength is measured against fixed opponents plus the agent's own history. Elo curves inside a self-play run measure progress against earlier selves, which can drift; the external matches are what ground the claim. See held-out evaluation for why a score measured on the thing being optimised needs an outside check.

Lessons for AI engineers

  • Search is a way to turn compute into better decisions, and a learned prior makes that compute go further. The same idea shows up in language models as inference-time search, where a verifier or reward model plays the role of the value.
  • The training signal came from the environment, not from people. That is the same ingredient that makes reinforcement learning with checkable rewards work for reasoning models: see DeepSeek-R1. Both need a cheap, trustworthy way to say who won.
  • Remove stages and see what breaks. Each version deleted a human-supplied component and the system got stronger or simpler. When a pipeline has many hand-built parts, ask which of them is only there because of the previous generation's limits.
  • Self-play data generation is the parallel part. The paper used roughly 80 times as many accelerators for generating games as for training (5,000 first-generation TPUs against 64 second-generation ones). Generation is many independent searches; training is one synchronised job. See parallelism and GPUs for why these two shapes of work get provisioned differently.
  • Search plus learning as a loop reappears in program search. The evolutionary systems in the FunSearch case and the AlphaEvolve case keep the same shape, with a language model in the policy role and an automatic evaluator in the role of the game result.

Common misconception

"AlphaZero learned without any knowledge." It was given the rules, the board encoding, the search algorithm, the network architecture, the loss and a large compute budget. What it was not given was human play or human-written evaluation. "Without human knowledge" (the AlphaGo Zero title) is the accurate phrase.

Key takeaways

  • A network supplies a prior over moves and a value for positions; tree search spends compute where the prior says to look.
  • Search results (visit counts and the final result) are better training targets than the network's own raw outputs, so the loop improves itself.
  • Each version deleted a human input: game records, features, rollouts, gating, per-game tuning.
  • AlphaZero searched about a thousand times fewer positions per second than Stockfish and still won the reported matches.
  • The method relies on a perfect simulator and a clear win condition, and all headline numbers are the authors' own.

Check your understanding

When an AlphaZero search reaches a new leaf position, how is that leaf scored?

  1. By playing a fast random game to the end and counting the winner
  2. By a hand-written evaluation function tuned by experts
  3. By the network's value output for that position
  4. By looking it up in a database of human games
The AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero papers use only the network at leaves. The first AlphaGo mixed the value network with rollouts, which is the tempting wrong answer; random playouts are the older MCTS recipe that the learned value replaced.

What is the policy head trained to match?

  1. The move probabilities produced by the tree search at each position
  2. The moves a human expert played in the same position
  3. The final result of the game, as a single number
  4. Its own previous output, to keep training stable
The cross-entropy term pulls p toward the search distribution pi. The game result z trains the value head, not the policy head. Human moves were used only in the 2016 AlphaGo's supervised stage.

AlphaZero searched about 80 thousand positions per second in chess against Stockfish's 70 million, yet won the reported match. What is the best explanation?

  1. The match was played at a time control that favoured AlphaZero
  2. The network's policy and value let it spend its evaluations on far more relevant lines, so each one counts for more
  3. Stockfish could not use more than one thread
  4. MCTS always beats alpha-beta regardless of the evaluation
The paper says the network focuses the search on the most promising variations. Both programs used the same one-minute-per-move limit, Stockfish ran with 64 threads, and the paper does not claim MCTS wins without a good learned evaluation.

In the toy Nim program, why is it fair to say the table was learned "from self-play only"?

  1. It was initialised with the known losing piles, then refined
  2. It was trained on games played by a perfect Nim strategy
  3. The search used random rollouts to label every pile
  4. It started uniform and was updated only from the results and search distributions of its own games
The known solution appears only in the final check. Option C describes the plain MCTS baseline used for comparison, not the learning loop. As in the real system, the rules of the game are given; the strategy is not.
