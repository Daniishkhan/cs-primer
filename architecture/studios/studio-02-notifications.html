Design a Notification Service

Every product eventually grows a notification service, and every one starts as a function that sends an email. Then marketing wants to message five million people on Tuesday morning, security needs a login code to arrive in seconds in the middle of that, users want it quiet at night, and the SMS provider starts rejecting you. This studio designs the platform that makes all of that safe.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing a platform service that other teams call to notify users by push, email and SMS.

  • Users have preferences (which channels and categories) and quiet hours.
  • Messages use templates and are localised to the user's language.
  • Volume is about 50 million notifications a day, with bursts: marketing campaigns on one side, urgent security alerts on the other.
  • The same event must never produce two sends to the same user.
  • The external providers (push, email, SMS) rate-limit you, and sometimes fail.

Your notes

Keep notes as you go: decisions, numbers, open questions. Plain text is fine; try sketching boxes with characters, as in an interview whiteboard.

1Requirements: priorities and guarantees

Which notifications matter most, and what does "never send twice" actually promise? Can a distributed system deliver exactly once? When a message is missing and when it is duplicated, which mistake is worse for a password reset, and which for a marketing email? What is out of scope?

Compare with a worked answer

Not all notifications are equal. Sort them by what failure costs:

  • Urgent: security alerts, one-time login codes, fraud warnings. Must arrive in seconds, and a code that arrives after it expired is useless. Bypass quiet hours. A duplicate is annoying; a missing one can lock a user out or hide a break-in.
  • Normal (transactional): receipts, shipping updates, "someone replied". Minutes are fine; respect preferences and quiet hours.
  • Bulk (marketing): campaigns and digests. Hours are fine; strictly opt-in; first to be delayed or dropped when something is wrong. A duplicate looks spammy and costs money; a missing one costs almost nothing.

What "never send twice" can mean. Over an unreliable network you cannot achieve literal exactly-once delivery: when you send to a provider and the connection drops, you cannot tell whether it went through (networking ch. 16). The practical design is at-least-once processing plus deduplication: the system may try more than once, and a unique key and state machine ensure the visible effect happens once, in all but a narrow window we then shrink and measure. Be honest about that window in the answer. And choose the bias per priority: for urgent messages prefer a rare duplicate to a miss; for bulk, prefer a miss to a duplicate.

Functional requirements: send to one or many users over one or more channels; honour preferences and quiet hours; render localised templates; report status to the caller; schedule for later. Non-functional: urgent latency in seconds even during a campaign; throughput to match provider limits; no double sends; auditable history; the platform does not fall over when one provider does. Out of scope: building the push, email or SMS providers themselves; an in-app inbox; analytics dashboards; A/B testing of content.

2Estimates: average, peak and storage

Convert 50 million a day into per-second numbers, average and peak. Estimate the history storage per day and per year. What is the real bottleneck: your servers, your database or something else? State your assumptions.

Compare with a worked answer
  • Average: 50,000,000 / 86,400 s is about 580 notifications per second.
  • Peak: traffic is not smooth. Suppose a campaign sends 5 million pushes in 10 minutes: 5,000,000 / 600 is about 8,300 per second, on top of the normal load. Plan for roughly 10,000 per second at intake and in the queues. That is only a small multiple of what a handful of ordinary servers handle; the interesting numbers are elsewhere.
  • The real bottleneck is the providers. Suppose (an assumption; real limits vary and are set by your contract) the email provider accepts 1,000 sends per second. Five million emails then take 5,000,000 / 1,000 = 5,000 s, about 83 minutes, however fast your code is. So the system must queue and pace rather than try to send as fast as it can, and urgent traffic must not wait in that line.
  • Channel mix (assumption): perhaps 60% push, 30% email, 10% SMS. SMS is the expensive one per message, which is a reason to dedupe hard and to let callers choose fallbacks carefully.
  • Storage: a notification record (ids, user, channel, template, parameters, status, timestamps, provider message id) is around 500 bytes. 50M × 500 B = 25 GB per day, about 9 TB per year. Add roughly three status changes per notification if you keep an append-only attempt log. Keep 90 days hot (about 2.25 TB), partitioned by day so old partitions are dropped or archived cheaply, and archive longer-term audit data to cheap object storage.
  • Database writes: about 580/s average and ~10,000/s peak inserts, plus several updates each. That is within reach of a sharded or well-provisioned relational database, but hot enough that you want batched writes and no needless updates per row.

What worries me is not volume, it is pacing against provider limits, isolation between priorities and not sending twice when things crash.

3The API

Design the call other teams make to send a notification. The caller's own request may be retried, and a campaign has millions of recipients. What goes in the request? What does the service return immediately, and how does the caller learn what happened later?

Compare with a worked answer

Apply chapter 9: the caller should never wait for a provider (they are slow and flaky), so the API is asynchronous: accept, record, and acknowledge.

POST /v1/notifications
Idempotency-Key: inv-42-paid
Content-Type: application/json

{ "user_id": 7,
  "template": "invoice_paid",
  "params": { "amount": "12.50", "invoice": "INV-42" },
  "category": "billing",
  "priority": "normal",
  "send_after": null,
  "expires_at": "2026-03-08T12:00:00Z" }

409 Conflict   { "error": "idempotency_key_reused_with_different_request" }
429            { "error": "caller_quota_exceeded" }  Retry-After: 2

GET /v1/notifications/n_91f
200            { "id": "n_91f", "status": "sent", "channel": "push" }
  • 202 Accepted, not 200. The honest answer is "we have taken responsibility for this", not "it is delivered". The notification is durably recorded before the 202 is returned.
  • The idempotency key comes from the caller, scoped to that caller, and usually built from the business event (invoice-42-paid), not a random value per attempt. Same key and same request returns the original id again (no second notification); same key with a different body is an error. This is the layer that stops the caller's retries and double-fires from becoming two sends.
  • Status queries: queued, scheduled, sending, sent, delivered (if the provider reports it), failed and suppressed (with a reason such as opted_out or quiet_hours). Offer a webhook as an alternative to polling.
  • The caller does not choose the provider or the template's channel logic, just the template, parameters and a category; the platform applies preferences. It may allow a hint, such as "push first, fall back to SMS" for urgent items.
  • Priority is a privilege, not a parameter. If any caller can send "urgent", every marketing job will. Each API key is allowed certain priorities, and the service enforces it.
  • Expiry. A login code is worthless after ten minutes, so expires_at lets the platform drop it rather than send stale.
  • Campaigns do not send five million items in one body. They create a campaign that references an audience (a saved segment or a file in object storage), a template and a schedule; the platform expands it into notifications in batches. A per-caller quota with 429 and Retry-After protects intake (chapter 17).

Sketch the tables: the notification records, the user preferences and the templates. Where does the uniqueness that prevents double sends live? How do you handle multiple languages and changes to a template over time? What grows without bound, and how will you cope?

  • Templates and localisation. A template has one body per locale, with a fallback chain (fr-CA, then fr, then the default en), so a missing translation degrades gracefully instead of failing. Placeholders are filled from params at send time with a safe renderer (escape HTML in emails; never evaluate caller-supplied code). Store the template version used. Dates, numbers and currencies are formatted per locale, not by the caller.
  • Preferences by category. Users opt in and out per channel and per category (marketing, billing, social). Security alerts are a category users cannot fully disable, and the platform enforces legal consent rules for marketing, including a working unsubscribe.
  • What grows: notifications and attempts. Partition by time and drop or archive old partitions; keep an index on (status, send_after) for the scheduler, but not on everything. Parameters may contain personal data, so set retention and access controls on purpose.

5Architecture

Draw the pipeline from the caller's request to the user's phone. Where are the queues, how many, and keyed by what? How do you keep the code for each provider from leaking into the rest? Where do preferences, rendering and rate limiting happen?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Intake API (stateless). Authenticates the caller, checks its quota and allowed priority, validates the request, and does the first dedupe: inserting the notification row with the caller's idempotency key. Only after that commit does it reply 202. To avoid the dual-write trap (row saved but queue publish lost), a small relay publishes from the table to the queue, the outbox pattern of data chapter 19, or workers simply poll rows in queued state.
  2. One queue per priority, so a flood of bulk work physically cannot sit in front of an urgent message (stage 6). Partition or key by user id within each, so one user's messages stay ordered.
  3. Workers per channel. A worker takes a message, then in order: re-checks the row's state; loads preferences and applies quiet hours and suppression; picks the locale and renders the template; asks the rate limiter for a token; calls the provider adapter; records the attempt and the new status. Each channel scales independently: push is cheap and high volume, SMS is slow and costly.
  4. Provider adapters, one per provider (chapter 5). The core defines a port such as send(message) -> Result where Result is sent, retryable failure or permanent failure. Each adapter hides one vendor's API, authentication, error codes and quirks. That keeps vendor code out of the rules, makes a second provider an adapter rather than a rewrite, and lets tests use a fake provider that you can make slow, flaky or down (chapter 7).
  5. A scheduler for delayed and quiet-hours sends: rows with a future send_after are picked up when due and enqueued.

6Deep dives

Five hard parts, each worth its own whiteboard: (a) making "never twice" real with a key and a constraint; (b) keeping campaigns from starving security alerts; (c) staying inside provider rate limits; (d) retries, backoff and failover to a second provider; (e) quiet hours, scheduling and time zones. Take each in turn.

Compare with a worked answer

(a) Dedupe: an idempotency key and a unique constraint

The same intention can arrive twice: the caller retries after a timeout, a job runs twice, or the queue redelivers. Put the rule where it cannot race: a UNIQUE (caller, idem_key) constraint in the database. Intake inserts, and a unique violation means "I have seen this", so return the existing id. At send time a second guard: workers move the row from queued to sending with a conditional update, and only the worker whose update changes a row proceeds.

import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:", isolation_level=None)
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE notifications (
  id        INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT,
  caller    TEXT NOT NULL,
  idem_key  TEXT NOT NULL,
  user_id   INTEGER NOT NULL,
  status    TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'queued',
  UNIQUE (caller, idem_key)              -- the dedupe lives in the database
);
""")


def accept(caller, key, user):
    """Intake: returns (id, is_new). A retry with the same key gets the same id."""
    try:
        cur = db.execute("INSERT INTO notifications (caller, idem_key, user_id) VALUES (?,?,?)",
                         (caller, key, user))
        return cur.lastrowid, True
    except sqlite3.IntegrityError:
        row = db.execute("SELECT id FROM notifications WHERE caller=? AND idem_key=?",
                         (caller, key)).fetchone()
        return row[0], False


def claim(nid):
    """Worker: only one worker can move queued -> sending."""
    return db.execute("UPDATE notifications SET status='sending' WHERE id=? AND status='queued'",
                      (nid,)).rowcount == 1


print("first call   :", accept("billing", "inv-42-paid", 7))
print("caller retry :", accept("billing", "inv-42-paid", 7))
print("other caller :", accept("shipping", "inv-42-paid", 7))
print("worker A     :", claim(1))
print("worker B     :", claim(1))
first call   : (1, True)
caller retry : (1, False)
other caller : (2, True)
worker A     : True
worker B     : False

The retry gets the original id and creates nothing. A different caller using the same key text is a different notification, because keys are scoped to the caller. Two workers racing for the same row: exactly one wins. The remaining gap is the one this design cannot fully close: a worker claims the row, calls the provider, and crashes before recording sent. We handle that in stage 7.

(b) Priority lanes: campaigns must not starve security alerts

If one queue holds everything, an urgent code waits behind a million marketing messages: the head-of-line blocking you have seen in every queue. The fix is the bulkhead from chapter 17: isolate resources so one workload cannot consume them all.

  • Separate queues per priority (urgent, normal, bulk), and separate worker capacity: a pool reserved for urgent that bulk can never use, even if that pool is mostly idle. Idle reserved capacity is the insurance premium.
  • Workers shared between lanes use strict priority with a floor: always drain urgent first, but guarantee bulk a small share so it eventually completes (otherwise a steady trickle of normal traffic could starve it forever).
  • Separate rate-limit budgets. Reserve a slice of each provider's quota for urgent. A campaign may use at most, say, 70% of the SMS quota, never all of it.
  • For email, many providers recommend sending marketing and transactional mail from separate sending domains or IP pools so one's reputation problems cannot block the other.
  • Throttle at the campaign: a campaign has its own maximum rate and can be paused, so a mistaken one is stoppable.

(c) Provider rate limits: token buckets

A token bucket holds up to burst tokens and refills at rate tokens per second; each send takes one; with no token, the worker waits or requeues. It permits short bursts yet enforces the long-run rate, matching how providers meter you. Here is one with an injected clock so the output is exact:

class TokenBucket:
    """Allows `rate` sends per second, with bursts up to `burst`. Clock is passed in."""
    def __init__(self, rate, burst, now=0.0):
        self.rate, self.burst, self.tokens, self.last = rate, burst, float(burst), now

    def allow(self, now):
        self.tokens = min(self.burst, self.tokens + (now - self.last) * self.rate)
        self.last = now
        if self.tokens >= 1:
            self.tokens -= 1
            return True
        return False


bucket = TokenBucket(rate=100, burst=100)          # provider allows 100/s
sent = 0
for tick in range(300):                            # 300 attempts over 1 second
    if bucket.allow(now=tick / 300):
        sent += 1
print("attempts 300 in 1.0 s, sent immediately:", sent)

bucket = TokenBucket(rate=100, burst=100)
sent_per_second = []
for sec in range(3):                               # sustained 300/s offered for 3 s
    n = sum(bucket.allow(now=sec + i / 300) for i in range(300))
    sent_per_second.append(n)
print("sustained 300/s offered, sent per second:", sent_per_second)
attempts 300 in 1.0 s, sent immediately: 199
sustained 300/s offered, sent per second: [199, 100, 100]

In the first second 199 got through: the full burst of 100 plus about 99 refilled during that second. After the burst is spent, the bucket settles to exactly the configured 100 per second. The consequence for design: set the bucket a little below the provider's published limit, and choose the burst deliberately, since a burst equal to a full second of quota can itself trip a provider's per-second check. With many workers, the limit must be shared: either a central counter (a fast shared store) or a fixed slice of the quota per worker. The shared counter is exact but a dependency; slices are simple but waste quota when some workers are idle. When a provider answers 429 with a Retry-After, believe it, and drain the bucket until then.

(d) Retries, backoff, and failover through circuit breakers

  • Classify the failure. A permanent error (invalid device token, bad address, user unsubscribed) must not be retried: mark it and add the address to the suppression list. A transient one (timeout, 5xx, 429) is retried.
  • Exponential backoff with jitter: wait 1 s, 2 s, 4 s and so on, each randomised, up to a cap and a maximum number of attempts, then park the message in a dead-letter queue for inspection. Without jitter, every worker retries at the same moment (retry storms). Respect expires_at: do not retry a login code after it expired.
  • A circuit breaker per provider (chapter 17): after enough failures in a window, the breaker opens and the adapter fails fast instead of waiting on timeouts, then after a pause lets a trial request through (half-open) to test recovery.
  • Failover. With two email or SMS providers, an open breaker on A routes new sends to B. The caveat that matters here: failing over after an ambiguous failure (A timed out; did it send?) risks a duplicate through B. So fail over on clear failure or an open breaker, but for an ambiguous timeout, retry the same provider using the notification id as the provider's idempotency key if it supports one. Different providers have different sender identities and costs, so failover is a configured policy, not an accident.

(e) Quiet hours, scheduling and time zones

  • Store the user's IANA time zone name (America/New_York), not a fixed UTC offset, because the offset changes with daylight saving. Evaluate quiet hours at send time, not at request time, since the message may sit in a queue for hours.
  • A window can wrap past midnight (22:00 to 07:00), so the test is "after start or before end", not "between".
  • Outside quiet hours: send. Inside: reschedule to the window's end, by setting send_after, and let the scheduler release it. Urgent security categories bypass quiet hours; marketing never does.
from datetime import datetime, time, timedelta, timezone
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

QUIET_START, QUIET_END = time(22, 0), time(7, 0)      # quiet from 22:00 to 07:00 local


def in_quiet(local):
    t = local.time()
    return t >= QUIET_START or t < QUIET_END           # the window wraps past midnight


def next_allowed(now_utc, tz_name):
    """Return when we may send: now, or the next 07:00 in the user's own time zone."""
    local = now_utc.astimezone(ZoneInfo(tz_name))
    if not in_quiet(local):
        return now_utc
    day = local.date() if local.time() < QUIET_END else local.date() + timedelta(days=1)
    open_local = datetime.combine(day, QUIET_END, tzinfo=ZoneInfo(tz_name))
    return open_local.astimezone(timezone.utc)


now = datetime(2026, 3, 8, 6, 30, tzinfo=timezone.utc)
for tz in ("Europe/London", "America/New_York", "Asia/Tokyo"):
    local = now.astimezone(ZoneInfo(tz))
    when = next_allowed(now, tz)
    print(f"{tz:17} local {local:%a %H:%M}  quiet={in_quiet(local)!s:5}  send at {when:%Y-%m-%d %H:%M} UTC")
Europe/London     local Sun 06:30  quiet=True   send at 2026-03-08 07:00 UTC
America/New_York  local Sun 01:30  quiet=True   send at 2026-03-08 11:00 UTC
Asia/Tokyo        local Sun 15:30  quiet=False  send at 2026-03-08 06:30 UTC

The same instant is deep night in New York, early morning in London and mid-afternoon in Tokyo. Look at New York: 07:00 local is 11:00 UTC, not 12:00, because the United States moves its clocks forward on this date, so the code asks the time-zone database rather than adding a fixed offset. Two more design points. First, if every quiet-hours user is released at exactly 07:00, you create your own stampede, so release them with jitter across a few minutes and through the normal rate limiters. Second, "send at 9am local time" campaigns naturally spread over a day of time zones, which is gentler on providers than "send at 09:00 UTC".

7Failure modes and observability

A provider goes down. A worker crashes between calling the provider and recording the result. Marketing launches a campaign to everybody at once. And in all of these, how would you even know something is wrong before users tell you?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Provider outage. Calls time out or fail. The circuit breaker opens within seconds, so workers stop wasting time on a dead dependency. Policy takes over: route to the second provider if one exists; otherwise requeue with backoff, and the queue absorbs the backlog while the callers still get fast 202 replies. Because lanes are separate, bulk traffic waits while urgent traffic is tried on the fallback channel (for example SMS if push is down), where the user's preferences allow. Expired messages are dropped, not sent late. When the provider recovers the backlog drains through the token bucket, not in a thundering burst.
  • Duplicate send after a worker crash. The worker claimed the row, the provider accepted the push, and the worker died before writing sent. The queue redelivers the message. A new worker sees status sending, which is ambiguous: sent or not? The design is at-least-once, so dedupe at send time: (1) pass the notification id as the provider-side idempotency key when the provider supports it, so a repeat is ignored by them; (2) otherwise, query the provider by message id if it offers that; (3) otherwise, decide by priority: for urgent re-send (a duplicate code beats no code), for bulk skip (a missed promotion beats a double). Keep rows stuck in sending beyond a timeout visible on a dashboard. State plainly that this is a narrow, measured window, not zero.
  • Campaign stampede. A campaign to ten million users launches now. Defences stack: intake is separate from sending, so the 202s stay fast; the campaign expands into notifications in batches rather than all at once; bulk has its own queue, its own reserved share of provider quota and a maximum rate; the campaign can be paused; a per-caller quota stops one team from overwhelming intake; and auto-scaling workers cannot exceed the provider limit anyway, so scale on queue age, not just depth. Check the caller's approvals for sending to a large audience.
  • Observability. You cannot fix what you cannot see. Track, per priority lane and per channel: queue depth and the age of the oldest message (alert on age, since a deep queue that drains fast is fine and a shallow one that is stuck is not); end-to-end latency from 202 to provider acceptance, with a target such as "urgent p99 under 10 seconds"; success, retry and failure rates per provider; circuit-breaker state; token-bucket waits and provider 429s; dedupe hits; the size of the dead-letter queue; and notifications stuck in sending. Attach the notification id to every log line and trace so one message can be followed from intake to provider. Reconcile your sent counts with the providers' own reports regularly, which catches silent loss that no error ever signalled.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"I'd start by ranking messages: urgent security alerts, normal transactional, and bulk marketing, because the cost of a miss versus a duplicate differs for each. Exactly-once isn't possible with external providers, so the guarantee is at-least-once with deduplication: a caller-supplied idempotency key behind a unique constraint at intake, and a conditional claim at send time. The numbers are about 580 notifications a second on average and perhaps 10,000 at a campaign peak, which is easy for us; the real limit is the providers, so a five-million-email campaign at a thousand a second takes well over an hour, and the system has to pace and queue. The API is asynchronous: it records the notification, returns 202 and lets the caller poll or take a webhook. Internally it's intake, a queue per priority, workers per channel, and a provider adapter per vendor behind a port, so a second provider is an adapter, not a rewrite. The hard parts are isolation and pacing. Priority lanes with reserved workers and reserved quota keep campaigns from starving security alerts; token buckets keep us under provider limits; retries use exponential backoff with jitter and a dead-letter queue; and a circuit breaker per provider fails over to a second one, being careful that an ambiguous timeout can mean the first provider already sent. Quiet hours use the user's time zone, evaluated at send time, with jittered release. And I'd watch queue age per lane, not just depth, plus provider error rates and stuck-in-sending counts."

Notice the shape: rank by failure cost → pick the delivery guarantee honestly → find the true bottleneck → isolate priorities → pace against the outside world → name the failure modes → make it observable.

Key takeaways

  • Rank notifications by what failure costs. Urgent prefers a duplicate to a miss; bulk prefers a miss to a duplicate.
  • Exactly-once does not exist across an unreliable network. Build at-least-once with deduplication, and measure the small window that remains.
  • The bottleneck is the provider's rate limit, not your servers, so queue and pace; accept quickly with a 202.
  • Put dedupe in a unique constraint and a conditional state change, never in check-then-insert code.
  • Isolate priorities with separate queues, reserved workers and reserved quota, so a campaign cannot starve a security alert.
  • Token buckets keep you under the limit; backoff with jitter and circuit breakers handle failure; failing over after an ambiguous timeout can duplicate.
  • Store time zones by name, evaluate quiet hours at send time, and spread the release at the end of a window.
  • Monitor queue age per lane, provider errors and stuck sends, and reconcile with the providers.
