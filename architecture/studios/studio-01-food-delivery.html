Design Food Delivery's Service Boundaries

A delivery app has three very different customers (the hungry person, the restaurant and the courier) and one order that has to keep all three of them informed. The code is a monolith that works, but six teams keep stepping on each other. Where do you draw the lines, who owns which data, and how do you move there without a big-bang rewrite?

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing the service boundaries of a food-delivery app. Customers order from restaurants, restaurants prepare the food, and couriers carry it.

  • There are six teams, and they currently share one large monolith and one database.
  • The monolith works, but deploys conflict: teams queue for releases, and one team's bug rolls back everyone's work.
  • Peak load is 2,000 orders per minute (the Friday dinner rush).
  • An order must never be charged twice, never be lost, and a customer should never be charged for food that was never going to be made.
  • You may decide how many services there should be, including "fewer than you think".

Your notes

Keep notes as you go: decisions, numbers, open questions. Plain text is fine; try sketching boxes with characters, as in an interview whiteboard.

1Requirements and the forces: why change at all

Before drawing any boxes: what is actually wrong today, and is it a technical problem or a people problem? Do the numbers (2,000 orders a minute) justify splitting the system? What would you measure to know the change helped?

Compare with a worked answer

Start with the arithmetic, because it can embarrass a premature design. 2,000 orders per minute is about 33 orders per second. Each order triggers a handful of writes and a dozen or so status events, so a few hundred events a second. Assume 5,000 couriers online sending a location ping every 4 seconds: about 1,250 pings per second, which is the larger stream, and a different kind of data (high volume, short-lived, loss-tolerant). None of this needs a fleet of services. A well-run single database handles 33 orders a second comfortably.

So load is not the force. The force is organisational and about change (chapter 11):

  • Deploy contention. Six teams, one deployable. Releases queue, and a regression anywhere blocks everyone. The cost shows up as lead time, not CPU.
  • Blast radius. A slow query in the restaurant dashboard degrades checkout, because they share a process and a connection pool.
  • Different shapes of change. Dispatch (matching couriers) changes weekly and wants experiments; payments changes rarely and needs strict review. One cadence does not suit both.
  • Different scaling shapes. Courier location traffic and menu browsing scale very differently from order placement.

Requirements that shape the design: never double-charge; never lose an order; a customer sees accurate order status within a couple of seconds; restaurants must be able to accept or reject; the system must keep taking orders if one non-critical part (say, notifications) is down. Out of scope: routing algorithms, pricing and promotions, ratings, search.

How you would know it worked: deploy frequency per team, lead time from merge to production, change failure rate, and the number of incidents caused by another team's change. If those do not improve, you paid the distributed-systems tax for nothing. A senior answer says out loud that the first step is not services, it is clearer ownership.

2Find the bounded contexts

Look at the nouns and the verbs: customer, restaurant, menu, order, courier, payment, notification. Where does one word start to mean two different things? Group the system into bounded contexts, say which team owns each, and sketch how they relate. Which context is the competitive heart of the business?

Compare with a worked answer

Use the test from chapter 6 and chapter 12: a boundary goes where the same word means different things and where different people own the answers. Take "order". To the customer it is a basket and a promise. To the restaurant it is a ticket of dishes to cook by a deadline. To the courier it is a parcel to collect at one address and drop at another. To payments it is an amount to authorise and capture. Forcing one shared Order class to serve all four is how monoliths get tangled.

Seven contexts fall out, with six teams (the platform team owns two small, stable ones):

  • Relationships, in DDD terms. Ordering is the customer of Menu (it takes whatever Menu publishes) and of Customer accounts. Payments protects its model from the outside world with an anti-corruption layer around the payment provider's API, so provider quirks never leak into the rest of the system.
  • Why Menu and Restaurant ops are separate. Menu is read-heavy, changes in bulk and is cacheable. Restaurant ops is a live, real-time workflow. Different data, different load, different teams. (If you only had four teams you might sensibly merge them.)
  • Why not "user service", "order service" and "restaurant service" by noun? That is the entity-service trap from chapter 12: the rules would end up scattered through whoever calls them.
  • Where to be careful: Dispatch is the core domain, where the business differentiates, so it gets the best engineers and the freedom to iterate. Notifications and accounts are supporting or generic and could be bought or kept simple.

3Data ownership: who owns "order status"?

A customer's app shows "Preparing", then "Courier on the way", then "Delivered". Who owns that field? Restaurant ops knows when cooking starts; Dispatch knows when the courier picks up; Payments knows whether the card was charged. Pick one owner for "order status", and say how everyone else learns of changes without sharing a database.

Compare with a worked answer

The trap is that "order status" looks like one field but is really several state machines that happen to end up on one screen. Cooking progress belongs to the kitchen; delivery progress belongs to dispatch; the money state belongs to payments. Whoever owns each machine is the only one who can say authoritatively what state it is in.

  • Each context owns its own state. Restaurant ops owns ticket state (received, accepted, preparing, ready). Dispatch owns delivery state (searching, assigned, picked up, delivered). Payments owns payment state (authorised, captured, voided).
  • Ordering owns the order status the customer sees: a derived, customer-facing summary (placed, confirmed, being prepared, on the way, delivered, cancelled). It is a projection updated by reacting to the others' events. Ordering never writes into another context's tables, and no one writes into Ordering's.
  • Everyone learns by events. Each context publishes facts about its own state when they change: PaymentAuthorised, RestaurantAccepted, RestaurantRejected, CourierAssigned, OrderPickedUp, OrderDelivered. They are named in the past tense because they are facts, not requests (chapter 14). Ordering folds them into the customer-facing status and then publishes its own OrderStatusChanged for the notifications and the app to consume.
  • Publish reliably. A context must not update its database and then separately publish, or a crash between the two loses the event. Use the transactional outbox: write the event to an outbox table in the same transaction as the state change, and a relay publishes it (data chapter 19).
  • Local copies instead of live joins. Dispatch needs the pickup address and the drop-off address. Rather than calling Ordering and Menu on every match, the event OrderConfirmed carries what Dispatch needs (event-carried state transfer). Dispatch keeps its own small copy. If Ordering is down, matching continues. The price is eventual consistency: the copy can be a moment stale.

A good test for the answer: if you rename a column in Ordering's database, how many other teams are affected? The target is zero. They depend on the event schemas, which are versioned contracts (chapter 10), not on tables.

4Communication: sync or async?

Go through the main interactions of one order and label each as a synchronous call or an asynchronous message. For each, ask: if the other side is down for ten minutes, what should the customer experience? Which calls are on the critical path of "place order"?

Compare with a worked answer

Apply the question from chapter 13: if the other side is down, should the user wait or see an error? A call belongs on the synchronous path only when the user is actually waiting for its answer.

The shape that results: only two synchronous calls sit on the "place order" path (price quote and payment authorisation), each with a timeout and a clear failure message. Everything after "order confirmed" flows as events. That keeps the chain short, which matters because synchronous availability multiplies: two dependencies each at 99.9% give at best about 99.8% together. An obvious rule of thumb: synchronous for the part the customer is waiting on, asynchronous for everything that happens after you can reply.

5The order saga

No single database transaction spans Ordering, Payments, Restaurant ops and Dispatch any more. Write the steps of the happy path from "place order" to "delivered and paid", then for each step that can fail say what undoes the earlier steps. Who coordinates: a central orchestrator or the contexts reacting to each other's events?

Compare with a worked answer

This is the situation chapter 15 is about: a business transaction across services, built from local transactions and compensations instead of a rollback (the idea is from Garcia-Molina and Salem, 1987). The steps:

  1. Place order (Ordering): create the order as PLACED. Compensation: mark CANCELLED.
  2. Authorise payment (Payments): place a hold on the card for the total; do not charge yet. Compensation: void the authorisation.
  3. Restaurant accepts (Restaurant ops): the kitchen confirms it can make the food. Compensation: tell the restaurant to cancel the ticket.
  4. Assign courier (Dispatch): find and assign a courier. Compensation: release the courier.
  5. Deliver and capture payment: when the order is delivered, Payments captures the held amount.

Why authorise first and capture last. Authorising reserves the money without taking it, so undoing it is a cheap, clean void that the customer never sees on their statement. Capturing happens only once food is delivered. If you charged up front, every rejection would need a refund, which is slower, visible to the customer and sometimes costs fees. Choosing a cheap compensation by reordering the steps is a classic saga design move.

The pivot. Once the courier has picked the food up, you can no longer cleanly cancel: the food exists. After that point the saga only goes forward (deliver, or handle a failed delivery as a new business process with a refund). Name that point explicitly.

Orchestration or choreography? With five steps across four contexts, a small orchestrator inside Ordering makes the flow readable in one place, including the compensations and timeouts (what happens if the restaurant never answers in 3 minutes?). Pure choreography, where each context reacts to the previous event, spreads the logic across teams, and the question "why is this order stuck?" becomes a hunt through four codebases. Contexts still publish events for everyone else. Many experienced engineers would start with orchestration here.

Here is the skeleton running: each step has a compensation, and a closed restaurant triggers the undo of everything before it (the output is real; the capture step is left out for brevity).

class Rejected(Exception):
    pass


def run_saga(steps, ctx):
    """steps: list of (name, do, undo). On failure, undo completed steps in reverse."""
    done = []
    for name, do, undo in steps:
        try:
            do(ctx)
            print(f"  ok      {name}")
            done.append((name, undo))
        except Rejected as why:
            print(f"  FAILED  {name}: {why}")
            for done_name, done_undo in reversed(done):
                done_undo(ctx)
                print(f"  undone  {done_name}")
            return "cancelled"
    return "delivered"


def authorise(ctx):
    ctx["auth"] = "auth-77"


def void(ctx):
    ctx.pop("auth", None)


def restaurant(ctx):
    if ctx["restaurant_open"] is False:
        raise Rejected("restaurant is closed")


def courier(ctx):
    ctx["courier"] = "courier-9"


STEPS = [
    ("place order",       lambda c: c.update(status="PLACED"),   lambda c: c.update(status="CANCELLED")),
    ("authorise payment", authorise,                              void),
    ("restaurant accepts", restaurant,                            lambda c: None),
    ("assign courier",    courier,                                lambda c: c.pop("courier", None)),
]

for is_open in (True, False):
    ctx = {"restaurant_open": is_open}
    print(f"restaurant_open={is_open}")
    result = run_saga(STEPS, ctx)
    print(f"  -> {result}; auth held: {'auth' in ctx}; status: {ctx.get('status')}")
restaurant_open=True
  ok      place order
  ok      authorise payment
  ok      restaurant accepts
  ok      assign courier
  -> delivered; auth held: True; status: PLACED
restaurant_open=False
  ok      place order
  ok      authorise payment
  FAILED  restaurant accepts: restaurant is closed
  undone  authorise payment
  undone  place order
  -> cancelled; auth held: False; status: CANCELLED

Read the second run. The restaurant refuses, so the saga voids the authorisation and cancels the order, in reverse order, leaving no held money and a consistent final status. A production version also persists each step so it can resume after a crash (that is Lab 4).

6Migration path from the monolith

You cannot stop shipping for a year. Plan the sequence of moves from today's monolith to your target. What do you do first? Which context do you extract first and why? How do you move the data, and how do you avoid a stretch where nothing is shippable?

Compare with a worked answer

The principle from chapter 18: change the system in small, reversible steps, each of which leaves it working. A plausible sequence:

  1. Make it a modular monolith first (chapter 11). Inside the one codebase, create a module per context with an explicit public interface. Stop modules importing each other's internals, and stop them reading each other's tables, enforced by a CI test (the same import-rule check you write in Lab 3). Give each module its own database schema. This alone relieves much of the code-level contention, and every boundary you draw is cheap to move while you learn where it really belongs.
  2. Replace direct calls with the target contracts, still in-process. Where Ordering reaches into Dispatch's tables, route it through Dispatch's interface, then through an in-process event. If a boundary is painful here, it would have been agony over a network. Fix it now.
  3. Give teams their own pipelines. Even inside one deployable, separate module ownership (code owners), tests and release trains. Some of the "deploy conflict" pain is a process problem.
  4. Extract dispatch first, with the strangler fig (Martin Fowler's name for growing a replacement around the old system). Why Dispatch? It has the clearest independent scaling (location pings), the most different change cadence (frequent experiments), a dedicated team, and a boundary that is mostly asynchronous already. Payments is the usual alternative, but it is riskier, so many teams build confidence on a less critical context first. Dispatch is core, so it also benefits most from the team's autonomy.
  5. The mechanics of the strangling. Put a routing seam in front (a facade or flag). Build the new Dispatch service; run it in shadow, receiving the same events and making assignments that are compared, not used. Move a small percentage of cities to it behind a flag; watch error rates; keep the old path as the rollback. Then retire the old code.
  6. Move the data with expand/contract. Create the service's own database; at first dual-write or replicate from the monolith's tables via change data capture; backfill; switch reads; then stop the monolith writing those tables and finally drop them. The monolith's tables for that context should become unreadable to everyone but the new owner.
  7. Repeat only when it hurts. After Dispatch, ask whether the next extraction is justified by the same forces. Menu, accounts and notifications may be fine as modules for a long time.

What to avoid: a big-bang rewrite; splitting code but sharing the database (a distributed monolith); extracting a context whose boundary you have not yet stabilised; and extracting before you can observe the system (tracing, metrics, dashboards must exist first).

7Failure modes and trade-offs

Walk through what goes wrong: Dispatch is down for 20 minutes; a payment call times out and you do not know whether the card was charged; the same event is delivered twice; and the org chart does not match the architecture. What does each failure do to the customer, and what stops it?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Dispatch is down. Orders can still be placed, paid and accepted, because those steps do not depend on Dispatch. The order waits in "finding a courier": the event sits in the broker (or Ordering's outbox) and is processed when Dispatch recovers. The backlog is then a burst, so Dispatch consumers need backpressure and to prefer newest or most urgent first. Meanwhile apply the resilience patterns of chapter 17: a circuit breaker so Ordering stops hammering a dead dependency, a timeout on "no courier within N minutes" that either keeps the customer informed with a revised estimate or cancels and voids the authorisation, and a degradation choice (stop accepting orders from restaurants with no courier capacity, rather than accepting what you cannot deliver). The trade-off: fast fail-over versus the risk of cancelling food already cooking.
  • Payment timeout with unknown outcome. Ordering calls Payments to authorise, and the call times out. The card may or may not have been held (networking ch. 16: a timeout means "unknown"). Never assume failure and never blindly retry without a key. Every authorisation carries an idempotency key derived from the order id, so a retry returns the original result. If still unknown, keep the order in a "payment pending" state, query the outcome from Payments (and, behind it, the provider), and if it is truly unresolved after a deadline, void by the same key. A periodic reconciliation job compares your records with the provider's, to catch the cases nothing else did. The customer sees "confirming payment", not a double charge.
  • Duplicate events. Brokers deliver at least once, and relays and consumers retry, so RestaurantAccepted will arrive twice sometimes. Every handler must be idempotent. A common technique: record the id of each processed event in the same database transaction as the effect (a unique constraint on event id), so a second delivery is recognised and skipped. Also design for out-of-order events: use state-machine rules (accepting an already-cancelled order is ignored or compensated) and version numbers rather than relying on arrival order.
  • Team ownership and Conway's law. Melvin Conway (1968) observed that systems mirror the communication structures of the organisations that build them. Seven contexts for six teams means someone owns two; make that deliberate (the platform team owns accounts and notifications) and make sure no context is owned by nobody. Each service needs a team that is on call for it, can deploy it alone and is accountable for its contract. If two contexts need constant co-ordinated changes from two teams, either the boundary is wrong or the teams should merge. Treat team layout as part of the design (the "inverse Conway manoeuvre"): shape the teams to match the architecture you want.
  • The overall trade-off. You are exchanging the pain of a shared codebase for the pain of a distributed system: network failures, eventual consistency, harder debugging and more infrastructure. That is a good trade only if the organisational force is strong enough, which is why the order of moves in stage 6 (modules first, then extract the most justified context) matters. The cheapest architecture that gets the teams unblocked is the right one.

Self-check rubric

Tick what your own design (before reading the answers) covered. An experienced engineer would hit most of these. Missing several is normal the first time, and worth a second try in a week.

  • Identified that the driving force is deploy contention and team autonomy, not raw load, and did the arithmetic (about 33 orders per second).
  • Defined success with measurable outcomes (lead time, deploy frequency, incidents caused by other teams).
  • Found bounded contexts by where the same word means different things, not by database nouns.
  • Assigned each context to a team and called out the core domain (dispatch).
  • Drew a context map with the direction of each relationship, and noted an anti-corruption layer around the payment provider.
  • Gave "order status" a single owner and treated the customer-facing status as derived from events.
  • Shared data through events and local copies, with an outbox, not a shared database.
  • Labelled each interaction sync or async with a reason; kept only the price quote and payment authorisation synchronous.
  • Wrote the order saga with a compensation for each step, and chose authorise-then-capture so undoing is a void.
  • Named the pivot point and chose orchestration or choreography with a justification.
  • Planned the migration: modular monolith first, then strangle out dispatch with shadowing, a flag and expand/contract data moves.
  • Handled unknown payment outcomes with idempotency keys, status queries and reconciliation.
  • Made event handlers idempotent and tolerant of reordering.
  • Connected the design to Conway's law: every context has an owning team, on call and able to deploy alone.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"First I'd check why we're changing at all. Two thousand orders a minute is only about 33 a second, so load isn't the problem; six teams colliding in one deploy is. That points to ownership boundaries before network boundaries. I'd split by bounded context, where the word 'order' means different things: ordering, menu, restaurant ops, dispatch, payments, accounts and notifications, with dispatch as the core domain. Each context owns its data. Order status isn't one field, it's several state machines, so each context publishes events about its own state, and Ordering owns the customer-facing status as a projection of them, with an outbox so events are never lost. Only the price quote and the payment authorisation are synchronous, both with deadlines and idempotency keys; everything after 'order confirmed' is asynchronous. The order is a saga: place, authorise, restaurant accepts, assign a courier, deliver and capture, with compensations like voiding the authorisation if the restaurant rejects, and I'd authorise early and capture late so undoing is cheap. For migration I'd make the monolith modular first, enforce the boundaries in CI, then strangle out dispatch behind a flag with shadow traffic and expand/contract on the data. The risks are dispatch being down, which the queue and a circuit breaker absorb; payment timeouts with unknown outcomes, which idempotency keys and reconciliation handle; duplicate events, which idempotent handlers absorb; and Conway's law, so every service has a team that owns and is on call for it."

Notice the shape: question the premise → find the boundaries by language and ownership → say who owns each fact → keep the synchronous path short → make the multi-step flow explicit with undo → migrate in small reversible steps → name the failures.

Key takeaways

  • Work out why you are changing before you split anything. Here the force is team contention and blast radius, not load.
  • Find boundaries where the same word means different things, and give each one an owning team.
  • One owner per fact. "Order status" is several state machines; each context owns its own, and Ordering derives the customer's view from their events.
  • Keep the synchronous path short: only what the user is waiting on. Everything else flows as events through an outbox.
  • Model a multi-service business transaction as a saga with compensations, and order the steps so the undo is cheap (authorise early, capture late).
  • Migrate in reversible steps: modular monolith, enforce boundaries, then strangle out the most justified context.
  • Design for unknown outcomes and duplicates with idempotency keys and idempotent handlers.
  • Conway's law is a design constraint: align the teams with the architecture you want.
