Goodbye Microservices: Segment

Segment's pipeline forwarded customer events to over a hundred outside tools. To stop one slow tool from hurting the rest, they gave every destination its own service and its own queue. It worked, until the number of destinations grew and the cost of operating them grew with it. In a post published in 2018 they described folding more than 140 services back into one. The story is a clean example of a pattern that solves one problem and creates another.

The pattern in one sentence

Splitting into many services buys isolation but charges for it in operations, shared code and tooling: if you lack the tooling to test, deploy and upgrade many services as one, the per-service cost can outgrow the isolation benefit.

The cast

All facts about Segment below come from the engineering post by Alexandra Noonan, published on 10 July 2018. The ideas link to chapter 11 (monolith versus services) and chapter 12 (drawing service boundaries).

The destinations pipeline
It received a very large stream of events (the post says hundreds of thousands per second) and forwarded them to more than one hundred types of destination API.
Destinations
Third-party tools such as analytics and marketing services. Each has its own request format, so each needs custom transformation code. The post notes that traffic differs enormously: some receive a handful of events a day, others thousands per second.
Queues
Events were put on a distributed message queue and workers delivered them. Retryable failures (the post lists things like server errors, rate limits and timeouts) were retried; non-retryable ones (such as invalid credentials) were not.
Shared libraries
Common code for HTTP requests and field handling that destination services reused.
Centrifuge
A system the team built to replace the per-destination queues, so one service could send events to any destination. The post describes it as the way events reach the consolidated service, and later the back-end for Segment's Connections product. The post goes into little detail, and so does this page.

What happened, step by step

Step through

One queue, many queues, one service

  1. Start simple. The post describes an API writing events to a distributed queue and workers sending each event to every configured destination. All destinations shared one queue, so new events and retries were mixed together. When one destination slowed down, its retries flooded the queue and delayed delivery to the others. This is head-of-line blocking.
  2. One service and one queue per destination. A router copied each event to every selected destination, each with its own queue and service. A slow destination now only backed up its own queue.
  3. The bill arrives. Shared libraries made new destinations quick to build, but a change to one meant testing and deploying dozens of services, and versions drifted apart across codebases. Destinations also had very different traffic, so scaling was manual for quiet services during spikes and hard to auto-tune. The post says the team was adding about three destinations a month, with operational overhead growing with each.
  4. Consolidate. The team replaced the per-destination queues with Centrifuge and moved all destination code into one repository and one service: over 140 services became one.

The pattern explained

The first design was a reasonable response to a real problem. Chapter 12 describes service boundaries as a place to put independence: independent failure, independent scaling, independent deploys. Segment's per-destination services bought the first of those, and it bought it where it hurt, because destinations really do fail and slow down independently. The post is clear that this worked well early on.

What the post describes next is the cost side of the ledger, one item at a time.

Shared libraries diverge

The code was first kept in one repo, but a single broken test failed tests for every destination, so each destination moved into its own repo. Over 50 new destinations later, that meant 50 new repos. Improving a shared library meant testing and deploying dozens of services, so teams avoided it and versions drifted. The post reports that shared-library improvements numbered 32 over the microservices period and 46 in the year after consolidating.

Operational load

Because each destination had its own traffic pattern and its own CPU and memory profile, the team had to scale services individually. The post says three full-time engineers spent most of their time keeping the system running, and that velocity fell while the defect rate rose.

Flaky tests with no owner

Tests that made live HTTP calls to partner endpoints failed often, and with separate repos there was little incentive to fix them. The team built a tool called Traffic Recorder, on top of yakbak. It records HTTP traffic on a first run, saves it to files committed to the repo and replays it later. The post says the suite for all 140-plus destinations then ran in milliseconds, where some destinations had taken up to five minutes alone.

One set of dependencies

The monorepo forced decisions: the team standardised on one version of each of 120 unique dependencies and fixed breakages as destinations moved over. A large shared worker pool also absorbed spikes, so on-call engineers stopped getting paged for low-volume destinations.

Analogy: a branch office for every customer

Imagine a bank that opens a separate branch for each business customer so that one customer's queue never delays another. That works while there are ten customers. At a hundred and forty, each branch needs its own staff, its own copy of the rulebook and its own cash handling, and every rule change must be rolled out to all of them. A single large hall with enough tellers handles the quiet customers' rare visits and the busy ones' rush, but one fire alarm now empties the whole building.

Where it breaks down: software tooling (shared libraries, automated tests, deploy pipelines) can in principle make many branches cheap to run. The post's own conclusion is that Segment lacked that tooling at the time.

Trade-offs and lessons

The post is candid that the monolith has costs of its own. It lists three.

  • Fault isolation is harder. A crash caused by one destination can take down the whole service. The post's answer is a robust test suite and accepting the trade-off, not a different architecture.
  • In-memory caching is less effective. Cached control-plane data is spread over a very large number of processes (the post says more than 3000), so cache hit rates fall. Redis was considered but would add another thing to scale.
  • Dependency updates can break several destinations at once. The automated tests make that visible quickly.

Look at what has to be true for this to work: a test suite that runs fast enough to be trusted, and a team that accepts these downsides. The post names both as prerequisites. Note also what it does not say. It does not claim that microservices are a bad idea. It says the right choice depends on context, that microservices suit some parts of an infrastructure, and that for these destinations a single service was the better fit. General pattern, not from the post: many experienced engineers argue that a modular monolith (the topic of the Shopify case) can keep the clean internal boundaries without paying for a network between them.

Try it yourself

Simulate head-of-line blocking

This toy runs the same 18 events through one shared queue with one worker, and through one queue and one worker per destination. One destination is slow (five ticks per event instead of one). The numbers are invented; it is not Segment's code. It writes nothing and uses only the standard library.

# Head-of-line blocking: one shared FIFO queue vs one queue per destination.
# A worker handles 1 event per tick; a "slow" destination needs 5 ticks per event.
from collections import deque
COST = {"A": 1, "B": 1, "SLOW": 5}
events = [d for _ in range(6) for d in ("SLOW", "A", "B")]   # 18 events

def shared(events):
    q, t, done = deque(events), 0, {}
    while q:
        d = q.popleft(); t += COST[d]; done.setdefault(d, []).append(t)
    return {d: max(v) for d, v in done.items()}

def per_dest(events):
    qs = {}
    for d in events: qs.setdefault(d, deque()).append(d)
    # one worker per queue, running in parallel
    return {d: len(q) * COST[d] for d, q in qs.items()}

print("finish time of the last event, by destination (ticks)")
print("shared queue, one worker :", shared(events))
print("queue+worker per dest.   :", per_dest(events))

Output when run:

finish time of the last event, by destination (ticks)
shared queue, one worker : {'SLOW': 40, 'A': 41, 'B': 42}
queue+worker per dest.   : {'SLOW': 30, 'A': 6, 'B': 6}

With the shared queue, the healthy destinations A and B do not finish until tick 41 and 42, behind the slow one. With separate queues they finish at tick 6. The slow destination still takes 30 ticks either way: isolation protects the neighbours, it does not fix the slow one. Now ask what each extra queue and worker costs you to run, deploy and keep up to date. That is the other half of the story.

Distributed systems connection

Head-of-line blocking is a queueing effect: one slow job at the front delays everything behind it (see systems chapter 14 on queues and backpressure). Splitting queues is a form of bulkhead, which you will meet again in chapter 17 and in the Netflix case. Segment's story shows that a bulkhead need not be a separate deployable service: separate queues feeding a shared pool can give much of the isolation with less operational weight. That last sentence is our reading of the post, not its claim.

Key takeaways

  • One service and one queue per destination solved head-of-line blocking, but shared libraries diverged and the operational load grew with every new destination.
  • Segment consolidated more than 140 services into one service in one repo, built Centrifuge to replace the per-destination queues, and standardised dependencies.
  • A test-suite change (Traffic Recorder, replaying recorded HTTP traffic) was a prerequisite: it made the tests fast and reliable.
  • The monolith has real costs: weaker fault isolation, less effective in-memory caching, and dependency upgrades that touch many destinations.
  • Whether to split depends on the tooling you have to run many services, not only on the isolation you want.
