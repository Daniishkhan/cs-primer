Uber's Domain-Oriented Microservices

Microservices solved real problems for Uber, and then created a new one: so many services that nobody could see the whole. In 2020 an Uber engineer described the response, Domain-Oriented Microservice Architecture (DOMA): group services into domains, stack domains in layers, put one gateway in front of each domain, and add extension points so other teams can customise behaviour without editing someone else's code.

The pattern in one sentence

When there are too many services to reason about, add structure above them: collect related services into domains with a single front door, restrict which layers may depend on which, and offer extension points so the platform's owners are not the bottleneck for every product change.

The cast

The post is by Adam Gluck of Uber Engineering, published on 23 July 2020. It says DOMA was a cross-functional effort involving nearly 60 engineers. The words below are Uber's; the link to the earlier chapters is mine. Domains relate to the bounded contexts of chapter 6, and the question "where do we draw service boundaries?" is chapter 12.

Domain
In the post's words, a domain is "a collection of one or more microservices tied to a logical grouping of functionality." The post gives no guidance on size, and says a domain can contain a single service.
Layer
A collection of domains. Layers determine what each domain may depend on. The post says lower layers have a larger blast radius and more general functionality. Uber defined five: Infrastructure, Business, Product, Presentation and Edge.
Gateway
A single point of entry into a domain. It hides internal services, data tables and pipelines, and exposes interfaces such as RPC APIs, events and queries.
Extension
A way to extend a domain without changing its core implementation. The post describes logic extensions (a provider or plugin pattern) and data extensions (attaching extra data).
The teams
Platform teams own the lower, general domains; product teams build on top and need to tailor behaviour for their own cases.

The problem: "can't live with them, can't live without them"

The post opens with a candid summary. Microservices gave Uber independent deployments, clear ownership and better stability. They also increased complexity sharply. The author's line for this is "can't live with them, can't live without them." By the post's count Uber had about 2,200 critical microservices. In one investigation, engineers "had to work through around 50 services across 12 different teams" before they could finish. Simple features needed coordination with many owners.

The post also names a failure mode that chapters 11 and 12 warned about. In its words, "networked monoliths" can form, where services that look independent all have to be deployed together. That is the distributed monolith: the costs of a network with none of the independence. DOMA's goal, as the post states it, is to reduce complexity while keeping microservice flexibility.

Extending a domain without editing it

Gateways answer "how do I call this domain?" Extensions answer a harder question: "how do I change what it does, for my case, without making the platform team my bottleneck?" The post's example of a logic extension is a driver's "go online" validation. A product team can add a check without modifying core code. The step-through shows the shape of that flow; domain and team names are illustrative.

Step through

A product team adds a check without touching the core

  1. A product team has a need. The post's motivation is that platform teams otherwise become the bottleneck: a simple feature can need changes owned by many teams.
  2. It calls the domain through its gateway. The gateway is the single point of entry; internal services, tables and pipelines stay hidden.
  3. The core does its work. The core implementation belongs to the domain's team.
  4. It reaches an extension point. For logic extensions the post describes a provider or plugin pattern.
  5. The product team's code runs. Its decision comes back. The domain's core stayed unchanged; the post's example is a "go online" validation for drivers.

The pattern explained

Domains: a unit above the service

A service is a deployment unit; a domain is a unit of meaning. This is the same move as the bounded context in chapter 6. That comparison is mine, not the post's. Its purpose is that other teams think about "payments" and not about the dozen services that implement it. The Maps example is concrete: three domains and 80 microservices behind three gateways.

Layers: rules about who may depend on whom

A layer says what a domain is allowed to depend on, so a change in one place has a bounded blast radius. This is the same idea as the dependency rule of chapter 5, but at organisational scale: as a general principle, higher layers lean on lower, more general ones, and the reverse would turn the stack into a tangle (my gloss, not a quotation).

Gateways: hide the inside

The post says gateways enabled two major platform rewrites without forcing migrations on hundreds of dependent services. That is the payoff of the information hiding in chapter 2: callers who depend only on the front door do not notice that the room behind it was rebuilt.

Extensions: open for extension, closed for modification

Logic extensions use a provider pattern. Data extensions attach arbitrary data through Protobuf's Any type, so the core data model does not bloat with every team's fields; the post notes that a JSON string could work as a simpler alternative, and that Any brings some infrastructure overhead in exchange for stronger typing.

Analogy: a city and its zoning

Think of the city from earlier chapters. Domains are neighbourhoods, layers are zoning rules (heavy infrastructure underneath, shopfronts on top), gateways are the signed entrances where you must announce yourself, and extensions are permits that let a tenant fit out their own unit without rebuilding the building's structure.

Where it breaks down: a city grows by accretion, and nobody redraws it on a whiteboard. The post says DOMA is still young at Uber and likens evolving it to "trimming a hedge", incremental and ongoing.

Trade-offs and lessons

What Uber reports

Platform support costs "often dropped an order of magnitude." One platform consumer cut the time to prioritise and integrate a feature from three days to three hours. Onboarding time fell 25 to 50 percent. At the time, Uber had classified its 2,200 microservices into 70 domains, with roughly half implemented. These are Uber's own reports in a blog post; the post itself says detailed data was still pending.

Costs and caveats

The post acknowledges that microservices add infrastructure overhead and trade performance for operational benefits, and that DOMA is "still young at Uber". Structure above the services is more to design, document and keep honest.

It depends on company stage

The post's advice: startups should consider delaying microservices because the benefits may not offset the complexity; midsize companies may find them useful as separation of concerns blurs; large ones get the most out of DOMA.

Sizes are your call

The post gives no rule for how large a domain should be. A domain with one service is allowed. Boundaries still have to follow the business, as chapter 12 argues.

What this page does not claim

It does not claim DOMA is the right design for a team of ten, and the post itself suggests otherwise for startups. General industry pattern, not from the post: many organisations reach the same place by a different road, a modular monolith or a platform team with a stable API, because the underlying problem (too many fine-grained dependencies for anyone to understand) is not specific to microservices.

Try it yourself

Check a layer rule in a dozen lines

Layers are only useful if something enforces them. This sketch is my own: it assigns made-up domains to Uber's five layer names and flags any call from a lower layer to a higher one. The rule is an illustration of the general idea, not a claim about how Uber enforces DOMA. Nothing is written to disk.

LAYERS = ["infrastructure", "business", "product", "presentation", "edge"]  # low -> high
DOMAINS = {"payments": "business", "maps": "infrastructure",
           "trip-ui": "presentation", "rider-product": "product"}
CALLS = [("rider-product", "payments"), ("trip-ui", "rider-product"),
         ("payments", "maps"), ("maps", "rider-product")]   # last one is a violation
rank = {l: i for i, l in enumerate(LAYERS)}
for a, b in CALLS:
    ok = rank[DOMAINS[a]] >= rank[DOMAINS[b]]
    print(f"{a:14} -> {b:14} {'ok' if ok else 'VIOLATION: lower layer depends on higher'}")

Output when run:

rider-product  -> payments       ok
trip-ui        -> rider-product  ok
payments       -> maps           ok
maps           -> rider-product  VIOLATION: lower layer depends on higher

Now try your own system. List five services you know, group them into two or three domains on paper, and ask: which calls would cross a domain boundary, and would each one go through a single front door? The calls that do not are your "networked monolith" candidates. Chapter 2 and its import-graph tooling help here.

Distributed systems connection

Every service boundary is a network boundary, with partial failure, latency and versioned contracts (chapter 10). More boundaries mean more of all three. DOMA tries to keep the benefit of independent deployment while cutting the number of boundaries a developer must reason about, by drawing a few coarse ones around domains and keeping the fine ones as an internal detail. The post's closing view, that a microservice architecture is "really just one, large, distributed program", is the right frame for it: it is a design problem across the whole, not only inside each service.

Key takeaways

  • Uber reported about 2,200 critical microservices and investigations that touched around 50 services across 12 teams; the problem DOMA targets is complexity, not any single service.
  • A domain is a collection of related services; a layer is a collection of domains with rules about dependencies; a gateway is the single entry point; an extension adds behaviour or data without editing the core.
  • Gateways let Uber rewrite platforms behind them without forcing hundreds of dependents to migrate.
  • The post says the architecture is still young, and advises that startups consider delaying microservices.
  • Structure above services is the same information hiding you apply to modules, repeated at a larger scale.
