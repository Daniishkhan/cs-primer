Domain-Driven Design Essentials

Chapter 5 kept the business rules clean. This chapter is about finding out what those rules are, naming them so everyone agrees, and drawing the boundaries around them. Domain-driven design gives you a shared language, a way to cut a big business into contexts, and a small set of building blocks (value objects, entities, aggregates) for the code inside each one.

Why this matters

Most software projects do not fail because the team cannot write code. They fail because the code models the wrong thing, or models three different things under one name, or because nobody can tell where one team's model ends and the next one's begins. The ideas in Eric Evans's 2003 book Domain-Driven Design are mainly answers to that: speak one language, draw the boundaries on purpose, and make the code protect the rules. They also quietly underpin the rest of this course. Where you cut a monolith into modules or services (chapters 11 and 12) is largely a bounded-context question, and the aggregate rule below is the origin of the "one transaction, one service" advice you will meet there.

Two halves of one idea

Evans's book is often summarised as two sets of ideas. Strategic design is about the big picture: the language, the boundaries between parts of the business, and how teams relate. Tactical design is about the code inside one boundary: a handful of patterns for modelling rules. Most of the value comes from the strategic half, and most of the folklore (and boilerplate) comes from the tactical half. We take them in that order.

One term runs through both: the domain is the area of the business your software is about (food delivery, insurance claims, flight booking). The domain model is your team's simplified, working picture of it, held in people's heads, in documents and in code. A model is not the real world; it is a deliberately limited map made for a purpose.

Ubiquitous language

Ask a restaurateur, a waiter and a delivery rider about "an order" and you will get three slightly different things. The kitchen thinks of a ticket with dishes to cook. The waiter thinks of a table's bill. The rider thinks of a bag to carry to an address. A software team makes the problem worse by adding its own words: "transaction", "record", "request", "item".

Analogy: the restaurant chain

Picture a chain of restaurants. The kitchen, the front of house and the suppliers each have their own vocabulary, and inside each team that vocabulary is precise: to the kitchen a "cover" means a seat to prepare for, to the front of house it means a diner served, to the accountant it is a unit of revenue reporting. Inside a team this works, and the order ticket passed between teams is a carefully agreed form. Trouble starts only when people assume the word means the same thing everywhere.

Where it breaks down: a chain's vocabulary grew over centuries of hospitality and is shared by the whole trade. A software domain is new, small and ours, so we have to invent and agree the words deliberately, and keep them agreed as the product changes.

The ubiquitous language is Evans's name for the discipline of using one agreed vocabulary, everywhere: in conversations with domain experts, in the user stories, in the diagrams, in the documentation, and, crucially, in the names of classes, methods and database tables. If the business says "an order is placed, then paid, then handed over to a courier", the code has Order.pay() and a hand_over_to_courier use case, not setStatus(3) or updateRecord.

The benefit is mundane and large. Misunderstandings between business and engineering shrink because the code can be read aloud to an expert. When the business changes its mind ("a courier is now assigned, not handed the order"), the language changes first and the code follows, instead of a translation layer in everyone's head quietly drifting. And the effort of finding precise words is how you discover the rules: the sentence "an order can't be changed after it is paid" turns into an invariant you will implement below.

Common misconception

"Ubiquitous language means one global dictionary for the whole company." It does not, and chasing that is a classic mistake. The language is ubiquitous within a context. The same word legitimately means different things in different parts of the business, which is exactly what the next section is for.

Bounded contexts: the same word, different meanings

Consider the word "customer" in a company that sells subscriptions. The sales team means a lead or account: a name, a region, an account manager, a likelihood of closing. The support team means a person who needs help: contact channels, open tickets, how the last call went. The billing team means a legal entity that owes money: a legal name, a tax number, payment terms, outstanding invoices.

The tempting design is one Customer class with the union of every field, shared by all teams. It becomes a dumping ground: fifty columns, most null for most callers, every change negotiated with every team, and rules that conflict (can a customer exist without a tax number? yes in sales, no in billing). The alternative is Evans's bounded context: an explicit boundary inside which one model, and one ubiquitous language, applies consistently. Outside the boundary the same word may mean something else, and that is fine, because the contexts only touch at deliberate points.

A bounded context is a linguistic and model boundary, not automatically a deployment unit. One context might be one module in a monolith or one service, and a team commonly owns one or a few contexts. The tendency of teams and contexts to line up is not an accident; it is Conway's law (Melvin Conway, 1968: systems mirror the communication structure of the organisation that builds them), which chapter 11 returns to. For now the point is that contexts give you the first and best candidate cut-lines for modules.

Context maps: how contexts relate

Contexts are not islands. Orders have to reach delivery, delivery needs payment status, and so on. Evans's context map is a diagram of the contexts and of the relationship at each seam, because the kind of relationship decides who has to adapt to whom. These are the ones to know at first, at a high level:

  • Customer–supplier. One context (the upstream supplier) provides something another (the downstream customer) needs. The downstream team can ask for what it needs and the upstream team plans for it, so the two negotiate. Compare this with a conformist relationship, where the downstream team simply accepts whatever the upstream gives, usually because it has no leverage.
  • Shared kernel. Two contexts share a small piece of model and code (for example an Address type), owned jointly. It saves duplication but couples the teams: changing the kernel needs agreement, so keep it tiny.
  • Anti-corruption layer (ACL). A translating layer a context puts between itself and an outside model it does not want to adopt, such as a legacy system or a third-party API. It converts the outsider's concepts into your own language, so the foreign model cannot leak in and "corrupt" yours. (It is an adapter in the sense of chapter 5, with a stronger mission.)

Other patterns exist, such as an open-host service with a published language (a context offers a well-documented, stable interface to many consumers) and separate ways (contexts that choose not to integrate at all). Finding the contexts: a paragraph on event storming

How do you find the boundaries? One popular workshop technique, event storming (devised by Alberto Brandolini), gathers domain experts and developers in front of a long wall and a stack of coloured sticky notes. First, everyone writes domain events (orange notes, past tense: "Order Placed", "Payment Captured") and arranges them in time order. Then they add the commands that cause them (blue), the aggregates that decide (here purple), and hot spots where people disagree or do not know (here red; sticky-note colours vary between workshops). The boundaries emerge from the wall: wherever the vocabulary changes, or the people who care change, or an aggregate is always the one deciding, you have probably found a seam. It is cheap, it is visual, and it surfaces misunderstandings in an afternoon instead of in production.

Tactical building blocks

Inside one bounded context, Evans offers a vocabulary of patterns for the model in code. They are tools, not a checklist.

Entities and value objects

An entity is something with an identity that persists through change: customer 4711 is the same customer after moving house and changing her name, because the identity (the id), not the attributes, defines it. Two entities with the same id are the same thing even if their other fields differ. A value object has no identity: it is defined entirely by its attributes, it is interchangeable with any equal one, and it is immutable. Five euros is five euros; you do not ask "which five euros?". If you want a different value you create a new one. Money, an address, a date range and an email address are classic value objects.

Python makes immutability cheap with @dataclass(frozen=True): attributes cannot be reassigned, equality compares all fields, and instances are hashable. Here are the two side by side:

from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Address:                      # value object: two addresses with the same fields ARE the same address
    street: str
    city: str

class Customer:                     # entity: identity is the id, everything else may change
    def __init__(self, customer_id: str, address: Address):
        self.id, self.address = customer_id, address
    def __eq__(self, other): return isinstance(other, Customer) and self.id == other.id
    def __hash__(self): return hash(self.id)

home = Address("1 Main St", "Lisbon")
print(home == Address("1 Main St", "Lisbon"))
a, b = Customer("c-7", home), Customer("c-7", Address("9 Side Rd", "Porto"))
print(a == b)                       # same customer, even though the address differs
True
True

Immutability buys a lot for little: a value object can be shared freely between objects and threads without defensive copying, its validity is checked once in the constructor (a Money can never be negative, because a negative one cannot be created), and equality is simply "same contents". The big modelling habit to adopt is to prefer value objects. Many things that start as strings and ints (an amount, an email, a quantity) deserve a small type of their own that carries its rules with it. This is called fighting primitive obsession.

Aggregates: consistency boundaries

An aggregate is a cluster of entities and value objects that is treated as a single unit for the purpose of changing data. One entity is its aggregate root, and the rule is: outside code may hold a reference to, and call, only the root. The root enforces the aggregate's invariants (rules that must always be true, such as "an order's total equals the sum of its lines" or "nothing can be added to a paid order"). Because every change passes through the root, the invariant cannot be bypassed.

Two practical consequences follow, and they connect directly to the data course.

  • One transaction per aggregate. Save an aggregate atomically, as a unit (data chapter 10). Do not expect one use case to atomically change two aggregates: if "place order" must also update a customer's loyalty points, the loyalty change is a separate step, often triggered by a domain event, and the system is eventually consistent between them. This sounds like a limitation, and it is a deliberate one. It keeps transactions short, avoids long lock chains, and means aggregates could one day live in different databases or services.
  • Reference other aggregates by id. An Order holds a customer_id, not a Customer object. Holding the object would let code reach through it and change the customer from inside an order, silently breaking the one-aggregate rule, and loading an order could drag in half the database.
  • Keep aggregates small. Concurrent writers collide on an aggregate: two people editing one huge aggregate conflict even if they touch unrelated parts. This is the lost-update and write-skew problem of data chapter 11 and the locking of chapter 12, now appearing as a modelling decision. The usual defence is optimistic locking: a version column on the root, so a stale write is rejected rather than lost. Draw the boundary around what must be immediately consistent, and let everything else be eventually consistent.

Common misconception

"An aggregate is every object related to the main one." It is the opposite: an aggregate is the smallest cluster that must be consistent together. "Customer owns orders owns lines owns products" as one aggregate is a famous way to build a slow, contention-ridden system. If two things have separate lifecycles and no shared invariant, they are separate aggregates linked by id.

Domain events, repositories and domain services

A domain event records that something meaningful happened in the domain, named in the past tense by the ubiquitous language: OrderPaid, CourierAssigned. In the aggregate pattern the root appends an event when its state changes, and after the transaction commits the application layer publishes them so that other aggregates and contexts can react (loyalty points, a kitchen notification). Events are how consistent-within-an-aggregate becomes eventually-consistent-across-aggregates. This chapter only previews them; chapter 14 covers events versus commands, delivery guarantees, and why choreography has hidden costs. (Publishing reliably needs the outbox from chapter 5.)

A repository is the collection-like object through which the application loads and saves aggregates: orders.get(id) and orders.save(order). It is the driven port from chapter 5, with one repository per aggregate root and none for inner entities. Its contract is "give me the whole aggregate, consistent", which is why it fits so well with the one-transaction rule.

A domain service holds domain logic that does not naturally belong to any one entity or value object, usually because it involves several aggregates or an outside calculation. "Compute a delivery fee from a restaurant's location, the customer's address and the time of day" belongs to none of them, so it becomes a stateless function or small class named in the language of the domain. Use them sparingly: a model in which every rule has moved into services, leaving entities as bags of getters (an anaemic domain model), has lost the main benefit.

A worked example: the Order aggregate

Time to make the invariants real. This is the aggregate from the diagram, in plain Python. Money is a frozen value object that refuses nonsense; OrderLine is an immutable record; Order is the root. It enforces three invariants: nothing can be added after payment, the total is always the sum of the lines (it is computed, never stored separately, so it cannot disagree), and all lines share one currency. Paying records an OrderPaid event for the application layer to publish.

# ordering.py: an Order aggregate that protects its own invariants.
from __future__ import annotations
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Money:                                   # value object: equal by value, never mutated
    cents: int
    currency: str = "EUR"

    def __post_init__(self):
        if self.cents < 0:
            raise ValueError("money cannot be negative")

    def __add__(self, other: "Money") -> "Money":
        self._same_currency(other)
        return Money(self.cents + other.cents, self.currency)

    def times(self, n: int) -> "Money":
        return Money(self.cents * n, self.currency)

    def _same_currency(self, other: "Money") -> None:
        if self.currency != other.currency:
            raise ValueError(f"cannot mix {self.currency} and {other.currency}")

    def __str__(self) -> str:
        return f"{self.cents // 100}.{self.cents % 100:02d} {self.currency}"


class OrderError(Exception): ...


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class OrderPaid:                               # domain event: something that happened, past tense
    order_id: str
    total: Money


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class OrderLine:
    dish_id: str                               # a reference to another aggregate, by id only
    quantity: int
    unit_price: Money

    @property
    def subtotal(self) -> Money:
        return self.unit_price.times(self.quantity)


class Order:                                   # aggregate root: the only way in
    MAX_LINES = 20

    def __init__(self, order_id: str, customer_id: str):
        self.id, self.customer_id = order_id, customer_id
        self._lines: list[OrderLine] = []
        self._paid = False
        self._events: list[object] = []

    @property
    def lines(self) -> tuple[OrderLine, ...]:  # callers get a read-only view
        return tuple(self._lines)

    @property
    def total(self) -> Money:
        total = Money(0, self._lines[0].unit_price.currency) if self._lines else Money(0)
        for line in self._lines:
            total = total + line.subtotal
        return total

    def add_item(self, dish_id: str, quantity: int, unit_price: Money) -> None:
        if self._paid:
            raise OrderError("cannot change an order after payment")
        if quantity < 1:
            raise OrderError("quantity must be at least 1")
        if self._lines and unit_price.currency != self._lines[0].unit_price.currency:
            raise OrderError("all lines must use one currency")
        for i, line in enumerate(self._lines):         # same dish twice: merge into one line
            if line.dish_id == dish_id:
                self._lines[i] = OrderLine(dish_id, line.quantity + quantity, line.unit_price)
                return
        if len(self._lines) >= self.MAX_LINES:
            raise OrderError(f"at most {self.MAX_LINES} different dishes per order")
        self._lines.append(OrderLine(dish_id, quantity, unit_price))

    def pay(self) -> None:
        if self._paid:
            raise OrderError("already paid")
        if not self._lines:
            raise OrderError("cannot pay for an empty order")
        self._paid = True
        self._events.append(OrderPaid(self.id, self.total))

    def pull_events(self) -> list[object]:             # the application layer publishes these
        events, self._events = self._events, []
        return events


if __name__ == "__main__":
    order = Order("o-1", "ana")
    order.add_item("pad-thai", 2, Money(1250))
    order.add_item("spring-rolls", 1, Money(500))
    order.add_item("pad-thai", 1, Money(1250))
    print(order.total, [(l.dish_id, l.quantity) for l in order.lines])
    order.pay()
    print(order.pull_events())
    try:
        order.add_item("tea", 1, Money(300))
    except OrderError as e:
        print("rejected:", e)
    print(Money(500) == Money(500), Money(500) == Money(500, "USD"))
    try:
        Money(500).cents = 1
    except Exception as e:
        print(type(e).__name__)

Read the output line by line. Adding "pad-thai" twice merged into one line of 3, so the invariant "one line per dish" held. After pay() the event was pulled out, and the late add_item was rejected with a message in the domain's own words. Money compares by value, and trying to assign to a frozen field raised FrozenInstanceError. Now tests that read like the rules:

import unittest
from ordering import Money, Order, OrderError, OrderPaid

class MoneyTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_value_equality_and_addition(self):
        self.assertEqual(Money(250) + Money(50), Money(300))
    def test_refuses_mixed_currencies_and_negatives(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): Money(100, "EUR") + Money(100, "USD")
        with self.assertRaises(ValueError): Money(-1)

class OrderTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def setUp(self):
        self.order = Order("o-1", "ana")
        self.order.add_item("pad-thai", 2, Money(1250))

    def test_total_always_equals_sum_of_lines(self):
        self.order.add_item("tea", 3, Money(300))
        self.order.add_item("pad-thai", 1, Money(1250))            # merges into the first line
        self.assertEqual(self.order.total, sum((l.subtotal for l in self.order.lines), Money(0)))
        self.assertEqual(self.order.total, Money(3 * 1250 + 3 * 300))
        self.assertEqual(len(self.order.lines), 2)

    def test_cannot_add_after_payment(self):
        self.order.pay()
        with self.assertRaises(OrderError): self.order.add_item("tea", 1, Money(300))

    def test_cannot_pay_twice_or_pay_empty(self):
        self.order.pay()
        with self.assertRaises(OrderError): self.order.pay()
        with self.assertRaises(OrderError): Order("o-2", "ben").pay()

    def test_paying_records_one_event(self):
        self.order.pay()
        self.assertEqual(self.order.pull_events(), [OrderPaid("o-1", Money(2500))])
        self.assertEqual(self.order.pull_events(), [])

    def test_outside_code_cannot_sneak_a_line_in(self):
        with self.assertRaises(AttributeError):
            self.order.lines.append("bypass the rules")        # lines is a tuple: no append

if __name__ == "__main__":
    unittest.main()

Notice what is absent: no database, no framework, no mocks. The aggregate is plain objects, so its whole rule set runs in under a millisecond. Notice also what is deliberately limited: lines returns a tuple so callers cannot append to it behind the root's back. In Python nothing is truly private (order._lines is reachable), so the boundary is held by convention and review, which is one reason to keep the aggregate small enough to read in one sitting.

When DDD is overkill

Tactical DDD in particular is easy to over-apply. Be skeptical when:

  • The application is mostly CRUD. If the work is "store this form, show it again" and the rules are field validation, there is no domain complexity for aggregates to protect. A table, a form and some validation are the right amount of design.
  • You have no access to domain experts, or the domain is thin. A ubiquitous language needs people who know the business to build it with.
  • The team is small and the product is young. Heavy modelling before you know the domain locks in guesses. Many experienced engineers argue for learning first with a simple design and then deepening the model where the complexity turns out to live (the core domain, the part that makes the business different) while keeping commodity parts simple.
  • You are applying the vocabulary as ritual. Aggregates, repositories and factories for every table, mapped across five layers, is a lot of ceremony around a table.

The strategic ideas (shared language, explicit boundaries, a context map) are cheap and pay off almost everywhere there is more than one team. The tactical ideas pay off where there are real invariants. A good default: use the strategic half widely, and the tactical half only in the part of the system where the rules are rich.

  3. Make lines return the underlying list instead of a tuple. Write a test that bypasses the invariant. Which of the aggregate's guarantees does it break?

Distributed systems connection

Bounded contexts, and aggregates in particular, are the design-time version of distributed-systems facts. The rule "one transaction per aggregate, eventual consistency between them" is exactly what you are left with when the contexts end up in different services: there is no distributed transaction to rely on (chapter 15 shows the saga that replaces it). Referencing by id rather than by object is what lets two services own their own data (chapter 12). Domain events become the messages on a broker (chapter 14), with all the duplicate, reorder and lost-message problems from the networking and data courses. Teams that draw bounded contexts and aggregate boundaries well find that splitting into services later is mostly mechanical; teams that don't discover the missing boundaries the expensive way, as distributed transactions they cannot have. See Case 6 for how one large company organised thousands of services around domains.

Key takeaways

  • Domain-driven design (Eric Evans, 2003) has a strategic half (language, boundaries) and a tactical half (building blocks in code). The strategic half is the more broadly useful.
  • Ubiquitous language: one vocabulary shared by conversations, code and documents, within a context. Code that can be read aloud to a domain expert is a sign it is working.
  • A bounded context is a boundary inside which one model applies. The same word (Customer, Order) legitimately means different things in different contexts; do not force one global model.
  • A context map shows relationships at each seam (customer–supplier, shared kernel, anti-corruption layer, and others) and thus who has to adapt to whom.
  • Entities have identity; value objects are immutable and compared by value. Prefer value objects. In Python, dataclass(frozen=True) does it.
  • An aggregate is the smallest consistency boundary: outside code uses the root, the root enforces invariants, you save one aggregate per transaction, and other aggregates are referenced by id only. Keep them small.
  • Domain events, repositories and domain services complete the toolkit. DDD is overkill for simple CRUD; apply the tactical patterns only where the rules are rich.

Check your understanding

The sales, support and billing teams each need a "Customer", with different fields and rules. What does DDD suggest?

  1. Build one big Customer class with all the fields and let every team use it
  2. Force all three teams to agree one definition of "customer" for the whole company
  3. Give each context its own Customer model, linked by an identifier, and make the relationships between contexts explicit
  4. Rename the word so that it is never used
A bounded context lets the same word mean different things in different places, each model small and consistent. The shared giant class becomes a coordination bottleneck, and a single enterprise definition satisfies nobody. Contexts connect through identifiers and deliberate interfaces.

Your Ordering context must talk to a legacy billing system with an awkward model and cryptic field names. Which context-map pattern protects your own model?

  1. An anti-corruption layer that translates between the legacy model and yours
  2. A shared kernel with the legacy system
  3. Making your own model match the legacy one (conformist) so nothing needs translating
  4. Deleting the legacy system's database
An anti-corruption layer translates the outsider's concepts into your own language so the foreign model cannot leak in. A shared kernel couples you tightly to a small shared model, and conforming is a legitimate choice only when the outside model is good enough, which this one is not.

Which is the best candidate for a value object rather than an entity?

  1. A customer account that keeps its history after a name change
  2. An order whose status changes over time
  3. A courier with a unique employee number
  4. A price of 12.50 EUR
A value object has no identity of its own and is defined entirely by its attributes: any 12.50 EUR is interchangeable with any other. The others have identities that persist while their attributes change, which makes them entities.

A use case needs to place an order and update the customer's loyalty points "atomically". How does aggregate design suggest handling it?

  1. Put the Customer object inside the Order aggregate so one transaction covers both
  2. Treat them as separate aggregates: commit the order, then update loyalty in a separate step (often triggered by a domain event), accepting eventual consistency
  3. Hold a lock on both tables for the whole request
  4. Merge Customer and Order into one table
The rule is one transaction per aggregate and references to other aggregates by id. Pulling the customer into the order makes a bloated aggregate that every concurrent writer fights over. Across aggregates, accept eventual consistency, and publish an event reliably (the outbox from chapter 5).

When is full tactical DDD (aggregates, repositories, domain services) probably overkill?

  1. In an application with rich, changing business rules and many invariants
  2. In the core domain of a company that sells software
  3. In an application that mostly stores and displays records with simple validation
  4. Whenever there is more than one developer
Aggregates exist to protect invariants. With few invariants (CRUD), they add layers, mapping and ceremony without protecting anything. Rich, changing rules are exactly where the patterns pay for themselves. Team size alone is not the criterion.
