Complexity & Why Software Rots

Nobody sets out to build a system that is painful to change. Yet most codebases drift there, one reasonable shortcut at a time. This chapter gives you a precise vocabulary for the problem (what complexity is, how you feel it, and what causes it) and a mindset for fighting it, so you can spot the rot while it is still cheap to remove.

Why this matters

Every later idea in this course (modules, layers, services, events) is a tool for managing complexity. If you do not feel the problem, the tools look like ceremony. If you do, they look like what they are: ways to stop one change from rippling through a whole system. Ousterhout's framing in A Philosophy of Software Design (2018) is the clearest short account of the problem that I know, and this chapter follows it closely.

What complexity is

John Ousterhout defines complexity as anything related to the structure of a system that makes it hard to understand and modify. Two things in that sentence matter.

First, complexity is about what a developer experiences, not about size. A 50,000-line system whose parts are clearly separated can be simpler to work in than a 2,000-line script where everything touches everything. Lines of code, number of features and number of servers are all poor stand-ins.

Second, it is about understanding and modifying, the two things programmers actually do all day. A messy piece of code that nobody ever needs to read again costs you nothing. Ousterhout's point, roughly put, is that the complexity of a system is dominated by the parts you touch most. A tangle in a corner you never visit is far less harmful than a small knot in the code you change every week.

Three symptoms: how you feel it

Complexity is invisible until you try to change something. It then shows up in three recognisable ways.

Change amplification

Change amplification means a seemingly simple change requires edits in many places. You want to change the colour of a link and have to touch forty files because the colour was copied into each. You want to store prices in cents and find the assumption "prices are floats" in the cart, the invoice template, the export job and a spreadsheet macro. The measure of amplification is simple: how many places did I have to touch for what I thought was one change?

Cognitive load

Cognitive load is how much a developer needs to know in order to complete a task. A function with seven flag arguments, where four combinations are illegal and the order of two calls matters, has a high cognitive load even though it is short. High load makes mistakes likely: you cannot hold all of it in your head. Importantly, more code is not the same as more load. A hundred lines you can read top to bottom with no hidden rules can be lighter than ten lines that depend on a global you did not know existed.

Unknown unknowns

The worst symptom is unknown unknowns: it is not obvious which pieces of code must change to finish a task, or what information you need before you can do it safely. With change amplification you at least know there are many places; with unknown unknowns you do not even know where to look, and you find out in production. Ousterhout calls this the worst of the three, because you cannot be careful about something you do not know exists.

Two causes: dependencies and obscurity

Symptoms are what you feel; causes are what you can design against. Ousterhout names two.

A dependency exists when a piece of code cannot be understood or changed in isolation. Dependencies are not evil; they are how software gets built from parts. But every dependency is a place where a change can leak. The goal is not zero dependencies, which is impossible, but fewer, simpler and more obvious ones.

Obscurity exists when important information is not obvious. A variable called data; an amount that is sometimes dollars and sometimes cents; a function that also sends an email, and does not say so; two modules that must be called in a particular order, which is written nowhere. Obscurity is what turns a dependency from a known cost into an unknown unknown.

Analogy: a city's plumbing

Picture a city where each building's pipes were joined to the neighbours' as it grew. Dependencies are the pipes: you cannot fix one building without checking the others. Obscurity is the missing map. Nobody knows which pipe feeds which, so a repair that should take an hour becomes a week of digging and guessing, and sometimes you find out you cut off a hospital. A planned city has separate mains, clear valves and a map in the city office.

Where it breaks down: real pipes are physical and visible once you dig. Software dependencies can be completely invisible (a shared name, a timing assumption, a convention) and, unlike pipes, they cost almost nothing to add, which is exactly why they pile up.

A thousand small cuts

The key property of complexity is that it is incremental. No single change makes a system complex. A flag here, a special case there, a copy-pasted block "just for now". Each is easily justified and each costs a little. But the costs add, and they compound, because every new shortcut has to be written around the old ones.

This has a consequence that is easy to miss: you cannot fight complexity by being vigilant only about big decisions. A system dies of a thousand small cuts, so the defence is to refuse to make them, which means caring about small design improvements in every change.

Tactical vs strategic programming

Ousterhout contrasts two mindsets. In tactical programming your main goal is to get the feature or bug fix working, as fast as possible. Each task is small, and each shortcut is rational for that task. In strategic programming the main goal is a great design that also happens to work. His slogan is: "working code isn't enough." Code that works but is badly structured passes the tests today and charges interest on every change after.

The extreme tactical programmer has a name: the tactical tornado. This is the prolific developer who ships features faster than anyone, leaves a trail of quick hacks behind, and is often a hero to management because the cost is invisible and falls on other people, who must work in the wreckage. Everyone has met one; a few of us have been one on a deadline.

Being strategic does not mean big up-front design or polishing forever. Ousterhout suggests investing a small, steady fraction of your effort in design, on the order of 10 to 20 percent, with each change leaving the code a little better than it found it. And he advises designing important interfaces twice: sketch two quite different approaches and compare them, because your first idea is rarely the best.

Common misconception

"Being strategic means never taking shortcuts." Tactical is the right call sometimes: a throwaway prototype, a startup still searching for a product, an emergency fix with an explicit plan to clean up. The failure is not taking a shortcut; it is taking shortcuts by default, with no plan to repay them, in code that will live for years.

Essential vs accidental complexity

Not all complexity can be designed away. In his 1986 essay "No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accident in Software Engineering", Fred Brooks separated two kinds. Essential complexity is inherent in the problem: tax law really has hundreds of special cases, an airline schedule really has many constraints, and any correct program must deal with them. Accidental complexity comes from our own tools and choices: awkward languages, needless indirection, a design that spreads one rule across ten files.

Brooks argued that no single technique would, by itself, give a tenfold improvement in productivity within a decade, because most of the remaining difficulty was essential and more tools could only attack the accidental part. You do not have to agree with the whole essay to take away its practical use. When something feels complicated, ask: is the world this complicated, or did we make it so? Essential complexity must be organised well, in one place with a clear name. Accidental complexity should be deleted.

Analogy: a tax return

The tax rules are essential complexity: they exist whether or not you have software. A confusing form layout, a website that loses your progress and a spreadsheet with six tabs that must be filled in a special order are accidental. A good accountant cannot simplify the law, but can simplify how you meet it.

Where it breaks down: in real projects the line is blurry. A "business rule" that looks essential is sometimes an accident of how a legacy system once behaved, and it is worth asking a domain expert whether it must really be kept.

Technical debt

Ward Cunningham, in the early 1990s, described shipping code you know is imperfect as taking on a debt: you get something sooner, and you pay interest afterwards in the form of slower changes, until you repay the principal by improving the design. The metaphor is useful because it makes the trade-off discussable with non-programmers: borrowing can be sensible, as long as you know you are doing it and can afford the interest.

Not all debt is the same. Martin Fowler adds a helpful distinction: some debt is deliberate ("we know this is a shortcut, and we choose it to hit the date"), and some is inadvertent ("we did our best, and only later learned how it should have been done"). Within each, the debt can be reckless or prudent. Deliberate and prudent debt is a considered loan. Inadvertent debt is unavoidable in any project where you learn as you go: the design that was right with the knowledge you had becomes wrong as you learn more.

Common misconception

"Technical debt is any code I don't like." Debt is a design that makes future change more expensive than it needs to be. Ugly code that nobody touches carries no interest. And unlike a loan, the interest rate is unknown and uneven: it is paid only when someone works in that part of the system, which is why the next section matters.

Measuring complexity honestly

Nobody has found a single number for "how complex is this system". Be suspicious of dashboards that claim one. But you can get useful signals.

The most practical idea is: cost is how often you change something multiplied by how hard it is to change. A gnarly module that has not been edited for three years is cheap. A mildly awkward one that every team edits every week is expensive. Sorting your files by change frequency (from version control history) and then looking at the hard ones among them finds your hot spots: the places where refactoring pays back soonest. (Adam Tornhill popularised this approach for analysing codebases in Your Code as a Crime Scene.)

Cyclomatic complexity, proposed by Thomas McCabe in 1976, counts the independent paths through a function. In its common form it is one plus the number of decision points (if, loops, and/or, exception handlers). It is a rough guide to testing effort (covering every branch never takes more tests than this number, and the function has at least this many distinct paths), and it flags monster functions. But its limits are important:

  • It counts branches, not understandability. A flat function with fifty simple if lines can score worse than a short but deeply tricky one.
  • It is local. It says nothing about dependencies between modules, which is where change amplification lives.
  • It can be gamed or merely moved: splitting a function into three leaves the total branch count the same.

Other signals are cheaper than they sound: how many files does a typical small change touch (from your commit history), how long does a new developer take to make a first safe change, and how often do releases break something unrelated. These measure the symptoms directly.

The same feature, before and after

Let us make this concrete. Here is a pricing function of the kind that grows in real shops. It applies discounts by customer type, a holiday sale, a coupon, tax, and shipping. Every special case was added to fix a real request. Read it once, then notice how much you need to hold in your head.

# BEFORE: one function, flags and special cases accumulated over time.
def total(items, customer, holiday=False, coupon=None, express=False, country="GB"):
    subtotal = sum(price * qty for price, qty in items)
    if customer == "vip":
        subtotal = subtotal * 0.90
    elif customer == "staff":
        subtotal = subtotal * 0.70
    if holiday and customer != "staff":
        subtotal = subtotal * 0.95
    if coupon == "SAVE5":
        subtotal = subtotal - 5
        if customer == "staff":          # added after a bug report
            subtotal = subtotal + 5
    tax_rate = 0.20 if country == "GB" else 0.0
    if country == "GB" and customer == "staff":
        tax_rate = 0.0                   # staff are tax-free (really?)
    tax = subtotal * tax_rate
    ship = 0 if subtotal > 50 else (9.99 if express else 4.99)
    if express and country != "GB":
        ship = ship * 2
    return round(subtotal + tax + ship, 2)

print(total([(30.0, 2)], "vip", coupon="SAVE5"))
print(total([(30.0, 2)], "staff", holiday=True, coupon="SAVE5", express=True))
print(total([(10.0, 1)], "regular", express=True, country="US"))
$ python3 c1_before.py
63.79
51.99
29.98

Now a new requirement arrives: students get 15% off, no holiday sale, but coupons and tax work as usual. To make the change safely in this version you must find every place the customer type is mentioned. There are five (the lines that compare customer), and you must decide for each whether students behave like regular customers, like staff, or like VIPs. Two need edits:

    elif customer == "student":              # NEW (edit 1)
        subtotal = subtotal * 0.85
    ...
    if holiday and customer not in ("staff", "student"):   # NEW (edit 2)

The coupon rule and the tax rule need no edit, but only because you read them and concluded students are not staff. Miss the holiday line and students silently get a sale discount they should not. That is change amplification plus cognitive load, and a small unknown unknown: the rule about who gets the holiday sale is spread over several lines that do not mention each other.

Here is the same behaviour, redesigned so the knowledge about customer types lives in one table:

# AFTER: the customer-specific rules live in one table; the code has no special cases.
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Terms:
    multiplier: float = 1.0      # price multiplier
    holiday_ok: bool = True      # does the holiday sale apply?
    coupon_ok: bool = True       # may they use coupons?
    taxed: bool = True           # do we charge tax (in the UK)?

TERMS = {
    "regular": Terms(),
    "vip":     Terms(multiplier=0.90),
    "staff":   Terms(multiplier=0.70, holiday_ok=False, coupon_ok=False, taxed=False),
}

def total(items, customer, holiday=False, coupon=None, express=False, country="GB"):
    t = TERMS[customer]
    amount = sum(price * qty for price, qty in items) * t.multiplier
    if holiday and t.holiday_ok:
        amount *= 0.95
    if coupon == "SAVE5" and t.coupon_ok:
        amount -= 5
    tax = amount * 0.20 if country == "GB" and t.taxed else 0.0
    return round(amount + tax + shipping(amount, express, country), 2)

def shipping(amount, express, country):
    base = 0.0 if amount > 50 else (9.99 if express else 4.99)
    return base * 2 if express and country != "GB" else base

print(total([(30.0, 2)], "vip", coupon="SAVE5"))
print(total([(30.0, 2)], "staff", holiday=True, coupon="SAVE5", express=True))
print(total([(10.0, 1)], "regular", express=True, country="US"))
$ python3 c1_after.py
63.79
51.99
29.98

Same three answers as before. Now the same new requirement:

    "student": Terms(multiplier=0.85, holiday_ok=False),          # NEW: the whole change
$ python3 c1_student_before.py
60.19
$ python3 c1_student_after.py
60.19

Both versions agree (60.19), but the second needed one added line, and the question "who is eligible for what?" is answered in a single place that reads like a policy document. Now run a simple complexity counter over both:

import ast, sys

def cyclomatic(func: ast.FunctionDef) -> int:
    """1 + the number of decision points (a simple, common approximation)."""
    n = 1
    for node in ast.walk(func):
        if isinstance(node, (ast.If, ast.For, ast.While, ast.IfExp, ast.ExceptHandler)):
            n += 1
        elif isinstance(node, ast.BoolOp):
            n += len(node.values) - 1          # each and/or adds a path
    return n

for path in sys.argv[1:]:
    tree = ast.parse(open(path).read())
    for node in ast.walk(tree):
        if isinstance(node, ast.FunctionDef):
            print(f"{path:14} {node.name:10} complexity {cyclomatic(node)}")
$ python3 cyclo.py c1_before.py c1_after.py
c1_before.py   total      complexity 14
c1_after.py    total      complexity 7
c1_after.py    shipping   complexity 5

Notice the humbling part. The branch count fell from 14 to 7 + 5 = 12, barely different, yet the redesign is dramatically easier to change. Cyclomatic complexity measured the branches; the real improvement was where the knowledge about customers lives. That is exactly the limit of the metric.

A fair caveat

The table version is not free. It adds a small abstraction (Terms) that a newcomer must learn, and if customer types genuinely had different logic (not just different numbers) a table would be the wrong tool. Good design removes the complexity you actually have, not the one in a textbook. We come back to the cost of abstraction in chapter 3.

Explore: change amplification

The next widget models a small shop system of nine modules. An arrow from A to B means "A depends on B", and a change spreads backwards along the arrows. Try the Tangled preset, where modules reach into one another's internals and share mutable globals, then the Modular preset, where they talk through narrow interfaces. Pick a request and send it. The final preset lets you edit the graph yourself.

Things to try, with what they teach:

  • Change the tax rules on Tangled, then Modular. The same change hits most of the system, or exactly one module. The only difference is how the others were allowed to depend on Tax.
  • Rename a customer field on Modular. Three modules change, not one: when a request alters an interface, its direct users must adapt. A narrow interface limits the damage but does not abolish it, so interfaces should change rarely and deliberately (see chapter 10).
  • On Build your own, start from Modular and turn a single dependency from "via interface" into "direct", for example Pricing's dependency on Tax. Then rerun "change the tax rules". One careless shortcut and a one-module change becomes a two-module change. Now press "Make every dependency direct" and see the whole system collapse into one blob.
Go deeper: how the widget decides what is affected optional

The ripple is a breadth-first walk over the reversed dependency arrows, with one rule about interfaces. A direct or global dependency always carries the change to its dependant. A dependency through an interface carries it only when the change starts in the module behind that interface and the request alters the interface itself. Modules at distance 0 or 1 are shown as "must change"; further out they are "must be retested", meaning their code may be fine but their behaviour could have shifted. It is a model of an idea, not a measurement: in real code the answer depends on exactly how the dependency is written.

Try it yourself

Try it yourself: find your hot spots

Pick a repository you work in. This command lists the files changed most often in the last six months (it only reads version-control history). The output below is an example; yours will differ.

$ git log --since="6 months ago" --name-only --pretty=format: | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -5
     87 src/checkout/pricing.py
     64 src/orders/service.py
     41 src/api/routes.py
     19 src/checkout/tax.py
     12 README.md

Now open the top two files. For each, ask the three symptom questions: when someone changes it, how many other files change too (amplification)? How much must you know before editing (load)? Has it ever broken something you did not expect (unknown unknowns)? A file that is both frequently changed and painful to change is where the next hour of refactoring pays most. For a second experiment, run the cyclo.py script above on one of your own Python files and compare it with your gut feeling about which functions are hard.

Distributed systems connection

Distribution magnifies all three symptoms. A change that used to be a compile error in one program becomes a break in a service you cannot see: that is an unknown unknown made of network calls and teams. Change amplification becomes coordinated deployments. And cognitive load now includes failure: a call that was a function can now time out, be retried, or half-succeed (networking ch. 16). If your system is already tangled in one process, splitting it into services does not untangle it; it gives you a distributed tangle, which is harder to debug. We return to that trap in chapter 12.

Key takeaways

  • Complexity is anything in a system's structure that makes it hard to understand and modify. It is what developers experience, not a count of lines.
  • Its symptoms are change amplification, cognitive load and unknown unknowns; its causes are dependencies and obscurity.
  • It accumulates through many small, individually reasonable shortcuts, so the defence is small improvements in every change.
  • Tactical programming optimises for the next feature; strategic programming treats a good design as the goal. "Working code isn't enough", and the tactical tornado's cost falls on everyone else.
  • Essential complexity comes from the problem and should be organised; accidental complexity comes from our choices and should be removed (Brooks, 1986).
  • Technical debt is a useful metaphor when it is deliberate and repaid where the interest is highest; measure cost as change frequency times difficulty, and treat cyclomatic complexity as a weak, local signal.
