Sync vs Async Communication

Once two parts of a system live in different processes, one question decides much of their behaviour: does the caller wait for the answer, or hand off the work and move on? This chapter compares the two styles honestly, shows the same order flow succeed and fail in each, and gives you tools for the hardest part of the synchronous style: choosing timeouts across a chain of calls.

Why this matters

The choice between calling a service and sending it a message shapes availability, latency, consistency and how hard the system is to debug. Choose synchronous everywhere and one slow dependency freezes everything upstream. Choose asynchronous everywhere and users face an "accepted, we'll get back to you" experience for operations that deserved an immediate answer, while engineers trace behaviour through queues. Most good systems use both, deliberately. The skill is knowing which interaction deserves which.

Synchronous request/response

In the synchronous style the caller sends a request and waits for the response before continuing. HTTP APIs and gRPC calls are the usual examples (chapter 9). The appeal is real:

  • It is simple to reason about. The code reads top to bottom like a local function call. Errors come straight back to the caller, which can decide what to do.
  • The answer is immediate. You know now whether the payment was authorised, so you can tell the shopper now.
  • Debugging is easier. A request has one path, one trace, one place where it failed.

The price is temporal coupling: caller and callee must both be up at the same time for the request to succeed. Everything from chapter 11 follows from that. Availability of a chain is the product of availabilities. Latency of a chain is the sum (or the max of the slowest, for parallel calls) of its parts, and tails compound. And failures cascade: when a downstream service slows down, callers hold threads, connections and memory waiting for it, their own callers pile up behind them, and a problem in one corner becomes an outage everywhere. That is the queueing mechanism from systems chapter 14, and the timeout-and-retry dance of networking chapter 16, now with business consequences.

Asynchronous messaging

In the asynchronous style the sender puts a message on a broker and carries on; one or more receivers handle it later. The broker is typically one of two things (data chapter 19):

  • a queue, where each message is delivered to one consumer of a group and then removed, ideal for distributing work; or
  • a log (such as Kafka-style systems), where messages are appended to a durable, ordered sequence that many consumers read independently at their own pace, and can re-read.

What this buys:

  • Decoupling in time. The receiver can be down, slow or being deployed, and the sender is unaffected. The broker absorbs the gap. This turns "dependency outage" into "dependency delay".
  • Load levelling. A burst of requests becomes a backlog drained at the consumer's pace, not a crushed service.
  • Fan-out. One message can feed several independent consumers without the sender knowing any of them.

What it costs:

  • Eventual consistency. After the sender returns, the work is not done yet. Other parts of the system see old state for a while, and the user interface has to say so honestly.
  • No immediate answer. If the sender needs a result (is this card valid?) a message is the wrong shape unless you add a way to get the reply back.
  • Harder reasoning and tracing. The flow is spread across producers, brokers and consumers with no single call stack. You need correlation ids, distributed tracing, and dashboards for queue depth and age.
  • New failure modes. Messages can be delivered more than once (so consumers must be idempotent), arrive out of order, or sit in a dead-letter queue because they can never be processed. The broker itself becomes a critical piece of infrastructure.

Analogy: a phone call and a postal letter, in a restaurant chain

Back in the restaurant chain: when a waiter walks to the kitchen and asks the chef "can you do a well-done steak for table 9?" and waits for a yes, that is a phone call: immediate, and it only works if the chef is free and answering. When the waiter instead clips an order ticket to the rail, goes back to the dining room, and the kitchen picks up tickets in order whenever it can, that is messaging. A busy night makes the rail longer, not the waiter's wait longer; a cook who steps out does not stop service, as the tickets simply wait.

Where it breaks down: a paper ticket rail is a single, physical, always-ordered thing that everyone can see. A real broker can reorder, duplicate or lose messages (depending on its settings) and it can be full or unavailable. And the diner at table 9, who wanted to hear "yes, well-done" now, may not be happy with "your order has been taken".

The same flow, two ways

The clearest way to see the difference is to run one flow through both. Shoppers place an order. Orders must reserve stock from Inventory, and Inventory is down at the moment. The widget runs the synchronous version on top and the asynchronous version below, in lock-step. Watch the "User sees" line.

Step through

Place an order while Inventory is down

  1. The shopper places an order. Both versions start the same way: a request arrives at Orders. Inventory, which will be needed to reserve stock, is currently down.
  2. Synchronous: Orders calls Inventory inside the request and gets no response. Asynchronous: Orders saves the order as PENDING and puts a ReserveStock message on a queue. It does not call Inventory at all.
  3. Synchronous: Orders is still waiting for its timeout, and so is the shopper. Asynchronous: Orders replies at once with "202 accepted"; the shopper sees a receipt.
  4. Synchronous: the timeout fires and the shopper gets an error; the order was not placed. Asynchronous: Inventory is still down, but the message waits safely in the queue.
  5. Synchronous: the shopper retries; Inventory is still down, so the retry fails the same way. Asynchronous: Inventory recovers and the queue delivers the waiting message.
  6. Synchronous: no order, and probably no customer. Asynchronous: stock is reserved, the order moves to CONFIRMED and the shopper is emailed. Slower than ideal, but the work was not lost.

Don't walk away thinking asynchronous "wins". Look at what the asynchronous column quietly required:

  • The order may fail later. If stock runs out while the message waits, Orders must cancel an order the shopper was already told was received, so you need a compensating path and a clear message to the shopper.
  • A PENDING state now exists in the model, and every part of the system that reads orders must handle it.
  • The consumer must be idempotent, because a delivery that is retried after a crash would otherwise reserve stock twice.
  • Someone must watch the queue. A message that waits an hour is an incident even if no error was ever raised.

Common misconception

"Async is more reliable." It moves the failure rather than removing it. Synchronous failures are loud and immediate; asynchronous failures are quiet and delayed, which can be worse when nobody is watching. A queue improves availability of the sender, and only if you also build monitoring, retry limits, dead-letter handling and idempotent consumers.

Commands, events and queries

The word "message" hides three different intents, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion. Chapter 14 goes deep; here is the preview.

  • A command asks one specific receiver to do something: ReserveStock(order 42). It is addressed, imperative, and may be rejected. The sender cares about the outcome.
  • An event states that something already happened: OrderPlaced(order 42). It is not addressed to anyone; zero, one or many parties may react. The sender does not care who listens.
  • A query asks for information without changing anything: GetOrder(42). It usually needs an answer now, which is why queries are most often synchronous.

A useful rule of thumb: commands and queries create a dependency on the receiver, events reverse it (the receiver depends on the sender's event). Events keep the sender ignorant of its consumers, which is why they decouple so well, and why they are also harder to follow: the effect of an event is wherever someone happened to subscribe.

Request/reply over messaging

Sometimes you want a queue's buffering and an answer. The standard pattern: the sender puts a message with a correlation id and a reply-to address (for example, a private reply queue). The receiver handles it, and sends the result to the reply-to queue with the same correlation id; the sender matches replies to requests by that id. It works, but notice what you have built: a synchronous interaction with an extra hop in each direction, plus the need to time out and give up if the reply never comes. Use it when the sender can continue meanwhile (so it need not block a thread on the answer) or when the buffering matters, and not as a way to make a synchronous problem look asynchronous.

Timeouts across a call chain: the deadline budget

Synchronous chains need timeouts (networking chapter 16; resilience patterns in chapter 17). The trap is setting them hop by hop. If the gateway gives each downstream call a 500 ms timeout, and Orders also gives Inventory 500 ms, then the gateway may give up at 500 ms while Orders is still waiting, and Inventory is still working, on a result that nobody will read. That abandoned work still consumes capacity, and it is the fuel for retry storms.

The fix is a deadline budget. The edge sets one absolute deadline for the whole user request ("this must be done by 12:00:00.500") and every hop passes it on. Each service then derives its own timeout as what remains, and refuses to start work it cannot finish. gRPC bakes this in: a client sets a deadline on a call, it travels with the call (on the wire as the time remaining, in the grpc-timeout header), the server can see how long is left, and well-behaved servers hand the remaining time to their own downstream calls. Many HTTP stacks have no standard equivalent, so teams invent a header, and each service must honour it.

Here is the idea in runnable form. The first half simulates a synchronous chain (orders → inventory → payments) on a fake clock so it is deterministic; each hop gets the same absolute deadline and fails fast when the remaining time is short. The second half runs a queue-based version where Inventory has an outage for four ticks.

import queue

# ---------- Part 1: a deadline budget propagated through a synchronous chain ----------
class DeadlineExceeded(Exception):
    pass

class Clock:
    """A fake clock so the demo is deterministic. Calls 'sleep' by advancing time."""
    def __init__(self): self.now = 0.0
    def sleep(self, ms): self.now += ms

def call(clock, name, work_ms, deadline, downstream=None):
    """One service hop. It refuses work it cannot finish and passes the SAME
    absolute deadline downstream, rather than starting a fresh timeout."""
    remaining = deadline - clock.now
    if remaining <= 0:
        raise DeadlineExceeded(f"{name}: budget already spent at t={clock.now:.0f}ms")
    if work_ms > remaining:
        clock.sleep(remaining)
        raise DeadlineExceeded(f"{name}: needed {work_ms}ms, only {remaining:.0f}ms left")
    clock.sleep(work_ms)
    if downstream:
        downstream(deadline)
    return "ok"

def place_order(clock, budget_ms, inventory_ms):
    deadline = clock.now + budget_ms
    payments = lambda d: call(clock, "payments", 120, d)
    inventory = lambda d: call(clock, "inventory", inventory_ms, d, payments)
    return call(clock, "orders", 30, deadline, inventory)

print("-- synchronous chain, 500 ms budget --")
for inv in (100, 250, 400):
    c = Clock()
    try:
        r = place_order(c, 500, inv)
        print(f"inventory takes {inv:>3}ms -> {r}, finished at t={c.now:.0f}ms")
    except DeadlineExceeded as e:
        print(f"inventory takes {inv:>3}ms -> FAILED at t={c.now:.0f}ms: {e}")

# ---------- Part 2: the same work through a queue; an outage delays, not fails ----------
print("\n-- queue-based, inventory down for ticks 2-5 --")
inbox = queue.Queue()
done, tick = [], 0
for order_id in (1, 2, 3):            # the user-facing step only enqueues: instant "accepted"
    inbox.put(order_id)
    print(f"tick 0: order {order_id} accepted (queued)")

def inventory_up(t):
    return not (2 <= t <= 5)

while len(done) < 3:
    tick += 1
    if not inventory_up(tick):
        print(f"tick {tick}: inventory down, {inbox.qsize()} waiting in queue")
        continue
    if not inbox.empty():
        oid = inbox.get()
        done.append((oid, tick))
        print(f"tick {tick}: order {oid} processed")
print("completion ticks:", done)
-- synchronous chain, 500 ms budget --
inventory takes 100ms -> ok, finished at t=250ms
inventory takes 250ms -> ok, finished at t=400ms
inventory takes 400ms -> FAILED at t=500ms: payments: needed 120ms, only 70ms left

-- queue-based, inventory down for ticks 2-5 --
tick 0: order 1 accepted (queued)
tick 0: order 2 accepted (queued)
tick 0: order 3 accepted (queued)
tick 1: order 1 processed
tick 2: inventory down, 2 waiting in queue
tick 3: inventory down, 2 waiting in queue
tick 4: inventory down, 2 waiting in queue
tick 5: inventory down, 2 waiting in queue
tick 6: order 2 processed
tick 7: order 3 processed
completion ticks: [(1, 1), (2, 6), (3, 7)]

Read it in two parts.

  • Synchronous. With a 500 ms budget the chain succeeds when inventory takes 100 or 250 ms. At 400 ms, orders (30) and inventory (400) have used 430 ms, leaving 70 ms for a payment that needs 120. The request fails at exactly t = 500 ms: the system did not hang, and it did not carry on past what the user would wait for. A refinement that real systems use is to fail before starting a hop whose expected cost exceeds the time left, so the 70 ms is not spent at all.
  • Queue-based. Three orders are accepted instantly at tick 0. Order 1 is processed at tick 1, then inventory goes down for ticks 2 to 5. Orders 2 and 3 simply wait, and complete at ticks 6 and 7. Nothing failed; it was delayed. (The demo does not show what a real system also needs: a bounded queue, a maximum age for messages, and a dead-letter queue for the ones that never succeed.)

Fan-out and backpressure

Asynchronous messaging makes fan-out cheap: publish one OrderPlaced event and let Shipping, Notifications, Analytics and Fraud each react independently. The sender's cost does not grow with the number of consumers, which is a large part of the style's appeal. But the system's total work does grow, and each consumer has its own speed.

When producers outpace consumers, the queue grows. A queue is a buffer that buys time, not capacity. If the overload is a short spike, the buffer smooths it out. If it is sustained, the queue grows without bound, latency climbs (every new message waits behind the whole backlog), memory or disk fills, and eventually something breaks. Backpressure is the mechanism by which a slow consumer pushes back on the producer so the system stays stable, as in systems chapter 14. The options are the familiar four:

  • Bound the queue and block or reject when it is full, so the pressure reaches the producer (and, ultimately, the user: "try again shortly").
  • Shed load deliberately: drop or defer the least important work, instead of letting everything get slow.
  • Scale consumers, if the work can be done in parallel and the downstream resource can take it.
  • Let consumers pull at their own rate (log-based systems work this way), so a slow consumer falls behind without hurting others. Falling behind is then a number you can monitor (consumer lag).

The hazard to remember: an unbounded queue never says "no", so the first symptom of overload is not an error but an ever-growing delay. Monitor queue depth and the age of the oldest message.

Choreography and orchestration

When one business process spans several services, someone must make sure the steps happen in order and failures are handled. There are two basic ways to arrange it, which we meet properly in chapters 14 and 15.

  • Orchestration has a central coordinator (a workflow engine, or just a service) that calls each step, tracks progress and decides what to do on failure. The flow is explicit and easy to find, at the price of a component that knows about everyone, and can become a god-service.
  • Choreography has no coordinator: each service reacts to events and may emit new ones. It is loosely coupled and easy to extend, but the overall flow exists only as a pattern of subscriptions, which becomes hard to see, test and change as it grows.

Neither is better in general. Orchestration tends to suit workflows with many steps, strict ordering and complicated compensation; choreography suits simple reactions to facts, where parties are genuinely independent. Chapters 14 and 15 compare their costs honestly.

Two habits help more than any table. First, ask of every call: if the other side is down for ten minutes, what should happen to this user? If the honest answer is "wait", you are in queue territory; if it is "show an error", synchronous is fine. Second, mix styles at the seam where each fits. A common shape is a synchronous request to accept and validate an order, followed by asynchronous events for everything that follows.

Try it yourself: break the demo on purpose

Save deadline.py and run it with python3 deadline.py (nothing to install). Your output should match the one above exactly, because the clock is simulated.

  1. Change the budget from 500 to 700. Which of the three inventory times now succeed? Compute by hand first: orders 30 + inventory + payments 120.
  2. Make call raise immediately when work_ms > remaining instead of sleeping to the deadline. How does the failure time change? What did you save?
  3. Make the outage last for ticks 2 to 50. What happens to orders 2 and 3? What would a real system do about them after, say, 10 minutes? (Think: maximum message age, dead-letter queue, notifying the user.)
  4. Give the queue a maximum size of 2 with queue.Queue(maxsize=2) and use put_nowait. What happens when the third order arrives? That error is backpressure, delivered to the producer.

Distributed systems connection

Synchronous calls inherit every problem of networking chapter 16: a timeout leaves you not knowing whether the callee acted, so retries need idempotency. Asynchronous messaging inherits different ones: delivery guarantees (at-most-once, at-least-once, "exactly-once" achieved by idempotent consumers), ordering, and consistency lag. These are the same trade-offs as replication in networking chapter 18 seen from the application level. A queue is also a replicated, durable log under the hood, which is why the same data-systems ideas (offsets, consumer groups, compaction) from data chapter 19 show up again. The Distributed Systems course later returns to ordering, time and agreement properly.

Key takeaways

  • Synchronous request/response is simple and immediate, but it creates temporal coupling: both sides must be up together, latency and failures cascade along the chain.
  • Asynchronous messaging (queues or logs) decouples in time, levels load and fans out cheaply. In exchange you get eventual consistency, no immediate answer, harder tracing, and duplicates and reordering to handle.
  • Commands ask one receiver to act, events announce facts to any listener, queries ask for information. Events reverse the dependency, which helps decoupling and hurts traceability.
  • Request/reply over messaging (correlation id plus reply-to) gives you a buffered but still synchronous-feeling interaction. Use it deliberately.
  • Set one absolute deadline at the edge and propagate it, so each hop's timeout is the time remaining. gRPC deadlines work this way. Independent per-hop timeouts waste work and feed retry storms.
  • Queues are buffers, not capacity. Bound them, apply backpressure or shed load, and monitor depth and message age.
  • Orchestration puts the flow in one place; choreography spreads it across subscriptions. Chapters 14 and 15 compare them in depth.
  • Most systems use both styles. Ask: if the other side is down for ten minutes, what should this user see?

Check your understanding

What does "temporal coupling" mean in synchronous communication?

  1. The two services must use the same time zone
  2. The two services must be written in the same language
  3. Caller and callee must both be available at the same time for the request to succeed
  4. Calls must be made in alphabetical order
Temporal coupling is a dependency on availability at the same moment. A queue removes it: the receiver can be down when the message is sent and process it later.

A shopper pays by card, and your page must say "payment accepted" or "declined" before moving on. Which style fits that interaction best?

  1. Synchronous request/response, because the result decides the next step and the user is waiting
  2. Asynchronous events only, because they are more scalable
  3. A fire-and-forget email
  4. A nightly batch job
When the outcome is needed immediately, asynchronous messaging only adds machinery to wait for the answer. A common compromise is a synchronous authorisation followed by asynchronous events for everything that comes after.

The gateway gives each downstream call a 500 ms timeout, and every service in a three-hop chain does the same. What goes wrong?

  1. Nothing; this is best practice
  2. The calls become asynchronous
  3. Messages are delivered twice
  4. Inner calls can keep running after the outer caller has given up, wasting work and encouraging retry storms
Per-hop timeouts do not know how much of the user's time budget is already spent. Propagating one absolute deadline lets each hop compute what remains and stop together.

Which statement about commands and events is most accurate?

  1. Both are addressed to one specific receiver
  2. A command asks a particular receiver to do something and may be rejected; an event states that something already happened and any number of parties may react
  3. An event can be rejected by its receiver, a command cannot
  4. They are different names for the same thing
Commands are imperative and addressed; events are past-tense facts with no particular audience. Mixing them up leads to events that secretly require a specific reaction, which is a command in disguise.

A queue between Orders and Inventory is growing steadily because Inventory is slower than the order rate. What is the most important consequence if nothing changes?

  1. Nothing: queues are unlimited, so it is fine
  2. Orders will start returning HTTP errors immediately
  3. Delay keeps growing, because each new message waits behind the whole backlog, until memory, disk or message age limits are hit
  4. Inventory will speed up to match
A queue buys time, not capacity. Sustained overload shows up first as ever-growing latency, not an error. That is why you bound queues, apply backpressure or shed load, and watch queue depth and the age of the oldest message.
