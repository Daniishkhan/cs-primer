Where Next: On to Distributed Systems

You started this course with a tangle of code and finished it with a vocabulary for taking one apart, drawing boundaries, connecting the pieces and keeping them alive. This last page is a map of what you now have, a checklist to test yourself against, and a pointer to the hard problems waiting in the next course.

Why this matters

Architecture is learned by doing, not by reading. Knowing which skills you have, and which are still shaky, tells you what to practise next. And knowing exactly where this course stops (at the edge of the problems that only appear when many machines must agree) tells you why the next one exists.

The map of the course

The seven parts build on each other. First came the problem (complexity), then the tools for taming it inside one codebase, then the contracts between codebases, then splitting into services, then making them cooperate through events and sagas, and finally keeping the whole thing running and changing it safely.

What you can now design and explain

Use this as a self-test. For each line, try to explain it aloud to a colleague, with a small diagram, in two minutes. Where you stumble, the chapter is one click away.

  • Say why software rots, and spot change amplification and cognitive load in real code: chapter 1.
  • Judge a module by its coupling, cohesion and depth, and hide information behind a small interface: chapter 2 and chapter 3.
  • Apply (and refuse to over-apply) the patterns that earn their keep: chapter 4.
  • Keep business rules independent of the database and the web with ports and adapters: chapter 5.
  • Find bounded contexts and model aggregates with a shared language: chapter 6.
  • Make a design testable with seams and fakes: chapter 7, and handle configuration, logging and security consistently: chapter 8.
  • Design an HTTP API with idempotency keys, pagination and good errors: chapter 9, and change it without breaking clients: chapter 10.
  • Argue honestly for a monolith, a modular monolith or services, including Conway's law: chapter 11.
  • Draw service boundaries along data ownership and avoid the distributed monolith: chapter 12.
  • Choose between synchronous calls and messages for each interaction: chapter 13.
  • Use events well, and see choreography's hidden costs: chapter 14.
  • Run a business transaction across services with a saga and compensations: chapter 15.
  • Explain when CQRS and event sourcing pay off, and when they do not: chapter 16.
  • Protect a system from slow and failing dependencies with timeouts, breakers and bulkheads: chapter 17.
  • Migrate a live system safely with strangler fig, flags and expand/contract: chapter 18.
  • Record decisions, draw C4 diagrams and weigh trade-offs: chapter 19.

If you can do most of these, you are ready for system-design interviews and, more importantly, for design reviews at work. Do the two studios as a final check: they ask you to combine many of these ideas on a single open-ended problem.

Common misconception

"Now I know the patterns, I should use them." The honest lesson of the whole course is the opposite: every pattern is a trade, and the skill is knowing when not to pay. The best architecture decision on many projects is the boring one, taken early, recorded in an ADR, and left alone.

What comes next: Distributed Systems, Deep

Throughout this course you have met problems that we described and then handed off: "this needs a distributed transaction", "the leader might be wrong", "events may arrive out of order". The next course, Distributed Systems, Deep (planned, not yet written), takes those on directly. It will cover:

  • Consensus in depth: how groups of machines agree on one value despite crashes and delays, beyond the sketch in the networking course.
  • Distributed transactions: two-phase commit, its blocking problem, and the alternatives, the rigorous cousin of the sagas you built in Lab 4.
  • CRDTs: data types designed so replicas can be updated independently and still converge without coordination.
  • Testing distributed systems: fault-injection checkers in the style of Jepsen, and formal specification with TLA+, for finding the bugs that only appear in rare interleavings.

Analogy: from city planning to air-traffic control

This course was about planning a city: neighbourhoods, roads, zoning, building codes. The next is about air-traffic control: many independent actors, no shared clock, messages that vanish, and the requirement that nothing collides anyway. Planning gets you a layout; control gets you safety while everything moves.

Where it breaks down: an air-traffic controller can look at one radar. A distributed system has no single place from which the whole state can be seen at a single instant.

Further reading

Each of these is a book or site that goes much deeper than a chapter can. They disagree with each other in places, which is a feature.

  • John Ousterhout, A Philosophy of Software Design (2018): short and opinionated on deep modules and complexity; the backbone of chapters 1 to 3.
  • Eric Evans, Domain-Driven Design (2003): the original. Vaughn Vernon's Implementing Domain-Driven Design is the more practical companion.
  • Sam Newman, Building Microservices: a balanced guide to splitting systems, including when not to.
  • Michael Nygard, Release It!: stability patterns such as circuit breakers and bulkheads, taught through production failures. Nygard also proposed ADRs.
  • Chris Richardson, Microservices Patterns: sagas, the outbox, CQRS and API composition, with worked examples.
  • Martin Fowler's bliki (martinfowler.com/bliki): hundreds of short, careful essays on patterns, including the strangler fig.
  • Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais, Team Topologies: how team structure and architecture shape each other, the practical follow-up to Conway's law.

Keep practising

Reading gets you a vocabulary; building gets you judgement. The course has three kinds of practice, and they train different things:

  • Labs: write the thing yourself. Start with Lab 1, refactor to hexagonal, then work through the API lab, module boundaries, a saga orchestrator, event sourcing and a resilience toolkit.
  • Case studies: see the trade-offs play out in real systems, such as Prime Video's return to a monolith, Shopify's modular monolith and Knight Capital.
  • Studios: open-ended design problems with a worked answer to compare against. Food delivery's service boundaries and a notification service both exercise the whole course.

Key takeaways

  • The course moved from one codebase (complexity, modules, structure) to contracts, to services, to events and sagas, to resilience and change.
  • Every idea is a trade-off. Knowing when not to apply a pattern is the senior skill.
  • The unresolved problems (consensus, distributed transactions, convergence without coordination, testing under failure) are the subject of Distributed Systems, Deep.
  • Practise with the labs, cases and studios, and write your decisions down.

Check your understanding

A team has eight engineers, one product and no clear boundaries yet. Based on this course, what is the most defensible starting architecture?

  1. Microservices, one per noun in the database
  2. A modular monolith with enforced module boundaries, reviewed as the team and product grow
  3. Serverless functions for every endpoint
  4. Whatever is fashionable, and change later
Many experienced engineers argue for this when boundaries are still being discovered: it keeps change cheap and avoids paying the distributed-systems tax before it buys anything. Record the reasoning in an ADR with a trigger for revisiting it.

Which problem does a saga address?

  1. Making a single database query faster
  2. Hiding a library behind an interface
  3. Versioning a public API
  4. Completing a business transaction that spans several services when there is no shared database transaction
A saga replaces one atomic transaction with a sequence of local transactions plus compensating actions that undo earlier steps if a later one fails (chapter 15).

Why can a retried request after a timeout be dangerous, and what makes it safe?

  1. The caller cannot tell whether the first attempt succeeded, so the operation must be idempotent, for example via an idempotency key
  2. Retries are always safe because networks are reliable
  3. It is dangerous only for GET requests
  4. It is safe only if you wait exactly one second
A timeout means "unknown", not "failed". Without idempotency the retry may repeat a charge or an order. This idea runs through API design, sagas and resilience, and returns in the distributed systems course.
