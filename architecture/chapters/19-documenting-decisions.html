Documenting & Deciding

The code shows what the system does. It almost never shows why it is built that way, what else was considered, or what would make you change your mind. This chapter covers the small set of habits that keep that knowledge alive (decision records, design documents and diagrams that scale), plus the thinking tools for making the decision in the first place and the way senior engineers present it.

Why this matters

A design is a pile of decisions, and a decision made without a record gets made again. Someone new asks "why on earth do we use X?", nobody remembers, and the team either spends a week re-arguing it or, worse, quietly rips out something that was there for a good reason. Writing decisions down is cheap, and being able to explain a design clearly is the skill that separates "I can build it" from "I can lead it". Everything in the earlier chapters (boundaries, contracts, sagas, resilience) turns into something other people can use only when it is documented and defended well.

Why decisions get lost

Teams lose their reasoning in predictable ways. The people who decided move on. The discussion happened in a chat thread or a meeting nobody recorded. The pull request says what changed but not what was rejected. The wiki page describes the system as it was two years ago and nobody dares delete it. After a while all that remains is the outcome, which looks arbitrary, and arbitrary things attract "cleanup".

The cost shows up as re-litigation: reopening a settled question because the context is gone. Some of it is healthy (the world did change). Much of it is waste, and it has an uglier cousin, Chesterton's fence: the advice not to remove a fence until you know why it was put there. A record of "we chose a message queue here because the payment provider is down for maintenance every Sunday night" is exactly the thing that stops a well-meaning engineer from replacing the queue with a direct call.

Analogy: a city's planning records

A city does not keep only the streets; it keeps the planning records: why this road was widened, why that district was zoned for housing, which options the council rejected. Without them, every new council re-debates the same bypass. A decision record is the zoning minute for your system: short, dated, and attached to the neighbourhood it concerns.

Where it breaks down: a city's records are legally binding. Yours are advisory, so they only work if the team actually reads them, which means keeping them short and close to the code.

Architecture decision records

The lightest-weight fix is the architecture decision record (ADR), proposed by Michael Nygard in 2011: one short document per significant decision, kept in the repository. The classic template is just a title and four sections:

  • Title: a short noun phrase with a number, such as "ADR 7: Use a modular monolith with Postgres".
  • Status: proposed, accepted, deprecated, or superseded by another ADR.
  • Context: the forces at play: the requirements, constraints, team size, deadlines and what we know and do not know. Written neutrally, as facts.
  • Decision: what we will do, in full sentences, starting "We will…".
  • Consequences: what becomes easier, what becomes harder, what we now have to do. Include the bad news.

Four habits make ADRs work, and they matter more than the template:

  1. Short. One page. If it needs ten pages it is a design doc (next section), and the ADR links to it.
  2. One decision each. "We use Postgres" and "we deploy on Kubernetes" are two records, because they will be revisited at different times.
  3. Immutable. You never rewrite an accepted ADR to match what you now think. History that gets edited stops being history.
  4. Superseded, not deleted. When circumstances change, write a new ADR that says "this replaces ADR 7", and change only the old one's status line to "superseded by ADR 12". The trail of how thinking evolved stays in the folder.

A complete example

Here is a realistic ADR for the kind of decision chapter 11 argued most teams should make. It lives in docs/adr/0007-modular-monolith-postgres.md.

# 7. Use a modular monolith with Postgres

Date: 2026-03-04
Status: Accepted

## Context
We are building an online ordering product. One team of 8 engineers,
growing to perhaps 20 within a year. Peak load is estimated at a few
hundred requests per second. The domain has clear areas (catalog,
orders, payments, notifications) but we do not yet know where the
stable boundaries are; the product is still changing weekly.
Operating many services would take time we do not have, and we have
no on-call rota yet.
Constraints: the team knows Postgres well; orders and payments need
strong consistency; we must launch in about four months.

## Decision
We will build one deployable application, organised as modules with
explicit public interfaces and no cross-module imports of internals
or access to another module's tables. We will use a single Postgres
database, one schema per module. An import-rule test in CI will
enforce the boundaries. Slow work (emails, exports) goes through a
queue consumed by a worker running the same codebase.

## Alternatives considered
- Microservices from day one: independent deploys, but a network
  hop and a distributed-data problem on every feature, before we
  know the boundaries.
- A single unstructured monolith: fastest start, but we expect it
  to become hard to split or even to change.

## Consequences
+ One deploy, one database transaction across modules, simple local
  development and debugging.
+ Boundaries are cheap to move while we are still learning them.
- One scaling unit: a hot module cannot scale on its own, and a bad
  deploy affects everything.
- Boundaries are held up by discipline and tests, not by the network;
  we must keep the CI check green.
- We will need to revisit this if a module needs independent scaling
  or if more than ~4 teams are blocked on shared deploys.
  Review date: 2027-03.

Notice what it does well: the context states facts and constraints rather than arguing; the alternatives show the decision was actually a decision; the consequences include the costs; and it names what would make the team revisit (a trigger) and when to check. A reader in two years can disagree with it, but cannot say they do not know why it was made. A command-line helper called adr-tools can create and number these files for you; it is described in the toolbox entry on ADRs, and a text editor works just as well.

Common misconception

"We should write an ADR for every decision." No. Reserve them for decisions that are expensive to reverse, affect several teams, or that someone will plausibly ask about later: the database, the integration style, a framework, a boundary. A record per variable name drowns the useful ones. A good test: would a new hire want to know why?

Design docs and RFCs

An ADR records a decision; a design doc (sometimes called an RFC, "request for comments") is how you reach a big one. It is a written proposal circulated for feedback before the work starts. Writing forces you to find the holes in your own thinking, and review catches problems while they are still cheap to fix: a paragraph, not a rewrite.

Formats vary between organisations, but a good design doc usually contains:

  • Context and problem: what is wrong or missing, for whom, and why now.
  • Goals: what success looks like, ideally measurable ("p99 under 300 ms at 500 requests/s").
  • Non-goals: what this deliberately does not try to do. This is the most underrated section, because it fences the scope and heads off arguments.
  • The proposal: the design itself, with a diagram, the main data model and the important flows.
  • Alternatives considered: the other serious options, with the reasons they lost. A doc with no alternatives is a decision already made, not a proposal.
  • Risks and open questions: what could go wrong, what you do not know, how you will find out.
  • Rollout and migration, testing, and sometimes cost and security notes (chapter 18 covers how to roll out safely).

The review culture around the doc matters as much as the template. Healthy ones share a few traits: reviewers comment on the design, not the author; objections come with reasons or alternatives; there is a stated deadline and a named person who makes the call, so review cannot go on forever; and "I disagree but commit" is acceptable once a decision is made. Unhealthy ones turn into design by committee, where every reviewer's pet concern gets absorbed and the result pleases nobody. Keep docs proportionate: a day of work does not need a six-page doc, and a quarter of work does.

The two documents work together: the design doc is the argument, the ADR is the verdict. When the doc is approved, the key decisions it contained are distilled into ADRs that outlive the doc.

The C4 model: diagrams at four zoom levels

Architecture diagrams usually fail in one of two ways: a single enormous picture that mixes everything, or boxes-and-arrows with no stated meaning. Simon Brown's C4 model fixes both by treating diagrams like maps with zoom levels. You do not use one map for both planning a flight and finding a door; you zoom. C4 names four levels, each answering a different question for a different audience:

LevelShowsAudience
1. System contextYour system as one box, the people who use it, and the other systems it talks to.Everyone, including non-technical people.
2. ContainersThe separately running pieces inside: web app, API, database, queue, worker. (A "container" here means a deployable or runnable thing, not necessarily a Docker container.)Developers, operations.
3. ComponentsThe major building blocks inside one container: modules, ports and adapters.Developers working on that container.
4. CodeClasses and functions. Usually generated from the code, or skipped.Whoever is in the file.

Analogy: a map app

Open a map and you start with the country, zoom to the city, then the street, then the building's floor plan. Each zoom drops detail that no longer helps and adds detail that does. The mistake C4 prevents is the map that shows every door handle at country scale.

Where it breaks down: a real map is drawn once from the territory. Your diagrams drift from the system the moment someone forgets to update them, which is why keeping them as code, close to the repository, helps.

Diagrams as code

Pictures drawn in a GUI tool go stale because nobody wants to open the tool. Diagrams as code stores the diagram as text in the repository, so it is reviewed in the same pull request as the change and diffed like any file. Mermaid is a popular text format that many Markdown renderers, wikis and code hosts display as a picture; see the toolbox entry on Mermaid. The level 2 diagram above looks like this in Mermaid (the syntax is shown, not run, since rendering needs a Mermaid-aware viewer):

Mermaid also has C4-flavoured syntax, and other tools (Structurizr, PlantUML) exist for the same job. The tool matters much less than the rule: every box and arrow has a label, and the diagram says which C4 level it is. A diagram where a box might be a team, a database or a deployment is not telling you much.

Trade-off analysis

Deciding is choosing between things that are each good at something. The honest way to do it starts by naming quality attributes (also called non-functional requirements or "-ilities"), the properties other than "does the feature work":

  • Performance: latency and throughput.
  • Availability: how much of the time it works, and how it degrades (chapter 17).
  • Modifiability: how cheaply it can change (chapter 1, 2).
  • Security, testability (chapter 7), operability, and cost, both money and people.

These pull against each other. Caching improves performance and hurts consistency. Splitting into services improves team autonomy and hurts latency, simplicity and debuggability (chapter 11). There is no best architecture, only the best one for the attributes that matter most here. So step one is to rank them for this system. A payments ledger ranks correctness first; a news site ranks availability and speed.

Formal methods exist for this. The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM), from the Software Engineering Institute, runs a structured workshop that turns attributes into concrete scenarios, often organised as a utility tree (a tree whose root is "utility" and whose leaves are specific scenarios such as "survive loss of one data centre with under a minute of downtime"), ranked by importance and difficulty. You are unlikely to run a full ATAM, but the idea is worth borrowing: make the attributes concrete as scenarios with numbers, and rank them.

The weighted decision matrix

A weighted decision matrix is a simple tool for comparing options: list the criteria, give each a weight (summing to 1), score each option against each criterion (say 1 to 5), then multiply and add. For a team choosing a structure:

CriterionWeightModular monolithMicroservicesServerless
Simplicity0.25513
Scalability0.25354
Team autonomy0.30253
Cost0.20523

The matrix is good at forcing you to say what you value and to compare options on the same terms. Its pitfalls are real, though:

  • False precision. The scores are opinions. A total of 3.60 against 3.40 is not a result; it is two opinions summed.
  • The weights are the decision. Whoever sets the weights picks the winner. People often reverse-engineer the weights to justify the option they already liked.
  • Criteria are not independent. "Simplicity" and "cost" overlap, so you may be counting the same thing twice.
  • It hides deal-breakers. An option that fails a hard requirement (it cannot meet the legal data-residency rule) should be eliminated first, not averaged away.
  • It ignores reversibility and risk, both of which come below.

The best defence is a sensitivity check: nudge each weight up and down and see whether the winner changes. If it does, the matrix is telling you the options are close and the real argument is about the weights. If it does not, you have a robust choice. This tiny program does exactly that:

from itertools import product

# Scores 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) for each option against each criterion.
OPTIONS = {
    "Modular monolith": {"simplicity": 5, "scalability": 3, "team autonomy": 2, "cost": 5},
    "Microservices":    {"simplicity": 1, "scalability": 5, "team autonomy": 5, "cost": 2},
    "Serverless":       {"simplicity": 3, "scalability": 4, "team autonomy": 3, "cost": 3},
}
WEIGHTS = {"simplicity": 0.25, "scalability": 0.25, "team autonomy": 0.30, "cost": 0.20}


def totals(weights):
    return {name: sum(weights[c] * s for c, s in scores.items())
            for name, scores in OPTIONS.items()}


def winner(weights):
    t = totals(weights)
    return max(t, key=t.get)


def sensitivity(weights, step=0.10):
    """Nudge each weight up and down by `step`, renormalise, and report any flip."""
    base = winner(weights)
    flips = []
    for crit, delta in product(weights, (+step, -step)):
        w = dict(weights)
        w[crit] = max(0.0, w[crit] + delta)
        total = sum(w.values())
        w = {k: v / total for k, v in w.items()}
        if winner(w) != base:
            flips.append((crit, delta, winner(w)))
    return base, flips


for name, t in sorted(totals(WEIGHTS).items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    print(f"{name:18} {t:.2f}")
base, flips = sensitivity(WEIGHTS)
print("winner:", base)
if not flips:
    print("robust: no +/-0.10 weight change flips the winner")
for crit, delta, new in flips:
    print(f"fragile: {crit} {delta:+.2f} -> {new} wins")
Modular monolith   3.60
Microservices      3.40
Serverless         3.25
winner: Modular monolith
fragile: simplicity -0.10 -> Microservices wins
fragile: team autonomy +0.10 -> Microservices wins
fragile: cost -0.10 -> Microservices wins

The monolith wins, but three small nudges to the weights hand victory to microservices. That is the useful output: not "monolith", but "this is close, and it hinges on how much we value simplicity and autonomy right now". The next step is a conversation about those weights, with evidence (how many teams? how often do deploys collide?), not a bigger spreadsheet.

One-way and two-way doors

Not all decisions deserve the same care. The "one-way door versus two-way door" framing, popularised by Amazon and Jeff Bezos, sorts decisions by how hard they are to undo. A two-way door can be walked back through if it turns out wrong: choose quickly, learn, adjust. A one-way door is costly or impossible to reverse: slow down, gather evidence, write the ADR. Most decisions are two-way doors, and treating them all as one-way is a classic way for a team to become slow.

Two practical tricks move decisions towards the easy end. Hide the choice behind an interface (ports and adapters, chapter 5) so that swapping it later is a local change. And defer: decide at the last responsible moment, when you have the most information, as long as waiting is not itself expensive. Deferring is not procrastinating if you say what you are waiting to learn.

Fitness functions: decisions that defend themselves

A documented decision still erodes if nothing stops it. "The domain layer must not import the database" is an ADR on Monday and a violation by Friday. In Building Evolutionary Architectures, Neal Ford, Rebecca Parsons and Patrick Kua call the answer a fitness function: an automated check that measures how well the system meets some architectural property, run on every change. The property can be structural (no import cycles, layering respected), operational (p99 latency under a budget in a load test) or about quality (no dependency with a known severe vulnerability).

The simplest, and often most valuable, is the import rule. Using Python's ast module we can read the imports of each module without running it and fail the build if a forbidden one appears. Here a tiny in-memory "project" has one deliberate violation, so you can see the failure message:

import ast, unittest

# A fitness function: "domain code must not import adapters or web frameworks".
# Here the 'project' is a dict of module name -> source so the test is self-contained.
PROJECT = {
    "domain.pricing": "from domain.money import Money\nimport math\n",
    "domain.orders": "from adapters.postgres import save\n",   # a violation
    "adapters.postgres": "from domain.orders import Order\nimport sqlite3\n",
}
FORBIDDEN = {"domain": ("adapters", "flask", "sqlalchemy")}


def imports(source):
    for node in ast.walk(ast.parse(source)):
        if isinstance(node, ast.Import):
            yield from (a.name for a in node.names)
        elif isinstance(node, ast.ImportFrom) and node.module:
            yield node.module


def violations(project):
    found = []
    for module, source in project.items():
        layer = module.split(".")[0]
        for imp in imports(source):
            if imp.split(".")[0] in FORBIDDEN.get(layer, ()):
                found.append(f"{module} imports {imp}")
    return sorted(found)


class DependencyRule(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_domain_does_not_import_adapters(self):
        self.assertEqual(violations(PROJECT), [], "layering broken")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    unittest.main()

How senior engineers communicate a design

A design that nobody understands does not get adopted, whatever its merits. The pattern strong engineers follow, in docs and in meetings, is consistent:

  1. Lead with the problem and constraints, not the solution. If the audience does not share your picture of the problem, your solution looks like arbitrary taste. "We have 8 engineers, deploys collide twice a week, and a launch in four months" makes the answer feel obvious.
  2. Show alternatives. Present at least two real options and why each is plausible. This builds trust, because it shows you weighed things rather than defended a favourite, and it invites better ideas.
  3. State the trade-offs explicitly. Say what you are giving up. "This costs us independent scaling; we accept that because…" People trust a design more when its owner volunteers the downsides.
  4. Name the risks and how you would find out. "The riskiest assumption is that Postgres handles our write rate; we will load-test it in week two before building more." That turns fear into a plan.
  5. Pitch to the audience. An executive needs the outcome and cost; a teammate needs the flows and the data model. Use C4 levels: context for the former, containers and components for the latter.
  6. Say what would change your mind. A decision with a stated trigger (the review date in the ADR above) is easier to agree to, because it is not a trap.

Notice that these are the same ingredients as an ADR and a design doc. The artefacts and the speaking habits reinforce each other: if you can fill in the template, you can explain it out loud.

Try it yourself: write and stress-test a decision

Save matrix.py and run it with python3 matrix.py; the output should match the one shown. Then change things one at a time.

  1. Move 0.15 of weight from "simplicity" to "scalability". Does the winner change? What does that say about how firmly the original result was held?
  2. Add a fourth option, "Monolith plus one extracted service", and score it yourself. Defend each score in a sentence. Did writing the justification change any number?
  3. Pick a real decision from a project you know and write a one-page ADR using the template above. Include one alternative and one negative consequence.
  4. Save fit.py, run it, then fix the violation so it passes. What would you have to change in the design to remove the dependency, not just the import? (Hint: chapter 5.)

Distributed systems connection

Distributed systems multiply the cost of getting a decision wrong, because the choices (consistency model, partitioning key, replication scheme) are woven through every service and rarely reversible once data exists. That makes trade-off analysis and written records matter more, not less. Many of the choices you will face next are classic trade-offs with known names, such as consistency against availability under partition (networking chapter 18) and latency against durability (data chapter 15). Write the trade-off down while you remember it.

Key takeaways

  • Decisions without records get re-litigated or silently undone. Capture the context, the options and the consequences while they are fresh.
  • An ADR is short, covers one decision, is never edited after acceptance, and is superseded by a new record rather than rewritten.
  • A design doc is the argument (goals, non-goals, alternatives, risks); the ADR is the verdict. Healthy review has a deadline and a named decider.
  • The C4 model gives four zoom levels (context, containers, components, code). Label every box and arrow, and keep diagrams as text so they live with the code.
  • Rank quality attributes for this system first. A weighted matrix helps you compare, but the weights are the decision, so always run a sensitivity check.
  • Match care to reversibility: decide two-way doors quickly, and slow down for one-way doors. Hide choices behind interfaces to turn the latter into the former.
  • Fitness functions turn architectural decisions into automated tests so they survive the team's turnover.
  • Explain a design by leading with the problem and constraints, then alternatives, trade-offs and risks.

Check your understanding

A team realises ADR 7 ("use a single Postgres database") is no longer right. What is the best way to record that?

  1. Edit ADR 7 so it describes the new decision
  2. Delete ADR 7 so nobody is confused
  3. Write a new ADR that replaces it, and change only ADR 7's status to "superseded by" the new one
  4. Add a comment in the code and leave the ADRs alone
ADRs are history. The old record still explains why the first choice was reasonable in its context, which is valuable when you later ask whether to change back. Editing or deleting it throws away exactly the reasoning the practice exists to keep.

Which of these belongs in a design doc's "non-goals" section?

  1. Things the project deliberately will not try to do, to fence the scope
  2. Features that failed in testing
  3. Goals the team has not decided on yet
  4. Reasons the existing design was bad
Non-goals are explicit scope boundaries ("this does not handle multi-currency"). They head off reviewers asking for everything and make the real goals sharper.

In the C4 model, which level would you show a non-technical stakeholder to explain which outside systems yours depends on?

  1. Level 4, code
  2. Level 3, components
  3. Level 2, containers
  4. Level 1, system context
The context diagram shows your system as one box with its users and neighbouring systems and no internal technology, so anyone can read it. Containers and components are for people building or operating the system.

A weighted decision matrix gives option A 3.6 and option B 3.4. Nudging one weight by 0.1 makes B the winner. What is the most reasonable conclusion?

  1. Option A is the clear winner, since it scored highest
  2. The options are close, so the real discussion is about the weights and the evidence behind them
  3. The matrix is broken and should be discarded
  4. Option B is better, because the result can change
A sensitivity check reveals how fragile a result is. A flip means the scores and weights are opinions that matter, so argue about them with data. The matrix is not useless; it located the disagreement.

What is a fitness function in the sense of Building Evolutionary Architectures?

  1. A function that measures how fast each developer commits
  2. A formula that assigns each service a score out of ten at the yearly review
  3. An automated check, run continuously, that guards an architectural property such as a layering rule or a latency budget
  4. A diagram generated from the code
Fitness functions turn architectural intent into executable checks (an import-rule test, a performance test in CI), so the property is protected on every change instead of relying on memory and review.
