Drawing Service Boundaries

Whether you are carving a monolith into modules or into services, the hard part is the same: deciding where the lines go. Draw them well and teams move independently. Draw them badly and every change crosses three of them. This chapter gives you a way to find good lines (business capabilities, data ownership and change patterns), a way to measure whether you found them, and the traps to avoid.

Why this matters

Almost every painful microservices story is a boundary story. The technology (HTTP, containers, queues) is the easy part and is mostly interchangeable. The boundaries are what you live with for years: they decide which teams must talk, which changes are cheap, which data you can join and which failures spread. A boundary is also the thing that is most expensive to move once other teams and databases depend on it. So it is worth spending real design effort here, and worth knowing how to check your work with evidence rather than opinion.

Boundaries follow the business, not the technology

The most common first instinct is to split by technical layer: a "UI service", a "business logic service", a "database service". It feels tidy, and it is almost always wrong, for the reason you saw in the Conway's-law diagram in chapter 11: nearly every feature then needs all three layers to change at once. The better instinct, from domain-driven design (Eric Evans, 2003; chapter 6), is to cut along bounded contexts: areas of the business where a particular model and vocabulary hold. "Product" means one thing to the catalog (title, description, images), another to shipping (weight, dimensions), another to billing (tax category, price). Each context gets its own model of the thing instead of sharing one giant, compromise "Product".

A related lens is the business capability: something the business does that creates value ("take payments", "ship parcels", "recommend products"). Capabilities tend to be stable even as the technology and org charts around them change, which makes them good boundaries. A quick test: can you name the service as a verb phrase or a noun that a business person would recognise? "Payments" and "Fulfilment" pass. "Utility service" and "Common" do not.

Analogy: a restaurant chain

Picture a restaurant chain. The kitchen, the front of house and the suppliers each speak their own language: the kitchen talks about stations and prep, the front of house about tables and covers, the suppliers about cases and delivery windows. Each has its own records and its own boss. The same "order" means a ticket of dishes to the kitchen, a bill to the front of house, and a stock requisition to the supplier. They coordinate by passing order tickets (events: "table 4 ordered two pastas") instead of reaching into each other's work. Nobody in the kitchen edits the front-of-house cash book.

Where it breaks down: in a real restaurant the people can see and shout across the pass, and a human absorbs ambiguity ("did you mean the large one?"). Between services, a ticket is all you have, and a ticket that is lost, duplicated or out of order does exactly what the bytes say.

The anti-pattern: entity services

Splitting by nouns from the database schema produces entity services: a CustomerService, an OrderService, a ProductService, each a thin create-read-update-delete wrapper around one table. It looks like data ownership, but it moves business logic out to whoever calls them. Placing an order then needs the orchestrating code to call Customer, Product, Inventory, Pricing and Order services and enforce all the rules itself, so the real logic ends up scattered through the callers, and every service is a dependency of everything. The CustomerService that "everybody calls" is not a capability. It is a shared database with an HTTP front, the worst of both worlds.

Compare what each design must do to apply a loyalty discount at checkout:

  • Entity services: the checkout code fetches the customer, then the order history, then the product categories, then computes the discount itself. Any change to loyalty rules touches the checkout code and probably two other callers.
  • Capability services: a Pricing context owns the rules and the data they need. Checkout asks "what is the price for this basket and customer?" and gets an answer. The rule changes in one place.

Each service owns its data

A boundary is only as real as the data behind it. The defining rule is database-per-service (or schema-per-module in a modular monolith): only the owning service reads or writes its tables. Everyone else goes through its API or consumes its events. The reasons are the same as information hiding (Parnas, 1972): once another team reads your tables, you cannot change them, and your "independent" service has an invisible, undocumented API called "the schema". A shared table is a shared secret that every reader depends on.

Common misconception

"We have separate services, so we have separate data." Not if two of them read the same tables, even read-only, even "just for reporting", even through a replica. Count how many codebases would break if you renamed a column. If the answer is more than one, you have a shared database, and with it a distributed monolith (chapter 11).

How do I get data I do not own?

The practical objection to data ownership is "but I need the customer's address / the product's price / the order's status". There are three standard answers, each with a different cost.

ApproachHow it worksYou gainYou pay
Call the ownerAsk the owning service for the data when you need it (a synchronous request).Always current; simple; no copy to keep in sync.Temporal coupling: you fail when they are down (chapter 13); added latency on every use.
Replicate via eventsThe owner publishes change events; you keep your own read-only copy of the fields you need.Fast local reads; you keep working when the owner is down.Eventual consistency (data chapter 19): your copy can be stale, and you run a pipeline to maintain it.
API compositionFor queries that span services, a gateway or backend-for-frontend calls several owners and merges the results.No shared tables; reads stay current.Joins in application code; latency is the slowest call; no cross-service transactional view; partial results to handle.

None of these is free, and there is no fourth option where the problem disappears. A useful rule of thumb: replicate what you read often and can tolerate being slightly stale (a product's name), call for what must be current (an account's balance before a withdrawal), and compose for pages that assemble data from several places. Notice also that sometimes the data is not "theirs" at all. When an order is placed, the price at that moment is part of the order, so Ordering stores its own copy of the price as a fact about the order. That is not duplication to be eliminated. It is two different pieces of information that happened to start equal.

Cohesion and coupling, measured by change

High cohesion means the things inside a boundary change together and belong together. Low coupling means that things on opposite sides rarely need to change in the same breath (chapter 2). These are easy to claim and hard to feel, so measure them: look at your change history and ask which changes span boundaries. If a pair of services appears together in a large share of commits or pull requests, then in practice they are one unit of change that happens to be deployed twice, and every such change pays the coordination tax of a distributed system. Version control already holds the evidence. Here is a short script that takes past change sets (which modules each commit touched), counts how often each pair changed together, and suggests groups. The strength of a pair is the fraction of commits touching either that touch both (the Jaccard index).

from collections import Counter
from itertools import combinations

# Each commit = the set of modules it touched (past change sets).
commits = [
    {"cart", "pricing"}, {"cart", "pricing"}, {"cart", "pricing", "catalog"},
    {"catalog", "search"}, {"catalog", "search"}, {"catalog", "search"},
    {"orders", "payments"}, {"orders", "payments"}, {"orders", "payments", "shipping"},
    {"orders", "payments"}, {"shipping"}, {"shipping", "notifications"},
    {"notifications"}, {"notifications"}, {"cart"}, {"pricing"},
    {"orders"}, {"catalog"}, {"payments"}, {"orders", "shipping"},
]

solo = Counter()            # how often each module changed
pair = Counter()            # how often each pair changed together
for files in commits:
    solo.update(files)
    pair.update(combinations(sorted(files), 2))

def strength(a, b):
    """Jaccard: commits touching both / commits touching either."""
    both = pair[tuple(sorted((a, b)))]
    return both / (solo[a] + solo[b] - both)

print(f"{len(commits)} commits")
print("\nstrongest co-change pairs:")
for (a, b), n in sorted(pair.items(), key=lambda kv: -strength(*kv[0]))[:6]:
    print(f"  {a + ' + ' + b:<24} together {n}x  strength {strength(a, b):.2f}")

# Suggest groups: merge modules whose strength passes a threshold (union-find).
THRESHOLD = 0.5
parent = {m: m for m in solo}
def find(m):
    while parent[m] != m:
        parent[m] = parent[parent[m]]
        m = parent[m]
    return m
for (a, b) in pair:
    if strength(a, b) >= THRESHOLD:
        parent[find(a)] = find(b)

groups = {}
for m in solo:
    groups.setdefault(find(m), []).append(m)
print(f"\nsuggested groups (strength >= {THRESHOLD}):")
for g in sorted(groups.values()):
    print("  ", " + ".join(sorted(g)))

Read the result as a question-generator, not an answer. The sample data was written to be a bit awkward, and the output shows it:

  • cart + pricing and catalog + search change together at 0.60. Those are candidates for the same boundary (or at least the same team), and a split between them would pay a coordination tax on most of their changes.
  • orders + payments is also strong. That does not automatically mean they should merge. Payments may deserve a separate boundary for compliance, security or scaling reasons. But it does mean the interface between them is churning, and you should look at why: is the contract unstable, is data in the wrong place, or is one half missing a capability?
  • orders + shipping is weak but not zero (0.25). That is the healthy shape: occasional shared changes, mostly independent.

Real use needs care. Commit history is noisy (a rename or a formatter touches everything), so filter out mechanical commits, look at pull requests rather than raw commits, and compare against intent. The point is to replace "I feel these belong together" with a number that you then argue about.

Chatty boundaries and granularity

A chatty boundary is one where a single piece of work makes many small calls across it. It usually means the line is in the wrong place, or that data is not where it is used. Common smells:

  • N+1 across the network: to render a list of 50 orders, you call the product service 50 times. Fix it with a bulk call, a local replicated copy, or by moving the capability.
  • Ping-pong: service A calls B, which calls A back for a detail. They want to be one service, or the data B needs should travel with the request.
  • Anaemic services: every method is a getter or a setter. All the interesting behaviour lives in the callers.
  • Transaction envy: two services keep trying to keep each other's state consistent in lock-step. They share an invariant and probably belong together (chapter 6: an aggregate is a consistency boundary).

As for how big a service should be, there is no honest number of lines or people to quote. These heuristics work better:

  • One team can own, understand and operate it without needing other teams for routine changes (cognitive load, chapter 11).
  • It keeps its own invariants inside a single transaction. If a rule must hold atomically, both sides of the rule belong in the same boundary.
  • It changes for one reason and the changes mostly stay inside it (the co-change measurement).
  • There is a reason to deploy or scale it separately. If you cannot name one, keep it a module.
  • Merge before you split. When unsure, start with a larger boundary. Splitting a service later is easier than gluing two services (and their databases) together.

Worked example: an online shop

Let's apply the ideas. An e-commerce shop has six obvious capabilities. We assign four teams, and decide who owns what data and how the pieces talk.

Walk the flow of one purchase:

  1. Cart holds the shopper's basket. When the shopper views it, Cart asks Catalog for current names and prices (a synchronous call, because the shopper wants current prices). Catalog owns product data and nobody else writes it.
  2. On checkout, Cart sends a command to Ordering, including the prices the shopper saw. Ordering stores those as its own order-line prices, so later price changes never rewrite history. This is the "price at the time" case from earlier.
  3. Ordering asks Payments to authorise the charge (synchronous: the shopper is waiting and the answer decides the next step). Payments owns card tokens and ledger entries, and tells Ordering the outcome with a PaymentCaptured event.
  4. Once the order is confirmed, Ordering emits OrderPlaced. Shipping and Notifications subscribe independently; Ordering does not know they exist. Adding a fraud check or an analytics consumer later requires no change to Ordering.
  5. Shipping, once it dispatches, emits OrderShipped, which Notifications turns into an email.

Questions this design raises (the interesting part): Should Cart and Catalog really be separate (both belong to one team and the co-change script showed catalog and search moving together)? Should Notifications be a service at all, or a module in Shipping until it has a reason to scale on its own? Is the Ordering and Payments split worth it given how often they change together? There is no right answer in the abstract. The design above is a reasonable starting hypothesis, and you refine it with evidence. In a modular monolith the same picture works unchanged, with "service" read as "module", "event" as an in-process event, and "DB" as a schema.

Splitting an existing monolith

Most real work is not greenfield design but dividing something that exists. The usual approach has four moves.

  1. Find the seams. A seam is a place where behaviour can be separated with the least entanglement: a cluster of code that mostly calls itself, a table that few other tables join to, a feature with clear inputs and outputs. The import graph and the co-change analysis above are your maps (chapter 2).
  2. Make the seam explicit in the monolith first. Put the candidate behind an interface inside the monolith, move its callers onto that interface, and give it its own schema. If the boundary is wrong you will find out cheaply, with a refactor instead of a migration.
  3. Strangle incrementally. Route traffic through a façade that can send each request either to the old code or the new service, move one slice at a time, and retire the old path when it is empty. This is Martin Fowler's strangler fig pattern, and chapter 18 covers the mechanics (including feature flags and canary releases).
  4. Move the data. Here the literature gives two orders, and both can work:

Split the code first, data later

Extract the service but keep it reading the shared database for a while. Pro: quick first win; the service can be deployed and tested early; the risky data migration waits until the interface is stable. Con: you live with a shared database for a long time and many teams stop there. The result is a distributed monolith, and the hardest part (data) is left for last.

Split the data first, code later

Separate the schema inside the monolith, replace cross-table joins and transactions with calls, and only then move the code out. Pro: you meet the real difficulty (lost joins and transactions) early, when the code is still in one place and easy to change. Con: a long period of awkward code with nothing visibly shipped as a service, and a harder first step.

Many authors (for example Sam Newman in Monolith to Microservices) discuss both orders and the intermediate patterns, and the right choice depends on risk appetite and how tangled the data is. A common middle path: give the future service its own schema first, with all other code going through its interface, then lift the code. Whatever the order, expect the data to be the longest part of the job, and do not call the migration finished while two services still write to the same table.

Shared libraries, shared services and the shared kernel

Every system has code that several services need: logging helpers, authentication, money and date types. There are three ways to share it, each with a different failure mode.

MechanismCouplingGood forDanger
Shared libraryBuild-time and version coupling; runs in-process, no network.Stable, generic, domain-free code: logging, serialisation, HTTP clients.Version skew across services; a "breaking" change forces upgrades everywhere; domain logic in a library becomes a hidden monolith.
Shared serviceRun-time coupling; a network call; a single place to change.Capabilities that need one owner and one data store: identity, payments.It becomes a bottleneck and a single point of failure; every caller waits on it.
Shared kernelTwo contexts agree to share a small piece of model and code (a DDD pattern named by Evans).A tiny, stable model that two closely-cooperating teams genuinely co-own.It grows. Each "just one more field" couples more services to one team's release schedule.

The danger zone is the shared domain library: a package with the Order and Customer classes that every service imports so that "everyone agrees what an order is". It feels like reuse and acts like a shared database in code form: change it and every service must rebuild and redeploy. Prefer to share contracts (schemas for messages and APIs, chapter 10) and let each context hold its own model of the concept. A little duplication across contexts is cheaper than coupling them. The Segment case shows what accumulated shared code and many near-identical services can do to a team.

Common misconception

"Don't repeat yourself means we must never duplicate code between services." DRY is about knowledge, and each context legitimately has its own knowledge of a concept. Two services that each define a small Address type are not violating anything. Forcing them to share one means one team's change schedule governs the other, which costs far more than a few duplicated lines.

Try it yourself

Try it yourself: find your own seams

Run the co-change script on a real repository. Your output will differ, and that is the point.

# one line per commit: the top-level directories it touched (macOS, Linux, WSL)
git log --name-only --pretty=format:'--commit--' -n 500 \
  | awk '/^--commit--$/ {if (n) print line; line=""; n=1; next} NF {split($0,p,"/"); line = line p[1] " "} END {print line}' \
  | head -5
  1. Turn each output line into a set of top-level directory names and paste them into commits in the script (or read them in with sys.stdin). Repeated names in a line are harmless, because each line becomes a set.
  2. Drop merge and formatter commits, which touch everything, and re-run. Do the strong pairs match the boundaries your team believes in?
  3. Pick the strongest cross-boundary pair and open the last three commits that touched both. Were they contract changes, data moves or just a missing method? What would remove the need to change both?
  4. On the shop diagram above, remove Payments as a separate service by merging it into Ordering, and list what gets simpler and what you lose. Then try the reverse for Notifications.

Distributed systems connection

Every approach in the "how do I get data I do not own" table is a position on the consistency spectrum from networking chapter 18: calling the owner is the strongest and the most fragile, replicating via events is eventually consistent and resilient. Data ownership is also what makes cross-boundary invariants hard, since no transaction can span two stores, which is why chapter 15 introduces compensating workflows. And the idea of one writer per piece of data is the same single-writer principle behind leaders in replication and partition owners in sharding (data chapter 16).

Key takeaways

  • Draw boundaries around bounded contexts and business capabilities, not technical layers. Avoid entity services: CRUD wrappers over one table push the real logic into every caller.
  • Each service (or module) owns its data. No shared tables, not even read-only ones.
  • To use data you do not own: call the owner (current, but coupled in time), replicate via events (fast and resilient, but eventually consistent), or compose in an API layer for queries. Data that is a fact about your own record, such as the price at order time, is yours.
  • Measure cohesion and coupling by change history. Pairs that often change together are one unit in practice, whatever the diagram says.
  • Chatty boundaries (N+1, ping-pong, getter-only services) mean the line is in the wrong place. When unsure, start bigger and split later.
  • When splitting a monolith, find seams, make them explicit inside the monolith, strangle incrementally, and plan the data move. Splitting code first or data first are both viable, with opposite risks.
  • Share contracts rather than domain code. A shared library or shared kernel with domain types quietly recreates the monolith.

Check your understanding

What is wrong with a CustomerService that exposes create, read, update and delete for the customers table and is called by every other service?

  1. Nothing: it is the cleanest form of data ownership
  2. It is an entity service: the business logic ends up scattered through its callers, and the service becomes a shared dependency of everything
  3. It should use GraphQL instead of REST
  4. It should have been split into one service per column
Splitting by database nouns gives anaemic services with no behaviour of their own. The rules live in the callers, so a change to a business rule touches many services, and everyone depends on this one. Boundaries should follow capabilities and contexts.

Service A needs a product's name to show on a page, and can tolerate it being a few seconds out of date. A must keep working when the Catalog service is down. Which approach fits best?

  1. Read the Catalog database directly
  2. Call Catalog synchronously on every page view
  3. Share a domain library containing the Product class
  4. Keep a local read-only copy of the needed fields, updated from Catalog's change events
Replication via events trades freshness for independence, which is what is asked for. Reading the database breaks ownership, a synchronous call fails when Catalog is down, and a shared library shares code, not data.

Your commit history shows that changes to the billing and invoicing services appear together in 70% of the commits touching either. What is the most reasonable conclusion?

  1. They behave like one unit of change; consider merging them or find out why the boundary between them keeps moving
  2. The services are well designed, since they interact a lot
  3. You need a faster CI server
  4. You should add a third service between them
High co-change means low cohesion across the boundary. Each such change needs coordinated edits and releases. Investigate whether the contract is unstable, data sits in the wrong place, or they simply should be one. Adding a middle service adds another hop.

A monolith is being split. The team extracts a service quickly but lets it keep reading the shared database "for now". What is the main risk?

  1. The service will be too fast
  2. The monolith will stop compiling
  3. The shared database becomes permanent, giving a distributed monolith whose hardest part (the data) was never split
  4. There is no risk; this is the recommended approach in every case
Splitting code first gives early wins but defers the difficult part. Many teams never come back to it, ending with services that cannot change a schema independently. Splitting data first has the opposite risk: a long stretch of awkward work before anything visibly ships.

Which is the best way to make "what is an order?" consistent between the Ordering and Shipping services?

  1. A shared library with an Order class that both import
  2. A versioned message contract for the events they exchange, with each service keeping its own model of what it needs
  3. One database table both services write to
  4. Each service calls the other for every field, every time
Share contracts, not domain code. A shared model library ties both release schedules together; a shared table is worse; and calling for every field creates a chatty, tightly coupled boundary.
