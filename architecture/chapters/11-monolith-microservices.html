Monoliths, Modular Monoliths & Microservices

Few topics in software attract as much fashion as this one. This chapter strips the fashion away. You will learn what each style actually is, what it buys and what it costs, how to see the cost of a network hop in numbers you can play with, and how to decide, for your team and your product, without copying whatever a famous company did.

Why this matters

Splitting a system into services is one of the most expensive decisions you can make, and one of the hardest to undo. Done for the right reasons, at the right time, it lets many teams ship independently. Done too early, or for the wrong reasons, it turns every function call into a network call, every transaction into a negotiation and every bug into a detective story. Senior engineers are valued less for knowing how to build microservices than for knowing when not to, and for being able to explain the choice in terms of trade-offs rather than taste.

Four words, defined carefully

The vocabulary is muddy, so let's pin it down first. All four terms describe how a system is packaged and deployed, which is a separate question from how well its code is organised.

Monolith
A system built and deployed as one unit: one codebase (usually), one build, one deployable artefact, often one database. Modules talk to each other with ordinary function calls inside one process. "Monolith" describes the deployment, not the quality of the code.
Modular monolith
Still one deployable unit, but internally divided into modules with enforced boundaries: each module exposes a small public interface, hides its internals, and (ideally) owns its own tables. The boundaries are checked by tooling and code review, not just by good intentions.
Microservices
The definition most people quote comes from James Lewis and Martin Fowler's 2014 article: a suite of small services, each running in its own process, communicating over lightweight mechanisms (often HTTP APIs), organised around business capabilities, independently deployable, and each managing its own data. Independent deployability and data ownership are the load-bearing parts. A pile of small services that must be released together is not what the article describes.
SOA
Service-oriented architecture, popular in the 2000s, also split enterprises into services, but typically bigger ones, often connected through a central enterprise service bus with heavy standards (SOAP, WSDL) and shared governance. Many people see microservices as a descendant of SOA that favoured smaller services, dumb pipes and decentralised ownership. The boundary between the two is blurry, and you will hear both words used loosely.

Analogy: a city

Think of a software system as a city. A monolith is one huge building with every department inside: you can walk from the tax office to the library through a corridor in seconds, but a fire anywhere is a fire everywhere, and the whole building must close for renovation. A modular monolith is the same building with proper walls, fire doors and one marked door per department, so you cannot wander into the records room. Microservices are separate buildings in separate neighbourhoods, each with its own utilities, connected by roads. The roads and their signs are the APIs; the building codes are the contracts. Each building can be rebuilt without closing the others, but now every errand means a trip down a road that can be slow, jammed or closed.

Where it breaks down: a city grows by accretion and zoning is argued over for decades. Software boundaries are chosen by a few people, often early, with little information, and they are expensive to move once other teams depend on them. Also, unlike a building, a code module can be moved to a different "neighbourhood" with a refactor, if you are still in the same building.

Common misconception

"Monolith" does not mean "mess". The messy one has a name of its own, the big ball of mud: code with no discernible structure, where everything depends on everything. You can build a ball of mud as a monolith or as thirty microservices (you will meet that version below). Conversely, a monolith with strong modules is one of the most productive structures a small or mid-sized team can have. Deployment shape and internal structure are two different axes.

What microservices buy

Microservices solve real problems, and it helps to name exactly which. There are five benefits worth taking seriously.

  1. Independent deployment. One team can ship its service on Tuesday afternoon without a release meeting, a shared freeze or a merge train, provided the interfaces it exposes still honour their contracts (chapter 10). In a large monolith, every change ships with every other team's change, so a bug in one area can block everybody's release. This is the benefit that matters most, and the one that is easiest to lose by accident.
  2. Team autonomy. A team can own a capability end to end: code, data, release schedule, on-call. Decisions that used to need a committee (a library upgrade, a schema change) become local decisions. This is as much an organisational benefit as a technical one, and we return to it under Conway's law.
  3. Independent scaling. If image resizing needs 40 times the CPU of the rest of the product, you can run many copies of just that part, on different hardware if need be. In a monolith you scale the whole thing, which wastes resources but is often perfectly acceptable: the cost of running extra copies of a mostly idle module is usually smaller than the cost of operating a second service.
  4. Fault isolation. A memory leak or crash in the recommendations service need not take the checkout down, if the checkout is written to survive its absence (timeouts, fallbacks, bulkheads: chapter 17). Note the "if": isolation is a possibility you must earn with design, not something the network gives you for free.
  5. Technology heterogeneity. A service can use the language or datastore that suits it. In practice this is the weakest of the five. Each new language or database adds operational burden, and most organisations that start out welcoming heterogeneity end up standardising on a small set of "paved road" choices.

Notice what these have in common: they matter when there are many people and diverse load. For a team of six with one product, most of them are solutions to problems you do not have yet.

What microservices cost

The costs are just as real, and they are paid continuously, not once.

Network calls instead of function calls

A function call inside a process takes nanoseconds, cannot be lost, and either returns or throws. A network call takes at least hundreds of microseconds inside a data centre and milliseconds or more across regions, and it has a third outcome that function calls lack: you do not know. The request may have been lost, or processed with the response lost, or still be running. That is partial failure, the central theme of networking chapter 15, and it forces you to handle timeouts, retries and idempotency (networking chapter 16) at every service boundary. Latency also stops being a number and becomes a distribution with a tail (networking chapter 13), and tails compound across hops. The widget in the next section makes this concrete.

Distributed data and no cross-service transactions

Inside one database, a transaction can debit one table and credit another atomically (data chapter 10). When each service owns its database, that guarantee ends at the service boundary. "Reserve stock, take payment, create shipment" can no longer be one transaction. You replace it with a saga: a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions for undoing earlier steps (the idea goes back to Garcia-Molina and Salem in 1987; chapter 15). Sagas work, but they expose intermediate states to users and put the burden of correctness on your application code. Joins across services become API calls or replicated copies, with eventual consistency (data chapter 19).

Operational overhead

Each service needs a build pipeline, deployment, configuration, secrets, health checks, dashboards, alerts, logging and an on-call story. With ten services that is ten of everything; the tooling (containers, orchestration, service discovery, a gateway, centralised logs, metrics and distributed tracing) is a platform that someone must build and run. Teams that adopt microservices without investing in this platform find that engineering time disappears into plumbing.

Versioning and compatibility

Once service A calls service B, they are on different release schedules. B cannot simply change a field. It must keep the old contract working until every caller has moved, or version the API, or both. That is manageable (chapter 10), but it is permanent work, and Hyrum's law (every observable behaviour will be depended on by somebody) applies at every boundary.

Harder debugging and testing

A bug in a monolith is usually one stack trace. A bug across five services is a trace reconstructed from five log streams, with clock skew and sampling in the way. Testing the real interactions needs either a shared test environment (slow, flaky, contended) or contract tests that check each side against an agreed interface. Local development gets heavier too: "run the whole system on my laptop" quietly stops being possible.

Common misconception

"Microservices are more resilient." They can be, but by default they are less available than the equivalent monolith. A request that needs five services in a row can only succeed when all five are up, so the availability of the chain is the product of the availabilities, as you are about to compute. Resilience comes from the patterns you add (timeouts, fallbacks, bulkheads, queues), and each of those is more code, more tests and more operations.

The cost of a hop, in numbers

Two facts, both from earlier courses, are enough to quantify the cost.

  • Serial dependencies multiply availability. If a request needs services A, B, C and D, each of which is up independently 99.9% of the time, the request succeeds only when all four are up: 0.9994 ≈ 99.6%. That is a difference between roughly 43 minutes and almost 3 hours of unavailable requests in a 30-day month (assuming, generously, that failures are independent).
  • Tail latency compounds. A service whose median is 20 ms and whose p99 is 100 ms looks fine alone. But a request that makes ten calls hits the slow 1% of some call about one time in ten (1 − 0.9910 ≈ 9.6%). If the ten calls are made in parallel and the request waits for all of them, the slowest call decides, and the request's own p99 rises well above any single service's p99 (networking chapter 13 on the tail at scale).

Things to try, and what to look for:

  1. Start with the defaults (serial, four hops at 99.9%, median 20 ms, p99 100 ms). Compare the end-to-end p99 with the sum of the four p99s. Why is it lower? (A request would need all four hops to be unlucky at the same moment to reach the sum.) Why is it still much higher than one hop's p99?
  2. Drag the number of calls from 1 to 10 and watch the availability fall. Now change every service to 99.99% (set hop 1, then use Copy hop 1 to all hops). How many extra "nines" does each service need so that a ten-hop path is as available as one 99.9% service?
  3. Switch to parallel fan-out. The median barely moves, but the p99 does. Compare the 1-call and 10-call bars. This is the shape of a search or recommendation page that fans out to many backends.
  4. Make one hop's p99 very large (say 1,000 ms) and the others small. The tail of the whole request is now mostly that one hop. This is why a single slow dependency can dominate a call chain.
  5. Look at the green tile. Thousands of module calls inside one process cost microseconds in total and cannot fail separately from the process. That is the baseline a service boundary has to justify itself against.

The model is deliberately kind. It has no retries, no queueing, no overload and no correlated failures. In real systems a slow dependency holds threads and connections, callers retry, retries add load, and the failure spreads (chapter 17). Read the numbers as a lower bound on the pain.

The microservice premium and "monolith first"

All those costs add up to what Martin Fowler called the microservice premium: a fixed tax, in complexity and effort, that you pay whatever the size of the system. Below some level of system complexity the tax exceeds the benefits; above it, the benefits win. His related advice, often shortened to monolith first, is to start with a monolith, find the real seams as the product matures, and extract services only where there is a concrete reason. The case usually made has three parts, and you should evaluate each rather than treat the slogan as scripture:

  • You do not know the boundaries yet. Early in a product's life the domain is poorly understood. Wrong boundaries inside a monolith are a refactor; wrong boundaries between services are a migration with data, contracts and teams on both sides.
  • Premium costs are paid up front, benefits arrive later. A team of five does not yet need independent deployment from itself.
  • Extraction is easier than assembly. A well-modularised monolith can have a module lifted into a service later (chapter 12 and chapter 18). Merging badly drawn services back together is harder.

Be fair to the other side. Counter-arguments exist: a team that has already built the platform, or has done it many times, pays a lower premium; some products are obviously decomposable from day one (a payment gateway distinct from a content site); and a monolith that is never modularised can decay into the ball of mud you cannot extract anything from. "Monolith first" works when the monolith is modular. Without that, it merely postpones the problem.

Conway's law and team structure

In 1968 the programmer Melvin Conway observed that organisations which design systems are constrained to produce designs that copy the communication structure of those organisations. This is Conway's law. Four teams building a compiler will tend to produce a four-pass compiler. If the front-end team and the database team only talk through a ticket queue, the interface between their parts will be shaped like a ticket queue.

The practical use is the so-called inverse Conway manoeuvre: decide which system structure you want (for example, a checkout that can change without touching search) and arrange the teams to match, because the structure of the teams will win otherwise. This is why microservices are usually described as an organisational pattern as much as a technical one: services owned by teams aligned to business capabilities, rather than teams aligned to technical layers.

Two refinements keep this from becoming a slogan. First, the effect runs both ways: a system that is hard to change in parts will freeze the organisation around it, so reorganising teams without reshaping the code gives you the old architecture with new managers. Second, team size and cognitive load are the real constraint. A team can only hold so much of a system in its head, and a sensible service (or module) is one that a single team can understand, operate and change confidently. Skelton and Pais develop this at length in Team Topologies, which is worth reading; for this course, the takeaway is to size boundaries to what a team can own, not to a line-count rule such as "a microservice should have fewer than N lines".

The distributed monolith

The most common microservices failure is not too many services. It is services that are separate in deployment but not in behaviour: a distributed monolith. You get the network costs of microservices and the coupling of a monolith, which is the worst combination available.

Signs you may have one:

  • A change to one feature routinely needs coordinated changes and releases in several services.
  • Services share a database, or reach into each other's tables "just for reporting".
  • You deploy services in a fixed order, or "all together on Thursday".
  • A shared library holding domain types must be upgraded everywhere at once.
  • A request to one service fans out into long synchronous chains, so one outage anywhere is an outage everywhere.
  • You cannot test one service without starting most of the others.

The cure is rarely "more services" or "fewer services" in the abstract. It is to restore the property that defines the style: independent deployability, backed by data ownership and well-chosen boundaries (chapter 12). Sometimes that means merging services back into one. The case studies on Prime Video and Segment describe teams that did exactly that. Described qualitatively here: in both, a design that was split into many parts paid a persistent price in coordination and moving data between the parts, and the teams consolidated. Both are about right-sizing, not about microservices being bad, and each case study gives sources and the details.

Deciding: a checklist, not a verdict

Instead of asking "monolith or microservices?", ask what you would gain and what pressure you are actually under. This table lists the signals that matter.

QuestionPoints to a (modular) monolithPoints to services
How many teams change the code?One to a few, who can coordinate over lunch.Many teams, each needing to move at its own pace.
How often do deploys collide?Rarely; releases are quick and uneventful.Constantly: merge queues, release trains, one team blocking another.
How different are the scaling needs?Roughly uniform; scaling the whole app is cheap enough.One or two parts need very different hardware or scale by orders of magnitude.
How well do you understand the domain?Still discovering it; boundaries move monthly.Stable, well-understood capabilities with clear owners.
Do you need strong cross-entity consistency?Yes: invariants span many tables and one transaction is a great tool.No: operations are naturally independent or tolerate eventual consistency.
Can you operate a platform?No dedicated platform or on-call depth yet.Deployment, observability and tracing are solved, or you have people for them.
Is there a regulatory or isolation need?No.Yes: for example, payment data that must be isolated and audited separately.

If most answers sit in the left column, the cheapest good answer is a modular monolith. If a few sit on the right, extract only those parts. A hybrid, a modular core with a handful of services around it, is common and perfectly respectable. And if you are unsure, remember the asymmetry: it is much easier to extract a service from a well-modularised monolith than to unpick a premature split.

Building a modular monolith

A modular monolith keeps the operational simplicity of one deployable while giving the team most of the design benefits of services. The techniques are unglamorous but effective. Shopify, which runs a very large monolith, is the best-known public account of making this work; see case 3.

  • Package boundaries that mirror the business. Organise code by capability (orders/, payments/, catalog/), not by technical layer (controllers/, models/). This applies the information-hiding principle David Parnas wrote about in 1972: each module hides a design decision (chapter 2).
  • A small public interface per module. Other modules may import only the module's API (a facade, a set of functions, a few types). Everything else is private. In Python, a convention such as orders.api as the only importable surface, plus an underscore for the rest, goes a long way.
  • Enforced import rules. A rule that exists only in a wiki is a suggestion. Run a check in CI that fails when payments imports orders.models, or when a dependency cycle appears. Real tools exist for most languages, and a basic one is short enough to write yourself, as you will in Lab 3.
  • Separate schemas per module. Give each module its own tables (or its own database schema) and forbid cross-module joins. Modules then exchange data through the public API, so that later, if a module becomes a service, its data is already separated.
  • Communicate through events or interfaces, not shared state. Inside the process, a module can publish an in-memory event that others subscribe to, which is cheap and keeps modules from knowing each other's names (chapter 14).

Here is a deliberately tiny boundary checker. It has a module graph as plain data, a set of public interfaces and an "allowed dependencies" table, and it reports the violations. It is a sketch of what Lab 3 builds on real files.

# A module graph as data: which module imports which, and what each module exposes.
PUBLIC = {            # each module's public interface (what other modules may import)
    "orders":   {"orders.api"},
    "payments": {"payments.api"},
    "catalog":  {"catalog.api"},
}
IMPORTS = [           # (importing file, imported module path)
    ("orders.service",   "payments.api"),     # ok: public interface
    ("orders.service",   "catalog.api"),      # ok
    ("orders.repository","orders.models"),    # ok: same module
    ("payments.charge",  "orders.models"),    # BAD: reaches into another module's internals
    ("catalog.search",   "orders.api"),       # ok
]
ALLOWED = {"orders": {"payments", "catalog"}, "payments": set(), "catalog": {"orders"}}

def module(path: str) -> str:
    return path.split(".")[0]

violations = []
for src, dst in IMPORTS:
    a, b = module(src), module(dst)
    if a == b:
        continue
    if dst not in PUBLIC[b]:
        violations.append(f"{src} imports {dst}: not part of {b}'s public interface")
    elif b not in ALLOWED[a]:
        violations.append(f"{src} imports {dst}: {a} may not depend on {b}")

print("\n".join(violations) or "no violations")
print(f"{len(violations)} violation(s) in {len(IMPORTS)} imports")
payments.charge imports orders.models: not part of orders's public interface
1 violation(s) in 5 imports

One import reached into another module's internals and was flagged. If this runs in CI, that structure stays honest without any service boundary at all. Notice the second rule too: even public imports are only allowed along declared dependency arrows, which keeps the module graph from collapsing into a tangle.

Go deeper: when does "one database, many schemas" stop being enough? optional

Separate schemas inside one database give you ownership boundaries but share a failure domain, a connection pool and a hardware budget. A heavy report from one module can slow every other module, and a long transaction in one can hold locks others need. That is usually the right trade at first, since you keep cheap joins in tooling and a single backup. You move a module's data to its own database when its load, availability needs or compliance constraints differ from the rest, or when a team's schema changes need to be decoupled from the others' release schedule. The earlier you kept the data separate in the code, the cheaper that move is.

Try it yourself

Try it yourself: price your own call chain

You need only Python 3. Take a real request in a system you know (or an imaginary checkout) and count how many services it needs in series.

  1. Write down each service's availability target (or guess 99.9%), then multiply them: python3 -c "print(0.999**6)" for six hops. Compare it with the availability you promise users.
  2. Reproduce one of the widget's results by hand: simulate fan-out p99 with random.lognormvariate for ten calls and take the maximum of each batch. The numbers will not match to the digit (a different random generator), but the shape will.
  3. Look at your own codebase. Pick two top-level packages and ask: could I list, in one sentence each, what the other is allowed to import from it? If you cannot, you have no boundary yet, whatever the deployment diagram says.
  4. Pull the architecture diagram of a system you work on and colour each box by owning team. Count the lines that cross team colours. Those are the places where Conway's law is already working on you.

Distributed systems connection

The moment you split a process, you inherit the problems of networking chapter 15: unreliable networks, partial failure, no shared clock and no global transaction. Every later chapter in this part of the course is, in a sense, a toolbox for living with that choice: boundaries in chapter 12, communication styles in chapter 13, events and sagas in chapters 14 and 15, resilience in chapter 17. The deeper theory (consistency models, consensus, time) continues in the Distributed Systems course after this one. If you keep one idea from here, keep this: a service boundary is a distributed-systems problem you have chosen to have, so choose it on purpose.

Key takeaways

  • A monolith is one deployable unit; a modular monolith adds enforced internal boundaries; microservices are independently deployable services that own their data and are organised around business capabilities (Lewis and Fowler, 2014). Deployment shape is separate from code quality: a monolith is not a ball of mud by definition.
  • Microservices buy independent deployment, team autonomy, independent scaling, fault isolation (if designed for) and technology choice. They are worth most when many teams or very different workloads are pulling against each other.
  • They cost network latency and partial failure, loss of cross-service transactions, operational overhead, versioning work and harder debugging. Serial dependencies multiply availability; fan-out multiplies the tail.
  • "Monolith first" (Fowler) is a well-known argument that you rarely know the right boundaries early, and that a modular monolith is cheaper to run and easier to split later. It is a strong default, not a law.
  • Conway's law (1968): systems mirror the communication structure of the organisation. The inverse Conway manoeuvre shapes teams to get the architecture you want. Size boundaries to what one team can own (cognitive load).
  • A distributed monolith (separate deploys, shared data, lock-step releases, long synchronous chains) has the costs of both styles and the benefits of neither.
  • To keep a monolith modular: business-shaped packages, small public interfaces, import rules enforced in CI, and separate schemas per module.

Check your understanding

A request passes through five services in sequence, each 99.9% available and failing independently. Roughly what availability does the whole request have?

  1. 99.9%, the same as one service
  2. 99.99%, because redundancy helps
  3. About 99.5%, because the availabilities multiply
  4. About 95%
For serial dependencies you multiply: 0.9995 ≈ 0.995. The request can succeed only if every one of the five is up. Redundancy would help only if you added parallel alternatives, not more required steps.

Which statement best describes a distributed monolith?

  1. Several separately deployed services that are so coupled (shared database, lock-step releases, long synchronous chains) that they cannot change independently
  2. A monolith deployed on several servers behind a load balancer
  3. A monolith with a very large codebase
  4. A microservice system that uses a message queue
The defining feature is lost independence: you pay the network and operational costs of services but still release and change them together. Running several copies of a monolith is ordinary horizontal scaling, and a queue does not by itself create coupling.

Your team of eight is building a new product in a domain you are still learning. Which approach do many experienced engineers recommend, and why?

  1. Microservices from day one, so you never need to migrate
  2. A single unstructured codebase, because structure slows you down
  3. One service per database table
  4. A modular monolith, because boundaries are still uncertain and are cheaper to move inside one process
This is the "monolith first" argument, with the important caveat that the monolith should be modular. An unstructured one will not be extractable later, and one service per table gets the costs of services with none of the cohesion.

Which of these is an application of the "inverse Conway manoeuvre"?

  1. Rewriting the code to match the current org chart
  2. Deciding on the system boundaries you want, then organising teams around business capabilities to match them
  3. Splitting teams by technical layer so each has specialists
  4. Asking every team to communicate more through tickets
Conway's law says systems copy the communication structure of the organisation. Since the structure will win anyway, you shape the organisation to produce the architecture you want. Splitting by technical layer tends to produce layered systems where every feature crosses every team.

A page fans out to ten backends in parallel and waits for all of them. Each backend has median 20 ms and p99 100 ms. What happens to the page's p99, compared with one backend's p99?

  1. It stays at 100 ms, since the calls run in parallel
  2. It becomes about 1,000 ms, ten times larger
  3. It rises above 100 ms, because the page waits for the slowest of ten and so is slow whenever any one of them lands in its own slow tail
  4. It falls, since the median is low
Roughly 9.6% of pages will include at least one call slower than its own p99, so the page's p99 sits well above a single backend's p99. It does not reach 10x, because latency is the max rather than the sum. Parallelism hides the sum but not the tail.

Which technique does not belong in a modular monolith's boundary toolkit?

  1. Import rules checked in CI
  2. Letting each module read the others' database tables directly for speed
  3. A public API per module
  4. A separate schema per module
Direct table access defeats the boundary: once other code depends on your table layout, you cannot change it, and extracting the module later means untangling hidden dependencies. The other three are the standard tools for keeping boundaries real.
