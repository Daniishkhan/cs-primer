Start Here

This course is not a syllabus to finish. It is the reference you open while building your first public artifact: a version of Karpathy's autoresearch loop whose runner is written in Rust and whose extension measures how far a five-minute score can mislead. This page says what that artifact must achieve, which chapters to read first, and which to keep open.

Why this matters

The path you are on ends in four public artifacts, each a replication of one published result at small scale plus one honest extension, released as code and a write-up. A reader who can show those has produced evidence that a research lab can inspect without taking anyone's word. This chapter is the entry to the first one, so it tells you the gate you must reach, the order in which to read, and what you will be able to do at each stage. It builds on nothing in this course, but it points to the chapters of earlier courses that the artifact needs.

Four artifacts instead of a finished curriculum

Most courses ask you to complete everything and then build something. This one runs the other way: pick the artifact, read what it needs, build, and use the chapters as reference when you are stuck. A chapter nothing needs is demoted to optional reading. The four artifacts form a ladder, each the testbed for the next, and the next one starts only when the previous gate is met.

The ladder follows how the field itself has moved: first a single-file research loop, then a harness that searches, then a harness that rewrites itself, then the measurement of whether any of it was real. Every artifact uses a cost-metered machine learning experiment as its inner task, which is why Artifact 1 comes first. It is the cheapest to start and the one that teaches the measurement habits all the others depend on.

Artifact 1: autoresearch-rs

The artifact has three parts, and ends at a gate: a public result, not a private milestone. It replicates karpathy/autoresearch: an agent edits train.py, the script trains for five minutes, and the change is kept only if the validation score improves (chapter 01 reads it closely). It rewrites the runner, the sandbox and the run store in Rust, so the parts that must be trusted are a small program you can read (chapter 05). And it adds one extension: for every experiment, log the gap between the gain on the five-minute proxy and the gain on held-out data, so that overfitting to the proxy is measured instead of assumed (chapter 02).

The gate

You have finished Artifact 1 when you have: 100 or more overnight experiments on one GPU; a chart of proxy gain against held-out gain; and a two-to-three-page write-up with seeds and the noise floor. The course plan's estimate is $150 to $300 of GPU rental and one to three months at ten to fifteen hours a week. These are planning estimates for one person, not guarantees.

Notice what the gate does not say. It does not ask for the best score, the biggest model or a new architecture. It asks for a number you can defend: a hundred experiments is enough to see the loop's behaviour, a chart exposes its blind spot, and the write-up forces you to state seeds and noise. That is the standard a reviewer applies.

What to keep open, and what to build with

While you build, keep these open rather than read them ahead of time: A0's chapters on reading papers and technical writing, A2's initialisation and normalisation and tokenisation (val_bpb depends on the tokeniser), and the RE-Bench (Research Engineering Benchmark) scaling-law task from the evaluation group METR, which is a ready-made proxy problem (chapter 03).

The course plan also suggests a one-week warm-up: a small bin-packing loop, with the chapters on functions and objects, programs as data, running candidates safely, evolution for builders and evolutionary algorithms. The cases are modded-nanogpt, Chinchilla and the logbook of training OPT-175B, three real research records.

Three standards that run through everything

Every claim of improvement in this course follows rules you will see repeated, because they are what a lab reviewer checks first. A fair baseline given the same budget. A sustained trend rather than one lucky accept. Scores on data the loop never saw. A stated budget that is never exceeded. And, from the next artifact onward, the evaluator outside the proposer's reach, with three or more seeds, confidence intervals and a paired test. In Artifact 1 the last two are already your job: chapter 02 gives the statistics.

Try it yourself

Before reading further, write three sentences in a notes file: what you think the loop would find in a hundred overnight experiments; how large an improvement you would be willing to believe from a single five-minute run; and what you would check before telling someone the loop works. Come back to them after chapter 02. If your second answer has not changed, reread the noise-floor table.

Key takeaways

  • The course serves four artifacts. Artifact 1, autoresearch-rs, replicates Karpathy's loop, rewrites its runner in Rust and logs the proxy-versus-held-out gap.
  • Its gate is 100 or more overnight experiments on one GPU, a chart of proxy gain against held-out gain and a two-to-three-page write-up with seeds and the noise floor.
  • Read the eight foundation chapters first, then this course's other six in order; keep the others open while you build.
  • Four standards recur: a fair baseline, a sustained trend, scores on unseen data and a fixed stated budget.

Which of these is part of Artifact 1's gate?

  1. A new model architecture that beats the autoresearch baseline.
  2. A chart of proxy gain against held-out gain for 100 or more overnight experiments.
  3. A merged pull request to a kernel benchmark.
  4. A trained model of at least one billion parameters.
The gate asks for a hundred or more experiments, the proxy-versus-held-out chart and a short write-up. A kernel-benchmark pull request is Artifact 3's gate; architecture and model size are not required.

What is the extension that Artifact 1 adds to the original autoresearch loop?

  1. A larger GPU.
  2. A tree search over experiments.
  3. A Python rewrite of the loop.
  4. Logging the gap between proxy gain and held-out gain for each experiment.
Tree search is Artifact 2. The Rust rewrite is part of the artifact's replication, but the one new finding is the measured gap. The hardware is not the point.

Why is the course organised around artifacts instead of finishing chapters in order?

  1. Each chapter is scheduled by the artifact that needs it, and a chapter nothing needs is optional reading.
  2. Because the later chapters are easier.
  3. Because the chapters cannot be read separately.
  4. Because artifacts replace the need for any statistics.
The path is just-in-time: build, and use the chapters as reference. The chapters can be read separately, and the artifacts depend on statistics, not the reverse.

A friend shows you a loop that raised its score by 3% in one overnight run and says it works. Which of the four standards is the most obviously missing?

  1. A fixed budget.
  2. A fair baseline with the same budget.
  3. A sustained trend and evidence it was not one lucky accept; it is also unclear whether seeds were used.
  4. None: one run is enough.
A single overnight run offers no trend and no noise floor, so a lucky accept cannot be ruled out. The baseline and budget may also be missing, but we are not told; the stated outcome alone shows the single-run weakness.
