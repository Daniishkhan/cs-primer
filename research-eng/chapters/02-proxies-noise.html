Proxies, Noise Floors & Seeds

A research loop turns one noisy number into a decision, a hundred times a night. This chapter asks how much a five-minute score moves when nothing has changed, what that does to a keep-or-revert rule, how a paired test over seeds sees through it, and how far the proxy score can drift from the score you actually care about. It is the chapter that decides whether the chart at the end of Artifact 1 means anything.

Why this matters

Artifact 1's gate asks for a chart of proxy gain against held-out gain and a write-up "with seeds and the noise floor". Both are statistical claims, and a reviewer at a lab will check them first. This chapter gives you the three tools behind them: a measured noise floor, a paired test over seeds, and a per-experiment log of the gap between proxy and held-out gain. It builds on the statistics of A0 chapter 15 and A0 chapter 4, and assumes the loop of chapter 01. After it you can say, before running anything, how large a gain is detectable with a given number of seeds. You can also explain why a loop that keeps "whatever scored lower" over-reports its progress, and write the keep rule that fixes it. Lab 1 implements the noise floor, the paired decision and the gap warning.

Is 0.9170 better than 0.9210?

Suppose the baseline scores a val_bpb of 0.9210 and your latest experiment scores 0.9170. The loop of chapter 01 would keep it: lower is better, and the difference is 0.004. Whether that is progress depends on a number the loop never measured, namely how much the score changes when you run the same thing again.

The size of that run-to-run wobble is the noise floor: the smallest improvement you can tell from no change at all. Training is a random process. The weights start from a random initialisation, the data is shuffled in a random order, dropout (if used) draws random masks, and GPU kernels may add numbers in a different order on each run, which changes the rounding. A single integer called the seed fixes the pseudo-random draws, and two runs with different seeds are two samples from the same distribution of outcomes. The first thing to do with a research loop is to measure that distribution.

The script below does so for a stand-in training run. The true score of 0.9200 and the seed-to-seed standard deviation of 0.0030 are illustrative assumptions; on real hardware you would replace the gauss call with ten actual runs of the unmodified train.py, which costs about an hour of budget and is the best hour you will spend.

The idea

  1. run the unmodified config under n different seeds; record each score
  2. noise floor ← the sample standard deviation of those scores
  3. the difference of two means over n seeds each has standard error sd × sqrt(2 / n)
  4. a gain smaller than about 2 standard errors cannot be told from no change
"""Measure a noise floor: run the same config under many seeds.

The 'run' is a stand-in for one five-minute training run. The true score
(0.9200) and the seed noise (sd 0.0030) are illustrative assumptions.
"""
import math
import random
import statistics as st

TRUE, SIGMA = 0.9200, 0.0030

# ── 1. Ten runs of one unmodified config, ten seeds ──────────────────────
runs = [random.Random(seed).gauss(TRUE, SIGMA) for seed in range(10)]
for seed, v in enumerate(runs):
    print("seed %d  val_bpb %.4f" % (seed, v))

# ── 2. The noise floor is the spread of those scores ─────────────────────
mean, sd = st.mean(runs), st.stdev(runs)       # sd uses n-1 (sample sd)
print("mean %.4f  sd %.4f  range %.4f" % (mean, sd, max(runs) - min(runs)))

# ── 3. How big must a gain be to stand out, for n seeds per arm? ─────────
# The difference of two means has standard error sd * sqrt(2 / n).
print("\nseeds/arm  SE(diff)  smallest gain detected at ~95%")
for n in (1, 3, 5, 10, 20):
    se = sd * math.sqrt(2 / n)
    print("%9d  %.4f    %.4f" % (n, se, 1.96 * se))

Read the output in three parts. The ten scores run from 0.9165 to 0.9270, a range of 0.0106, and no seed changed anything but the random draws. The sample standard deviation, the usual summary of spread, is 0.0031. And the last table says what that means for a comparison. With one run per arm, the standard error of the difference is 0.0044, so a gain must exceed about 0.0086 to be detectable at roughly 95% confidence. The 0.004 improvement above is inside that band. It is also the picture below: one new run at 0.9170 sits comfortably inside the cloud the unmodified config fills by itself.

The table also shows the cost of precision. Standard errors fall with the square root of the number of seeds, so halving the detectable gain costs four times the runs. Five seeds per arm detect gains of about 0.0039; ten detect about 0.0027; twenty about 0.0019. (The 1.96 multiplier assumes the differences are roughly normal, which is only a rough guide at small n; the paired test below does better.) Whatever the loop's search finds next, it cannot be a gain smaller than what the budget can resolve.

standard error of a difference of two means = sd × √(2 ÷ n)

Where the noise comes from

It helps to know the sources, because some can be removed and some cannot. Initialisation and data order are controlled by the seed: fix the seed and they repeat exactly. Non-deterministic kernels are not, unless you ask the framework for deterministic algorithms, usually at a speed cost; this is the residual noise of a run whose seed is fixed. And under a wall-clock budget there is a third source specific to this loop: the number of steps itself varies. Chapter 01 showed that the budget buys a number of steps equal to the counted seconds divided by the step time. If another process shares the GPU, or clocks throttle, one run gets 868 steps and the next 850, and the score moves with them. A time budget makes the experiment depend on the machine's mood.

Two practical consequences follow. Record the step count with every run, so an unusually slow run is visible rather than silently scored. And fix what you can: same hardware, same driver, nothing else on the card, the seed written into the log row. This is the reproducibility discipline of A0 chapter 4, now with a number attached to what is left over.

The winner's curse in keep-or-revert

Now put the noise floor inside the loop. The keep rule compares a candidate's one noisy score with the incumbent's one noisy score and keeps the lower. Consider the case where an idea does nothing at all. Its measured score is the true score plus noise, and the incumbent's is the same true score plus other noise, so the candidate wins the comparison about half the time. In the first stepper below that happens, and the loop records a gain that is pure luck.

Step through

How a lucky accept raises the bar

  1. One baseline run. The unmodified config is measured once and scores 0.9215. Its true mean is 0.9210; the shaded band is the spread it produces by itself. All numbers here are illustrative.
  2. Candidate A changes nothing real. By chance its run lands at 0.9188, below the baseline.
  3. The loop keeps it. Lower is better, so A becomes the incumbent. The bar is now the lucky number 0.9188, not the true mean 0.9210.
  4. Candidate B really helps. Its true mean is 0.9190, but one run lands at 0.9195. That is worse than the incumbent's lucky 0.9188, so B is reverted.
  5. The log and the truth disagree. The record shows a gain of 0.0027 from a change that did nothing, and a real improvement thrown away. More runs per candidate, or a margin, would reduce both errors.

The mistake is not only a wrong keep. It moves the bar. After the lucky accept, the incumbent's score of record is the lucky number, below the true mean. Every later candidate must beat that number. A genuinely better idea can then be rejected because its single run landed a little above the lucky incumbent. Selecting the minimum of many noisy numbers is biased downwards; this is the winner's curse, the same effect that makes the best-looking of many drug trials or A/B tests disappoint on replication.

The script measures how large the effect is. It simulates a thousand nights of 84 runs each. Of all ideas, 10% truly lower the score by 0.002, 70% do nothing and 20% truly raise it by 0.003. Those fractions are an assumption (good ideas are rare), and so is the noise of 0.003. Three keep rules are compared: the naive one, a rule that demands a one-standard-deviation margin, and a rule that re-runs a promising candidate on two more seeds and compares three-run means against a three-run baseline, charging the extra runs to the same 84-run budget.

The idea

  1. true ← 0.920; incumbent's measurements ← the baseline run(s)
  2. until 84 runs are spent: draw an idea with a true effect; measure it with noise
  3. naive: keep if measured < incumbent score. margin: also demand a 1 sd margin.
  4. confirm: if it looks better, run two more seeds and keep only if the 3-run mean is better
  5. report: the gain the log shows (first run minus final score) and the true gain
"""The winner's curse in keep-if-better: simulate 1,000 overnight runs.

Assumptions (illustrative): seed noise sd 0.003; of all ideas 10% truly
lower the score by 0.002, 70% do nothing, 20% truly raise it by 0.003.
Each night has a budget of 84 training runs.
"""
import random
import statistics as st

SIGMA, NIGHTS, RUNS = 0.003, 1000, 84
EFFECTS = [-0.002] * 10 + [0.0] * 70 + [0.003] * 20   # true change per idea

# ── 1. One night under one policy ────────────────────────────────────────
def night(rng, policy):
    true = 0.920                                  # true score of incumbent
    inc = [true + rng.gauss(0, SIGMA)]            # incumbent's measured runs
    first, used = inc[0], 1
    if policy == "confirm":                       # baseline gets 3 seeds
        inc += [true + rng.gauss(0, SIGMA) for _ in range(2)]
        used += 2
    while used < RUNS:
        cand_true = true + rng.choice(EFFECTS)
        m = [cand_true + rng.gauss(0, SIGMA)]
        used += 1
        inc_score = st.mean(inc)
        if policy == "margin":                    # demand a 1-sigma margin
            keep = m[0] < inc_score - SIGMA
        else:
            keep = m[0] < inc_score
        if keep and policy == "confirm" and used + 2 <= RUNS:
            m += [cand_true + rng.gauss(0, SIGMA) for _ in range(2)]
            used += 2
            keep = st.mean(m) < inc_score
        if keep:
            true, inc = cand_true, m
    return first - st.mean(inc), 0.920 - true     # reported gain, true gain

# ── 2. Compare policies over many nights ─────────────────────────────────
print("policy     reported gain  true gain  inflation")
for policy in ("naive", "margin", "confirm"):
    rng = random.Random(1)
    out = [night(rng, policy) for _ in range(NIGHTS)]
    rep = st.mean(o[0] for o in out)
    tru = st.mean(o[1] for o in out)
    print("%-9s  %13.4f  %9.4f  %8.1fx" % (policy, rep, tru, rep / tru))
policy     reported gain  true gain  inflation
naive             0.0089     0.0020       4.5x
margin            0.0070     0.0008       9.2x
confirm           0.0065     0.0029       2.2x

The naive loop reports a gain of 0.0089 per night; the true improvement of the final config is 0.0020. The log over-states progress by a factor of 4.5. Two things are going on. The reported gain compares the final lucky score with a single baseline run that is itself noisy, and the kept configs include some that did nothing.

The margin rule looks sensible and does worse on the ratio: it reports 0.0070 against a true 0.0008. Why? A margin of one standard deviation (0.003) is larger than the true gain of a good idea (0.002), so it throws away most real improvements along with the false ones. This is a general trap: a threshold tuned to stop false accepts can silently stop true ones, and with a rare-good-ideas prior, true ones are what you are searching for. The confirmation rule keeps the largest true gain (0.0029) and has the smallest inflation (2.2 times). It pays for this by spending extra runs only on candidates that already look good, so most of the budget still goes to exploring.

Common misconception

"If the final p-value is below 0.05, the result is real." A p-value answers a question about one pre-specified comparison. A loop makes eighty-four comparisons a night and keeps the winners, so the question being asked is "what is the best of eighty-four?". At a 5% false-positive rate per test, four or five candidates that did nothing will look significant by chance alone. This is the peeking and multiple-comparisons trap of A0 chapter 15, and it is why the confirming step must use fresh seeds, not the runs that produced the lead. Reusing the original run in the confirmation mean would carry its luck along.

Paired tests over seeds

The cure for most of this is to compare candidate and baseline on the same seeds and test the differences. Two runs with the same seed share their initialisation and data order, so they share most of their luck: a seed that happens to give an easy shuffle makes both arms look better. Subtracting removes that shared part and leaves a much smaller noise. This is a paired test: instead of two samples of five numbers, you have one sample of five differences.

The test statistic divides the average difference by its standard error. It is the decision rule of the confirm step in Artifact 1, so it is worth taking apart: read it, see each part coloured, build it up one operation at a time, then plug in numbers.

Plug in numbers. The script simulates five seeds in which each seed's luck hits both arms alike (a shared standard deviation of 0.0028) and each arm adds a little of its own (0.0010), with a true gain of 0.0020. It prints one line per build-up step, then an exact test that makes no normality assumption, then what you would see if you ignored the pairing.

The idea

  1. for each seed, run baseline and candidate with that seed; d ← baseline − candidate
  2. d̄ ← mean of d; s_d ← sample sd of d; SE ← s_d ÷ sqrt(n); t ← d̄ ÷ SE
  3. sign-flip test: if there is no effect each d is as likely negative as positive, so flip signs in every possible way and count how often the mean is at least as extreme
  4. compare with the unpaired standard error, which ignores the shared luck
"""A paired test over seeds, one printed line per step of the equation.

Both arms use the same five seeds, so seed-level luck (data order, init)
is shared. Numbers are simulated: shared luck sd 0.0028, own noise sd
0.0010, true gain of the candidate 0.0020. Lower val_bpb is better.
"""
import itertools
import math
import random
import statistics as st

T_CRIT_DF4 = 2.776      # two-sided 95% critical value of t, 4 d.f.
rng = random.Random(11)

# ── 1. Five seeds, both arms; the seed's luck hits both arms alike ───────
base, cand = [], []
for seed in range(5):
    luck = rng.gauss(0, 0.0028)
    base.append(0.9200 + luck + rng.gauss(0, 0.0010))
    cand.append(0.9180 + luck + rng.gauss(0, 0.0010))
print("seed  baseline  candidate  gain (base - cand)")
d = [b - c for b, c in zip(base, cand)]
for i in range(5):
    print("%4d  %.4f    %.4f     %+.4f" % (i, base[i], cand[i], d[i]))

# ── 2. The paired statistic, step by step ────────────────────────────────
n = len(d)
d_bar = st.mean(d)                       # step 1: average the differences
s_d = st.stdev(d)                        # step 2: spread of the differences
se = s_d / math.sqrt(n)                  # step 3: standard error of d_bar
t = d_bar / se                           # step 4: how many SEs from zero
print("\nd_bar %+.4f  s_d %.4f  SE %.4f  t %.2f" % (d_bar, s_d, se, t))
print("95%% CI for the gain: [%+.4f, %+.4f]" %
      (d_bar - T_CRIT_DF4 * se, d_bar + T_CRIT_DF4 * se))

# ── 3. Exact sign-flip test: if no effect, each d_i is as likely - as + ──
flips = list(itertools.product((1, -1), repeat=n))
extreme = sum(abs(st.mean(f * x for f, x in zip(fl, d))) >= abs(d_bar)
              for fl in flips)
print("sign-flip p-value: %d / %d = %.3f" % (extreme, len(flips),
                                               extreme / len(flips)))

# ── 4. Ignoring the pairing (Welch, unpaired) is much less sharp ─────────
se_u = math.sqrt(st.variance(base) / n + st.variance(cand) / n)
print("unpaired SE %.4f  t %.2f  (paired SE %.4f)" %
      (se_u, (st.mean(base) - st.mean(cand)) / se_u, se))
seed  baseline  candidate  gain (base - cand)
   0  0.9170    0.9156     +0.0014
   1  0.9172    0.9165     +0.0007
   2  0.9224    0.9181     +0.0043
   3  0.9171    0.9149     +0.0022
   4  0.9253    0.9222     +0.0031

95% CI for the gain: [+0.0006, +0.0041]
sign-flip p-value: 2 / 32 = 0.062
unpaired SE 0.0022  t 1.09  (paired SE 0.0006)

Three lessons sit in this output. First, pairing is worth a great deal: the paired standard error is 0.0006 and the unpaired one is 0.0022, nearly four times larger, because the unpaired comparison treats each seed's shared luck as noise. The same data gives t = 3.73 paired and t = 1.09 unpaired. Second, the interval is honest about uncertainty: the gain is somewhere between +0.0006 and +0.0041, so "0.0023 better" must be written with that range beside it. Third, look at the sign-flip p-value, 2 of 32 or 0.062, which is above 0.05 even though all five gains are positive. With five seeds there are only 25 = 32 sign patterns, and the most extreme pair (all positive, all negative) already accounts for 2/32. With five seeds an exact two-sided test cannot go below 0.0625. The t-based interval is tighter because it assumes the gains are roughly normal. Use six or more seeds for an exact test, or state which test you used, and never report only one of the two.

The guide for this path asks for three seeds or more with a paired test. Three seeds can never give an exact two-sided p below 0.25, so treat three as the minimum for a confidence interval and ten as a comfortable number for a headline claim. Lab 1 implements this decision, and A6 chapter 4 covers the same ideas for model evaluations, including clustered questions and sample sizes.

The proxy and the thing you care about

So far the target has been the five-minute score. But Artifact 1's extension is about a different question: does the five-minute score predict what you actually want? A proxy is a cheap measurement standing in for an expensive one, and the loop optimises the proxy very hard. Whatever difference there is between proxy and target, the loop will find it and exploit it. There are four distinct sources of that gap, and the chart you must produce needs to separate them.

  1. Selection on noise. The winner's curse above: the kept change looks better than it is because it was selected for looking better. Re-measuring it removes this part. It exists even if the proxy and the target are the same quantity.
  2. Reusing the validation set. The validation data used by the keep rule is the same data on every run. Each keep tunes the model, a little, to that finite sample. After a hundred rounds the score on those documents is optimistic about the score on new ones. The remedy is a second split the loop never reads.
  3. Proxy-specific effects. Some changes help only because the run is short: a higher learning rate that converges quickly but plateaus higher, a smaller model that fits more steps in five minutes. These are real gains at this budget, and are not gains at a larger one.
  4. Distribution shift in the target. If the proxy uses one corpus and the target another, some of what the model learns is specific to the first.

Chapter 03 deals with the third source at the level of scale. Here we measure the first three together. The simulation below gives every experiment a true transfer effect that helps at any scale, a proxy-specific effect that helps only the short run, and independent measurement noise on each score. The loop sees only the proxy. A separate score on data and a budget the loop never reads is the held-out gain. All parameters are assumptions, chosen to show the shape of the effect and not to predict yours.

The idea

  1. per experiment: transfer effect g, proxy-only effect h
  2. proxy gain ← g + h + noise; held-out gain ← g + other noise
  3. the loop keeps the experiment if the proxy gain beats a bar of 0.004
  4. report, for the kept ones: mean proxy gain, mean held-out gain, mean gap and how many have negative held-out gain
"""Log proxy gain and held-out gain for every experiment, then compare.

Simulated, to show the shape of the effect (all numbers are assumptions):
  true transfer effect  g ~ N(0, 0.0020)   helps at any scale
  proxy-only effect     h ~ N(0.0005, 0.0030)  helps only the short run
  each measurement adds seed noise, sd 0.0030
Gains are baseline minus candidate val_bpb, so positive is better.
"""
import random
import statistics as st

N = 100                                   # experiments in the night
rng = random.Random(3)

# ── 1. One experiment: what the loop sees, and what it never sees ────────
def experiment():
    g = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.0020)
    h = rng.gauss(0.0005, 0.0030)
    proxy = g + h + rng.gauss(0, 0.0030)  # the five-minute score
    held = g + rng.gauss(0, 0.0030)       # a score the loop never reads
    return proxy, held

rows = [experiment() for _ in range(N)]

# ── 2. The loop keeps whatever the proxy says is better ──────────────────
kept = [(p, h) for p, h in rows if p > 0.004]     # a 4-milli-bpb bar
print("experiments %d, kept %d" % (N, len(kept)))
print("all   : mean proxy %+.4f  mean held-out %+.4f" %
      (st.mean(p for p, _ in rows), st.mean(h for _, h in rows)))
print("kept  : mean proxy %+.4f  mean held-out %+.4f" %
      (st.mean(p for p, _ in kept), st.mean(h for _, h in kept)))

# ── 3. The per-experiment gap, and how well the proxy predicts transfer ──
gaps = [p - h for p, h in kept]
print("kept gap (proxy - held-out): mean %+.4f, sd %.4f" %
      (st.mean(gaps), st.stdev(gaps)))
print("kept with a NEGATIVE held-out gain: %d of %d" %
      (sum(h < 0 for _, h in kept), len(kept)))
print("correlation, proxy vs held-out, all runs: %.2f" %
      st.correlation([p for p, _ in rows], [h for _, h in rows]))
experiments 100, kept 21
all   : mean proxy -0.0000  mean held-out -0.0003
kept  : mean proxy +0.0063  mean held-out +0.0005
kept gap (proxy - held-out): mean +0.0059, sd 0.0039
kept with a NEGATIVE held-out gain: 12 of 21
correlation, proxy vs held-out, all runs: 0.19

Across all 100 experiments the average proxy gain is zero, as it should be, because the ideas on average do nothing. The loop keeps 21 of them. Their average proxy gain is +0.0063, and their average held-out gain is +0.0005: about a twelfth of what the log claims. Twelve of the 21 kept experiments have a negative held-out gain. They were kept and they hurt. The correlation between proxy and held-out gain over all runs is only 0.19. The per-experiment gap (proxy minus held-out) averages +0.0059 with a spread of 0.0039, so it cannot be treated as a constant correction.

That gap column is the artifact's extension, and it earns its place in the run store for a simple reason. If the average gap among kept experiments is close to zero, the proxy is trustworthy for this task and the loop's chart is credible. If the gap grows through the night, the loop is learning the proxy, and a warning that fires on a rising gap is the "proxy-versus-held-out gap that warns before the loop overfits" the lab builds. See A7 chapter 16 on held-out evaluation, and chapter 04 for the schema that stores the two columns together.

What the held-out measurement should be

Two designs are common, and they answer different questions. A held-out split measures the same budget on documents the loop never touched. It tests sources 1 and 2 above and costs almost nothing, because evaluation is cheap next to training. A longer re-run trains a kept config for several times the budget and measures it on the held-out split. It also tests source 3, at a cost of one long run per kept experiment, which is why you only do it for the kept ones. The write-up of Artifact 1 should say which it used, because "held-out" with nothing else is ambiguous. The standards in the roadmap require scores "on data the loop never saw", and that is the minimum: the split is never read by the proposer, never printed into a log the agent reads, and never used by the keep rule.

Analogy: practice exams

A student who sits the same practice paper every night, and revises whichever topics lost marks, will improve on that paper faster than on the real exam. Some of the gain is knowledge, some is memorising that paper's quirks, and some is luck on the night. A fresh paper marks the knowledge alone.

Where it breaks down: a student can see the paper and learn the answers, while the training loop never sees the validation answers, only the score. The leak is through the selection of which edits to keep, not through the model reading the data. That makes the overfitting much slower and harder to notice, which is the reason to measure it.

A keep rule you can defend

Putting the pieces together gives a decision procedure whose parts you can already justify. Screen each candidate with a single run, against the incumbent's seed-matched run, as the cheap filter the loop needs to explore. Confirm any candidate that passes the screen on fresh, paired seeds, and keep it only if the interval for the gain excludes zero. Check the gap on each kept config using the held-out split, and log both numbers. Re-baseline now and then: re-run the incumbent on new seeds, because a long night of keeps drifts away from the noise floor you measured at the start. Each step has a cost in runs, and the budget has to be split between exploring and confirming; the simulation above suggests a good deal of the confirming budget is well spent. The numbers (screening threshold, seeds per confirmation) are choices to make with your measured noise floor in hand, and to write down before the night starts. Choosing them after seeing the results is a form of the peeking this chapter warns about.

Systems connection

Benchmarking a service has exactly the same problem. A single load test that shows p99 latency improving by 4% is not a result; the next run may show it worsening. Mature teams run a benchmark several times, compare against a baseline run in the same conditions, and flag a regression only when it exceeds the measured variance. Flaky tests are the sample-size version of the same fact. The noise floor is your run-to-run variance, a paired test is "compare against the baseline in the same conditions", and the held-out gap is the difference between a staging environment and production.

State of play, October 2026

The speedrun community already enforces this chapter's rules, which is a useful standard to copy. The modded-nanogpt README, which I read on 15 October 2026, requires that a new record show "a statistical significance level of p<0.01 that their mean val loss is ≤3.28", because of run-to-run variance. Systems-only changes that do not change the ML are exempt. Its target is a validation cross-entropy of 3.28 on FineWeb for a roughly 124-million-parameter model at the scale of GPT-2 small (a generative pre-trained Transformer, GPT) on 8 H100s; its Track 1 table had 92 numbered records, the latest dated 30 August 2026 at 0.665 minutes. For RE-Bench (Research Engineering Benchmark), the paper from the evaluation group METR (arXiv 2411.15114, revised May 2025) reports 7 environments and 71 eight-hour attempts by 61 human experts, and notes that results at a 2-hour budget favour agents while the 8-hour and 32-hour budgets favour humans: the budget changes the answer, which is the proxy problem again. What may change: the record count, the benchmark's versions and every score. The statistical rules above do not.

Key takeaways

  • The noise floor is the spread of scores when nothing changes. Measure it with ten or more baseline runs before trusting any keep, and record the step count with every run.
  • Keeping whatever scores lower selects luck. The log over-reports gains (4.5 times in the simulation) and the lucky score raises the bar for later, better ideas.
  • A paired test on shared seeds removes shared luck. It shrank the standard error nearly fourfold in the example. Report the interval, not only the p-value, and remember that five seeds cannot give an exact two-sided p below 0.0625.
  • A margin rule can discard real small gains. Confirming promising candidates on fresh seeds is the better use of extra runs.
  • The proxy gain and the held-out gain differ for four reasons: selection on noise, reuse of the validation set, proxy-specific effects and distribution shift. Log both gains for every experiment so the gap can be charted.
  • Write the keep rule, seed counts and thresholds down before the night starts.

Check your understanding

Ten baseline runs give a seed-to-seed standard deviation of 0.003. You will compare a candidate against the baseline with five seeds per arm, unpaired. About what gain is detectable at roughly 95% confidence?

  1. 0.0006
  2. 0.0020
  3. 0.0039
  4. 0.0086
The standard error of a difference of two means is sd × √(2/n) = 0.003 × 0.632 = 0.0019, and about 1.96 of those is 0.0037 to 0.0039. 0.0086 is the figure for a single run per arm; 0.0020 is the standard error itself, not the gain you can detect.

A candidate that does nothing is run once and beats the incumbent by luck. What is the lasting damage beyond the wrong keep?

  1. The incumbent's score of record is now a lucky low value, so every later candidate has a higher bar, and real improvements can be rejected.
  2. The loop stops making proposals.
  3. The seed is permanently changed.
  4. Nothing: the next run corrects it.
The recorded best is a single draw from a distribution, and a low one. Later runs of the incumbent config would regress towards the true mean, but the loop does not re-run it. The loop does not stop and the seed is unaffected.

A paired comparison on five seeds gives t = 3.7, but an exact sign-flip test gives a two-sided p of 0.0625. What is the best reading?

  1. The data are contradictory, so discard them.
  2. The sign-flip test is wrong, because t = 3.7 is significant.
  3. The candidate certainly has no effect.
  4. The exact test cannot go below 2/32 with five seeds, so 0.0625 is the best it can do even when all gains are positive; report the interval and add seeds.
With five seeds there are 32 equally likely sign patterns under the null, and the two most extreme ones already make up 2/32. That is a limit of the test at this sample size, not evidence for or against an effect. The t-test and the exact test answer slightly different questions (one assumes normality) and are not in conflict.

Why does a paired comparison have a much smaller standard error than an unpaired one in the example?

  1. It uses more runs.
  2. Both arms share each seed's luck (initialisation, data order), and subtracting per seed cancels it.
  3. It ignores outliers.
  4. It assumes the candidate is better.
The run counts are identical in both analyses. Pairing helps only when the shared component is large relative to each arm's own noise: with no shared luck the two standard errors would be the same. Outliers are not removed, and nothing is assumed about the sign of the effect.

In gap.py 12 of the 21 kept experiments have a negative held-out gain. Which change to the loop most directly addresses this?

  1. Lowering the proxy keep bar from 0.004 to 0.002.
  2. Running the night for twice as long.
  3. Confirming each kept change on fresh seeds and on a held-out split the loop never reads, and logging the gap.
  4. Using a smaller model.
The experiments were kept because their proxy scores were lucky or proxy-specific. Only a measurement independent of the proxy can see that. A lower bar keeps more of the same kind of change, and a longer night produces more keeps and more of the same bias. Model size does not touch the selection problem.

The margin rule in winner.py rejected many real improvements. Why?

  1. Margins always make a test less powerful than no margin at any sample size, by definition.
  2. The margin (one noise sd, 0.003) was larger than the true gain of a good idea (0.002), so one noisy run rarely cleared it.
  3. The simulation had no good ideas.
  4. The margin was applied to the wrong sign.
A margin is a threshold on a noisy single measurement. If the true effect is smaller than the threshold, the chance of clearing it is small, and real gains are lost along with false ones. The simulation did include good ideas (10%), and the sign was right: the rule demanded the score be lower by the margin.
