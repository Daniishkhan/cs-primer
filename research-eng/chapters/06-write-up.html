The Write-Up

A research result that cannot be read in five minutes and rerun with one command does not count. This chapter covers the two-to-six-page report that ends each artifact: the one-sentence claim and what it must contain, the four checks a lab reviewer applies first, how to write negative results, and how to make the whole thing reproducible. It closes the course by turning the loop's log into something another person can trust.

Why this matters

This chapter serves the last step of Artifact 1: the write-up named in its gate, "a two-to-three-page write-up with seeds and the noise floor". The runs, the paired tests and the proxy-versus-held-out chart of chapters 01 to 05 are worth little if the reader cannot find the claim, the budget and the uncertainty in the first minute. After this chapter you can draft the claim sentence for your artifact from its numbers, audit your own record against the standards a reviewer will check, and lay out the repository so that a stranger can reproduce the headline result. It builds on A0 chapter 18 (experiment reports) and A0 chapter 16 (how a reader checks claims), and the same shape is reused for Artifacts 2 to 4.

What the reader does first

A reviewer at a lab opens a write-up with a narrow job: decide in a few minutes whether this is worth the next hour. They read the title and the first sentence, look at the main figure, and then go straight to the places where weak work hides: how many seeds, what the baseline was, what the budget was, and whether the headline number was measured on data the search could see. If all four are answered on the first page, the reader trusts the rest more. If any is missing, they assume the worst.

The roadmap that this course follows names four conditions, taken from Weco AI's account of its AIDE² system, to apply "whenever you claim an improvement". I read Weco's post (dated 14 July 2026) and the accompanying arXiv report (2609.26457, submitted 22 September 2026) on 15 October 2026. The post lists four conditions for what it calls Level-1 evidence of recursive self-improvement, in these words: "a fair human baseline", "a sustained multi-step trend", "generalization beyond the optimized measurement" and "a fixed physical budget". They are useful far outside that setting, because each blocks a specific way a loop can fool its author:

The roadmap adds evaluator rules for later artifacts: the evaluator outside the agent's write boundary, public and private splits, hacking rate reported next to the score, and three or more seeds with confidence intervals and a paired test. For Artifact 1, the last of these is already your obligation (chapter 02).

The six-part template

The roadmap prescribes one shape for every write-up, two to six pages, so that reviewers learn to read yours quickly. I find it easiest to think of it as six answers to six questions a sceptical reader asks, in the order they ask them.

  1. One-sentence claim, with its number and its budget. What you found, how big, with what uncertainty, at what cost. Everything else supports this sentence.
  2. What was replicated, with the exact command and the original's figure beside yours. For Artifact 1: the autoresearch loop on the same kind of task, your chart next to Karpathy's progress plot. Matching the original before extending it is what makes the extension believable.
  3. The extension: hypothesis, design, result. State, before the results, what you expected the proxy-versus-held-out gap to do and why. Then the design (how many experiments, which held-out split, which statistics), then the result.
  4. Negative results and what you would try next. What did not work, which checks failed, what you do not know.
  5. Integrity: hacking rate, seeds, costs, hardware. How many seeds, the noise floor, what the evaluator could and could not be influenced by, total dollars and GPU-hours, the GPU model.
  6. Repro: one command, one config, a pinned environment. A reader should be able to run a single command and regenerate the headline figure.

Writing the claim sentence

The first sentence, the claim sentence, is a small contract: if a number, interval or budget is not in it, the reader has no way to hold you to it. The anatomy below has five clauses, and each one answers a question the reader would otherwise have to ask.

The script turns a results record into that sentence and then audits the record against four of the standards in the table above. The per-seed values are invented for the demonstration: they are not results from any real run, and the sentence they produce is a template, not a finding. The point is the mechanics: the confidence interval is a paired bootstrap over seeds, and the audit refuses to pass a record that lacks a budget, a matched baseline, three seeds or a held-out score.

The idea

  1. per seed: gain ← baseline score − loop score; mean gain ← average of gains
  2. interval ← resample the gains with replacement 10,000 times; take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the means
  3. sentence ← budget, gain, interval, seeds, held-out share, integrity statement
  4. audit: budget stated? baseline given the same attempts? three or more seeds? a held-out score?
"""Turn a results table into the write-up's first sentence, and audit it.

Per-seed numbers are invented for the demonstration (val_bpb, lower is
better). The audit encodes the artifact standards of this course.
"""
import random
import statistics as st

# ── 1. A results record: what a write-up must be able to state ───────────
good = {
    "budget": "100 runs x 5 min on one H100 (8.3 h of training)",
    "baseline_runs": 100, "loop_runs": 100,     # same number of attempts
    "base": [0.9751, 0.9742, 0.9760, 0.9748, 0.9755],
    "loop": [0.9702, 0.9695, 0.9716, 0.9699, 0.9709],
    "heldout_gain": [0.0031, 0.0028, 0.0040, 0.0030, 0.0033],
    "hacking_rate": "0 of 100 kept changes touched the evaluator",
}
bad = dict(good, baseline_runs=10, base=good["base"][:1],
           loop=good["loop"][:2], heldout_gain=None, budget=None)

# ── 2. Paired bootstrap CI for the gain over seeds ───────────────────────
def gain_ci(base, loop, reps=10000, seed=0):
    d = [b - l for b, l in zip(base, loop)]
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    means = sorted(st.mean(rng.choices(d, k=len(d))) for _ in range(reps))
    return st.mean(d), means[int(0.025 * reps)], means[int(0.975 * reps)]

# ── 3. The first sentence of the write-up, from the numbers ──────────────
def claim(r):
    m, lo, hi = gain_ci(r["base"], r["loop"])
    h = st.mean(r["heldout_gain"])
    return ("With %s, the loop lowers val_bpb by %.4f (95%% CI %.4f to "
            "%.4f, %d seeds); %.4f of that held out. %s." %
            (r["budget"], m, lo, hi, len(r["base"]), h, r["hacking_rate"]))

# ── 4. The audit: one line per standard ──────────────────────────────────
def audit(r):
    checks = [
        ("budget stated up front", bool(r["budget"])),
        ("baseline gets the same number of attempts",
         r["baseline_runs"] == r["loop_runs"]),
        ("three or more seeds", len(r["loop"]) >= 3),
        ("score on data the loop never saw", bool(r["heldout_gain"])),
    ]
    for name, ok in checks:
        print("  [%s] %s" % ("ok" if ok else "FAIL", name))

print(claim(good))
print("audit of the good record:"); audit(good)
print("audit of the bad record:"); audit(bad)
With 100 runs x 5 min on one H100 (8.3 h of training), the loop lowers val_bpb by 0.0047 (95% CI 0.0045 to 0.0049, 5 seeds); 0.0032 of that held out. 0 of 100 kept changes touched the evaluator.

  [ok] budget stated up front
  [ok] baseline gets the same number of attempts
  [ok] three or more seeds
  [ok] score on data the loop never saw
audit of the bad record:

  [FAIL] baseline gets the same number of attempts
  [FAIL] three or more seeds
  [FAIL] score on data the loop never saw

The sentence is dense on purpose. A reader who stops there knows the budget (100 runs of five minutes on one GPU), the effect and its uncertainty (a gain of 0.0047 with a 95% interval of 0.0045 to 0.0049 over five seeds), how much of it survived on data the loop never saw (0.0032 of the 0.0047, about two thirds), and a statement of integrity. Compare it with the weak versions people actually write: "The agent improved val_bpb by 0.005" omits the budget, the seeds and the held-out check; "The loop found significant improvements" omits every number. The audit's second block shows what a record that cannot support the sentence looks like: no budget, a baseline given a tenth of the attempts, one or two seeds and no held-out score. All four checks fail.

One bootstrap caveat belongs in your own write-up: with five seeds a bootstrap interval can be too narrow, because resampling five values cannot produce more variety than the five contain. The interval of 0.0045 to 0.0049 here is narrow because the invented gains are nearly identical. With real data, report the t-interval of chapter 02 alongside it, and say which you used.

The figure and the table

Artifact 1 needs one headline figure: proxy gain against held-out gain, one point per experiment (see the chart in chapter 02). Treat it with the same discipline as the sentence. Draw both axes on the same scale so the diagonal means "the proxy told the truth". Mark the kept experiments differently from the discarded ones. State the number of experiments in the caption. If you fit a line, give the slope and its interval. And report the number the chart exists to expose: the share of kept experiments whose held-out gain is not positive. Pair it with a small table of runs, seeds and costs.

Negative results

Section 4 is where write-ups most often go soft, and it is where trust is earned. A negative result is not a failure of the work; it is information about the method. Three kinds are worth stating plainly:

  • The extension did not appear. "The gap between proxy and held-out gain was not larger for late experiments than for early ones (slope 0.01, interval including zero)." That is a finding. It says the proxy held up for this task and budget.
  • A check failed. "Three replays of the kept runs differed from the originals by more than the noise floor; the cause was an unseeded data shuffle, fixed in commit X; all numbers below are from after the fix." This tells the reader the record is honest.
  • A limit of scope. "All 100 experiments used one model size and one GPU; I make no claim about larger runs." This is the proxy-transfer caveat of chapter 03 stated as a fact about the study.

Say what you would try next as a testable statement, not a wish: "Re-run the 12 kept experiments at four times the budget to test the third source of gap from chapter 02."

Common misconception

"A more convincing write-up has more results." A reviewer is convinced by the fewest claims that are fully supported. Ten charts of uneven quality raise ten questions; one chart with its seeds, its baseline and its budget raises none. Write one claim per artifact. Put the rest in the repository's results/ folder where a curious reader can find it, and leave out any result whose method you would not be comfortable explaining to the reviewer.

Reproduction: one command

The roadmap's release recipe is specific: publish on GitHub with the write-up as the README, a results/ folder with raw logs, and a short post that links both, tagging the maintainers of the repository you built on. Reproducibility (another person getting your result from your materials) has three layers, and the third is the one people skip.

  1. The record. The run store of chapter 04: every row with its config, seed, code version and environment.
  2. The environment. Pinned library versions, the GPU model and driver, the data version (a hash of the shard list). The lockfile belongs in the repository.
  3. The command. One command that regenerates the headline figure from the raw logs, with no manual steps. If it needs a GPU for a full re-run, provide a second command that regenerates the figure from the stored rows alone (minutes, CPU). Reviewers run the second one.

Test it the way a stranger would: clone into an empty directory on a machine you have not used, run the command, and compare the figure with the one in the write-up. Whatever breaks is what the reviewer will hit.

Analogy: a good bug report

The best bug reports state what happened, what you expected, the smallest steps that reproduce it, and the environment. Maintainers fix them first, because they cost the least to act on. A write-up is a bug report about the world: here is a claim, here is the evidence, here is the one command that reproduces it, here is where it might be wrong.

Where it breaks down: a bug either reproduces or it does not, and a bug report can be tiny. A research claim is statistical: a reproduction will land near your number, not on it, and the report has to say how near counts as agreeing.

Systems connection

The shape of a write-up is the shape of an incident post-mortem or a release note: a one-line impact statement with a number, a timeline, the cause, what was checked, what is still unknown, and follow-ups. Engineering teams settled on this because readers arrive with little time and a lot of scepticism. The same applies to a pull request description to a project you want to contribute to; the roadmap's rule that a substantive pull request "carries a result, not a feature" (a number, a chart and the command that reproduces it) is this template in miniature.

State of play, October 2026

Read on 15 October 2026. Weco AI's account of AIDE² (blog post dated 14 July 2026; arXiv 2609.26457, "Recursive self-improvement of AI research agents", submitted 22 September 2026) describes an eight-day autonomous run of 100 outer-loop steps that produced seven successive improved versions, with about nine in ten proposed changes rejected. The authors place it at Level 1 of their four-level scale and say it is not at Level 2, because the improved inner-loop agent did not clearly become a better outer-loop agent. They report that gains transferred to held-out benchmarks, and in the abstract that a reward-hacking rate on a separate held-out task family fell from 55% to 32% during the run. The post also lists its own caveats: the evolved agent is complex and contains some dead code, and the loop's statistical anti-reward-hacking layer had a bug and had no effect in the final agent. Everything here is reported by the company that built the system, using its own hand-tuned agent as the baseline, and I found no independent replication in the sources I read. Treat it as an example of a well-structured claim to imitate, not as independently verified. modded-nanogpt requires run logs showing p<0.01 for a new record (chapter 02). What may change: every figure in the reports, any replications, and the scale's definitions.

Key takeaways

  • Reviewers check four things first: a fair baseline with the same budget, a sustained trend, scores on data the loop never saw, and a fixed stated budget. Answer all four on page one.
  • Use one six-part template every time: claim, replication, extension, negative results, integrity, reproduction.
  • The claim sentence holds the budget, the effect with its interval and seeds, the held-out share and an integrity statement. If a number is missing, the reader cannot hold you to it.
  • Negative results, failed checks and scope limits build trust. State them as facts, with a testable next step.
  • Make reproduction one command, with a CPU-only path from stored rows to the headline figure, and test it from a clean clone.
  • Be clear about what is self-reported and what you verified; Weco's own results are a model of structure, not independent evidence.

Check your understanding

Which first sentence is closest to what a reviewer wants?

  1. "The agent found significant improvements over the baseline."
  2. "The loop reduced val_bpb by 0.005."
  3. "With 100 five-minute runs on one H100, the loop lowers val_bpb by 0.0047 (95% CI 0.0045 to 0.0049, 5 seeds); 0.0032 held out."
  4. "After extensive experimentation, the approach proved effective."
Only the third states a budget, an effect, an interval, the seeds and the held-out share. The first and fourth have no numbers; the second has one number and no budget, seeds or interval.

Your loop made 100 attempts. Your hand-tuned baseline got 10. Which of Weco's four conditions fails?

  1. A fair baseline: the same budget and the same number of attempts.
  2. A sustained trend.
  3. Generalisation beyond the optimised measurement.
  4. A fixed physical budget.
The loop and the baseline differ in attempts, which is exactly what the first condition fixes. The other three concern trend, data the loop did not see and the stated spend, which this scenario does not violate.

Why does the write-up report the share of kept experiments with non-positive held-out gain?

  1. It makes the result look bigger.
  2. It replaces the need for a baseline.
  3. It is required by the Rust runner.
  4. It states directly how often the proxy was fooled, which the headline gain hides.
The average gain can be positive while many kept changes did nothing or hurt. The share exposes that. It does not replace the baseline and has nothing to do with the runner's language.

A five-seed bootstrap interval for the gain comes out very narrow. What is the best response?

  1. Report it with confidence; narrow is good.
  2. Check against a t-interval and report both, because resampling five values cannot show more spread than they contain.
  3. Remove the interval.
  4. Add more bootstrap resamples to widen it.
More resamples do not add information: the spread of the data is fixed. A t-interval with n−1 degrees of freedom is wider at small n and usually more honest. Removing the interval hides the uncertainty.

Which statement about Weco's AIDE² results is supported by what I read?

  1. They were independently replicated by three labs.
  2. They show the loop reached Level 2, ignition.
  3. They are reported by the company that built the system, who say Level 1 is reached and ignition is not demonstrated, and list caveats including a bug in the anti-hacking layer.
  4. They show reward hacking was eliminated.
The post and report are by the system's authors and I found no independent replication. They say ignition is not demonstrated and report a hacking rate that fell (55% to 32% in the abstract), not one that reached zero.

Why provide a second reproduction command that regenerates the headline figure from stored rows on a CPU?

  1. Because reviewers rarely have a GPU or the budget to redo your runs, but can check the analysis from your raw logs in minutes.
  2. Because the GPU command is unreliable.
  3. Because figures cannot be made on a GPU.
  4. Because it removes the need to store raw logs.
The analysis path is the part most readers will actually run. It still needs the raw logs, so the fourth option is backwards. The GPU command may be perfectly reliable; it is just expensive.
