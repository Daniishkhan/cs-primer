Benchmarks for Research Agents

Five benchmarks are used to judge research agents in 2025 and 2026: MLE-bench, PaperBench, RE-Bench, FML-Bench and KernelBench. This chapter says what each one actually asks an agent to do, how it is scored, what its cheaper Lite version gives up, and how much a score on a few dozen tasks can tell you.

Why this matters

Every artifact in this path is scored on a benchmark, and the choice of benchmark decides what your result can claim. This chapter serves Artifact 2 (three FML-Bench tasks, with MLE-bench Lite as the natural cross-check), Artifact 3 (a KernelBench subset) and, later, the six-task mix of Artifact 4. After it you can say what each benchmark measures and how it is graded, pick a subset you can afford, estimate how large a difference your subset can detect, and read a leaderboard number without taking its error bar for more than it is. How to build a benchmark is A6 Building a Domain Benchmark, and how benchmarks leak is A6 Contamination, Saturation & Decay; this chapter is about using the ones research-agent papers already depend on.

These benchmarks differ in what the agent produces and who or what scores it. That second question is the one that determines how the benchmark can be gamed (chapter 03).

What each one measures

MLE-bench: can it do the whole Kaggle workflow?

MLE-bench has 75 Kaggle competitions. Because Kaggle does not release its held-out test sets, the benchmark builds its own train and test split from the public training data and supplies a grading script for each competition. Scores are compared with the human leaderboard, so an agent earns a bronze, silver or gold medal, or lands above or below the median. The paper’s headline is that the best setup it tried, o1-preview with the AIDE scaffold of chapter 02, reached at least a Kaggle bronze medal in 16.9% of competitions. The repository defines the Lite version as the 22 competitions in its Low complexity split, about 158 GB of data against 3.3 TB for the full set, and recommends at least three seeds per evaluation because “the task and grading are deterministic, but agents and LLMs are high-variance”.

Its repository lists known validity issues, which is the sort of caveat to read before you build on any benchmark: in the Lite set, a field in random-acts-of-pizza leaks the outcome so that trivial perfect prediction is possible, the prepare script for ranzcr-clip-catheter-line-classification produces missing sample-submission columns, and several competitions have crowded leaderboards with little difference between top and median.

PaperBench: can it replicate a paper?

PaperBench asks an agent to replicate 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight and Oral papers from scratch: understand the contributions, build a codebase, run the experiments. Each paper has a hierarchical rubric, co-developed with the original authors, that breaks the task into 8,316 individually gradable items. An LLM judge applies the rubric and the paper evaluates the judge separately. At the time of the paper (April 2025) the best agent, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New) with open-source scaffolding, reached an average replication score of 21.0%, and top ML PhD students attempting a subset did better. Because a model grades the rubric, everything in A6 LLM-as-Judge applies to it.

RE-Bench: how does it compare with human experts?

RE-Bench (METR) has seven open-ended research-engineering environments, with data from 71 eight-hour attempts by 61 experts. In its abstract, 82% of expert attempts scored above zero and 24% matched or exceeded the reference solutions. With a two-hour budget the best AI agents scored four times higher than the human experts; at eight hours humans narrowly beat the top agent, and at 32 hours humans reached twice the top agent’s score. The abstract adds that one agent wrote a Triton kernel faster than any human expert’s, and that agents generate and test solutions over ten times faster than humans at much lower cost. The lesson for design is that the time budget changes the answer: a benchmark comparison at one budget can reverse at another.

FML-Bench: how does the search behave?

FML-Bench (chapter 02) has 18 tasks over 10 areas, and a Lite set of 8 that drops the continual-learning and unlearning tasks because of high variance and large metric scale. The repository says the agent ranking on Lite “closely tracks” the full benchmark. Unlike the others, it scores the process: 12 metrics across exploration, generalisation, reliability, efficiency and cost, so you can ask how an agent reached a score, not only what it was. An earlier version of the paper (arXiv 2510.10472) lives on the repository’s legacy branch, so check which version a quoted number comes from.

KernelBench: is the speed-up real?

Chapter 04 covered it. The point for this chapter is that its validity has been revised in public, with KernelBench-Verified and the v0.1 update, and a number quoted without its protocol is hard to interpret.

Analogy: the test drive

A full benchmark is a long road test: city, motorway, hills, a week of weather. A Lite subset is a ten-minute drive around the block. It tells you whether the engine starts and which of two cars is much better, and it is cheap enough to do before every change. It cannot tell you about the hills.

Where it breaks down: a short drive is a random sample of a road you know. A Lite subset is a particular set of tasks chosen for cost, and what was dropped (the high-variance tasks, in FML-Bench’s case) may be exactly the cases where agents differ.

What a small benchmark can tell you

Lite sets exist because the full benchmarks are expensive: you have a total budget of a few hundred dollars for Artifact 2, not a few thousand. But fewer tasks means wider error bars, and the tasks are the unit that matters, not the number of runs. Consider an agent that passes 36% of n pass-or-fail tasks, close to the 36.4% that the AIDE paper reports for o1-preview with AIDE on MLE-bench Lite. How uncertain is that? The Wilson interval is a standard 95% confidence interval for a proportion, better behaved than the textbook formula when the count is small or the proportion is near 0 or 1; A6 Statistics for Evals derives the usual approximations. The second half of the script asks the paired question: how large a gap between two agents can n tasks detect, if the agents differ on a fraction D of tasks?

The idea

  1. Wilson interval for p with n tasks: centre and half-width from p, n and z = 1.96
  2. print the interval for the benchmark sizes in this chapter
  3. paired: if two agents disagree on a share D of tasks, the standard error of the win-rate difference is at most √(D ÷ n); the smallest detectable gap is about 1.96 times that
"""How much can a benchmark with few tasks tell you?

Wilson 95% interval for a pass rate over n tasks, and the smallest gap
between two agents that a PAIRED test on n tasks can see, assuming the
agents disagree on a fraction D of tasks (an ASSUMPTION; measure yours).

    python3 bench_noise.py
"""
import math

Z = 1.96                  # 95% two-sided


# ── 1. Wilson interval for a proportion ───────────────────────────
def wilson(p, n):
    centre = (p + Z * Z / (2 * n)) / (1 + Z * Z / n)
    root = math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + Z * Z / (4 * n * n))
    half = Z * root / (1 + Z * Z / n)
    return centre - half, centre + half


print("an agent passing 36% of n pass-or-fail tasks:")
SIZES = [("FML-bench-Lite", 8), ("FML-bench", 18), ("MLE-bench Lite", 22),
         ("MLE-bench", 75), ("KernelBench L1", 100)]
for name, n in SIZES:
    lo, hi = wilson(0.36, n)
    print(f"  {name:15s} n={n:3d}  [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}]  width {hi - lo:.2f}")

# ── 2. Smallest paired gap detectable (normal approximation) ──────
D = 0.30                  # fraction of tasks where the two agents differ
print("smallest detectable difference in win rate, paired, D = 0.30:")
for n in (8, 18, 22, 75, 250):
    se = math.sqrt(D / n)         # sd of (wins - losses)/n at most this
    print(f"  n={n:3d}  about {Z * se:.2f}")

  FML-bench-Lite  n=  8  [0.13, 0.68]  width 0.55
  FML-bench       n= 18  [0.18, 0.59]  width 0.41
  MLE-bench Lite  n= 22  [0.19, 0.57]  width 0.37
  MLE-bench       n= 75  [0.26, 0.47]  width 0.21

On 22 tasks, a 36% pass rate has a 95% interval from 0.19 to 0.57, about 38 percentage points wide. On 75 tasks the interval narrows to 0.26 to 0.47, and on 100 tasks to 0.27 to 0.46. FML-Bench’s tasks are not pass-or-fail (they carry a continuous normalised improvement), so the 8-task and 18-task rows show only the effect of the task count, with the caveat that a continuous score carries more information per task than a yes or no. In the paired half, with the agents differing on 30% of tasks (an assumption you should measure rather than copy), 22 tasks can detect only gaps of about 0.23, and 75 tasks about 0.12. Two agents that differ by 10 points on MLE-bench Lite are, from the task count alone, indistinguishable.

An error bar over seeds is not an error bar over tasks

Leaderboards usually report a mean and a standard error over seeds: several runs of the agent on the same benchmark. That measures how repeatable the score is on those exact tasks. It does not measure what would happen if the tasks were different, which is what you need if you want to say something about “ML engineering” and not about these 22 competitions. The toy below separates the two with a made-up population of competition difficulties. It runs a 22-task benchmark with three seeds and reports the seed standard error, then draws 2,000 different 22-task benchmarks from the same population.

"""A '+/-' over seeds is not a '+/-' over tasks.

An agent has some chance p_c of earning a medal on competition c. A
benchmark is a fixed set of 22 competitions. Run it with 3 seeds and the
standard error across seeds says how reliable THIS benchmark's score is.
It says nothing about how much the score would move if the 22 competitions
had been chosen differently. The p_c are a made-up Beta(1, 2) population.

    python3 seeds_vs_tasks.py
"""
import random
from statistics import mean, stdev

N_TASKS, SEEDS, DRAWS = 22, 3, 2000
rng = random.Random(11)
population = [rng.betavariate(1, 2) for _ in range(5000)]   # chances p_c


def run_benchmark(tasks):
    """Score of one 3-seed run: medal rate on each seed."""
    return [mean(rng.random() < p for p in tasks) for _ in range(SEEDS)]


# ── 1. One fixed benchmark: error bar over seeds only ─────────────
bench = rng.sample(population, N_TASKS)
scores = run_benchmark(bench)
sem = stdev(scores) / SEEDS ** 0.5
print(f"one benchmark, {SEEDS} seeds: {mean(scores):.3f} +/- {sem:.3f} (SEM)")

# ── 2. Many benchmarks of 22 tasks from the same population ───────
means = [mean(run_benchmark(rng.sample(population, N_TASKS)))
         for _ in range(DRAWS)]
print(f"{DRAWS} different 22-task benchmarks: mean {mean(means):.3f}, "
      f"spread (sd) {stdev(means):.3f}")
print(f"true rate in the population: {mean(population):.3f}")
one benchmark, 3 seeds: 0.424 +/- 0.055 (SEM)
2000 different 22-task benchmarks: mean 0.334, spread (sd) 0.072
true rate in the population: 0.335

The seed-based standard error is 0.055. The spread of the score across different 22-task benchmarks is 0.072, and the mean over those draws (0.334) matches the population’s true rate (0.335). So in this toy the two sources of uncertainty are of similar size, and the seed error bar, which here comes from three numbers, understates the total. Notice also that the single benchmark in the first line happened to score 0.424, well above the true rate: one benchmark is one draw. For Artifact 2 this means the three FML-Bench tasks are a sample of three tasks, and the write-up should say its conclusions are about those three, with an interval that reflects both seeds and tasks, rather than imply a result for the benchmark as a whole.

Common misconception

“The Lite version ranks agents the same way as the full benchmark, so Lite results are as good.” The FML-bench authors report that agent rankings on Lite closely track the full benchmark. That is a claim about order, not about the size of differences or the confidence you can place in them. A ranking that matches on average can still hide a flip between two close agents, and eight tasks cannot resolve small gaps (the table above). Use Lite to iterate and the largest subset you can afford to report.

Choosing for your artifacts

  • Artifact 2. Pick three FML-Bench tasks before looking at results, ideally at least one you expect to be dense and one sparse, using the density idea of chapter 02. The paper’s own task grouping is post hoc; if you borrow a group, say so. Run three seeds, and keep a note of the cost of each run (the paper allows each validation run 40 minutes on one A100-80GB). Consider MLE-bench Lite only as a sanity check, since its data (158 GB) and its leaks (the pizza competition) cost time.
  • Artifact 3. Use at least 20 KernelBench tasks across Levels 1 and 2, and use your own baseline timing on your own card, since the repository warns that baselines depend on hardware and software versions.
  • Artifact 4. The six-task mix pairs FML-Bench, autoresearch-style tasks and one kernel pair under a fixed dollar budget; the benchmark choices of Artifacts 2 and 3 carry over.
  • All of them. Record the benchmark version or commit. FML-Bench has already had two public versions, KernelBench has a v0.1 revision, and MLE-bench’s leaderboard is closed to new submissions as of its README date.

Systems connection

Benchmarks in this chapter are load tests for agents. The same discipline applies as for benchmarking a service: a synthetic workload is only as good as its resemblance to real traffic, you need enough requests to see a difference between two builds, and you must separate the variance of the system under test (seeds) from the variance of the workload (tasks). The 40-minute-per-run limit in FML-bench plays the role of a request timeout, and pinning the benchmark commit plays the role of pinning a dependency.

Key takeaways

  • MLE-bench (75 Kaggle competitions) is scored against human leaderboards; PaperBench (20 papers, 8,316 rubric items) by an LLM judge; RE-Bench (7 environments) against human experts at fixed time budgets; FML-Bench (18 tasks) by hidden test metrics plus process metrics; KernelBench (250 tasks) by correctness and speed.
  • What judges the score decides how the benchmark can be gamed: a leaderboard, a model judge, a timer.
  • Lite subsets (22 competitions for MLE-bench, 8 tasks for FML-Bench) are cheaper and track the full ranking, but their intervals are wide and small gaps are invisible.
  • A 36% pass rate is [0.19, 0.57] on 22 tasks and [0.26, 0.47] on 75.
  • An error bar over seeds does not include the variation from which tasks were chosen. Say which one you report, and pin the benchmark version.

Which benchmark is scored mainly by an LLM judge applying a rubric?

  1. MLE-bench
  2. KernelBench
  3. PaperBench

PaperBench applies hierarchical rubrics with an LLM judge. MLE-bench compares with human leaderboards, KernelBench uses tests and timing, RE-Bench uses task scores against human experts.

A paper compares two agents on MLE-bench Lite (22 competitions) and finds one 8 points higher. If the agents differ on about 30% of competitions, what is the right reading?

  1. The gap is below the roughly 23-point gap that 22 tasks could reliably detect, so it is not established
  2. The gap is significant because 8 is more than 0
  3. The gap proves the better agent is better on the full benchmark
  4. The gap is meaningless because Lite is invalid
From the script, 22 tasks detect only gaps of about 0.23 under this assumption. An 8-point gap is within what task sampling alone can produce, though not evidence of no difference.

RE-Bench found agents scoring four times the experts at two hours but experts ahead at eight. What does this say about benchmark design?

  1. Agents are better than humans
  2. Humans are better than agents
  3. Time limits do not matter
  4. The comparison depends on the time budget, so a budget must be stated with any head-to-head claim
The ranking reversed with the budget. A claim like “agents beat experts” is incomplete without it.

In the seeds-versus-tasks toy, why is the seed standard error (0.055) an incomplete error bar for a claim about ML engineering in general?

  1. It uses too many seeds
  2. It measures repeatability on these 22 tasks, not variation from which tasks were chosen
  3. Seeds do not affect the score
  4. It should be larger than the task spread
The seed error bar holds the tasks fixed. A claim about the wider class of tasks also carries uncertainty from the choice of tasks, here about 0.072.

FML-bench reports that Lite “closely tracks” the full ranking. For Artifact 2 you have three tasks. Which statement is justified?

  1. Three tasks are as good as 18 if rankings match
  2. Rankings on three tasks cannot be compared
  3. Your results are about those three tasks, with an interval that reflects both seeds and tasks, and no claim about the full benchmark
  4. You should add tasks until the gap is significant
The rank-tracking claim concerns the authors’ 8-task Lite set, not your three. Adding tasks until a gap is significant is a way to manufacture a result; choose the tasks first.

Why record the benchmark version or commit with every result?

  1. Benchmarks are revised (FML-bench has two public versions, KernelBench a v0.1 update), so numbers from different versions may not be comparable
  2. Because licences require it
  3. Because version numbers affect the model’s weights
  4. Because it makes runs faster
Tasks, problem sizes and graders change between versions. Without the version, two numbers can look comparable and not be.
