Start Here

This course is the middle of the artifact track. It teaches the two things that decide whether a research-agent result is believable: the harness that runs the search, and the evaluator that scores it. This page says what Artifacts 2 and 3 need from you and which chapters, here and in other courses, to read first.

Why this matters

Artifacts 2 and 3 are where the path first produces a number that a researcher at a lab could cite, and where that number is most likely to be wrong. An agent that searches over experiments will find every gap in the way you score it. So this course is half about building the search (the harness) and half about building a scorer the search cannot cheat (the evaluator). After this page you will know the gate each artifact must pass, the order to read the chapters in, and which parts of the earlier courses you are expected to remember. After the whole course you can build the Artifact 2 agent and the Artifact 3 kit.

Two artifacts, one idea

The path exists to produce four public artifacts (see the curriculum roadmap). Each replicates one published result at small scale, adds one honest extension, and ships as code plus a short write-up. This course serves the middle two, and they share a single worry: is the improvement real?

Artifact 2: the mini-FML search study

An AIDE-style tree-search agent writes, runs and improves machine-learning code (chapter 02). You run it on three tasks from FML-Bench, a benchmark of fundamental machine-learning research tasks (chapter 05). You then compare it head to head with a greedy hill-climber. About $300 to $600 and four to seven months at ten to fifteen hours a week, as the roadmap plans it.

Gate: paired results with confidence intervals, and a pull request to FML-Bench adding your agent or metric.

Artifact 3: the evaluator-hardening kit

A subset of KernelBench (a benchmark that asks a model to write fast GPU code, chapter 04) where you first break the grader, then harden it, and measure the hacking rate before and after. About $200 to $400 and months seven to ten.

Gate: hacking rate measured on 20 or more kernels, before and after hardening, and one upstream pull request to a kernel benchmark or to ShinkaEvolve.

The two connect. Artifact 3's library (typed and attested results, hidden splits, end-to-end timing) is built to drop into Artifact 2's agent. Hacking rate is meant to be reported next to every score, for every artifact after the second.

What each chapter gives you

01 Harness Engineering as Research
A harness is the software around a model that runs its loop: the tools, the context it sees, the files it keeps and the checks on its results. This chapter lays out the ladder from tuning prompts to rewriting the harness itself, and where ACE, Meta-Harness, STOP and the Darwin Gödel Machine sit on it.
02 Tree Search over Experiments: AIDE
The mechanism of the Artifact 2 agent, with numbers: nodes, a selection rule, and why FML-Bench found that a greedy hill-climber nearly matches tree search.
03 Public & Private Splits
How to keep the scorer outside the agent's reach, and how to report hacking rate as a metric.
04 Kernel Engineering for Agents
Enough Triton to read an agent's kernel, and the checks that tell a real speed-up from a trick.
05 Benchmarks for Research Agents
What MLE-bench, PaperBench, RE-Bench, FML-Bench and KernelBench each measure, and what their Lite subsets give up.

The course also has a lab (Greedy versus Tree Search), a workbench (AIDE on One Task, CPU-Sized) and a case (FML-Bench and AIDE²: When Search Beats Greedy). The lab is a literal step of Artifact 2: its code and the artifact's code are the same code.

What this course borrows from A6, and does not repeat

Four chapters of A6 stay in A6 because they are about evaluation in general, and this course would only copy them. Open them when this course points to them:

  • A6 LLM-as-Judge: when a model scores another model's output, and how that score is biased. Relevant to PaperBench (chapter 05), whose grader is a judge.
  • A6 Statistics for Evals: error bars, paired comparisons and sample sizes. Chapters 02 and 05 apply them to small benchmarks and do not re-derive them.
  • A6 Building a Domain Benchmark: how tasks, gold answers and splits are made. Chapter 03 here is about protecting a benchmark from the agent that is being scored on it.
  • A6 Contamination, Saturation & Decay: how benchmarks leak into training data. This course is about leaks the agent itself creates during search.

Common misconception

“The agent is the research; the evaluator is plumbing.” In every result this course covers, the evaluator decides what the number means. A search agent optimises whatever the scorer rewards, so a leaky scorer produces a confident, wrong improvement. Chapter 03 shows a toy where 41 of 60 candidates look perfect on visible tests and 23 of those fail the private ones. Treat the scorer as the part that deserves the most care, not the least.

State of play, October 2026

The two research results behind these artifacts are recent. The FML-Bench paper (Zou et al.) was posted on 17 May 2026 and revised on 29 May 2026 (arXiv 2605.17373). It evaluates six agents on 18 tasks and reports that a simple greedy hill-climber nearly matches the best tree-search agent. SpecBench (Zhao et al., arXiv 2605.21384, submitted 20 May 2026 and revised 9 September 2026) measures reward hacking as the gap between visible and held-out test pass rates. Both are preprints that I read as abstracts and, for FML-Bench, the paper text. Check for newer versions before you cite either.

Try it yourself

Before reading further, write one sentence for each artifact that starts “I will believe my improvement is real if…”. For Artifact 2 you will probably mention the same budget for both agents and several seeds. For Artifact 3 you will probably mention tests the agent never saw. Keep the sentences; chapters 02 and 03 will show how often those two conditions are the first to fail. The roadmap's standards (a fair baseline, a sustained trend, scores on data the loop never saw, a stated budget) are a longer version of the same sentences.

Check your understanding

Which pair of conditions is the first thing a lab reviewer will check in an Artifact 2 write-up?

  1. The model name and the GPU used
  2. That both agents had the same budget, and that results come from several seeds with a paired comparison
  3. That the code is written in Rust
  4. That the agent used tree search rather than a simple loop
A fair baseline with the same budget, and several seeds with a paired test, are the standards in section 17 of the artifact track. The model and GPU are reported but do not decide whether the comparison is fair; the search method is the thing being tested, not a requirement.

Artifact 3's library is designed to plug into Artifact 2's agent. What does it contribute there?

  1. A faster tree-search algorithm
  2. A larger model
  3. More FML-Bench tasks
  4. Typed and attested results, hidden splits and end-to-end timing, so the agent's scorer is harder to cheat
The kit hardens the evaluator, not the search. A faster search (first option) would make cheating more efficient, not less.

A friend says “the evaluator is just plumbing; the agent is the interesting part.” Which reply fits this course?

  1. The agent optimises whatever the scorer rewards, so a leaky scorer gives a confident, wrong improvement
  2. Evaluators are interesting only for language tasks
  3. Evaluators matter only once the agent is above human level
  4. Plumbing is the engineering underneath and is assumed, not taught
The first option states why evaluators are central: search finds loopholes. The others are wrong in different ways: evaluator gaps appear at every capability level, and the course teaches evaluators directly.
