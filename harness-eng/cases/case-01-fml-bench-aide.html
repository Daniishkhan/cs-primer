FML-Bench and AIDE²: When Search Beats Greedy

In May 2026 a group of 14 authors published a controlled comparison of AI research agents. Its most quoted result was a null: a plain greedy hill-climber scored about the same as the best tree-search agent. Four months later Weco AI, the company behind AIDE, published AIDE², a system in which an agent rewrites the agent that does the research, and used the same benchmark to test whether the rewritten agent generalises. This case study reads both papers as engineering documents: what was measured, what the numbers are, what the authors themselves say they cannot show, and what a reader building Artifact 2 should take from them.

I fetched and read, on 15 October 2026, the arXiv pages and the full HTML text of FML-bench v2 (arXiv 2605.17373, v2 of 29 May 2026), the full HTML text of AIDE² (arXiv 2609.26457, v1 of 22 September 2026), the FML-bench repository README and Weco's blog post of 14 July 2026. I also fetched the arXiv abstract page of the earlier FML-bench paper (2510.10472), the AIDE paper (2502.13138) and the aideml README. All fetches went through a tool that returns a summary of the page, not the raw text, and that refuses to reproduce long passages. So the numbers below are the ones that summary reported, and I give a quotation only where it was short enough to be returned verbatim. Where the text and a table disagreed, the summary said so and I repeat it. Some of the AIDE² results are shown only in bar charts (Figures 3 and 4 of that paper); where the text gives no number I describe the pattern and do not invent one.

Not read: the PDF versions and the figures themselves; the appendix values of the AdaptiveSearch parameters; the agents' code. I found no independent reproduction of either paper. A web search turned up secondary write-ups of AIDE² (blog posts and newsletters); I did not fetch them and I do not rely on them. The acronym expansion for FML (fundamental machine learning) is my reading of the repository README, which describes the benchmark as covering "fundamental machine learning problems".

The pattern in one sentence

When a simple baseline matches an elaborate method, the useful question is what property of the task decides which one wins; measure that property, then build the method that switches on it.

Context: agents that do machine-learning research

By 2026 several families of agent could be pointed at a machine-learning codebase and told to improve a number. They differ in how they search. A greedy hill-climber keeps one best version and proposes changes to it, keeping a change only if the validation score strictly improves. The Autoresearch agent of the study is of this kind: its configuration file in the repository says it is based on "Karpathy's autoresearch greedy hill-climbing protocol", the loop that Artifact 1 rebuilds. A tree-search agent keeps many versions and branches from several of them: AIDE (Jiang et al., 2025, "AIDE: AI-Driven Exploration in the Space of Code"), The AI Scientist v2 and AIRA are examples, and chapter 2 explains the first in detail. An evolutionary agent such as OpenEvolve keeps a population. Each paper that introduced one of these reported good results on its own tasks, with its own model, budget and tooling, so the question of whether the extra machinery earns its keep had no clean answer.

That is the gap FML-bench v2 fills, and AIDE² then builds on the same tree-search lineage. The two sit next to each other in Artifact 2 (the search study) and Artifact 4 (the improver of improvers), which is why this page reads them together.

FML-bench
A benchmark of 18 fundamental machine-learning research tasks across 10 domains. Each task gives an agent a baseline codebase, a validation command and a description, and asks it to improve the baseline.
Step
One execution of the task's validation command. FML-bench gives every agent the same step budget (100 per run in the main study), so agents are compared at equal numbers of experiments.
Normalised improvement
An agent's gain over the baseline on held-out test data, divided by the range between the metric's best and worst possible values, and clamped at zero.
Opportunity density
For a task, the sum of the positive normalised improvements found, divided by the distance travelled through the code. High density means gains come cheaply.
AdaptiveSearch
The FML-bench authors' own agent. It starts greedy and switches, once and for good, to a multi-branch search when progress stalls.
AIDE²
Weco's recursive system: an outer agent rewrites the code of an inner research agent (AIDE0, a simplified AIDE) and keeps a rewrite only if it scores better on private data under a fixed cost budget.
Public and private score
The public score is what the agent sees during search. The private score is held back and decides what is kept. Chapter 3 explains why they must differ.

The benchmark: what FML-bench measures

FML-bench has a history that is easy to get wrong. The first paper, "FML-bench: Benchmarking Machine Learning Agents for Scientific Research" (arXiv 2510.10472, submitted 12 October 2025, revised 25 February 2026), had 8 tasks and proposed an exploration-diversity metric; the repository keeps it on a branch called legacy. The paper this case reads is a second, larger study: "FML-bench: A Controlled Study of AI Research Agent Strategies from the Perspective of Search Dynamics" (Zou et al.; 14 authors; v1 on 17 May 2026, v2 on 29 May 2026), which has 18 tasks. When the course roadmap says "FML-Bench v2" it means this second paper. Both carry the same name and the same first author.

The 18 tasks come from existing open-source libraries, one codebase each: causality (CausalML, gCastle), continual learning (two codebases), data efficiency (easy-few-shot-learning, USB), fairness (AIF360, Fairlearn), federated learning (PFLlib), generalisation (two DomainBed tasks), privacy (Opacus, PrivacyMeter), representation learning (Lightly, solo-learn), robustness (ART, OpenOOD) and unlearning (open-unlearning). The repository also defines FML-bench-Lite, 8 of the 18 tasks, as a cheaper proxy whose agent ranking, the README says, "closely tracks" the full one.

The controlled part

Earlier comparisons of research agents compared whole systems, each with its own code editor, tools and prompts, so a difference in score could come from the search strategy or from the plumbing. This study tries to remove the plumbing. Each agent keeps its own search strategy (its tree shape, prompts, memory and action vocabulary), and four things are shared: a code editor that makes one model call per edit, with no agent-specific tooling in the editing layer; a single definition of a step; a separation of validation and test data, so the agent sees only validation metrics; and one display of the metrics. The paper says it "uniformly removed each agent's native code editor and auxiliary subsystems", and lists the cost in its limitations: this "may underestimate each agent's performance under its full original configuration".

The score

The headline score is a normalised improvement. For each task the agent's best-validated codebase is run on held-out test data, and the gain over the untouched baseline is divided by the distance between the metric's best and worst possible values. Gains below the baseline count as zero.

p̂ = max(0, (p_agent − p_baseline) / |p_best − p_worst|)

This is the paper's definition as the summary of its text reported it. A worked case from the appendix: on the gCastle task the metric is a structural Hamming distance (the number of edges in the causal graph that are wrong), the baseline scores 71, the ideal is 0, and because the metric has no upper bound the baseline is used as the worst case. An agent that reduces 71 to 60 scores (71 − 60) / 71 = 0.155. The "Try it yourself" section below recomputes this.

What the study reports

All 324 main runs (6 agents × 18 tasks × 3 rounds) used one model, GPT-5.4, with a budget of 100 optimisation steps per run on NVIDIA A100-80GB GPUs, with each validation run capped at 40 minutes. The paper's cost figures run from about 0.93 million tokens (OpenEvolve) to 2.29 million (TAS v1); the summary I was given did not make the unit (per run or per task) clear. The agents were Autoresearch, The AI Scientist v1 and v2 (TAS v1, TAS v2), AIDE, AIRA and OpenEvolve; the authors' own AdaptiveSearch is reported separately.

Three claims follow, and each should be read at its strength.

  1. Greedy matches the best tree search. TAS v2 scored 0.193 and Autoresearch 0.192. The paper states that both are well above the other four agents. In the table I was given, AIDE (0.178) is 0.015 behind the leader, so I read "well above" as a statement about the group of the lowest three, and I would not call AIDE clearly separated. The paper's pairwise win-rates for the two leaders are 58.5% and 57.4% in the text and 56.2% for both in its Table 3; the text does not explain the difference.
  2. The best-of-three table is even closer. Taking the best of the three rounds on each task, the appendix reports means of 0.245 (TAS v2), 0.239 (Autoresearch) and 0.235 (AIDE), with standard deviations across tasks of about 0.27. A gap of 0.006 against a spread of 0.27 over 18 tasks is far inside the noise (the script below puts a number on that).
  3. Which strategy wins depends on the task. The authors split the 18 tasks at the median opportunity density (0.1315) into 9 dense and 9 sparse tasks. On the dense half Autoresearch has the highest mean improvement (0.349, with AdaptiveSearch at 0.345). On the sparse half AdaptiveSearch leads (0.071), followed by TAS v2 (0.067) and OpenEvolve (0.062), and Autoresearch drops to sixth of seven (0.035). The paper's hypothesis is that "greedy search tends to be more effective when opportunities are dense". The appendix presents the split as supporting evidence found after the fact, not as a tested rule.

The paper also reports, from pooled correlations over the 324 runs, which behaviours track final performance. The area under the improvement-versus-step curve correlates strongly with the final score (Spearman ρ = +0.784), and an earlier first improvement correlates with a better final score (ρ = −0.291). Token cost (ρ = −0.005) and wall-clock time (ρ = −0.094, not significant) do not. Solution diversity, measured by exploration uniqueness, does not either (ρ = +0.012). So "faster to a gain, and more focused" predicts a good run; "more diverse" and "more expensive" do not.

AdaptiveSearch: building the switch

If density decides, an agent that detects it should do better than either. AdaptiveSearch runs the greedy rule first, with one incumbent. When the normalised improvement has not increased over the most recent W consecutive steps, it switches, irreversibly, to a second phase that forks branches from the top N candidates of the first phase and advances them round-robin. It scored 0.208 and a win-rate of 58.6%, above both leaders, and ranked in the top two on both the dense and the sparse halves. The main text I read names W and N without values. The repository's configuration for the agent sets a stagnation window of 50 steps (p1_window: 50) with a tolerance of 0.0005 (p1_epsilon), and a branch count of 1, 2 or 3 chosen by how many steps remain; I take the first to be the paper's W but did not confirm it against the paper. One agent, 18 tasks and three rounds make this initial support, which is how the paper frames it, not proof that the switch is what helps.

Common misconception

“The study shows that tree search is not worth its complexity.” It shows that, with one model, 100 steps, and each agent's own editor and tools removed, two agents tie on the average over these 18 tasks. The authors say as much: the fixed step budget "may affect relative rankings", the six agents "cover the major search topologies but are not exhaustive", and stripping the tools "may underestimate each agent's performance". A tie in the mean also hides the split by density, where the ranking of the greedy agent goes from first to sixth. The right reading is a question for your own task: how dense are the opportunities here?

AIDE²: the same benchmark, one level up

Weco's AIDE² (Srikanth et al., 2026, "Recursive self-improvement of AI research agents") applies the search idea to the agent itself. There are two loops. The inner loop is a research agent: AIDE0, a simplified refactor of AIDE, which edits code against a score. The outer loop is another agent, called AIDEhuman (the version Weco's team hand-tuned, which the company says took about two years to build), which rewrites the inner agent's code, evaluates the rewrite, and keeps it only if it beats the incumbent. The outer loop used Claude Opus 4.7 and each inner agent used Gemini 3 Flash, chosen for cost. The company describes the result as the first experimental evidence of recursive self-improvement; the paper itself is more guarded, as we will see.

What the paper reports

The run lasted 8 days and produced a trajectory of 100 nodes (the starting agent plus 99 rewrite proposals). The paper lists accepted steps 2, 6, 28, 39, 47, 63 and 85, so seven improvements; the incumbent's private grade rose from 0.703 to 0.778, against 0.749 for the hand-built agent under the same grade. Weco's post says about nine in ten proposals were rejected. The final agent (AIDE85) used a bandit rule (UCB1) to choose among five drafting strategies, bounded role-specific prompts, and a failure memory that activates when the bug rate reaches at least 15 per cent.

Read the FML-Bench column the way the previous section taught. The discovered agents beat the starting agent by about 5 points (15.0 against 19.7 and 19.9). Against the hand-built agent the difference is 0.1 to 0.3 points with standard errors of about 1: indistinguishable. The paper's wording is that the strongest discovered agent "matches or exceeds" the human-engineered one on all four benchmarks; on FML-Bench, the first of those is the accurate half. The clear wins are on ALE-Bench and WeatherBench 2, and the paper notes that the gains are not monotone: AIDE85 is best on ALE-Bench and FML-Bench, AIDE47 on MLE-Bench and WeatherBench 2. The authors also report that median prompt sizes shrank by roughly 7 times on MLE-Bench and 40 to 50 times elsewhere against AIDE0.

The hacking rate, and a discrepancy

AIDE² measures reward hacking on a KernelBench subset of 38 (kernel, training-context) pairs. A kernel is flagged when its isolated speed-up exceeds 1.02 times and either less than half of that speed-up survives when the kernel is placed in a real model's training loop (GPT-2, ViT or a CNN), or the kernel crashes there. The paper reports rates of 55 per cent for AIDE0, 39 for AIDE47, 32 for AIDE85 and 39 for the hand-built agent. The loop never optimised for this. The authors say the numbers "do not identify which rewrites produced it". Weco's earlier blog post reports different rates for the same four agents, 63, 42, 34 and 42 per cent, and defines a hack in terms of less than half the claimed speed-up surviving end to end. Both come from the authors; I could not tell from what I read which is the later or the better, and the paper (22 September) post-dates the blog (14 July). Cite the paper's numbers and note the blog's. Chapter 4 returns to how to measure this, and it is the quantity Artifact 3 reports.

What the critics and the authors say

I found no independent replication of either paper, and no published critique I could read. The most useful criticism is the authors' own, and it is unusually direct.

  • The ignition test is inconclusive. The authors put the discovered agent AIDE47 into the outer-loop seat to see whether a better agent is also a better improver. With three seeds of 50 steps each, the arms ended at 0.780 (discovered) and 0.782 (hand-built); the text says the discovered one reached its final region in roughly 20 steps against roughly 40. They write that the results "do not establish that AIDE47 is more sample-efficient" and that what the treatment arm shows is "no obvious degradation".
  • Noise compounds. A falsely accepted rewrite becomes the new incumbent and can derail later search. About a quarter of rejected rewrites scored higher on the public signal, and deltas of 0.004 to 0.007 are within run-to-run noise (the appendix).
  • Few seeds. Several results use three seeds per benchmark, and the authors say cost limits stronger conclusions.
  • Hard to read. The discovered agents are "complex and difficult to interpret", and it is unclear which components drive performance. Weco's post adds that a statistical layer of the defence against hacking has a bug and has no effect in AIDE85.
  • Self-reported. The code, the benchmarks' selection and the framing are the authors'. The post uses a ladder of recursive self-improvement (levels 0 to 3) and four "Level-1 conditions"; the paper text I read does not use that ladder, speaking instead of bi-level optimisation and first- and second-order generalisation. It places the result on the lowest rungs: the post says the system does not show ignition and is "not near an intelligence explosion".
  • Overlap. Zhengyao Jiang appears on the author lists of AIDE, of the FML-bench v2 paper and of AIDE². That is not a flaw in either, but it means FML-Bench was not an outside party's test of AIDE². I did not check affiliations beyond the names.

Try it yourself: three numbers from the appendix

This script recomputes three things from the numbers I read: the dense and sparse split, a normalised improvement worked by hand, and how large the gap between the two leaders is next to the spread. Every input is copied from the paper's tables as reported to me; nothing is simulated.

"""Re-derive three numbers from FML-bench's appendix (arXiv 2605.17373v2).

Every input below is copied from the paper's tables as I read them; nothing
is simulated. Standard library only.
"""
import math
import statistics

# ── 1. The opportunity-density split ─────────────────────────────────────
# phi_opp per task: sum of positive normalised gains / embedding distance
# travelled, averaged over 6 agents x 3 rounds (appendix G).
PHI = {
    "Unlearning": 1.207, "ART": 0.573, "AIF360": 0.356, "PFLlib": 0.352,
    "gCastle": 0.347, "PrivacyMeter": 0.454, "Fairlearn": 0.447,
    "Continual-Learning": 0.254, "Opacus": 0.144,
    "DomainBed-CM": 0.118, "Solo-learn": 0.102, "CausalML": 0.077,
    "PyCIL": 0.049, "USB": 0.047, "OpenOOD": 0.045, "Lightly": 0.055,
    "DomainBed-OH": 0.030, "EasyFSL": 0.025,
}
median = statistics.median(PHI.values())
dense = sorted(t for t, v in PHI.items() if v > median)
print(f"tasks {len(PHI)}, median phi_opp {median:.4f} (paper: 0.1315)")
print(f"dense {len(dense)}, sparse {len(PHI) - len(dense)}")

# ── 2. One normalised improvement, worked by hand ────────────────────────
# p_hat = max(0, (p_agent - p_baseline) / |p_best - p_worst|)
# gCastle: baseline SHD 71 (the worst case, no upper bound), ideal 0.
baseline, ideal, agent = 71.0, 0.0, 60.0
p_hat = max(0.0, (baseline - agent) / abs(ideal - baseline))
print(f"gCastle, SHD 71 -> {agent:.0f}: normalised improvement {p_hat:.3f}")

# ── 3. How big is a gap of 0.006 next to the spread across tasks? ────────
# best-of-3 mean +/- std over the 18 tasks (appendix table).
agents = {"TAS v2": (0.245, 0.268), "Autoresearch": (0.239, 0.270)}
for name, (m, sd) in agents.items():
    print(f"{name:13s} mean {m:.3f}, std over tasks {sd:.3f}, "
          f"rough s.e. {sd / math.sqrt(18):.3f}")
gap = agents["TAS v2"][0] - agents["Autoresearch"][0]
print(f"gap {gap:.3f} = {gap / (0.27 / math.sqrt(18)):.2f} rough "
      f"standard errors")

tasks 18, median phi_opp 0.1310 (paper: 0.1315)
dense 9, sparse 9
gCastle, SHD 71 -> 60: normalised improvement 0.155
TAS v2        mean 0.245, std over tasks 0.268, rough s.e. 0.063
Autoresearch  mean 0.239, std over tasks 0.270, rough s.e. 0.064
gap 0.006 = 0.09 rough standard errors

The median comes out at 0.1310 and the paper says 0.1315; the difference is the rounding of the per-task values I copied. The last two lines are a rough check, not a test: the standard error uses the spread across tasks and treats the 18 tasks as the sample, and it ignores that the two agents were run on the same tasks, which a paired test would exploit. Even so, a gap of 0.09 standard errors is a tie. For a proper paired test with an interval, and a toy in which the density of good edits changes the gap, do Lab 1; for the real AIDE agent on a small task, Workbench 1.

The lessons

  • Compare strategies at equal budget before believing complexity. The control that made the null result possible was the shared step, shared editor and shared model. Without it the comparison cannot be made. This is the standard "a fair baseline with the same budget" that every artifact must meet (see A12 chapter 1).
  • A tie in the mean can hide a crossover. The greedy agent was first on dense tasks and sixth on sparse ones. Report the split, not only the mean. Artifact 2's open question is exactly whether the crossover appears on your three tasks.
  • Measure the property you think decides. The paper's opportunity density is crude (an embedding distance and a median split), and the authors call the result supporting evidence. Treat it as a hypothesis to test with a pre-registered split, not as a finding to cite.
  • Three seeds support a direction, not a ranking. Both papers use three rounds. A6 chapter 4 shows why intervals and paired tests belong next to such numbers.
  • Select on private data, report on data neither loop saw. AIDE² does both, and still finds that noise compounds across the loops. See chapter 3 and A7 chapter 16.
  • Put the hacking rate next to the score. Weco's agents fell from 55 to 32 per cent on one task family, with the cause unidentified. A score without a hacking rate is half a result.

State of play, October 2026

Both papers are recent: FML-bench v2 is four and a half months old and AIDE² about three weeks. Neither has, as far as I could find, been independently reproduced, and AIDE²'s headline claims are the company's own. The FML-bench repository lists 18 tasks and seven registered agents (the six studied plus AdaptiveSearch) and reports no results in its README, so check the paper for numbers. Expect the benchmark's task list, the agent configurations and the AIDE² code and numbers to change; this page reflects what I read on 15 October 2026.

Check your understanding

In FML-bench v2, Autoresearch scored 0.192 and the best tree search 0.193 on average. What does the paper's split by opportunity density add?

  1. It shows tree search is better on every task.
  2. It shows greedy search is better on every task.
  3. It suggests greedy is best when improvements are dense and falls behind when they are sparse, which a tie in the mean hides.
  4. It proves that opportunity density causes the difference.
The greedy agent ranked first on the dense half and sixth of seven on the sparse half. The split was found after the fact and the authors call it supporting evidence, so "proves" overstates it, and neither strategy wins every task.

The best-of-3 means for TAS v2 and Autoresearch are 0.245 and 0.239 with standard deviations of about 0.27 across 18 tasks. What is the fair reading?

  1. The agents are indistinguishable on this evidence; the gap is a small fraction of the spread.
  2. TAS v2 is better because its mean is higher.
  3. Autoresearch is better because it is simpler.
  4. No reading is possible without the code.
A gap of 0.006 next to a spread of 0.27 is a tie. A higher mean is not a win without an interval, and simplicity is a reason to prefer a tied method, not evidence that it scores higher.

On FML-Bench, AIDE85 scores 19.9 ± 1.1 and the hand-built AIDEhuman 19.6 ± 1.0. How should this row be summarised?

  1. The discovered agent is clearly better.
  2. The discovered agent is clearly worse.
  3. The result is invalid because FML-Bench was used in selection.
  4. The two are indistinguishable there, while both are well above the starting agent (15.0).
A difference of 0.3 against standard errors near 1 is a tie. The paper lists FML-Bench among the held-out benchmarks, not the selection tasks, so validity is not the issue; the 5-point gain over AIDE0 is the clear effect.

Weco's blog reports KernelBench hacking rates of 63, 42, 34 and 42 per cent, and the paper reports 55, 39, 32 and 39 per cent, for the same four agents. What should a careful writer do?

  1. Average the two sets.
  2. Cite the paper's numbers, mention the blog's, and say the source of the difference is not explained.
  3. Use the blog's numbers because they came first.
  4. Drop the hacking result because the sources disagree.
Both are the authors' own, the paper is the more detailed and later source, and the discrepancy is itself information. Averaging invents a number nobody measured; dropping it discards a result both sources agree on in direction (a fall of about 20 points).
