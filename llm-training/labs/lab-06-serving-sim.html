Lab 6: A Serving Simulator

Chapter 18 said that generating a token moves the whole model through memory, chapter 20 said that an engine is a scheduler that batches one step at a time and manages the KV cache like virtual memory, and chapter 22 turned the result into dollars per million tokens. This lab lets you check every one of those claims by building the engine's brain in a few hundred lines. There is no model and no GPU: a clock, a cost model for one forward pass, a pool of cache pages, and a stream of requests. You write the paged allocator, the workload and the percentile metrics, static batching, continuous batching with paged allocation, preemption when the cache is full, and finally the question a capacity planner asks: how many requests a second can this GPU take while still meeting a latency target, and what does a token cost at that point?

Why this lab matters

When a serving system misbehaves, the symptoms are percentiles on a dashboard: first-token latency climbing, tokens per second flat, a sudden wall of "preempted" log lines. The causes are scheduling decisions made thousands of times a second, and reading about them does not tell you how large their effects are. Written as a simulation they become numbers you can change. You will see static batching waste 90% of its slots, a reserve-the-maximum allocator turn away traffic that a paged one serves comfortably, a shrinking cache turn into recomputation, and a TPOT figure that gets better while the system gets worse. The simulator is a model, so every number depends on assumptions that the lab states. What transfers is the shape of the trade-offs, and the habit of reading throughput, time to first token and time per token together, as in metrics and percentiles and queueing theory. Chapter 20's case for vLLM, case 4, is the real system this lab is a miniature of.

Deterministic, small, standard library only

Nothing here touches a real clock. The simulator keeps its own clock in milliseconds and advances it by the modelled duration of each forward pass, so 300 requests that take minutes of simulated time run in a few hundredths of a second. The workload comes from random.Random(seed); the check replays random cases from printed seeds (for example python3 check.py solution 1 7 replays the random allocator operations of milestone 1 with seed 7). Python 3.9 or newer, no NumPy, no downloads. The full check takes under a second. The lab does not use llm.py: no model is called.

Assumptions, not measurements

The simulated GPU is invented. It is a 24 GB card running an 8-billion-parameter model in bf16 with the Llama 3 8B shape of chapter 18 (32 layers, 8 KV heads of width 128, so 131,072 bytes of cache per token). Take 1.0 TB/s of memory bandwidth at 70% efficiency, 100 TFLOPS usable for prompt processing and 1.5 GB of overhead; chapter 22 derived the same kind of numbers for an H100, and the arithmetic is identical. Reading all 16.06 GB of weights costs 22.9 ms per step; reading one cached token costs 0.000187 ms; processing one prompt token costs 0.1606 ms. The model ignores the arithmetic of attention itself, multi-GPU communication, prefix caching, chunked prefill and speculative decoding. Treat the output as a worked example, not a benchmark of any product.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each building on the previous one:

  1. Sizing and paging: the bytes of cache per token, how many pages fit beside the weights, and a page allocator with block tables.
  2. The cost model, the workload and the metrics: the duration of a step, seeded Poisson arrivals with lognormal lengths, nearest-rank percentiles and the summary an SLO talks about.
  3. Static batching: run a batch to completion, then take the next.
  4. Continuous batching: schedule at every step, refill slots at once, allocate pages as sequences grow.
  5. Preemption: when the cache is full, evict the youngest request, and recompute it later.
  6. Capacity and cost: sweep the arrival rate, find the knee for each policy, and turn throughput into dollars per million tokens.

Milestone 1: sizing and paging

Start with the bill of bytes from chapter 18. Every token a request has seen leaves behind one key vector and one value vector in each layer, for each of the KV heads: 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × bytes. For the Llama 3 8B shape in bf16 that is 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes, 128 KiB. The memory left for these vectors is what is not the weights and not overhead, and the engine carves it into fixed-size pages of page_tokens tokens (16 here, a common block size in serving engines). The experiment prints the sizing for the lab's card:

3,070 pages of 16 tokens, 49,120 tokens of cache. At 4,096 tokens of context a sequence takes 256 pages, so only 11 fit; at 512 tokens, 95 do. The cache, not the arithmetic, is what limits how many conversations share the card, which is the point of chapter 18. The last rows show why grouped-query attention matters: the same model with 32 KV heads would need four times as much per token.

Now the data structure. Before paging, an engine had to give each sequence one contiguous slab sized for its worst case, because it could not know in advance how long the answer would be; most of the slab went unused. Paged attention (Kwon et al., 2023, "Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention") borrows the operating system's trick from virtual memory: a sequence's cache is a list of fixed-size pages anywhere in memory, found through a per-sequence block table, like a page table. Pages are handed out on demand as the sequence grows, so waste is bounded by one partly filled page per sequence. Your PageAllocator is that mechanism. It owns a pool of page numbers, reserve(rid, tokens) grows a request's block table to cover tokens tokens (all or nothing, never shrinking), and release returns the pages. Free pages are handed out lowest-number-first, which makes the behaviour reproducible and makes the scattering visible:

$ python3 exp_blocks.py
start         {'A': [0, 1, 2], 'B': [3, 4], 'C': [5, 6]} free 3
B finishes    {'A': [0, 1, 2], 'C': [5, 6]} free 5
A grows, D in {'A': [0, 1, 2, 3, 4], 'C': [5, 6], 'D': [7, 8]} free 1
a request of 12 tokens needs 3 pages; reserve -> False ( 1 page free)

After B finishes, pages 3 and 4 are free. When A grows by two pages it gets exactly those, so A's block table is [0, 1, 2, 3, 4] by luck; had C been released instead, A's new pages would have been numbered from elsewhere and its table would not be contiguous, and that is fine, because the attention kernel follows the table. The last line is the failure the scheduler will meet in milestone 5: a request that needs three pages when one is free is refused, and nothing is allocated.

def kv_bytes_per_token(layers, kv_heads, head_dim, bytes_per=2):
    """Bytes of KV cache one token occupies: 2 (keys and values) x layers x kv_heads x head_dim x
    bytes_per number. Llama 3 8B in bf16: kv_bytes_per_token(32, 8, 128) == 131072."""
    return 2 * layers * kv_heads * head_dim * bytes_per

def cache_pages(gpu_bytes, weight_bytes, overhead_bytes, kv_bytes_tok, page_tokens):
    """How many whole pages of KV cache fit once weights and overhead are subtracted from the GPU's

class PageAllocator:
    """A pool of `total_pages` physical pages of `page_tokens` tokens each, handed out like virtual
    memory: each request owns a BLOCK TABLE, a list of physical page numbers that need not be
    contiguous.

    * Free pages are handed out lowest-numbered first (use a heap), and released pages go back.
    * reserve(rid, tokens) makes sure rid owns at least pages_needed(tokens) pages, adding pages to
      the end of its block table as needed. Pages are never taken away by reserve. If the pool cannot
      supply all the missing pages it changes NOTHING and returns False; otherwise True.
    * release(rid) returns all of rid's pages to the pool and reports how many (0 if unknown).
    * peak_pages is the largest used_pages value ever seen (update it inside reserve).
    """
    def __init__(self, total_pages, page_tokens):
        self.total_pages = total_pages
        self.page_tokens = page_tokens
        self._free = list(range(total_pages))          # a sorted list is already a valid heap
        self._tables = {}
        self.peak_pages = 0

        self.peak_pages = 0

    def pages_needed(self, tokens):
        """ceil(tokens / page_tokens); 0 for tokens <= 0."""
        return 0 if tokens <= 0 else -(-tokens // self.page_tokens)

    @property
    def free_pages(self):
        return len(self._free)

    @property
    def used_pages(self):
        return self.total_pages - len(self._free)

    def block_table(self, rid):
        """The physical page numbers rid owns, in logical order (a copy); [] if none."""
        return list(self._tables.get(rid, []))

    def reserve(self, rid, tokens):
        """Ensure rid owns enough pages for `tokens` tokens. All or nothing. Returns True/False."""
        table = self._tables.get(rid, [])
        missing = self.pages_needed(tokens) - len(table)
        if missing <= 0:
            return True
        if missing > len(self._free):
            return False
        table = self._tables.setdefault(rid, table)
        for _ in range(missing):
            table.append(heapq.heappop(self._free))
        self.peak_pages = max(self.peak_pages, self.used_pages)
        return True

    def release(self, rid):
        """Return all of rid's pages to the pool; the number of pages freed."""
        table = self._tables.pop(rid, [])
        for page in table:
            heapq.heappush(self._free, page)
        return len(table)

Milestone 2: the cost model, the workload and the metrics

A simulator needs three pieces of physics and one piece of statistics. The physics first. step_time_ms(cfg, kv_tokens, prefill_tokens) is the duration of one forward pass of the whole batch:

step = base_ms + kv_ms × (cached tokens read by decoding requests) + prefill_ms × (prompt tokens processed in this step)

The base_ms term is the weights streaming out of HBM, paid once per step however many requests are in it: this is why a bigger batch is cheaper per token (chapter 18's memory-bound argument, and the "airline seat" of chapter 22). The kv_ms term is each decoding request's cache being read, so it grows with batch size and context, and is the part that does not amortise. The prefill_ms term is prompt processing, compute-bound and proportional to prompt tokens. Chapter 22 used a max of the memory time and compute time; the lab adds them, which is simpler and slightly pessimistic for steps that mix a prefill with decoding.

Now the load. Requests do not come in neat batches. make_workload(n, rate_per_s, seed) draws, for each request in turn, the gap to the previous arrival from an exponential distribution with mean 1000/rate ms (the signature of independent users: a Poisson process), a prompt length from a lognormal distribution (median about 245 tokens, a long right tail), and an output length from a second lognormal (median about 73, mean about 100, as in chapter 20's simulation), clamped to sensible ranges. The recipe is fixed down to the order of the three random draws so that the check can regenerate your workload. Each request also carries max_new, the limit its client put on the answer (max_tokens in an API call), which is typically far larger than what the model actually writes. The scheduler never sees output_len in advance, but a reserve-the-worst-case allocator is forced to plan around max_new. Look at what the generator makes:

def step_time_ms(cfg, kv_tokens, prefill_tokens):
    """Duration of one forward pass of the batch, in ms.

    cfg.base_ms + cfg.kv_ms * kv_tokens + cfg.prefill_ms * prefill_tokens, where kv_tokens is the
    total number of cached tokens that attention reads in this step (the contexts of the requests
    that are DECODING) and prefill_tokens the prompt tokens being processed in this step. The base
    is paid once per step however many requests run: that is the weights being read from HBM, and
    why batching is cheap (chapter 18)."""
    return cfg.base_ms + cfg.kv_ms * kv_tokens + cfg.prefill_ms * prefill_tokens

def make_workload(n, rate_per_s, seed, max_new=1024):
    """n requests with Poisson arrivals and lognormal lengths, from random.Random(seed).

    For each request i, in this order, make exactly three draws:
        gap    = rng.expovariate(rate_per_s / 1000.0)         # ms between arrivals; t += gap
        prompt = min(2048, max(16, int(rng.lognormvariate(5.5, 0.8))))
        output = min(500, max(5, int(rng.lognormvariate(4.3, 0.9))))
    Request(i, t, prompt, output, max_new). The first arrival is at t = gap, not 0."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    t, out = 0.0, []
    for i in range(n):
        t += rng.expovariate(rate_per_s / 1000.0)
        prompt = min(2048, max(16, int(rng.lognormvariate(5.5, 0.8))))
        output = min(500, max(5, int(rng.lognormvariate(4.3, 0.9))))
        out.append(Request(i, t, prompt, output, max_new))
    return out

def percentile(values, q):
    """The q-th percentile (0 <= q <= 100) by the NEAREST-RANK rule: sort ascending, take the value
    at rank ceil(q / 100 * n), with rank at least 1. percentile([15, 20, 35, 40, 50], 40) == 20.
    Raise ValueError on an empty list."""
    if not values:
        raise ValueError("percentile of an empty list")
    ordered = sorted(values)
    rank = max(1, math.ceil(q / 100.0 * len(ordered)))
    return ordered[rank - 1]

def summarise(sim):
    """Reduce a SimResult to the numbers an SLO talks about. Returns a dict with the keys

      completed, tokens, preemptions, peak_pages          copied or counted from sim
      throughput_tok_s    tokens / ((end_ms - earliest arrival) / 1000)
      tokens_per_step     tokens / steps
      ttft_p50, ttft_p95, ttft_p99    percentiles of first_token_ms - arrival_ms
      tpot_p50, tpot_p95, tpot_p99    percentiles of (finish_ms - first_token_ms) / (output_len - 1),
                                      over requests with output_len >= 2 (0.0 if there are none)
      e2e_p50, e2e_p95                percentiles of finish_ms - arrival_ms
    """
    d = sim.done
    ttft = [x.first_token_ms - x.arrival_ms for x in d]
    tpot = [(x.finish_ms - x.first_token_ms) / (x.output_len - 1) for x in d if x.output_len >= 2]
    e2e = [x.finish_ms - x.arrival_ms for x in d]
    span = sim.end_ms - min(x.arrival_ms for x in d)
    out = {"completed": len(d), "tokens": sim.tokens, "preemptions": sim.preemptions,
           "peak_pages": sim.peak_pages,
           "throughput_tok_s": sim.tokens / (span / 1000.0),
           "tokens_per_step": sim.tokens / sim.steps}
    for name, series in (("ttft", ttft), ("tpot", tpot)):
        for q in (50, 95, 99):
            out[f"{name}_p{q}"] = percentile(series, q) if series else 0.0
    out["e2e_p50"], out["e2e_p95"] = percentile(e2e, 50), percentile(e2e, 95)
    return out

Milestone 3: static batching

The textbook way to batch is static batching: collect up to max_batch requests, run them together until every one has finished, return, and collect the next. Your simulator forms a batch from the requests that have already arrived (it does not wait to fill a batch, which would only make things worse), runs one prefill step for all their prompts, and then decode steps until the longest output is done. Three details define the model. First, a finished request keeps its slot until the batch ends and a newcomer cannot take it: the idle slots are the waste chapter 20 measured. Second, a static engine cannot grow a cache in the middle of a batch, so it reserves prompt_len + max_new tokens per request up front (milestone 1's allocator again), and the number of requests per batch is limited by the cache as well as by max_batch. Third, tokens stream out as they are produced, so a request's finish time is the step of its own last token, not the end of the batch, but its slot is not freed until then.

Work the smallest example by hand before coding it. Request A arrives at 0 with a 20-token prompt and 3 output tokens; B arrives at 5 with a 10-token prompt and 2 output tokens. The test configuration has base_ms = 10, prefill_ms = 1 and no cache term. At t = 0 only A has arrived, so the batch is [A]: prefill costs 10 + 20 = 30 ms and A's first token appears at 30. Two decode steps of 10 ms each give tokens at 40 and 50, and A finishes at 50. B has been waiting since t = 5; the next batch starts at 50: prefill 10 + 10 = 20, first token at 70, one decode step, finish at 80. B waited 65 ms for its first token, 45 of them for A's batch to end. Now sweep the batch limit on a busier workload:

def simulate_static(requests, cfg):
    """Static batching: take a batch, run it until EVERY request in it has finished, then the next.

    requests are served first come first served (order by arrival_ms, then rid). The clock starts
    at 0. Loop until every request is done:
      1. If the GPU is idle, jump the clock forward to the next request's arrival if it is later.
      2. Form a batch: take waiting requests in order (arrival_ms <= clock) while there are fewer
         than cfg.max_batch and allocator.reserve(rid, prompt_len + max_new) succeeds. Static
         batching cannot grow a cache mid-batch, so it always reserves the worst case up front,
         whatever cfg.alloc says. Release every reservation when the batch ends.
      3. One PREFILL step for the whole batch: step_time_ms(cfg, 0, sum of prompt_len). Every
         request emits its first token at the end of it.
      4. Then decode steps s = 1, 2, ... up to max(output_len) - 1. In step s the ACTIVE requests
         are those with output_len > s; each reads a cache of prompt_len + s tokens, so the step
         lasts step_time_ms(cfg, sum of (prompt_len + s) over active requests, 0). Each active
         request emits one token at the end of the step; a request whose output_len == s + 1
         finishes then. A finished request still holds its slot until the batch ends, and nothing
         new can join.
    A request with output_len == 1 finishes at the end of the prefill step.
    Raise ValueError (via check_fits) if a request could never fit. Return a SimResult."""
    reqs = sorted(requests, key=lambda r: (r.arrival_ms, r.rid))
    alloc = PageAllocator(cfg.total_pages, cfg.page_tokens)
    check_fits([r for r in reqs], cfg._replace(alloc="reserve"), alloc)
    done, t, i, steps, tokens = [], 0.0, 0, 0, 0
    while i < len(reqs):
        t = max(t, reqs[i].arrival_ms)                     # idle: wait for the next arrival
        batch = []
        while (i < len(reqs) and len(batch) < cfg.max_batch and reqs[i].arrival_ms <= t
               and alloc.reserve(reqs[i].rid, reqs[i].prompt_len + reqs[i].max_new)):
            batch.append(reqs[i])
            i += 1
        t += step_time_ms(cfg, 0, sum(r.prompt_len for r in batch))          # prefill
        steps += 1
        first = t
        finish = {r.rid: t for r in batch if r.output_len == 1}
        for s in range(1, max(r.output_len for r in batch)):
            active = [r for r in batch if r.output_len > s]
            t += step_time_ms(cfg, sum(r.prompt_len + s for r in active), 0)  # one token each
            steps += 1
            for r in active:
                if r.output_len == s + 1:
                    finish[r.rid] = t
        for r in batch:
            done.append(Done(r.rid, r.arrival_ms, first, finish[r.rid], r.output_len, 0))
            tokens += r.output_len
            alloc.release(r.rid)
    done.sort(key=lambda d: d.rid)
    return SimResult(done, t, steps, tokens, 0, alloc.peak_pages)

Milestone 4: continuous batching

The fix named in chapter 20 is to make the scheduling decision after every step, not after every batch: continuous batching, or iteration-level scheduling (Yu et al., 2022, "Orca"). The batch is whoever is running right now. A request that emits its last token leaves at once and its pages are freed; a waiting request takes a slot in the very next step. The scheduler becomes a loop with a fixed shape, and the figure shows one iteration of it, which your simulate_continuous repeats until the work is done.

Three decisions in the loop deserve a sentence each, because the check pins them down. What a request must own. In "paged" mode it needs room for its context plus the one token this step adds, so the allocation grows a page at a time as the sequence does; in "reserve" mode it claims prompt_len + max_new once, at admission, and never grows. The helper tokens_needed (given) returns the right number for either mode, so one loop serves both. First come first served. If the request at the head of the queue does not fit, the scheduler stops, even if a smaller request behind it would fit; skipping ahead would starve large requests. A prefill step is also a decode step. A newly admitted request processes its whole prompt and emits its first token in that same step, so its TTFT is its queueing time plus one step that contains its prefill, and every decoding request in that step is delayed by the prefill's cost.

Take the A and B example again with continuous batching. At t = 0 A is admitted: prefill 10 + 20 = 30 ms, first token at 30. B (arrived at 5) is admitted in the next step, which also decodes A: 10 + 10 = 20 ms, B's first token at 50, A's second. The third step decodes both for 10 ms and both finish at 60.     """Continuous (iteration-level) batching with a paged or reserved KV cache.

    State: `pending` (not yet arrived), `waiting` (arrived, not running; a deque), `running` (a
    list in admission order), one PageAllocator, the clock t = 0.0, and counters. Validate with
    check_fits first. Repeat until nothing is pending, waiting or running:

      0. Move requests from pending to waiting when arrival_ms <= t. If nothing is waiting or
         running, set t to the next pending arrival and start the loop again.
      A. GROW. For each request s in `running`, in order, make sure the allocator holds
         tokens_needed(s, cfg) tokens for it. If allocator.reserve fails, call
         preempt_one(running, waiting, alloc) and count it; repeat until the reserve succeeds or
         the victim was s itself (then s is gone from `running`).
      B. ADMIT. Only if nothing was preempted in this step (or running is empty): while waiting is
         non-empty and len(running) < cfg.max_batch, try alloc.reserve(rid, tokens_needed(head,
         cfg)) for the head of the queue. On success move it to the END of `running` and remember
         it as new; on failure stop (first come first served: a big request blocks the ones behind).
      C. RUN one step. prefill_tokens = sum of ctx over the NEW requests (their whole prompt, or, for
         a request resuming after preemption, prompt plus the tokens it had already generated);
         kv_tokens = sum of ctx over the other (decoding) requests. t += step_time_ms(cfg,
         kv_tokens, prefill_tokens). EVERY running request then emits one token (generated += 1;
         the first time, first_token_ms = t). One that has emitted output_len tokens finishes at t:
         release its pages and record to_done(live, t).
    Return a SimResult (done sorted by rid; preemptions = number of preempt_one calls)."""

    waiting, running, done = deque(), [], []
    t, steps, tokens, preemptions = 0.0, 0, 0, 0
    while pending or waiting or running:
        while pending and pending[0].req.arrival_ms <= t:
            waiting.append(pending.popleft())
        if not waiting and not running:
            t = pending[0].req.arrival_ms
            continue
        # A. grow the running requests, preempting the youngest when the cache is full
        preempted = False
        i = 0

                preemptions += 1
                preempted = True
                if victim is s:

                i += 1

        new = []

            while waiting and len(running) < cfg.max_batch:

            tokens += 1

            if s.generated == s.req.output_len:
                running.remove(s)
                alloc.release(s.req.rid)
                done.append(to_done(s, t))
    done.sort(key=lambda d: d.rid)

Milestone 5: preemption when the cache is full

Paged allocation admits requests on the promise of a short prompt and a short answer, and the promise can fail: ten requests that were comfortable at 400 tokens each may all need a new page in the same step with none left. The engine must then free memory, and the simplest policy that always works is preemption by recomputation. Pick a victim, release its pages, put it back at the front of the queue, and when it is admitted again process its entire context (prompt plus the tokens it had already generated) as a fresh prefill. The tokens already sent to the user stay sent, so the user sees a stall, not a restart. The alternative is to copy the victim's pages to host memory and back, which saves the recomputation and costs bandwidth; the lab implements the first.

Which victim? The youngest running request (the last in running), for two reasons: it has the least progress to throw away, and the oldest requests are the nearest to finishing and to freeing their pages. Choosing the oldest would evict the request closest to done, make it redo the most work, and delay the user who has already waited longest. Step through the smallest case that forces it: a cache of 4 pages of 4 tokens, and two requests that each have a 6-token prompt and 6 output tokens (the test configuration: 10 ms a step, 1 ms per prompt token).

Step through

Preempting B so that A can grow

  1. Admit both. Two requests of 6-token prompts fill the four pages.
  2. Decode. Both contexts grow to 8 tokens, which still fits.
  3. Preempt. A needs a third page and the youngest request, B, pays for it.
  4. Wait. A runs alone, finishes at 72 ms and frees its pages; B had to wait for three pages.
  5. Recompute. B is prefilled again with its 8-token context and finishes at 120 ms.

The result has an instructive asymmetry. A's first token arrives at 22 ms and its last at 72; B's first token also arrives at 22 ms, but it does not finish until 120. B's preemption did not change its TTFT (the first token was already out), it stretched its tokens apart: the TPOT of B is (120 − 22) / 5 = 19.6 ms against A's 10 ms. Preemption protects the system and charges the cost to one user's streaming speed. Now shrink the cache under a fixed workload and watch the cost add up:

def preempt_one(running, waiting, alloc):
    """Free cache by evicting the YOUNGEST running request (the last one in `running`).

    Remove it from `running`, release its pages, count the event on the request
    (victim.preemptions += 1), and put it at the FRONT of `waiting` (waiting.appendleft). It keeps
    `generated`: the tokens already sent to the user stay sent, and when it is admitted again its
    whole context (prompt plus generated tokens) is prefilled again, a RECOMPUTE preemption.
    Return the victim. Raise RuntimeError if `running` is empty."""
    if not running:
        raise RuntimeError("nothing to preempt")
    victim = running.pop()
    alloc.release(victim.req.rid)
    victim.preemptions += 1
    waiting.appendleft(victim)
    return victim

Milestone 6: capacity under an SLO, and what a token costs

Throughput on its own is the wrong target. A system that serves 800 tokens a second with a first token after ten seconds is a batch job. Capacity planning starts from a service level objective (SLIs, SLOs and error budgets) and asks the reverse question: what is the highest arrival rate at which the objective still holds? The lab uses an interactive-chat style target: TTFT p95 at most 1,000 ms and TPOT p95 at most 80 ms. run_sweep replays the same seeded workload at a list of arrival rates through one policy (the workloads differ only in time scale, the property you tested in milestone 2), and max_rate_under_slo scans the rows in order and returns the last rate before the first violation. That first-violation rule is deliberate: a system that fails at 8 and passes at 10 is not safe to run at 10, because the failure at 8 shows the knee is already behind it (queueing theory: latency does not recover as load rises).

The last step is the one chapter 22 started with: a GPU costs the same whether busy or idle, so the cost of a token is the hourly price divided by the tokens served per hour, price / (tokens_per_s × 3600) × 106 per million. Evaluate it at the throughput the system delivers at its SLO capacity, not at its peak, because the peak is not a rate you are allowed to run at. The price below, $1.00 an hour, is an assumption for a 24 GB class card, chosen so that the ratios are easy to read; use your own.

def run_sweep(policy, rates, n, seed, cfg):
    """For each arrival rate (requests per second, ascending), simulate `policy` (a key of POLICIES)
    on make_workload(n, rate, seed) and return a list of dicts: summarise(...) plus the key "rate".
    All rates share the same seed, so the workloads differ only by their time scale."""
    rows = []
    for rate in rates:
        sim = POLICIES[policy](make_workload(n, rate, seed), cfg)
        row = summarise(sim)
        row["rate"] = rate
        rows.append(row)
    return rows

def max_rate_under_slo(rows, ttft_p95_ms, tpot_p95_ms):
    """The highest arrival rate the system sustains within its SLO: scan rows in order and return
    the "rate" of the last row BEFORE the first one that violates it (ttft_p95 > ttft_p95_ms or
    tpot_p95 > tpot_p95_ms). 0.0 if the first row already violates it; the last rate if none does."""
    best = 0.0
    for row in rows:
        if row["ttft_p95"] > ttft_p95_ms or row["tpot_p95"] > tpot_p95_ms:
            break
        best = row["rate"]
    return best

def cost_per_million(tokens_per_s, price_per_hour):
    """Dollars per million tokens for a GPU that costs `price_per_hour` and sustains `tokens_per_s`
    (chapter 22): price_per_hour / (tokens_per_s * 3600) * 1e6."""
    return price_per_hour / (tokens_per_s * 3600.0) * 1e6

Look under the hood

  • What a real engine adds to the loop. Chunked prefill splits a long prompt into pieces of a fixed token budget so that decoding requests are never stalled for 240 ms by one big prompt; in the lab's model it would bound the prefill_ms term per step and flatten the TPOT tail you saw rising in milestone 4. Prefix caching lets requests that share a prompt prefix share its pages (reference counts on pages, copy on write when they diverge), which lowers both TTFT and cache use. Both appear in chapter 20; neither changes the shape of the loop.
  • Swap versus recompute. The lab recomputes a preempted request's context. An engine may instead copy its pages to host memory over PCIe and copy them back; whichever is cheaper depends on context length and link bandwidth, and short contexts favour recomputation. Both choose the youngest victim for the reasons in milestone 5.
  • Why admission looks only one token ahead. The paged mode admits a request when its prompt and one token fit. That is optimistic, and preemption is the price of the optimism. A more cautious engine keeps a watermark of free pages, or admits only while the sum of the running requests' expected growth fits; each moves the knee in this lab's tables. Try it in the stretch goals.
  • Where the cost model is crude. It adds the memory and compute terms where a real step takes roughly the larger of the two, charges the same base_ms to a batch of 1 as to a batch of 64 (true while decode is memory-bound, false at very large batches), ignores attention's own FLOPs and ignores the all-reduce between GPUs when a model is sharded (chapter 3). The scheduler's behaviour comes from the structure, not from these constants, which is why changing DEFAULT moves the numbers and leaves the orderings alone.
  • Open loop, not closed loop. Requests arrive on a schedule whatever the system's state (an open workload). Real clients often wait for an answer before sending the next message, which makes a slow system see less load. A load test that lets the tool wait for responses understates the damage in exactly the failure you care about (load testing and capacity planning).

Common misconception

"Continuous batching makes every request faster." It makes the system faster, mostly by removing idle slots and queueing behind long answers, and it does so by running more requests at once, which makes each step a little slower for every request in it. In the tables, TPOT p95 rose from 30 ms to 83 ms between 2 and 12 requests a second while TTFT and throughput improved. A single request on an idle GPU is not served faster by any of this. The goal is a GPU that is full of useful work and a queue that stays short, and the SLO is how you decide how much per-request slowdown you will accept to get it.

Systems connection

The scheduler you wrote is an old idea in new clothes. The block table is the page table of virtual memory, and preemption by recomputation is thrashing with a policy: when the working set exceeds memory, the machine spends its time paging. The percentiles are the tail-latency story of tail latency at scale, and the knee in the tables is the utilisation knee of queueing theory, with the twist that the "server" runs many jobs per step. The SLO and capacity-planning method of milestone 6 is the same loop an SRE runs on any service (load testing, overload and load shedding): in production the right response when the cache is full and the queue is long is often to refuse new work early rather than to preempt and recompute. And the dollars per million tokens link back to the economics of a token and to the design of ML systems: the choice between self-hosting and an API starts with a capacity number like yours.

What you learned

  • The cache, not the arithmetic, decides how many conversations share a GPU: 128 KiB per token for an 8B model with grouped-query attention, 3,070 pages beside the weights on a 24 GB card, and 11 sequences of 4,096 tokens.
  • A paged allocator keeps a block table per request and hands out fixed-size pages on demand, so waste is at most one partly used page per request. Reserving prompt + max_new instead makes capacity depend on what clients put in max_tokens.
  • Static batching runs every batch as long as its longest answer, so slots idle (7 tokens per step from a nominal 64) and new requests queue behind other people's long outputs. Continuous batching decides at every step, which is the difference between tens of seconds and a quarter of a second of TTFT.
  • Preemption by recomputation keeps the system alive when the cache is full, at the price of repeated prefills and stretched TPOT for the victim, who should be the youngest request. A shrinking cache can lower TPOT while TTFT and throughput get worse, so no single metric is safe to watch alone.
  • Capacity is the highest arrival rate that meets the SLO, stopping at the first violation, and a token costs the hourly price divided by the tokens served per hour at that rate. In the lab paging raised the SLO capacity from 6 to 10 requests a second, a drop from $0.55 to $0.38 per million tokens on the assumed price.

When the cache is full the simulator evicts the youngest running request, not the oldest. Why?

  1. The youngest request is always the largest, so evicting it frees the most pages
  2. The youngest has had the most time to finish, so it is the most likely to be done
  3. The youngest has made the least progress, so it has the least to recompute, and the oldest are closest to finishing and to freeing their own pages
  4. The oldest request cannot be preempted because its pages are pinned
Recompute preemption throws away work in proportion to the victim's context, and evicting the oldest would also delay the user who has already waited longest.
