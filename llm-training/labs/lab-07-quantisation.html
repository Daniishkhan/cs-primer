Lab 7: Quantise a Model

Chapter 19 explained how a weight is squeezed into a few bits, why one large number spoils the grid for its neighbours, and how SmoothQuant and AWQ move that difficulty somewhere harmless. It showed numbers from a handful of scripts; this lab makes you build every piece and measure it. You write symmetric and asymmetric quantisers, the three scale granularities, a real int4 packer with a byte count, the activation fixes (mixed precision and SmoothQuant) and an AWQ-style scale search that uses calibration data. All of it runs on one small synthetic layer in plain Python, and every step is scored with the same three numbers: relative output error, top-1 agreement and bits per weight.

Why this lab matters

Quantisation is the cheapest large win in serving: a 4-bit model needs about a quarter of the memory and, in the memory-bound decode regime of chapter 18, reads about a quarter of the bytes per token. It is also the optimisation most likely to be applied by pulling a checkpoint off a hub without looking inside, and the failures are quiet. Nothing crashes. The model answers a little worse, on the inputs you did not test. Writing the quantiser yourself teaches the things a library hides: that the "4-bit" model is really 4.125 bits because the scales have to live somewhere, that a symmetric grid wastes half its levels on one-sided data, that two loud activation channels can make an int8 layer five to ten times worse than it needs to be, that an AWQ exponent is a hyperparameter you tune on data and that tuning on the wrong data is worse than not tuning. The lab also teaches the habit that makes quantisation safe to ship: never trust the average error, and always evaluate on tokens the search did not see (chapter 23).

Deterministic, small, standard library only

There is no real model, no GPU and no NumPy. The layer is generated from fixed seeds (make_weights(), make_activations(seed)), every random test input comes from a seeded random.Random, and a failure prints the seed so you can replay it (python3 check.py solution 2 17 runs milestone 2 with seed 17 only). Python 3.9 or newer. The whole check takes a couple of seconds. Quantisation here is simulated ("fake quantisation"): a value is rounded to its integer and immediately multiplied back by its scale, so every matrix stays a matrix of floats. That is how research code measures accuracy, and it gives the same answer as a real integer kernel up to float rounding; what it cannot show is speed. The sizes (48 × 256 weights, 64 tokens, outlier channels 40 times larger) are illustrative assumptions chosen to reproduce the pattern in chapter 19, not measurements of any real model.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each building on the last:

  1. Symmetric quantisation: qmax, the absmax scale, rounding and clamping to the grid −qmax to +qmax, and dequantising. Chapter 19's eight example weights must come out exactly.
  2. Asymmetric quantisation: an unsigned grid with a zero point, so that one-sided data uses all of its levels and real zero is still exact.
  3. Granularity and what scales cost: one scale per tensor, per output channel or per group, and the effective bits per weight once the scales are counted.
  4. Real bytes: pack int4 values two per byte, unpack them, count the bytes of a quantised model and work out what fits on a 24 GB card.
  5. Activation outliers: quantise activations per tensor or per token, find outlier channels, compute the LLM.int8()-style mixed-precision product and the SmoothQuant rewrite.
  6. AWQ-style scales: scale weight columns by activation magnitude raised to an exponent, search the exponent on calibration data, and test it on held-out tokens, including what happens when the calibration data is wrong.

The setting: one layer, two kinds of trouble

A transformer is mostly stacked linear layers, and a linear layer is a matrix multiplication. Following chapter 19 and A2, call the activations X (T tokens by C input channels) and store the weights output-first as W (D rows, one per output, by C columns). The layer computes Y = X WT, a T × D matrix. Per-channel quantisation means one scale per row of W, that is per output channel. In this lab a matrix is a list of rows and a row is a list of floats.

The given code builds a layer that has both of the troubles chapter 19 described:

ROWS, COLS, TOKENS = 48, 256, 64          # D, C, T
LOUD_ROWS = {5: 12.0, 30: 20.0}           # output channels whose weights are 12x and 20x larger
SPIKES = [(2, 40, 8.0), (17, 130, 8.0), (41, 200, 8.0), (44, 7, 8.0)]   # single weights 8x larger
OUTLIER_CHANNELS = (11, 201)              # input channels that are huge in the activations
OUTLIER_FACTOR = 40.0


def make_weights(seed=7):
    """D x C weights: N(0, 0.02^2), two loud rows and four spikes (all sizes illustrative)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    W = [[rng.gauss(0.0, 0.02) for _ in range(COLS)] for _ in range(ROWS)]
    for r, f in LOUD_ROWS.items():
        W[r] = [v * f for v in W[r]]
    for r, c, f in SPIKES:
        W[r][c] *= f
    return W


def make_activations(seed, channels=OUTLIER_CHANNELS, factor=OUTLIER_FACTOR, tokens=TOKENS):
    """T x C activations: N(0, 1) with the given input channels multiplied by `factor`."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    X = [[rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(COLS)] for _ in range(tokens)]
    for row in X:
        for c in channels:
            row[c] *= factor
    return X
  • The weights are Gaussian with standard deviation 0.02, except that two output rows are 12 and 20 times larger ("loud" rows) and four single weights are 8 times larger (spikes). A loud row ruins a per-tensor scale; a spike ruins the scale of whatever group contains it.
  • The activations are standard normal, except that input channels 11 and 201 are 40 times larger in every token. This is the pattern Dettmers et al. (2022) found in large transformers: a few channels with systematically huge values.

Three measurements are given, so that every milestone is judged by the same yardstick. rel_error(approx, ref) is ‖approx − ref‖ / ‖ref‖ (the same measure chapter 19 prints). matmul_nt(X, W) computes X WT. And top1_agreement(Yq, Y) treats the D outputs as logits and returns the fraction of tokens whose argmax is unchanged, a crude stand-in for "the model said the same thing". Average error can be small while the answer flips; seeing both numbers side by side is part of the point.

Milestone 1: symmetric quantisation

Pick a signed integer format with bits bits. The largest value on the symmetric grid is qmax = 2bits−1 − 1: 127 for int8, 7 for int4. (Two's complement also has −128 and −8, but the symmetric scheme leaves them unused so that the grid is balanced around zero; int4 therefore has 15 levels, not 16.) The recipe from chapter 19:

scale s = max|w| / qmax,    q = clamp(round(w / s), −qmax, qmax),    ŵ = q × s

The largest-magnitude value lands exactly on ±qmax and comes back exactly; every other value is off by at most s/2, half a step. So the whole game is keeping s small, and s is set by the single largest number in the group. Two details bite. Python's round rounds halves to the nearest even integer (round(2.5) is 2), which is fine as long as you use it consistently. And an all-zero group has absmax 0, so its scale would be 0 and the division would fail; the contract is scale 1.0 in that case.

Here is the effect of one large number, on chapter 19's eight weights, run with your code. In the second run one weight is 3.1 instead of 0.31:

Solution spoiler 
def qmax(bits):
    """The largest integer of a signed `bits`-bit symmetric grid: 7 for 4 bits, 127 for 8. (The grid is -qmax..qmax.)"""
    return 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1

def scale_sym(values, bits):
    """absmax / qmax(bits). An all-zero list has scale 1.0, so that nothing divides by zero."""
    m = max(abs(v) for v in values)
    return m / qmax(bits) if m > 0 else 1.0

def quantise_sym(values, bits):
    """Return (ints, scale): ints[i] = clamp(round(values[i] / scale), -qmax, +qmax), using Python's round()."""
    s = scale_sym(values, bits)
    q = qmax(bits)
    return [max(-q, min(q, round(v / s))) for v in values], s

def dequantise_sym(ints, scale):
    """ints * scale, as floats."""
    return [q * scale for q in ints]

Milestone 2: asymmetric quantisation and the zero point

A symmetric grid spends half its levels on negative numbers. That is right for weights, which are close to symmetric around zero, and wasteful for data that is all positive, such as the output of a ReLU. Asymmetric quantisation places the grid over the actual interval [lo, hi] of the data. The integers are now unsigned, 0 to 2bits−1, and a zero point z says which integer stands for real zero:

s = (hi − lo) / (2bits − 1),    z = round(−lo / s),    q = clamp(round(w / s) + z, 0, 2bits−1),    ŵ = (q − z) × s

One rule makes the scheme usable: real zero must be inside the range, so take lo = min(min(values), 0) and hi = max(max(values), 0). Zero padding, masked positions and ReLU outputs are exactly zero in a model, and the zero point is rounded to an integer precisely so that they stay exactly zero after quantisation. A grid that cannot represent 0.0 would add a small constant error to every padded position.

The price of the extra flexibility is one more stored number per group and an extra subtraction in the arithmetic (the zero-point term in the integer matrix multiply). That is why weights, which gain almost nothing, are usually symmetric, and why asymmetric schemes appear mostly for activations and for the key-value cache, where the values really are lopsided.

Solution spoiler 
def quantise_asym(values, bits):
    """Return (ints, scale, zero_point) on the UNSIGNED grid 0 .. 2**bits - 1."""
    top = 2 ** bits - 1
    lo, hi = min(min(values), 0.0), max(max(values), 0.0)
    if hi == lo:
        return [0] * len(values), 1.0, 0
    s = (hi - lo) / top
    z = round(-lo / s)
    return [max(0, min(top, round(v / s) + z)) for v in values], s, z

def dequantise_asym(ints, scale, zero_point):
    """(ints - zero_point) * scale, as floats."""
    return [(q - zero_point) * scale for q in ints]

Milestone 3: per-tensor, per-channel and per-group scales

So far one scale covered the list you gave it. A weight matrix offers a choice of which weights share a scale, and chapter 19's lesson is that the choice matters more as bits fall:

Per-tensor
One scale for the whole matrix. Cheapest, and one loud row sets the step for all of it.
Per-channel
One scale per row of W (per output channel). The dot product that produces output d uses a single scale, so applying it costs almost nothing.
Per-group
One scale per run of g consecutive weights within a row, with g typically 32, 64 or 128. It confines a spike to its group, and it costs one scale per g weights.

Scales are numbers that must be stored, usually in 16 bits. So the real storage per weight is bits + scale_bits × (number of scales) / (number of weights). With groups of 128 and a 16-bit scale that is 16/128 = 0.125 extra bits, and the 4-bit model is a 4.125-bit model; with groups of 32 it would be 4.5. Per-channel on a 4096 × 4096 matrix adds only 16/4096 bits, which is why it is the default for int8.

def fake_quantise_matrix(W, bits, granularity, group=128):
    """Quantise then dequantise a matrix (symmetric), so the result shows what the integers mean."""
    if granularity == "tensor":
        ints, s = quantise_sym(flatten(W), bits)
        flat = dequantise_sym(ints, s)
        c = len(W[0])
        return [flat[i * c:(i + 1) * c] for i in range(len(W))]
    out = []
    for row in W:
        if granularity == "channel":
            ints, s = quantise_sym(row, bits)
            out.append(dequantise_sym(ints, s))
        elif granularity == "group":
            new = []
            for i in range(0, len(row), group):
                ints, s = quantise_sym(row[i:i + group], bits)
                new.extend(dequantise_sym(ints, s))
            out.append(new)
        else:
            raise ValueError(f"unknown granularity {granularity!r}")
    return out

def n_scales(rows, cols, granularity, group=128):
    """How many scale numbers a rows x cols matrix needs: 1, rows, or rows * ceil(cols / group)."""
    if granularity == "tensor":
        return 1
    if granularity == "channel":
        return rows
    if granularity == "group":
        return rows * -(-cols // group)
    raise ValueError(f"unknown granularity {granularity!r}")

def bits_per_weight(bits, rows, cols, granularity, group=128, scale_bits=16):
    """Effective storage per weight: bits + scale_bits * n_scales / (rows * cols)."""
    return bits + scale_bits * n_scales(rows, cols, granularity, group) / (rows * cols)

Milestone 4: real bytes

Until now every quantised value has been a Python float standing in for an integer. A deployed model stores the integers themselves, and for 4-bit weights that means two values per byte. Packing puts the first value in the low four bits and the second in the high four. A negative value is stored as its 4-bit two's complement, v & 0xF (so −2 is 0xE and −8 is 0x8), and unpacking sign-extends: a nibble of 8 or more means nibble − 16. An odd number of values leaves a zero nibble of padding. Real file formats differ in the order they pack in and in how they interleave values to suit their kernels, so treat this as the idea rather than any one format.

With packing you can count bytes honestly. For n weights at b bits with one 16-bit scale per group of g: ceil(n · b / 8) + ceil(n / g) · 2 bytes. That is the whole memory story of a weight-only model, and it is the first line of every capacity plan. With the shapes of Llama 3 8B from the Llama 3 report (Dubey et al., 2024), which the given llama_params("8B") turns into 8.03 billion parameters, and the assumptions of chapter 19 (a 24 GB card, 2 GB set aside for the CUDA context and activations, a bf16 KV cache of 128 KiB per token, so exactly 1 GiB for an 8,192-token sequence), the question "how many conversations can this card hold?" becomes arithmetic:

sequences = floor((24 GB − 2 GB − weight bytes) / 1 GiB)

The 24 GB figure is the memory of consumer-class cards such as NVIDIA's RTX 4090; the 2 GB of overhead is an assumption, and real overhead depends on the engine and batch shape.

State of play, October 2026: block-scaled 4-bit floating-point formats

This lab uses integer grids with a 16-bit scale per group of 128. A newer family keeps small groups but makes both the element and the scale cheaper. The Open Compute Project's Microscaling (MX) specification, version 1.0 of September 2023, defines blocks of 32 elements that share one 8-bit power-of-two scale; with 4-bit floating-point (E2M1) elements that is about 4.25 bits per weight, against this lab's 4.125 for int4 with 16-bit scales and groups of 128. NVIDIA's NVFP4 is described as using blocks of 16 elements with an FP8 (E4M3) scale. I read these figures in secondary sources and did not fetch the specifications themselves, and hardware support and library defaults move quickly, so check the current documentation before relying on any of them. The accounting you write in this milestone applies unchanged: bits per element plus the scale bits divided by the block size.

Solution spoiler 
def pack_int4(ints):
    """Pack signed 4-bit integers (-8 .. 7) two per byte, as `bytes`."""
    nibbles = []
    for v in ints:
        if not -8 <= v <= 7:
            raise ValueError(f"{v} does not fit in a signed 4-bit integer")
        nibbles.append(v & 0xF)
    if len(nibbles) % 2:
        nibbles.append(0)
    return bytes(nibbles[i] | (nibbles[i + 1] << 4) for i in range(0, len(nibbles), 2))

def unpack_int4(data, n):
    """Inverse of pack_int4: the first n signed values (sign-extend each nibble)."""
    out = []
    for b in data:
        for nib in (b & 0xF, b >> 4):
            out.append(nib - 16 if nib >= 8 else nib)
    return out[:n]

def weight_bytes(n_weights, bits, group=None, scale_bits=16):
    """Bytes to store n_weights at `bits` bits each, plus one `scale_bits` scale per `group` weights (none if group is None)."""
    total = -(-n_weights * bits // 8)
    if group:
        total += -(-n_weights // group) * scale_bits // 8
    return total

def sequences_that_fit(weights_bytes, gpu_bytes=24e9, overhead_bytes=2e9, kv_bytes_per_sequence=KV_BYTES_PER_SEQUENCE_8B):
    """How many whole sequences' KV caches fit beside the weights: floor((gpu - overhead - weights) / kv), at least 0."""
    room = gpu_bytes - overhead_bytes - weights_bytes
    return max(0, int(room // kv_bytes_per_sequence))

Milestone 5: activation outliers

Weights are the easy half: known in advance, close to symmetric, quantised once. Activations are computed fresh for every input, and a few input channels are huge in every token. For a layer to run on fast integer hardware (W8A8: 8-bit weights and 8-bit activations) the activations need a scale too, and the scale cannot be per input channel, because the integer matrix multiplication sums over input channels and the scales would have to sit inside that sum. A per-tensor scale is set by the largest value in the matrix, which is an outlier channel, so every other channel is crushed onto a few levels. A scale per token (per row of X) helps a little, but the outlier channels are in every row.

The lab's activations have two such channels at 40 times the usual size. Two remedies from chapter 19 are yours to implement.

Mixed precision (LLM.int8(), Dettmers et al., 2022). Find the outlier channels, multiply those in full precision, and do the rest in int8: Y = Xnormal WnormalT (int8) + Xoutlier WoutlierT (float). The paper detects outliers with an absolute magnitude threshold; the lab uses a relative one, a channel whose absmax is more than 6 times the median channel absmax, because the synthetic scale is arbitrary.

SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022). Divide input channel c of X by a factor sc and multiply column c of W by the same factor. The product is unchanged, because (xc/sc)(sc wc) = xcwc, but the outlier has moved from the activations (hard to scale) to the weights (where per-channel scales absorb it). The factor balances the two sides:

sc = max|Xc|α / max|Wc|1−α

where α (usually 0.5) sets how much of the difficulty to migrate. At α = 0.5, after smoothing, channel c has the same absmax, √(max|Xc| · max|Wc|), on both sides; at α = 1 all of it moves and X's channels all have absmax 1.

def fake_quantise_activations(X, bits, granularity):
    """Quantise then dequantise activations X (T x C), symmetric."""
    if granularity == "tensor":
        ints, s = quantise_sym(flatten(X), bits)
        flat = dequantise_sym(ints, s)
        c = len(X[0])
        return [flat[i * c:(i + 1) * c] for i in range(len(X))]
    if granularity == "token":
        out = []
        for row in X:
            ints, s = quantise_sym(row, bits)
            out.append(dequantise_sym(ints, s))
        return out
    raise ValueError(f"unknown granularity {granularity!r}")

def find_outlier_channels(x_absmax, ratio=6.0):
    """Indices of input channels whose absmax exceeds `ratio` times the MEDIAN absmax, in increasing order."""
    s = sorted(x_absmax)
    n = len(s)
    med = s[n // 2] if n % 2 else 0.5 * (s[n // 2 - 1] + s[n // 2])
    return [c for c, v in enumerate(x_absmax) if v > ratio * med]

def mixed_int8_output(X, W, outliers):
    """LLM.int8()-style decomposition. Y = X_normal W_normal^T (int8) + X_outlier W_outlier^T (float)."""
    out_set = set(outliers)
    keep = [c for c in range(len(X[0])) if c not in out_set]
    Xn = [[row[c] for c in keep] for row in X]
    Wn = [[row[c] for c in keep] for row in W]
    if keep:
        Y = matmul_nt(fake_quantise_activations(Xn, 8, "tensor"), fake_quantise_matrix(Wn, 8, "channel"))
    else:                                    # every channel is an outlier: nothing left to quantise
        Y = [[0.0] * len(W) for _ in X]
    if out_set:
        idx = sorted(out_set)
        Xo = [[row[c] for c in idx] for row in X]
        Wo = [[row[c] for c in idx] for row in W]
        Yo = matmul_nt(Xo, Wo)
        Y = [[a + b for a, b in zip(r1, r2)] for r1, r2 in zip(Y, Yo)]
    return Y

def smooth_scales(x_absmax, w_absmax, alpha=0.5):
    """SmoothQuant factors s[c] = x_absmax[c]**alpha / w_absmax[c]**(1 - alpha); s[c] = 1.0 if either is 0."""
    return [(x ** alpha) / (w ** (1 - alpha)) if x > 0 and w > 0 else 1.0 for x, w in zip(x_absmax, w_absmax)]

def apply_smoothing(X, W, s):
    """Return (X', W') with X'[t][c] = X[t][c] / s[c] and W'[d][c] = W[d][c] * s[c]. X' W'^T equals X W^T."""
    return ([[v / s[c] for c, v in enumerate(row)] for row in X],
            [[v * s[c] for c, v in enumerate(row)] for row in W])

Milestone 6: AWQ-style scales and calibration

Weight-only int4 has no activation to quantise, yet the activations still decide where the error hurts. Output d is a sum of terms xc wdc; rounding error in weight wdc is multiplied by xc, so error on the weights in columns where the activations are large costs the most. Lin et al. (2023) ("AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization") protect those columns by scaling: multiply column c of W by sc before rounding, and divide by it afterwards. A weight scaled up by s is rounded on the same grid (the group scale is set by the group maximum, which has not changed if the scaled weight stays below it), so its relative rounding error falls by about s. The cost appears when s is large enough to raise a group's maximum: the step grows for every other weight in that group. Hence an optimum in between. The scales come from the activations' average magnitude raised to an exponent a:

sc = (mean|xc|)a / √(maxc s · minc s)

The division by √(max · min) only centres the scales around 1. You search a on a grid from 0 to 1 and keep the value with the lowest output error on calibration data: a few hundred (here, 64) representative inputs used to measure activation statistics and nothing else. Exponent 0 is plain round-to-nearest (RTN), the baseline.

The curve shows the two things you will check. It falls and then rises: a = 0.4 is best at 0.063 (RTN is 0.192) and a = 1.0 (0.276) is worse than plain rounding (0.192), because the scaled outlier columns raise their groups' maxima. And the red curve is the danger of calibration data that does not match deployment: scales built from the wrong activation statistics protect the wrong columns, and the damage grows with the exponent. The search cannot warn you, because on its own calibration data it still finds an improvement; only held-out data from the real traffic shows the harm.

def awq_scales(x_absmean, a):
    """s[c] = x_absmean[c]**a, then divided by sqrt(max(s) * min(s)) so the scales are centred around 1."""
    s = [max(v, 1e-12) ** a for v in x_absmean]
    norm = math.sqrt(max(s) * min(s))
    return [v / norm for v in s]

def awq_effective_weights(W, x_absmean, a, bits=4, group=128):
    """The weights the layer effectively uses after AWQ scaling: scale column c of W up by s[c], fake-quantise"""
    s = awq_scales(x_absmean, a)
    Ws = [[v * s[c] for c, v in enumerate(row)] for row in W]
    Wq = fake_quantise_matrix(Ws, bits, "group", group)
    return [[v / s[c] for c, v in enumerate(row)] for row in Wq]

def search_awq(X_cal, W, bits=4, group=128, grid=11):
    """Grid-search the exponent a in linspace(0, 1, grid) on calibration data X_cal."""
    Y = matmul_nt(X_cal, W)
    mean = col_absmean(X_cal)
    errors = []
    for i in range(grid):
        a = i / (grid - 1)
        errors.append(rel_error(matmul_nt(X_cal, awq_effective_weights(W, mean, a, bits, group)), Y))
    best = min(range(grid), key=lambda i: (errors[i], i))
    return best / (grid - 1), errors[best], errors

Look under the hood

  • What fake quantisation hides. A real W4A16 kernel reads the packed nibbles, multiplies by the group scale in registers and accumulates in 16- or 32-bit floats; a real W8A8 kernel accumulates integer products in 32 bits and applies the scales to the sum. Neither writes a dequantised copy to memory, which is where the speed comes from (chapter 19 on fused kernels). The values are the same as in your simulation up to rounding order. The speed is not simulated here at all: do not read "4.125 bits" as "3.9 times faster".
  • Why per-token scales do not fix outlier channels. A scale per token fits each token's range but the outlier channels are in every token, so each token's scale is still set by them. Per-token helps when the range varies between tokens, not when it varies between channels.
  • What GPTQ adds. AWQ changes the grid by scaling and then rounds to nearest. GPTQ keeps the grid and changes the rounding: after rounding one weight it adjusts the not-yet-rounded weights in the row to compensate, using second-order information from the calibration inputs. The lab's search_awq and the stretch goal below are the entry point; the full method needs a Cholesky factorisation of the input covariance.
  • Why the exponent is so sensitive. The best exponent is a trade between two errors, one that falls as protected columns get finer and one that rises as their groups get coarser. Both depend on which columns share a group with the protected ones, and the curve is not smooth (the chart has a bump at 0.2). With two calibration tokens the chosen exponent is mostly noise, as the experiment below shows.
  • What a real evaluation adds. The report measures a layer. A model has dozens, and error compounds: a rounding error in layer 3 changes the input to layer 4. Perplexity and task accuracy on held-out data, with an interval, are the measures that matter (chapter 23), and they should include the behaviours you care about most (long chains of arithmetic, tool-call formats, rare languages), because the damage is uneven.

Common misconception

"If the average error is small, the quantised model is fine." The report above shows average relative error and top-1 agreement moving at different rates, and neither is a task metric. Worse, the error is concentrated: in this layer the two loud rows hold about 92% of the weights' squared magnitude, so a single relative-error figure is dominated by them and says little about the other 46 rows. A 4-bit model can look indistinguishable on a general benchmark and fail on exactly the rare, important behaviour you shipped it for. You get the model you measured, so measure the cases you care about, on data the calibration never saw.

Systems connection

The byte count in milestone 4 is a capacity plan, and its consequences run through the serving stack: memory freed from weights becomes KV cache, which becomes concurrent sequences in the batch, which is what serving engines convert into throughput and token economics converts into price per million tokens. The memory-bound argument is the roofline reasoning of GPUs and memory bandwidth and performance engineering: measure the bytes moved before you believe a speed-up. And a third-party quantised checkpoint is an artefact you did not build, from calibration data you cannot see, with a scheme that may not match your kernel; it belongs under the same provenance and review as any other dependency (the supply chain), and a quantised release deserves the regression gate of any model change, on the cases that matter to your users.

Key takeaways

  • A scale is set by the largest number that shares it, so error is controlled by who shares a scale: one outlier costs every neighbour its resolution.
  • Symmetric grids suit zero-centred weights; asymmetric grids with a zero point suit one-sided data, and real zero must stay exact.
  • "4-bit" is 4.125 bits or more once scales are stored; count bytes, then count what fits.
  • Activation outliers, not weight grids, dominate int8 error: mixed precision and SmoothQuant fix them in different ways (detect and set aside, or rescale and migrate).
  • AWQ-style scaling is a hyperparameter search. It needs calibration data that looks like deployment, and held-out tokens to prove it worked.
  • Report relative error, an answer-level metric and bits per weight together; one number can hide the damage.

Check your understanding

A group of 128 weights quantised to symmetric int4 contains one weight about 10 times larger than the rest. What happens to the other 127?

  1. Nothing: the maximum itself is quantised exactly, so the others are unaffected
  2. They are clamped to the largest level, because the grid cannot represent the outlier
  3. They share a step about 10 times coarser than they need, so most round to zero or ±1 level
  4. They get an asymmetric zero point automatically that compensates
The scale is absmax/qmax, so a 10 times larger maximum makes the step 10 times larger for everyone in the group; weights that used to span the 15 levels now use two or three. The first option is the tempting one: the outlier is indeed exact, but the cost lands on its neighbours. Nothing is clamped, because the scale is derived from the maximum.

At int4 on ReLU outputs (all values between 0 and 6), why does an asymmetric quantiser beat a symmetric one at the same bit width?

  1. It puts all 16 levels on [0, 6] instead of spending the negative half of its grid on values that never occur, so the step is about half as large
  2. It stores more bits per value through the zero point
  3. It rounds more accurately, because it uses ceiling instead of round-to-nearest
  4. It removes the need for a scale
The symmetric grid spans −6 to 6 with 15 levels, so only the non-negative ones are reachable; the asymmetric grid spans 0 to 6 with 16. The zero point is a stored integer but does not widen the values (second option). Both use round-to-nearest, and asymmetric still has a scale.

An int4 model uses groups of 32 weights, each with a 16-bit scale. Roughly how many bits does each weight really cost, and how many bytes is a 7-billion-parameter model?

  1. 4 bits and 3.5 GB, since scales are negligible
  2. 4.125 bits and 3.6 GB
  3. 4.25 bits and 3.7 GB
  4. 4.5 bits and 3.9 GB
16/32 = 0.5 extra bits gives 4.5 bits per weight, and 7 billion × 4.5 / 8 = 3.94 GB. The second option is the right answer for groups of 128 (16/128 = 0.125), which is the tempting figure because it is the common default. Smaller groups buy accuracy at exactly this price.

SmoothQuant divides activation channel c by sc and multiplies weight column c by sc. Why does this not change the layer's output, and why does it help?

  1. The two changes cancel only on average, and the improvement comes from the rounding error cancelling too
  2. The product xcwc is unchanged term by term, and it moves the outlier from activations (which need one shared scale) to weights (which have per-channel scales)
  3. It makes the weights smaller, so fewer bits are needed
  4. It removes the outlier channels entirely, so the output no longer depends on them
(x/s)(s w) = x w exactly, for every term, so the output is identical before quantisation. Quantisation then happens on better-balanced matrices. The weights actually get larger in the outlier columns (the difficulty moved there), and nothing is removed.

In the AWQ-style search, the error is 0.192 at exponent 0, 0.063 at 0.4 and 0.276 at 1.0. Why does it rise again at large exponents?

  1. The activation statistics become inaccurate at high exponents
  2. Dividing by the scale at the end adds rounding error of its own
  3. Large scales push the protected columns' weights above their group's old maximum, which coarsens the grid for every other weight in the group
  4. The search grid has too few points to see the minimum
Scaling a column up helps while the group maximum is unchanged: that weight's relative rounding error shrinks. Past that point the group scale grows and the step increases for all the group's other weights. Dividing back is exact in floating point; the activation statistics do not depend on the exponent; and the grid has eleven points, which resolves a minimum at 0.4 easily.

You calibrate AWQ on 64 tokens from a benchmark whose activations have outliers in different channels from your production traffic. The search reports a clear improvement. What should you expect on production traffic?

  1. Possibly worse than plain rounding: the scales protect the wrong columns, and the search's own improvement was measured on the data it was fitted to
  2. The same improvement, because quantisation error depends only on the weights
  3. A smaller but still positive improvement, because the method is robust by construction
  4. No change, because calibration only affects activations, which are not quantised
Quantisation error in the output depends on weights and on which input channels are large, and the scales are built from the calibration activations. In the lab the mismatched calibration gives held-out error 0.283 against 0.192 for plain rounding. Always test on data that looks like deployment, and keep it disjoint from the calibration set.
