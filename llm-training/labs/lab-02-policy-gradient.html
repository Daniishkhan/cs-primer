Lab 2: Policy Gradients & PPO

Chapters 7 and 8 derived two formulas: the REINFORCE gradient, which turns "make good samples more likely" into something you can compute, and PPO's clipped objective with a KL penalty, which stops that gradient from wrecking the policy it improves. Every RL method used to train language models is a refinement of those two. In this lab you implement them from the softmax up, in standard-library Python, on a bandit where you can check every number by hand and on a small gridworld where an agent has to find its way. You will watch a baseline remove a thirty-fold amount of noise, watch the clip stop a reused batch from running away, and turn the KL coefficient up and down to see exactly what the leash costs and what it buys.

Why this lab matters

RLHF, RLVR and GRPO training runs are hard to debug because many things move at once: a generator, a reward, a baseline, a clip, a penalty and a few thousand GPU-hours. The formulas are short and the failure modes are quiet. A baseline that includes the sample's own reward shrinks the gradient by (B − 1)/B and nothing crashes. A ratio computed against the wrong "old" policy makes the clip meaningless. A KL penalty with the wrong sign rewards drift. A tabular policy cannot tell you how a transformer behaves, but it makes every one of these mistakes visible: the policy is a handful of numbers, the expected reward and the exact KL divergence can be computed in closed form, and a gradient can be checked against finite differences. Once you have written each piece and seen the checker catch its classic wrong version, a training log from a real run (ratio statistics, clip fraction, KL, reward) stops being a list of mysterious dials and becomes a set of quantities you know how to compute.

Deterministic, small, standard library only

Every random draw comes from a seeded random.Random, so the same code gives the same numbers on every machine, and the whole check takes about two seconds. Some checks are statistical (for example, that 3,000 random batches average to the true gradient); they use fixed seeds and generous margins, and a failure prints the seed so you can replay it: python3 check.py solution 4 3 runs milestone 4 with seed 3 for the cases that take a seed. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. There is no PyTorch and no NumPy: every vector is a list. That is slower than a tensor library, and it is also why nothing is hidden.

What you'll build

Five milestones. They share one loop, and each changes one box in it:

  1. The softmax policy: a numerically stable softmax and log-softmax, sampling by inverse CDF with exactly one random number, and the score function onehot(a) − π that is the heart of the policy gradient.
  2. REINFORCE with a baseline on a bandit: advantages with no baseline, a batch-mean baseline and a leave-one-out baseline, the gradient estimator, and a training loop. The checker measures bias and variance.
  3. REINFORCE on a gridworld: returns-to-go, an episode rollout, a tabular softmax policy trained against a learned state-value baseline.
  4. PPO's clipped objective: the probability ratio, the clipped surrogate, its gradient (zero exactly when the sample is clipped), and a bandit where one batch is reused ten times.
  5. PPO with a KL penalty: reward shaping with the log-ratio to a reference policy, the k3 estimator, and the gridworld trained from a reference policy at four values of β.

Milestone 1: the softmax policy

A softmax policy turns a vector of logits z into action probabilities π(a) = eza / ∑b ezb. A language model does exactly this at every position, over a vocabulary V instead of four actions. Three small functions carry the whole lab, and each has a classic way to go wrong.

  • Stable softmax. e1000 overflows a float. Subtracting the largest logit from every logit changes nothing mathematically (the common factor cancels) and keeps every exponent at or below zero. The same trick appears in floating point and numerical stability. The checker feeds softmax([1000, 1001]).
  • Log-softmax. The log-probability log π(a) = za − log ∑b ezb is what PPO's ratio and the KL penalty are made of. Computing it as log(softmax(z)[a]) breaks when a probability underflows to zero; the log-sum-exp form with the maximum subtracted does not. log_softmax([1000, 0]) must be [0, -1000].
  • Sampling. Draw u uniformly in [0, 1) and walk along the running total of the probabilities; the first action whose total exceeds u is the sample. This uses exactly one call to rng.random(), which matters for reproducibility (a rollout of 6 steps must consume 6 numbers) and is why the checker feeds you a fake random number generator with chosen values.

The fourth function is the score. Chapter 7 derived it: for a softmax policy the gradient of log π(a) with respect to the logits is onehot(a) − π. In words, a sampled action's logit rises by 1 − π(a) and every other logit falls by its own probability. A rare action that is sampled and rewarded moves a lot; an action that already has probability near 1 barely moves. The checker confirms the formula against finite differences on random logits, and that the entries sum to zero (probabilities always sum to one, so a logit shift cannot change their total).

def softmax(logits):
    """Probabilities proportional to exp(logit). Subtract the maximum first so that
    logits like 1000 do not overflow."""
    m = max(logits)
    e = [math.exp(z - m) for z in logits]
    total = sum(e)
    return [x / total for x in e]

def log_softmax(logits):
    """log of softmax(logits), computed stably as z - max - log(sum(exp(z - max)))."""
    m = max(logits)
    lse = m + math.log(sum(math.exp(z - m) for z in logits))
    return [z - lse for z in logits]

def sample_action(probs, rng):
    """Draw an index with the given probabilities using EXACTLY ONE call to rng.random():
    walk along the cumulative sum and return the first index whose running total is
    greater than u. If rounding leaves u above the last total, return the last index."""
    u = rng.random()
    total = 0.0
    for a, p in enumerate(probs):
        total += p
        if u < total:
            return a
    return len(probs) - 1

def grad_log_prob(logits, a):
    """Gradient of log pi(a) with respect to the logits: onehot(a) - softmax(logits)."""
    p = softmax(logits)
    return [(1.0 if k == a else 0.0) - pk for k, pk in enumerate(p)]

Milestone 2: REINFORCE with a baseline on a bandit

A bandit is the simplest RL problem: K arms, one decision per episode, a noisy reward. The policy is a softmax over K logits, and the quantity to maximise is the expected reward J(z) = ∑a π(a) μ(a). The policy gradient of chapter 7 estimates its gradient from samples:

g = (1/B) ∑i Ai · (onehot(ai) − π)

where Ai = Ri − bi is the advantage, the reward minus a baseline. This milestone is about the choice of b. The checker uses the bandit from chapter 7's variance experiment: four arms with mean rewards 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8, reward noise 0.3, a uniform policy, where the true gradient of the best arm's logit is 0.075. You implement three baselines:

  • "none": A = R. Unbiased, and noisy in proportion to the size of the rewards, not to the differences between them.
  • "mean": subtract the mean of the batch. It adapts to the prompt (here, to the reward scale), but the mean contains the sample being weighted, so the baseline depends slightly on that sample's action. The expected gradient is shrunk by (B − 1)/B.
  • "loo": leave-one-out, subtract the mean of the other B − 1 rewards. The baseline is independent of sample i's action, so there is no bias, and no second model is needed. This is the baseline behind RLOO and, with a scale normalisation added, GRPO (chapter 14).

The checker measures all of this rather than asserting it. It draws 3,000 batches of 16 with the rewards shifted up by 10 (adding a constant to every reward does not change which arm is best) and records the best arm's gradient entry from each batch. def advantages(rewards, baseline="loo"):
    """Turn rewards into advantages A_i = R_i - b_i.

        "none"  b_i = 0
        "mean"  b_i = the mean of ALL the rewards in the batch (includes sample i)
        "loo"   b_i = the mean of the OTHER rewards (leave-one-out); needs at least 2 rewards,
                else ValueError
    Any other name raises ValueError.
    """
    n = len(rewards)
    if baseline == "none":
        return list(rewards)
    if baseline == "mean":
        m = sum(rewards) / n
        return [r - m for r in rewards]
    if baseline == "loo":
        if n < 2:
            raise ValueError("leave-one-out needs at least two samples")
        total = sum(rewards)
        return [r - (total - r) / (n - 1) for r in rewards]
    raise ValueError(f"unknown baseline {baseline!r}")

def policy_gradient(logits, actions, advs):
    """The REINFORCE estimate: the mean over samples of A_i * grad log pi(a_i).
    Returns a list the length of `logits`."""
    g = [0.0] * len(logits)
    for a, adv in zip(actions, advs):
        for k, d in enumerate(grad_log_prob(logits, a)):
            g[k] += adv * d
    return [x / len(actions) for x in g]

def train_bandit(bandit, steps=300, batch=8, lr=0.5, baseline="loo", seed=0):
    """Train a softmax policy on a bandit and return its logits.

    Start from zero logits. Each step: sample `batch` actions from the current policy, pull
    each (action first, then its reward, for every sample in turn), compute advantages with
    `baseline`, and ascend: logits += lr * policy_gradient(...).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    logits = [0.0] * bandit.k
    for _ in range(steps):
        probs = softmax(logits)
        actions, rewards = [], []
        for _ in range(batch):
            a = sample_action(probs, rng)
            actions.append(a)
            rewards.append(bandit.pull(a, rng))
        g = policy_gradient(logits, actions, advantages(rewards, baseline))
        logits = [z + lr * gi for z, gi in zip(logits, g)]
    return logits

Milestone 3: REINFORCE on a gridworld

A bandit has one decision. A language model makes a sequence of them, and a gridworld is the smallest place to see what changes. The world is a 4 × 4 grid. The agent starts in the top-left corner, a goal in the bottom-right pays +1 and ends the episode, two pits pay −1 and end it, a wall blocks one cell, and every other step costs −0.01. An episode is cut off after 30 steps. The policy is a table: one row of four logits per state, the softmax of which is the probability of moving up, down, left or right. A uniformly random policy reaches the goal in about 9% of its episodes (the other 91% hit a pit or run out of steps) and averages a return of −0.79. The GridWorld class is given; you write the learning.

Three ideas turn the bandit estimator into a sequential one.

  • Return-to-go. An action at step t cannot affect rewards before step t, so each action is weighted by the discounted sum of what follows: Gt = rt + γ Gt+1 (chapter 6). With rewards 0, 0, 0, 10 and γ = 0.9 the returns are 7.29, 8.1, 9 and 10. The exact derivation also carries a factor γt; nearly every implementation, this one included, leaves it out.
  • A learned baseline. A table V[s] estimates the return from each state, and the weight of a step is Gt − V[st], the advantage. V is updated towards the returns actually observed, V[s] += α (Gt − V[s]), after the gradient step, so the baseline that weights a batch does not depend on that batch's own actions. It is the small tabular version of the critic of chapter 9.
  • Averaging. The gradient of the whole batch is the sum of every step's A · grad_log_prob, added to the row of the state in which the step happened, then divided by the number of episodes (a mean over trajectories, as in chapter 7), not the number of steps.

Which one a problem needs depends on the scale of its returns, and that is the reason RL libraries normalise or baseline rewards by default.

def returns_to_go(rewards, gamma):
    """G_t = r_t + gamma * G_{t+1}, for every t (the last return is the last reward)."""
    out, g = [], 0.0
    for r in reversed(rewards):
        g = r + gamma * g
        out.append(g)
    return out[::-1]

def run_episode(env, table, rng):
    """Play one episode with the sampling policy softmax(table[s]).

    Returns (states, actions, rewards), three lists of equal length: states[t] is the state
    BEFORE action t. Start at env.reset(); at each step sample an action with sample_action;
    stop when env.step says done or after env.max_steps steps.
    """
    s = env.reset()
    states, actions, rewards = [], [], []
    for _ in range(env.max_steps):
        a = sample_action(softmax(table[s]), rng)
        s2, r, done = env.step(s, a)
        states.append(s)
        actions.append(a)
        rewards.append(r)
        s = s2
        if done:
            break
    return states, actions, rewards

def reinforce_grid_gradient(table, episodes, V, gamma):
    """The REINFORCE gradient for a tabular softmax policy, as a table shaped like `table`.

    For every step t of every episode add (G_t - V[s_t]) * grad log pi(a_t | s_t) to row s_t;
    finally divide everything by the NUMBER OF EPISODES. G_t is the discounted return-to-go
    and V[s] is the state-value baseline. (The gamma^t factor of the exact derivation is
    left out, as nearly every implementation does.)
    """
    grad = [[0.0] * len(row) for row in table]
    for states, actions, rewards in episodes:
        G = returns_to_go(rewards, gamma)
        for s, a, g in zip(states, actions, G):
            adv = g - V[s]
            for k, d in enumerate(grad_log_prob(table[s], a)):
                grad[s][k] += adv * d
    n = len(episodes)
    return [[x / n for x in row] for row in grad]

def update_values(V, episodes, gamma, alpha):
    """Move each visited state's value towards the returns observed from it:
    V[s] += alpha * (G_t - V[s]), step by step in the order they happened. Changes V in place."""
    for states, _, rewards in episodes:
        for s, g in zip(states, returns_to_go(rewards, gamma)):
            V[s] += alpha * (g - V[s])

def train_reinforce_grid(env, iterations=150, episodes=16, lr=0.5, gamma=0.99, v_lr=0.2, baseline=True, seed=0):
    """Train a tabular softmax policy with REINFORCE and return (table, V, history).

    Each iteration: play `episodes` episodes with the current table, take one gradient step
    table[s][k] += lr * grad[s][k] (the baseline V is all zeros when `baseline` is False),
    THEN update V from the same episodes. history[i] is the mean undiscounted return of
    iteration i's episodes.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    table, V, history = zeros_table(env), [0.0] * env.n_states, []
    for _ in range(iterations):
        eps = [run_episode(env, table, rng) for _ in range(episodes)]
        history.append(sum(sum(r) for _, _, r in eps) / episodes)
        grad = reinforce_grid_gradient(table, eps, V if baseline else [0.0] * env.n_states, gamma)
        for s in range(env.n_states):
            for k in range(N_ACTIONS):
                table[s][k] += lr * grad[s][k]
        if baseline:
            update_values(V, eps, gamma, v_lr)
    return table, V, history

Milestone 4: PPO's clipped objective

Rollouts are the expensive part of RL for language models, so we want several gradient steps per batch. But after the first step the policy is no longer the one that produced the samples, and the REINFORCE formula is only valid for the policy that did. Chapter 8's fix is the probability ratio r = πθ(a | s) / πold(a | s), where πold is the policy frozen at the start of the iteration, and a clipped surrogate that stops paying the policy for moving further than ε from it in the direction the advantage asks for:

L = (1/n) ∑i min( ri Ai, clip(ri, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Ai )

Read it by cases, and let the checker's table be your test. With ε = 0.2:

Arobjectivepasses gradient?why
+11.51.2no (clipped)a good action already 50% likelier: no more credit
−10.5−0.8no (clipped)a bad action already 50% less likely: no more credit
−11.5−1.5yesa bad action that became more likely: the min picks the unclipped term, and the gradient corrects the mistake
+10.50.5yesa good action that became less likely: correct it
+10.90.9yesinside the clip range nothing is clipped

So the clip removes the incentive to overshoot and never the incentive to correct. For the gradient you need one identity: ∇r = r · ∇log π. An unclipped sample therefore contributes A · r · (onehot(a) − π), which at r = 1 is exactly the REINFORCE term, and a clipped sample contributes zero. Divide by the number of samples. The checker tests that at π = πold your PPO gradient equals your policy gradient exactly, and then compares it with finite differences of the objective on random cases away from the kinks of the clip.

Then the experiment of chapter 8. A six-armed bandit with true rewards 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 1.0 and 0.1 (arm 4 is best), reward noise 0.5, and only eight samples per iteration, deliberately noisy. Each batch is reused for ten gradient steps with a learning rate of 1.0. Without the clip, one lucky draw can make a poor arm look good and ten steps on that batch drive its probability towards 1. With the clip they cannot (script exp_clip.py, 100 seeds):

Two further rows are worth reading. A very small ε of 0.02 costs a little because it forbids most of the progress, and ε = 0.5 is hardly different from 0.2 at this scale. And a bigger batch of 64 fixes the problem without any clip, by averaging away the lucky draw: the clip is a way to be safe with a small, expensive batch. The first step already moves some ratios past the boundary, the gradient of the samples still inside the range keeps pushing through the shared logits, and what the clip guarantees is that the samples outside the boundary stop contributing, not that the policy stays inside it (the point of the "Common misconception" box in chapter 8).

def ratio(logp_new, logp_old):
    """The probability ratio pi_new(a|s) / pi_old(a|s), from log-probabilities."""
    return math.exp(logp_new - logp_old)

def clipped_surrogate(r, adv, eps=0.2):
    """min(r * A, clip(r, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * A): the PPO objective for one sample."""
    return min(r * adv, min(max(r, 1.0 - eps), 1.0 + eps) * adv)

def is_clipped(r, adv, eps=0.2):
    """True if the sample passes NO gradient: the clipped term is the smaller one.
    A sample at exactly the boundary (r == 1 + eps with A > 0) still passes gradient."""
    return r * adv > min(max(r, 1.0 - eps), 1.0 + eps) * adv

def ppo_gradient(logits, old_logits, actions, advs, eps=0.2):
    """Gradient, with respect to `logits`, of the MEAN clipped surrogate over the samples.

    For sample i the ratio is r_i = pi_logits(a_i) / pi_old_logits(a_i). A clipped sample
    contributes nothing; any other contributes A_i * r_i * grad log pi(a_i), because
    grad r = r * grad log pi. Divide by the number of samples.
    """
    p, p_old = softmax(logits), softmax(old_logits)
    g = [0.0] * len(logits)
    for a, adv in zip(actions, advs):
        r = p[a] / p_old[a]
        if is_clipped(r, adv, eps):
            continue
        for k, d in enumerate(grad_log_prob(logits, a)):
            g[k] += adv * r * d
    return [x / len(actions) for x in g]

def train_bandit_ppo(bandit, iters=30, batch=8, epochs=10, lr=1.0, eps=0.2, clip=True, seed=0):
    """Train a softmax policy on a bandit with PPO-style batch reuse; return the logits.

    Each iteration: freeze old = a copy of the logits; sample `batch` actions and rewards from
    it; normalise the rewards to advantages (R - mean) / (std + 1e-8) with the population
    standard deviation; then take `epochs` gradient steps on that SAME batch:
    logits += lr * ppo_gradient(...). With clip=False the ratio is used but never clipped
    (every sample passes gradient).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    logits = [0.0] * bandit.k
    for _ in range(iters):
        old = list(logits)
        probs = softmax(old)
        actions, rewards = [], []
        for _ in range(batch):
            a = sample_action(probs, rng)
            actions.append(a)
            rewards.append(bandit.pull(a, rng))
        mean = sum(rewards) / batch
        std = math.sqrt(sum((r - mean) ** 2 for r in rewards) / batch)
        advs = [(r - mean) / (std + 1e-8) for r in rewards]
        for _ in range(epochs):
            g = ppo_gradient(logits, old, actions, advs, eps if clip else float("inf"))
            logits = [z + lr * gi for z, gi in zip(logits, g)]
    return logits

Milestone 5: PPO with a KL penalty

The clip limits how far the policy moves per iteration and says nothing about how far it moves in total. After a thousand iterations the policy may be nowhere near where it started, which for a language model means a reward model being exploited outside the region where it was trained (chapter 13) and a fluent model turned into a degenerate one. The second safety rope, from Ziegler et al. (2019) and used in every RLHF system, is a penalty for drifting from a fixed reference policy πref. The objective becomes E[R] − β · KL(πθ ‖ πref), and it is implemented by charging each sampled action a small amount at the step where it was taken:

rt' = rt − β ( log πθ(at | st) − log πref(at | st) )

The sum of these log-ratios along a trajectory is a single-sample estimate of the KL, so the penalty is placed where the drift happened and the advantage estimator can see it. The sign is the thing to watch. A sampled action is, by construction, one the policy likes, so on average log π − log πref is positive and the penalty reduces the reward; individual steps can come out negative, which is noise and not a bug.

To report the KL you can use the same log-ratio (the estimator k1 in Schulman's 2020 note), but it is noisy and sometimes negative. Chapter 8 recommends the k3 estimator, (ρ − 1) − log ρ with ρ = πref(a) / πθ(a): unbiased and never negative. You write it, and the checker draws 40,000 samples from a four-token distribution and requires the mean to match the exact KL.

def kl_k3(logp, logp_ref):
    """Schulman's k3 estimator of KL(pi || ref) from ONE sample drawn from pi:
    (rho - 1) - log(rho) with rho = pi_ref(a) / pi(a). Always >= 0."""
    log_rho = logp_ref - logp
    return math.exp(log_rho) - 1.0 - log_rho

def shaped_rewards(rewards, logps, logps_ref, beta):
    """Per-step rewards with the KL penalty of chapter 8: r_t - beta * (log pi(a_t|s_t) - log ref(a_t|s_t))."""
    return [r - beta * (lp - lr_) for r, lp, lr_ in zip(rewards, logps, logps_ref)]

def train_ppo_grid(env, ref, beta=0.0, iterations=100, episodes=16, epochs=4, lr=1.0, eps=0.2,
                   gamma=0.99, v_lr=0.2, seed=0):
    """PPO with a KL penalty to the reference table `ref` on the gridworld. Returns (table, V).

    Start from a COPY of `ref` (the tuned model starts as the reference). Each iteration:
      1. old = a copy of the table; play `episodes` episodes with `old`.
      2. For every step, log pi_old(a|s) and log ref(a|s); shape the rewards with beta
         (shaped_rewards), take returns-to-go with gamma, and advantage = G_t - V[s_t].
      3. Group the steps by state. For each of `epochs` passes, for every state that appears:
         table[s][k] += lr * (n_s / N) * ppo_gradient(table[s], old[s], actions_s, advs_s, eps)[k],
         where n_s is the number of steps in that state and N the total number of steps.
      4. Update V towards the SHAPED returns (update_values-style, with v_lr).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    table = [row[:] for row in ref]
    V = [0.0] * env.n_states
    for _ in range(iterations):
        old = [row[:] for row in table]
        eps_data, groups, total = [], {}, 0
        for _ in range(episodes):
            states, actions, rewards = run_episode(env, old, rng)
            logps = [log_softmax(old[s])[a] for s, a in zip(states, actions)]
            refs = [log_softmax(ref[s])[a] for s, a in zip(states, actions)]
            shaped = shaped_rewards(rewards, logps, refs, beta)
            eps_data.append((states, actions, shaped))
            for s, a, g in zip(states, actions, returns_to_go(shaped, gamma)):
                groups.setdefault(s, ([], []))
                groups[s][0].append(a)
                groups[s][1].append(g - V[s])
                total += 1
        for _ in range(epochs):
            for s, (acts, advs) in groups.items():
                g = ppo_gradient(table[s], old[s], acts, advs, eps)
                w = lr * len(acts) / total
                for k in range(N_ACTIONS):
                    table[s][k] += w * g[k]
        update_values(V, eps_data, gamma, v_lr)
    return table, V

Look under the hood

  • Why test statistics, not exact values. Exact numbers would depend on the order in which you consume random numbers (action before reward, or all actions first). The checker therefore asserts properties that every correct implementation shares: an unbiased estimator averages to the truth, a baseline reduces the spread by a factor, a clip prevents a stuck run, a larger β lowers the KL. The thresholds have a wide margin over the reference solution so that legitimate variations pass and the classic bugs (a batch mean for leave-one-out, a sum for a mean, an unclipped "clipped" gradient, a wrong-sign penalty) do not.
  • Where the language model hides. Replace "state" by "prompt plus tokens so far", "action" by "next token" and the table of logits by a transformer, and every function here maps over. The gradient of the logits flows back into the network by backpropagation; the group of episodes for one prompt is the group of GRPO; the per-step shaped reward is the per-token KL penalty of RLHF. What is lost in the tabular version is generalisation: updating one state's row does nothing for any other, while in a network a single update moves every prompt, which is why clip fractions and KL are logged on every step of a real run.
  • What the milestone 4 table does not show. It uses advantages normalised within each batch of 8, which is common in practice and has a side effect: a batch where all rewards are equal gives all-zero advantages (the 1e-8 in the denominator keeps it finite). Chapter 14 returns to this when a group of answers is all right or all wrong.
  • Why V is small here and large in practice. A learned critic is a second network the size of the policy in PPO RLHF; chapter 8 counts 252 GB of model state for a 7B setup. The tabular V is free. That cost is the reason critic-free methods (leave-one-out, GRPO) took over for large models.

Common misconception

"The KL penalty and the clip do the same job, so one of them is enough." They bound different things. The clip limits how much the policy changes between two consecutive iterations and is blind to the total; the penalty charges for the total distance from the reference and does not prevent a single large step. The table of milestone 5 was produced with the clip on, and the drift at β = 0 is still 0.44 nats per visited step, because many small clipped steps add up. Real runs use both, and log both (clip fraction and KL to the reference) on every step.

Systems connection

The loop of this lab is also the shape of the infrastructure. The "rollout" box is generation: memory-bound, dominated by the KV cache and best served by a batching engine (KV cache, serving engines), and it is usually the slow part of an RL step. The "update" box is ordinary training with all its memory pressure (GPU memory), with a reference model and sometimes a critic held next to the policy. The old log-probabilities that PPO's ratio needs must be recorded at rollout time, from the same weights that generated the tokens; if the serving engine and the trainer disagree numerically (different kernels, different precision) the ratio is not 1 at the first step and the clip statistics lie. That mismatch can be a source of instability in RL training, and it is one reason a trainer may recompute the log-probabilities itself instead of trusting the serving engine's.

What you learned

  • The policy gradient of a softmax policy is A · (onehot(a) − π) averaged over samples. It is unbiased, and its noise scales with the size of the rewards unless a baseline removes the common part.
  • A leave-one-out baseline is unbiased and needs no second model; the batch mean includes the sample's own reward and shrinks the gradient by (B − 1)/B, which is half at B = 2.
  • Returns-to-go weight each step by what followed it; a learned state-value baseline helps when returns sit far from zero and matters less when they straddle it.
  • PPO's ratio lets one batch be reused. The clipped objective passes gradient while the sample's ratio is inside the range or on the wrong side of it, and stops it when the sample has already moved far enough in the helpful direction. The clip is not a hard constraint on the policy.
  • The KL penalty charges each sampled action β (log π − log πref). A larger β lowers the drift and the reward together; k3 is a non-negative unbiased estimate to log.
  • Every step of the loop can be tested in isolation: by hand, against finite differences, and by sampling its bias and variance.

Check your understanding

A training loop computes each sample's advantage as its reward minus the mean reward of the whole batch of 2 samples. Compared with the true policy gradient, the expected update is:

  1. Unbiased, because subtracting any constant leaves the gradient unchanged
  2. Half the size, because the baseline contains the sample's own reward
  3. Twice the size, because two samples are averaged
  4. Zero, because the two advantages sum to zero
A baseline leaves the gradient unchanged only if it does not depend on the sampled action. The batch mean includes the sample's own reward, which scales the expected gradient by (B − 1)/B, here 1/2. The advantages do sum to zero, but each is multiplied by a different score vector, so the update is not zero. Leave-one-out removes the problem.

In PPO with ε = 0.2, a sampled token has advantage −1 and its probability under the current policy is 1.5 times its probability under the old one. What happens to its gradient?

  1. It is zero, because the ratio is outside [0.8, 1.2]
  2. It is zero, because the advantage is negative
  3. It is clipped to the value at r = 1.2
  4. It passes through unclipped and pushes the token's probability down
The min of r·A = −1.5 and clip(r)·A = −1.2 is −1.5, the unclipped term, so the gradient is live. The clip blocks overshoots in the helpful direction, not corrections of a mistake: a bad token that became more likely must still be pushed down. The first option is the tempting one, because it is true for A = +1.

A run uses β = 0 and ends with a high reward and a large KL to the reference. You raise β to 1. Which pair of changes should you expect?

  1. Lower KL and lower reward: the policy stays closer to the reference and gives up some reward
  2. Lower KL and higher reward: staying close makes the reward model more reliable
  3. The same KL and lower reward: the penalty only changes the reward
  4. Higher KL and higher reward: the penalty acts as an exploration bonus
The penalty trades reward for closeness. In the lab, going from β = 0 to 1 cut the mean KL from 0.44 to 0.03 and the return from 0.88 to −0.03. Higher true reward at a higher β can happen when the reward signal is exploitable far from the reference, but on this grid the reward is exact and the trade is monotone.

An episode has rewards 0, 0, 1 and γ = 0.9. What are the returns-to-go, and which is the weight of the first action in REINFORCE (before any baseline)?

  1. [1, 1, 1]; the weight is 1
  2. [0.9, 0.9, 1]; the weight is 0.9
  3. [0.81, 0.9, 1]; the weight is 0.81
  4. [0, 0, 1]; the weight is 0
G2 = 1, G1 = 0 + 0.9 × 1 = 0.9, G0 = 0 + 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81. The first action gets credit for the later reward, discounted twice. Using the raw rewards would give the first action no credit at all, and ignoring the discount gives every action the same weight.

You reuse a batch of 8 samples for ten gradient steps with the clip on and still see the ratio of one sampled action reach 1.4 at ε = 0.2. Which explanation is correct?

  1. There is a bug: a clipped objective keeps every ratio inside [0.8, 1.2]
  2. This is possible: the clip zeroes the gradient of samples already past the boundary, but other samples' gradients and shared parameters can still push it further
  3. The learning rate must be zero for that sample, so the ratio should not change
  4. The clip applies to the advantage, not to the ratio, so the ratio is unbounded by design
The clip is a rule for which samples contribute gradient, not a projection of the policy. In the lab's drift experiment the clipped run still reaches a change of 0.37 against a clip range of 0.2, while the unclipped run exceeds 4. A real run therefore logs the clip fraction and the KL, instead of assuming the ratio is bounded.
