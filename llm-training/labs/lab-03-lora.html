Lab 3: LoRA from Scratch

Chapter 11 claimed that a correction with under 1% of a model's parameters can be enough, and that it forgets less than full fine-tuning. Those are claims about numbers, and numbers are easy to check on a layer small enough to hold in your head. In this lab you take a frozen linear layer that was "pretrained" on task A, adapt it to a different task B with a rank-r LoRA adapter whose B matrix starts at zero, derive and check the gradients of both factors, merge the adapter back into the weights, and then set it against full fine-tuning on three scores: trainable parameters, loss on task B and the damage done to task A. The last milestone sweeps the rank and asks the question a practitioner asks: how small can the adapter be before it stops being enough?

Why this lab matters

LoRA is the default way most teams outside the largest labs adapt a model, and most of its settings (rank, alpha, which layers, the learning rate) are chosen by feel. The feel comes from knowing what the pieces do, and every piece is a few lines: an initialisation that makes step 0 equal the base model, a scale factor, two gradients that look nearly identical and behave very differently, and a merge that is one matrix addition. Writing them by hand also removes a mystery. You will see for yourself why B must start at zero while A must not (the gradient of A is exactly zero on the first step), why the update has rank at most r however long you train, and why alpha / r makes a learning rate transfer across ranks. And you will measure the trade chapter 11 described: a low-rank adapter learns less of the training set than full fine-tuning, forgets less of what the model already knew, and, when the data are few and noisy, can even generalise better. When the data are plentiful and the change is not low rank, the same experiment shows the reverse, and the lab makes you look at both.

Deterministic, small, standard library only

There is no language model here. The "model" is one 24 × 24 linear layer and the tasks are synthetic regressions generated by World from a fixed seed, so the whole lab runs in seconds with no downloads. Every random draw comes from a seeded random.Random; a failure prints the seed so you can replay it (for example python3 check.py solution 4 2 runs milestone 4 with seed 2). Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. The full check takes about ten seconds, almost all of it in the last milestone, which trains eight models. Matrices are lists of rows, and the helpers matvec, matmul, transpose and Adam are given. The layer is tiny so that you can check gradients against finite differences; the arithmetic is the same arithmetic as in a 7-billion-parameter model, applied to one weight matrix.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each building on the last:

  1. Count, initialise, forward: the parameter count of a full matrix and of a rank-r adapter, an initialiser that leaves B at exactly zero, and the forward pass W0 x + (alpha / r) B (A x) through the narrow bottleneck.
  2. The gradients of A and B: the two outer-product formulas from chapter 11, summed over a batch and verified against finite differences. You will see that the gradient of A is exactly zero while B is zero.
  3. Training loops: full-batch Adam on the adapter and on the whole matrix, with the base weights left untouched.
  4. Merge and unmerge: fold the adapter into the weights, undo it, and confirm the merged layer gives the same outputs and an update of rank at most r.
  5. Run one experiment: train, merge and measure trainable parameters, held-out task B loss and task A loss, for both methods.
  6. LoRA against full fine-tuning: sweep the rank, ask how small the adapter can be, and see what happens when the data are plentiful.

The setting: one layer, two tasks

Before you write anything, here is the world you are adapting. World(seed=0) builds a 24 × 24 weight matrix W_A with entries of size about 1/sqrt(24) and treats it as the answer to task A: the target is y = W_A x for inputs x drawn from a standard normal. The pretrained layer W0 is W_A plus a speck of noise (0.003 per entry), standing in for a model that was fit on task A for a long time and is nearly perfect at it: its mean squared error on task A is 0.0002.

Task B is a different mapping, W_B = W_A + L + F. Here L is a rank-2 matrix of squared size 4 (the part worth learning: a few directions in which the new task disagrees with the old one) and F is a faint dense matrix of squared size 1.06 (a small change in every entry that no low-rank adapter can express). The task-B training set has 72 samples with label noise of standard deviation 0.4. A clean held-out set of 200 task-B samples measures how well each method learned the task, and 200 fresh task-A samples measure forgetting.

The loss everywhere is the mean squared error over samples and over the 24 outputs. Two facts about it will help you read the numbers. The untouched W0 scores 0.200 on clean task-B data, which is the number to beat. And because the inputs are isotropic, the task-A loss of any layer W is ||W − W_A||F2 / 24: forgetting is the squared size of the update, divided by 24. Even a perfect rank-2 update costs 4/24 = 0.167 on task A, because task B really does disagree with task A in those directions. The question is how much more than that each method costs.

This is deliberately the same shape of problem as the real one, shrunk until it can be inspected. The pretrained weights know task A. A new task needs a change that is mostly low rank (chapter 11's bet, from the intrinsic-dimension work of Aghajanyan and colleagues) plus some detail. A small, noisy fine-tuning set will happily be memorised by a method with enough freedom. And whatever the update does to the weights, it does to the old task as well.

Milestone 1: count, initialise, forward

A LoRA adapter (low-rank adaptation, Hu et al., 2021) replaces the weight update ΔW of a frozen layer with the product of two thin matrices. For a layer with d_out outputs and d_in inputs, the frozen weight is W0 (d_out × d_in) and the layer computes

h = W0 x + (alpha / r) · B (A x), with A of shape r × d_in and B of shape d_out × r.

The number r is the rank of the adapter: A x squeezes the input down to r numbers, B expands them back, and the constant s = alpha / r scales the result. Two things to settle before any code.

  • Counting. The full matrix has d_out · d_in numbers. The adapter has r · (d_in + d_out): it is smaller only while r is below d_in · d_out / (d_in + d_out), which for a square matrix is half its width. The run below prints the counts for this lab's layer and for two real shapes from chapter 11 (the Llama 2 7B attention and MLP projections).
  • Initialisation. B starts at exactly zero, so B A = 0 and the adapted layer equals the pretrained layer at step 0: the first loss you see is the base model's loss, not a random perturbation's. A starts random, with entries of standard deviation 1/sqrt(d_in) so that each entry of A x is of order one. If both were zero, neither would ever receive a gradient; milestone 2 shows why.

The forward pass should go through the bottleneck: compute A x (r numbers), then B times that, never the full d_out × d_in product B A. That is what makes the adapter cheap to run on top of the frozen layer, and it is the form in which gradients are computed.

On this lab's 24 × 24 layer the same ranks are huge (rank 8 is two thirds of the matrix and rank 12 equals it), which is why the lab's savings are modest and its conclusions are about shape, not scale.

Solution spoiler 
def count_full(d_out, d_in):
    """Trainable parameters when you fine-tune the whole matrix."""
    return d_out * d_in

def count_lora(d_out, d_in, r):
    """Trainable parameters of a rank-r adapter: A is r x d_in, B is d_out x r."""
    return r * (d_in + d_out)

def init_adapter(d_out, d_in, r, rng):
    """(A, B): A is r x d_in with entries N(0, 1/d_in); B is d_out x r and exactly zero."""
    A = [[rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0 / math.sqrt(d_in)) for _ in range(d_in)] for _ in range(r)]
    return A, zeros(d_out, r)

def lora_forward(W0, A, B, x, alpha):
    """h = W0 x + (alpha / r) * B (A x), with r = len(A). Never form B A: go through the r-dimensional bottleneck."""
    s = alpha / len(A)
    base = matvec(W0, x)
    low = matvec(B, matvec(A, x))
    return [b + s * l for b, l in zip(base, low)]

Milestone 2: the gradients of A and B

Training needs the derivative of the loss with respect to A and to B. Write s = alpha / r and let g = ∂L/∂h be the gradient of the loss with respect to this layer's output for one sample (a vector of length d_out; for the mean squared error, mse_grad_out gives it to you). With h = W0 x + s B A x, the chain rule from derivatives and the chain rule gives, as in chapter 11's "go deeper" box,

∂L/∂B = s · g (A x)T (d_out × r),    ∂L/∂A = s · (BT g) xT (r × d_in).

Each is an outer product. The gradient of B multiplies the output gradient by the r-dimensional bottleneck activation A x. The gradient of A first pulls the output gradient back through B and then multiplies by the input. Over a batch, the loss gradient is the sum of these matrices over samples (the 1/N of the mean is already inside mse_grad_out). Nothing here involves W0: it is frozen, so no gradient is kept for it, which is where LoRA's saving in gradient and optimiser memory comes from.

This is easy to see in the numbers. The script runs six steps of Adam on a rank-4 adapter and prints the size (Frobenius norm) of each gradient and of each matrix:

Solution spoiler 
def lora_grads(A, B, x, g, alpha):
    """(dA, dB) for ONE sample, given g = dL/dh (length d_out).

        dL/dB = s * g (A x)^T          (d_out x r)
        dL/dA = s * (B^T g) x^T        (r x d_in)
    """
    s = alpha / len(A)
    Ax = matvec(A, x)
    Btg = matvec(transpose(B), g)
    dB = [[s * gi * a for a in Ax] for gi in g]
    dA = [[s * c * xj for xj in x] for c in Btg]
    return dA, dB

def batch_loss_and_grads(W0, A, B, data, alpha):
    """(loss, dA, dB) of the mean squared error over a batch of (x, y) pairs."""
    n = len(data)
    preds = [lora_forward(W0, A, B, x, alpha) for x, _ in data]
    loss = mse_loss(preds, [y for _, y in data])
    dA, dB = zeros(len(A), len(A[0])), zeros(len(B), len(B[0]))
    for (x, y), h in zip(data, preds):
        da, db = lora_grads(A, B, x, mse_grad_out(h, y, n), alpha)
        dA = mat_add(dA, da)
        dB = mat_add(dB, db)
    return loss, dA, dB

Milestone 3: training loops

With gradients in hand, training is the same loop for both methods: forward and backward over the whole training set, record the loss, take an Adam step. The given Adam class updates the matrices you hand it in place, so for LoRA the parameter list is [A, B] and the frozen W0 never goes in. For full fine-tuning the parameter is the weight matrix itself, with gradient dW = ∑ g xT over the samples. Two details matter:

  • Leave the base weights alone. train_lora must never write to W0, and train_full must work on a copy. A bug that edits the base in place makes every later comparison quietly wrong: the "forgetting" of the next run would be measured from a model that has already moved.
  • The learning rates differ. Chapter 11 reports that the best learning rate for a LoRA adapter is typically much higher than for full fine-tuning (Thinking Machines found about ten times). Here the given defaults are 0.02 for the adapter and 0.005 for the full matrix, both with full-batch Adam; with fewer parameters sharing the work, the adapter's parameters each need larger steps.

def full_grads(W, data):
    """(loss, dW) for full fine-tuning: dW = sum over samples of g x^T."""
    n = len(data)
    preds = [matvec(W, x) for x, _ in data]
    loss = mse_loss(preds, [y for _, y in data])
    dW = zeros(len(W), len(W[0]))
    for (x, y), h in zip(data, preds):
        g = mse_grad_out(h, y, n)
        for i, gi in enumerate(g):
            row = dW[i]
            for j, xj in enumerate(x):
                row[j] += gi * xj
    return loss, dW

def train_lora(W0, data, r, alpha, lr, steps, seed=0):
    """Train a rank-r adapter by full-batch Adam. W0 is never touched. Returns (A, B, losses).

    Make rng = random.Random(seed), then init_adapter(d_out, d_in, r, rng). The trainable
    parameters are the two matrices A and B; hand [A, B] to Adam(...), which updates them in place.
    Each of the `steps` steps: compute the loss and gradients on ALL of `data`, append the loss
    to `losses` (so losses[0] is the loss of the untouched base model, because B = 0), then
    opt.step([dA, dB]).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    A, B = init_adapter(len(W0), len(W0[0]), r, rng)
    opt = Adam([A, B], lr)
    losses = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        loss, dA, dB = batch_loss_and_grads(W0, A, B, data, alpha)
        losses.append(loss)
        opt.step([dA, dB])
    return A, B, losses

def train_full(W0, data, lr, steps):
    """Fine-tune a COPY of W0 by full-batch Adam. W0 itself must not change. Returns (W, losses).

    Same loop as train_lora, but the parameter is the whole matrix W: losses[k] is the loss
    BEFORE step k, and the gradient comes from full_grads.
    """
    W = [row[:] for row in W0]
    opt = Adam([W], lr)
    losses = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        loss, dW = full_grads(W, data)
        losses.append(loss)
        opt.step([dW])
    return W, losses

Milestone 4: merge and unmerge

After training, the adapter is a linear correction, so it can be folded into the weights: W = W0 + (alpha / r) B A, one matrix addition per adapted layer. The merged layer is an ordinary layer with no extra matrices and no extra latency, which was an explicit design goal of the LoRA paper. Three consequences are worth checking.

  • Same function. W x equals W0 x + s B (A x) for every x, up to floating-point rounding. This is only true because the adapter is linear; chapter 11 notes that merging into a quantised base rounds the sum and loses a little of the adapter's effect.
  • Bounded rank. The product B A is a 24 × 24 matrix, but it is built from r columns of B and r rows of A, so its rank is at most r. However long you train, the update never leaves a rank-r set. Full fine-tuning has no such limit.
  • Reversible. Because the merge is an addition, subtracting the same s B A recovers the base weights (up to rounding). That is what lets a service keep one base model and apply, remove and swap many adapters, the mechanism behind chapter 17's adapter libraries.

Your task

Hints try without first
  • matmul(B, A) is d_out × d_in. Multiply each entry by alpha / len(A).
  • mat_add(P, Q, c) returns P + c * Q as a new matrix: merge uses c = 1 and unmerge uses c = -1.
  • If W0 changes after merge, you wrote into its rows. The given helpers never mutate their inputs.
Solution spoiler 
def adapter_delta(A, B, alpha):
    """The update the adapter represents: (alpha / r) * B A, a d_out x d_in matrix of rank at most r."""
    return [[(alpha / len(A)) * v for v in row] for row in matmul(B, A)]

def merge(W0, A, B, alpha):
    """A new matrix W0 + (alpha / r) B A. W0 is left alone (return a new list of rows, do not edit W0 in place)."""
    return mat_add(W0, adapter_delta(A, B, alpha))

def unmerge(W, A, B, alpha):
    """Undo merge: W - (alpha / r) B A, as a new matrix."""
    return mat_add(W, adapter_delta(A, B, alpha), -1.0)

Milestone 5: run one experiment

You now have everything needed to run the experiment the chapter described. A run trains a method on the task-B training set, produces a single weight matrix (for LoRA by merging), and reports one Result row with five facts: the method's name, the number of trainable parameters, that number as a fraction of the full matrix, the loss on the noisy training set, the loss on clean held-out task-B data, and the loss on task A.

The last number is the one this lab exists to produce. Fine-tuning changes the weights, the weights are shared between tasks, and nothing in the task-B loss asks the model to keep doing task A. The loss on task A is therefore a direct measure of what catastrophic forgetting (chapter 10) cost. It cannot be avoided entirely, since task B really does disagree with task A, but it can be larger or smaller depending on how much the update moves. A fair comparison needs a few rules. Both methods start from the same W0 and see the same data. Both are measured on the same merged-matrix footing, using evaluate. And the base model is the reference: it scores 0.200 on clean task-B data and 0.0002 on task A.

The ingredient to see before you write it is where the extra forgetting of full fine-tuning lives. Compare the squared size of each method's update with the true low-rank change L:

$ python3 exp_noise_fit.py

  true faint dense part   1.056
  LoRA r=2 update         4.093   distance to true low-rank  0.467
  full update             6.652   distance to true low-rank  2.992
  (task-A loss = ||update||_F^2 / 24 for isotropic inputs: 0.171 and 0.277)

The rank-2 adapter's update has squared size 4.09, almost exactly that of the true change (4.00) and 0.47 away from it. The full update has squared size 6.65 and is 2.99 away from the true low-rank change: on top of the real change it carries the faint dense part (1.06) and a good deal of fitted label noise, because 72 noisy samples cannot tell a real direction from a chance one. Every unit of squared size is paid for on task A at 1/24 per unit, which is 0.171 against 0.277.

def evaluate(W, data):
    """Mean squared error of the plain linear layer W on (x, y) pairs."""
    return mse_loss([matvec(W, x) for x, _ in data], [y for _, y in data])

def run_lora(world, r, alpha=16.0, lr=0.02, steps=200, seed=0):
    """Train a rank-r adapter on task B (on world.train_b, starting from world.W0), merge it, and measure the merged layer.

    Returns Result("LoRA r=<r>", params, params / count_full(...), train_b, test_b, task_a) where
    the three losses are evaluate(merged, world.train_b), evaluate(merged, world.test_b) and
    evaluate(merged, world.task_a). The last one is the FORGETTING measure: the base model scores
    about 0.0002 on it, so everything above that is damage to task A.
    """
    A, B, losses = train_lora(world.W0, world.train_b, r, alpha, lr, steps, seed)
    W = merge(world.W0, A, B, alpha)
    params = count_lora(world.d_out, world.d_in, r)
    return Result(f"LoRA r={r}", params, params / count_full(world.d_out, world.d_in),
                  evaluate(W, world.train_b), evaluate(W, world.test_b), evaluate(W, world.task_a))

def run_full(world, lr=0.005, steps=200):
    """Full fine-tuning on task B, measured the same way. Returns Result("full", params, 1.0, ...)."""
    W, losses = train_full(world.W0, world.train_b, lr, steps)
    return Result("full", count_full(world.d_out, world.d_in), 1.0,
                  evaluate(W, world.train_b), evaluate(W, world.test_b), evaluate(W, world.task_a))

Milestone 6: LoRA against full fine-tuning

One run is an anecdote; a sweep is a result. sweep runs full fine-tuning and LoRA at several ranks on the same world, and verdict turns the table into the answer a practitioner wants: what is the smallest rank that gets close to the best result, and what does it save? Here "close to the best" means a held-out task-B loss within a tolerance (10% by default) of the best row in the table, full fine-tuning included.

The reference solution produces this for the default world (72 noisy samples). The bars show the same numbers.

Read the three columns together. Rank 1 forgets least but cannot fit task B, since the change has two independent directions and rank 1 gives it one. Rank 2 matches the structure of the change, so it gets the best held-out loss while keeping task A loss at 0.170, hardly above the 0.167 floor. Ranks 4 and 8 have spare capacity that the noise soaks up: held-out loss rises (0.053, 0.071) and so does forgetting. Full fine-tuning is the limiting case of that trend with 576 free numbers. Fewer degrees of freedom here mean less noise fitted and less forgetting; the same effect is why the LoRA literature says it "learns less and forgets less" (Biderman et al., 2024).

def sweep(world, ranks, alpha=16.0, lr=0.02, steps=200, full_lr=0.005):
    """[full fine-tuning, LoRA at each rank in `ranks`], as a list of Result. The full row comes first.

    alpha, lr and steps go to run_lora; full_lr and steps go to run_full.
    """
    return [run_full(world, full_lr, steps)] + [run_lora(world, r, alpha, lr, steps) for r in ranks]

def verdict(rows, tolerance=0.10):
    """Summarise a sweep (rows[0] is the full fine-tune, the rest are LoRA ranks in increasing order).

    The target is the best held-out task-B loss of ANY row (full fine-tuning included).
    Returns a dict:
      "smallest_rank"    the integer rank (parse it from the name, "LoRA r=4" -> 4) of the first LoRA row
                         whose test_b is at most best * (1 + tolerance); None if no LoRA row gets there
      "param_fraction"   that row's fraction of trainable parameters (None if no row qualifies)
      "forgetting_saved" 1 - (that row's task_a) / (the full row's task_a)  (None if no row qualifies)
    """
    best = min(row.test_b for row in rows)
    for row in rows[1:]:
        if row.test_b <= best * (1 + tolerance):
            return {"smallest_rank": int(row.name.split("=")[1]), "param_fraction": row.fraction,
                    "forgetting_saved": 1 - row.task_a / rows[0].task_a}
    return {"smallest_rank": None, "param_fraction": None, "forgetting_saved": None}

Look under the hood

  • The alpha-over-r factor, tested. Because Adam fixes the size of each step, the scale s = alpha / r multiplies a step whose size is already set, and behaves like a second learning rate. The script exp_alpha.py trains a rank-4 adapter at lr 0.02 with five values of alpha. Alpha 1, 4 and 16 reach the same place (clean task-B loss 0.053 to 0.058 after 200 steps, differing only in how fast), alpha 64 is slower to start (0.305 after five steps against 0.164) and alpha 256 overshoots badly (3.7 after five steps, recovering to 0.061). The second half of the script holds alpha / r = 4 fixed at ranks 2 and 8 and gets 0.068 after 20 steps at both: with this scaling the learning rate you tuned at one rank carries over to another. Chapter 11 notes the debate between alpha / r and alpha / sqrt(r) (rsLoRA); this lab is too small to settle it.
  • What the 24 x 24 layer hides. A real adapter sits on dozens of matrices, and the original paper put it on attention only. Chapter 11 reports that applying LoRA to the MLP matrices matters more at a matched parameter count. The code here is the same for each matrix, so a model is a loop over layers; what the toy cannot show is how the choice of layers interacts with the rank.
  • Why the rank-2 adapter lands so close to the truth. The change L is exactly rank 2 by construction. A real fine-tuning update is not exactly low rank, only close enough for a small rank to carry most of its energy. How close is an empirical question about the task and the model; chapter 11's controlled experiment makes the same point with a full-rank update, and the research it cites reports the gap for code and mathematics.
  • The memory story is not in this lab. You counted trainable parameters, which sets gradient and optimiser memory (chapter 11 and chapter 2). The frozen weights still sit in memory for the forward and backward pass, and activations are still needed to back-propagate to the adapters; LoRA shrinks neither.

Common misconception

"LoRA forgets nothing because the original weights are frozen." The frozen W0 is untouched, but the model that answers requests is W0 plus the adapter (merged or not), and that sum is a different function. In this lab a rank-2 adapter raises the task-A loss from 0.0002 to 0.170. It is lower than full fine-tuning's 0.274, and it grows with rank, but it is not zero. What is true is that you can switch the adapter off, or unmerge it, and recover the base model exactly, which full fine-tuning cannot offer without keeping a second copy of all the weights.

Systems connection

The merge of milestone 4 is a design decision in a serving system. Merging gives the fastest inference and one more full-size copy of the weights per variant; keeping the adapter separate costs an extra pair of small matrix products per layer and lets one copy of the base serve hundreds of variants, with the adapter selected per request. That is a memory-versus-latency trade of the kind serving engines make everywhere, and it behaves like any multi-tenant system: per-customer state is small, shared state is large, and isolation between tenants has to be designed (the wider design questions are in Designing with ML & LLMs). The checkpoint-sized adapter is also an operations win: a 96-number file here, tens of megabytes at real scale, is cheap to version, ship and roll back compared with a full model.

What you learned

  • A rank-r adapter on a d_out × d_in layer has r(d_in + d_out) trainable numbers. It is a large saving only when r is far below the width: 0.78% at rank 16 on a 4096 × 4096 matrix, but two thirds of the matrix at rank 8 on a 24 × 24 one.
  • B starts at zero so that the adapted model equals the base model at step 0; A starts random so that B has something to learn from. The gradient of A is exactly zero until B has moved.
  • The two gradients are outer products, s g (A x)T and s (BT g) xT, checkable against finite differences. The frozen weights need no gradient at all.
  • Merging is one addition and is exactly reversible. The update has rank at most r however long you train, and the merged model is as large as the base.
  • Forgetting is measured on the old task, and it is the squared size of the update. A rank constraint limits that size, so adapters forget less than full fine-tuning, but never nothing.
  • With few, noisy samples a rank matched to the true change generalises best; with plentiful data and a change that is not low rank, full fine-tuning wins. Rank is capacity, and the right amount depends on the task and the dataset.

Check your understanding

You initialise both A and B to small random values instead of setting B to zero. What is the main consequence?

  1. Training cannot start, because the gradients are zero
  2. The adapted model no longer equals the base model at step 0, so training begins from a random perturbation of it
  3. The merged weights have a higher rank than r
  4. The adapter needs more trainable parameters
With both matrices random, B A is a non-zero matrix of rank at most r, so the first loss is the loss of a perturbed model. The gradients are not zero (the first option describes the case where both are zero). The rank bound and the parameter count do not depend on the initial values.

At the first training step, B = 0 and A is random. Which gradients are non-zero?

  1. Both, because the loss depends on both matrices
  2. Only the gradient of A, because B is already at a fixed value
  3. Neither, because B A = 0 and the output does not change
  4. Only the gradient of B: the gradient of A passes through BT and is exactly zero
∂L/∂B = s g (A x)T needs only the output gradient and the (non-zero) bottleneck activation, while ∂L/∂A = s (BT g) xT contains BT. The output not changing at a point does not mean its derivative is zero: the output would change if B moved, so B's gradient is not zero.

A 4096 × 4096 layer gets a rank-16 adapter. Roughly what fraction of the layer's weights is trainable?

  1. 0.78%, from 16 × (4096 + 4096) = 131,072 out of 16.8 million
  2. 0.39%, from 16 × 4096 = 65,536
  3. 16%, because the rank is 16
  4. 6.25%, because 16 is 1/256 of 4096 and there are two matrices
Both factors count: A is 16 × 4096 and B is 4096 × 16, together 131,072 numbers. The second option counts only one factor; the others confuse the rank with a percentage.

After training, you merge a rank-2 adapter into a 24 × 24 layer. What can you say about the update W − W0?

  1. It has rank 24, because the product B A is a full 24 × 24 matrix
  2. It has rank 2 only if training used a small learning rate
  3. It has rank at most 2 however long you train, because it is built from two columns of B and two rows of A
  4. It has rank 48, the sum of the ranks of A and B
The product of a 24 × 2 and a 2 × 24 matrix has rank at most 2 regardless of the values. Its shape is full-size, which is the reason for the first option, but shape and rank are different things. The rank of a product is bounded by the smaller of the ranks of its factors, not by their sum.

In the 72-sample world, rank 8 has a lower training loss than rank 2 (0.133 against 0.184) but a higher held-out loss (0.071 against 0.047). Why?

  1. Rank 8 is under-trained, because eight times more parameters need eight times more steps
  2. The extra directions let rank 8 fit label noise in the training set, which does not generalise
  3. The merge step loses precision at higher ranks
  4. The learning rate is wrong for rank 8, and the training loss is therefore unreliable
The true change has rank 2; directions beyond that can only chase noise, which lowers the training loss and raises held-out loss. Rank 8's training loss is genuinely lower, so it is not under-trained. The merge is exact up to rounding at any rank (milestone 4), and alpha / r keeps the learning rate usable across these ranks.

With 240 training samples instead of 72, full fine-tuning beats rank 2 on held-out task-B data (0.018 against 0.039). What does this show?

  1. The faint dense part of the change is learnable with enough data, and a rank-2 adapter cannot express it
  2. Full fine-tuning no longer forgets task A
  3. LoRA stops working beyond 100 samples
  4. The rank-2 adapter was initialised badly
The adapter's held-out loss is stuck near the squared size of the dense part divided by 24, because rank 2 cannot represent it; full fine-tuning can, once the data are plentiful enough to tell it from noise. It still forgets more (task A loss 0.219 against 0.168), so the second option is false. LoRA still works at 240 samples; it simply has less capacity than the task now uses.
