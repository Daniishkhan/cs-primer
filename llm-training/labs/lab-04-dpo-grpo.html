Lab 4: DPO & GRPO on a Toy Policy

Chapter 12 derived the DPO loss from the KL-regularised reward objective, and chapter 14 replaced the learned reward and the critic with a verifier and a group of sampled answers. Both chapters ended in a formula; this lab turns each formula into code and runs them. The policy is as small as a policy can be and still be one: for each of twelve arithmetic questions it is a softmax over six candidate answer strings, and its parameters are the six logits. You write the exact-match verifier, the DPO loss and its gradient, the construction of preference pairs from verified answers, the group-relative advantage, the clipped GRPO objective with a KL penalty, and the training loops. Then you put the two methods side by side, question by question.

Why this lab matters

Post-training recipes are mostly a few loss functions applied at scale, and a loss function you have not differentiated yourself is a black box with a learning-rate knob. Written out for a policy with six logits, DPO and GRPO stop being mysterious. The DPO gradient turns out to move exactly two logits by exactly β σ(−m), and you will see the self-limiting weight fall as pairs get ranked correctly. GRPO's advantage is three lines of arithmetic, and the claim in chapter 14 that the group mean is a slightly biased baseline becomes a number you can compute by enumerating every possible group. The clip is a piece of code with two inequalities, and you can watch it switch a sample's gradient off. The lab also teaches what no formula does: where each method has no signal. A verifier that accepts "391 or 392" trains a hedger; a question the reference never answers correctly gives neither method anything to learn from; a group where all eight answers tie contributes nothing and still costs eight generations. You will measure each of these.

Deterministic, small, standard library only

There is no language model and no GPU. The task is generated by make_task() from a fixed seed; every sampled answer comes from a seeded random.Random; a failure prints the seed so you can replay it (for example python3 check.py solution 5 2 runs milestone 5 with seed 2). Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. The full check takes a couple of seconds. The policy is tabular: each answer is a single choice among six strings, so the log-probability of an answer is one entry of a log-softmax, where a real model would sum over many tokens. That removes the sequence bookkeeping and leaves the algebra of the two losses intact, which is the part you can check by hand. What the toy cannot show (the memory cost of the reference and critic, generation throughput, reward hacking by a learned reward) is in the chapters.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each building on the last:

  1. The verifier, sampling and KL: an exact numeric match on answer strings (so "391.0" counts and "391 or 392" does not), inverse-CDF sampling from a probability vector, and the exact KL divergence between two policies.
  2. The DPO loss and its gradient: implicit rewards β log(πθ / πref), the margin of a preference pair, the mean loss over pairs and its gradient with respect to every logit, checked by finite differences.
  3. Preference pairs and DPO training: label pairs of reference samples with the verifier, then run gradient descent on the DPO loss and measure what it did to the policy.
  4. Group-relative advantages: the standardised advantage of GRPO, the mean-only and leave-one-out variants, and the test for a group with no signal. You will verify the bias of the group-mean baseline by exact enumeration.
  5. The GRPO objective: the probability ratio, PPO's clipped term, the k3 estimator of the KL penalty, and the gradient of the whole thing for one group, including the cases where the clip switches the gradient off.
  6. The GRPO training loop and the comparison: sample groups from the current policy, reuse each batch for several steps, and compare GRPO with DPO on expected reward, accuracy and drift.

The setting: twelve questions, six answers each

make_task() builds twelve arithmetic questions (four additions, four subtractions and four multiplications, such as 36 x 16), each with six candidate answers written as strings. One candidate is the true answer. Four are plausible mistakes: off by one, off by ten, two digits swapped, or the result of the wrong operation. The sixth is either a second correct surface form such as "576.0" (on even-numbered questions) or a hedge such as "576 or 577" (on odd-numbered ones) that mentions the right number and commits to nothing.

The policy for question q is a softmax over six logits z[q][0..5]: πθ(k | q) = softmax(z[q])k. A real model would produce the answer token by token and its log-probability would be a sum; here an answer is one draw, so log π(k) is one entry of log_softmax(z[q]). The reference policy task.ref is the frozen starting point, an "SFT model" that is right between 60% of the time on the easiest question and 0.8% on the hardest, 19.7% averaged over the twelve. Every method starts from the reference logits and may only change them.

Three scores describe any policy. Expected reward is the mean over questions of the probability that a sampled answer is correct. Greedy accuracy is the fraction of questions whose single most likely answer is correct. Mean KL is the average over questions of KL(πθ ‖ πref), how far the whole policy has drifted. The helpers expected_reward, greedy_accuracy and mean_kl are given. The script exp_verifier.py shows the task from the verifier's point of view:

Milestone 1: the verifier, sampling and KL

A verifiable reward (chapter 14) is computed by a program that checks the answer against ground truth. Here the program reads an answer string and returns 1.0 if it is the true number and 0.0 otherwise. Three details decide whether the verifier can be trusted, because the policy will search for every loophole you leave.

  • Exact, not approximate. fractions.Fraction("0.1") is exactly one tenth; float("0.1") is not, and comparing floats invites a tolerance that someone will tune until a wrong answer passes. With Fraction, "3.91e2" and "782/2" are exactly 391 and "391.0000001" is not.
  • Junk is a zero, not an error. Fraction("about 391") raises ValueError. A verifier that crashes on a hedge stops the training run; one that returns 0.0 trains the policy away from hedging. Chapter 14's lazy verifier, which gave credit for any answer that mentioned 391, rewarded exactly the hedges in this task.
  • Normalise the surface, not the meaning. Leading and trailing whitespace and leading zeros are surface; whether the answer is right is meaning.

Sampling should be exactly reproducible, so the lab fixes the method. sample_index(probs, rng) makes one call to rng.random(), call it u, and returns the first index whose running total of probabilities exceeds u. That is inverse-CDF sampling, and the strict inequality matters: an index with probability zero must never be drawn, even when u = 0.0. Fixing the recipe also means the checker can regenerate your preference pairs in milestone 3 and compare them exactly.

Finally the leash. The exact KL divergence KL(p ‖ q) = ∑k pk log(pk / qk) measures how far a policy p has moved from a reference q (entropy, cross-entropy and KL). It is zero only when they are equal, never negative, and not symmetric. With a tabular policy you can compute it exactly, which will let you check chapter 14's sample-based estimator in milestone 5.

def parse_number(text):
    """The exact numeric value of an answer string as a Fraction, or None if it is not a number.

    "391", " 391\n", "0391", "391.0" and "3.91e2" are all the number 391. "about 391", "391 or 392"
    and "" are not numbers. Use fractions.Fraction (it raises ValueError on junk) so that "0.1" is
    exactly one tenth and no tolerance is needed.
    """
    try:
        return Fraction(text.strip())
    except (ValueError, ZeroDivisionError):
        return None

def reward(text, answer):
    """The verifiable reward: 1.0 if `text` parses to a number exactly equal to `answer`, else 0.0."""
    value = parse_number(text)
    return 1.0 if value is not None and value == answer else 0.0

def sample_index(probs, rng):
    """Draw an index with the given probabilities by inverse CDF using ONE call to rng.random().

    u = rng.random(); return the first k whose running sum of probs exceeds u (the last index if
    rounding leaves u above the total).
    """
    u = rng.random()
    acc = 0.0
    for k, p in enumerate(probs):
        acc += p
        if u < acc:
            return k
    return len(probs) - 1

def kl_divergence(p, q):
    """Exact KL(p || q) = sum_k p_k log(p_k / q_k) over two probability vectors (terms with p_k = 0 are 0)."""
    return sum(pk * math.log(pk / qk) for pk, qk in zip(p, q) if pk > 0)

Milestone 2: the DPO loss and its gradient

Chapter 12 derived the DPO loss from the KL-regularised objective in five moves. Take a prompt x, a chosen answer yw and a rejected answer yl. The implicit reward of an answer is how much the policy has raised its log-probability above the reference's, scaled by β:

r̂(y) = β · [ log πθ(y | x) − log πref(y | x) ]

The margin of a pair is m = r̂(yw) − r̂(yl), and the loss is −log σ(m), the Bradley-Terry log-likelihood of the observed choice with the policy as its own reward model. The dataset loss is the mean over pairs. Three consequences are worth seeing in code before any training.

  • The starting loss is exactly ln 2. At πθ = πref every implicit reward is zero, the margin is zero and −log σ(0) = log 2 = 0.693. A DPO run that does not start there has a bug, usually in which model the reference is.
  • The gradient is tiny. Write the log-probability of candidate k as zk − logsumexp(z). In the margin, the two logsumexp terms are the same number (it is the same question's softmax) and they cancel, so m = β [ (zw − zl) − (zrefw − zrefl) ]. The margin depends on the logits of exactly two candidates. With ∂(−log σ(m))/∂m = −σ(−m), the gradient of one pair is ∂L/∂zw = −β σ(−m), ∂L/∂zl = +β σ(−m) and zero on every other logit. This is chapter 12's gradient, −β σ(−m) [∇ log π(yw) − ∇ log π(yl)], specialised to a softmax: the −p terms in ∇ log π = e − p are the same for both answers and cancel in the bracket.
  • The weight is self-limiting. σ(−m) is near 1 when the pair is ranked the wrong way and near 0 when it is ranked right by a wide margin. A pair stops pulling once the policy has learned it. The script below trains on 400 pairs with β = 0.5 and reports the mean weight over the pairs as it falls.

The same trace at β = 0.1, the TRL default, would be far slower, because β multiplies the margin: the same logit change produces one fifth the margin.

Hints try without first
  • implicit_rewards: [beta * (a - b) for a, b in zip(log_softmax(zq), log_softmax(zref_q))]; the margin is the difference of two entries of it.
  • The loss −log σ(m) overflows for very negative m if you compute sigmoid(m) first and take its log. For m < −30, −log σ(m) is −m to machine precision.
  • Gradient of one pair: weight = beta * sigmoid(-m) / len(pairs), then grads[q][w] -= weight and grads[q][l] += weight. The division by the number of pairs is the "mean" in the loss.
  • If the finite-difference check is off by a constant factor, it is β or the 1 / len(pairs). If it is off on the logits other than w and l, you have put a softmax term into the gradient that cancels.

def implicit_rewards(zq, zref_q, beta):
    """The implicit reward of every candidate: beta * (log pi_theta(k) - log pi_ref(k)), as a list (chapter 12)."""
    lp, lr = log_softmax(zq), log_softmax(zref_q)
    return [beta * (a - b) for a, b in zip(lp, lr)]

def dpo_margin(zq, zref_q, w, l, beta):
    """m = r_hat(w) - r_hat(l), the difference of implicit rewards of the chosen (w) and rejected (l) answers."""
    r = implicit_rewards(zq, zref_q, beta)
    return r[w] - r[l]

def dpo_loss(z, ref, pairs, beta):
    """Mean over pairs (q, w, l) of -log sigmoid(margin). z and ref are lists of logit vectors, one per question."""
    total = 0.0
    for q, w, l in pairs:
        m = dpo_margin(z[q], ref[q], w, l, beta)
        total += -math.log(sigmoid(m)) if m > -30 else -m          # stable -log sigmoid for very negative margins
    return total / len(pairs)

def dpo_grads(z, ref, pairs, beta):
    """Gradient of dpo_loss with respect to every logit, as a list of vectors shaped like z.

    For one pair: d loss / d z[w] = -beta * sigmoid(-m), d loss / d z[l] = +beta * sigmoid(-m), and
    nothing else moves (the log-sum-exp terms of the two log-probabilities cancel). Average over pairs.
    """
    grads = [[0.0] * len(row) for row in z]
    for q, w, l in pairs:
        m = dpo_margin(z[q], ref[q], w, l, beta)
        weight = beta * sigmoid(-m) / len(pairs)
        grads[q][w] -= weight
        grads[q][l] += weight
    return grads

Milestone 3: preference pairs and DPO training

DPO needs a dataset of comparisons. In chapter 12 these come from people; for a task with a verifier you can build them mechanically, which is how most open recipes produce DPO data for maths and code. The procedure the lab fixes is: pick a question at random, draw two answers from the reference policy, ask the verifier for both rewards, and keep the pair only if the rewards differ, with the higher-reward answer as yw. Pairs where both answers are right or both are wrong carry no preference and are thrown away.

That rejection step has a cost that deserves a number. A question on which the reference is right with probability p yields a usable pair with probability 2p(1 − p): 0.48 at p = 0.6, but 0.03 at p = 0.015 and 0.016 at p = 0.008. The hardest questions rarely produce a pair at all. Getting 400 pairs from the reference takes 3,074 sampled answers in this task, and the pairs are not spread evenly over the questions: question 0 contributes 67 pairs, question 7 contributes 15 and question 11 contributes 1.

Training is then plain full-batch gradient descent on the logits, starting from the reference: the logits are the parameters, and the gradient is the one you wrote in milestone 2. After it, the policy is its own reward model. The script shows what the implicit reward looks like on two questions after 300 steps at β = 0.1:

def make_pairs(task, n, rng, max_attempts=None):
    """Up to n preference pairs (q, chosen, rejected), labelled by the verifier, from the reference policy.

    Repeat: q = rng.randrange(n_questions); draw two candidates a, b from softmax(task.ref[q]) with
    sample_index (a first, then b); if their rewards differ, keep (q, winner, loser). Pairs whose
    rewards tie are discarded. Stop when n pairs are kept or after max_attempts draws
    (default 50 * n), and return what you have.
    """
    max_attempts = max_attempts or 50 * n
    probs = [softmax(r) for r in task.ref]
    rewards = [rewards_of(q) for q in task.questions]
    pairs = []
    for _ in range(max_attempts):
        if len(pairs) == n:
            break
        q = rng.randrange(task.n_questions)
        a = sample_index(probs[q], rng)
        b = sample_index(probs[q], rng)
        if rewards[q][a] == rewards[q][b]:
            continue
        pairs.append((q, a, b) if rewards[q][a] > rewards[q][b] else (q, b, a))
    return pairs

def train_dpo(task, pairs, beta, lr, steps):
    """Full-batch gradient descent on the DPO loss, starting from the reference logits.

    Returns (z, losses): the trained logits and the loss before each step. task.ref is not modified.
    """
    z = copy_logits(task.ref)
    losses = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        losses.append(dpo_loss(z, task.ref, pairs, beta))
        grads = dpo_grads(z, task.ref, pairs, beta)
        for zq, gq in zip(z, grads):
            for k in range(len(zq)):
                zq[k] -= lr * gq[k]
    return z, losses

Milestone 4: group-relative advantages

GRPO throws away the pairs and the reward model's replacement, the implicit reward, and starts from a different question: given a handful of answers to the same question, which ones were better than the rest? For each question it samples a group of G answers from the current policy and scores each with the verifier to get rewards r1, …, rG. The advantage of answer i is its reward standardised within the group (equation 3 of the DeepSeek-R1 paper, as chapter 14 writes it):

Ai = ( ri − mean(r) ) / ( std(r) + ε )

The lab uses the population standard deviation (divide by G) and a small ε = 10-4 so that a tied group divides by something non-zero. Two simpler rules appear in the literature and in libraries, and you implement all three behind a mode argument:

  • "std" as above: GRPO as published.
  • "mean": ri − mean(r), without the division (the choice Liu et al., 2025, argue for in "Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training", the variant sometimes called Dr. GRPO).
  • "rloo": ri − the mean of the other G − 1 rewards, the leave-one-out baseline of RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024).

The standard deviation boosts the rare event: a prompt where the model almost always fails or almost always succeeds is weighted up compared with a 50-50 prompt, which is a choice, not a fact, and the reason the two rules differ in practice. The leave-one-out advantage is always G / (G − 1) = 8/7 times the mean-only advantage. The top and bottom rows are all zeros: a group where every answer ties has no signal, whichever rule you use.

Is the group mean a valid baseline?

Chapter 7 proved that subtracting a baseline b leaves the policy gradient unbiased if b does not depend on the action being scored. The group mean contains ri itself, so it is not independent of answer i, and chapter 14 computed the consequence: the expected gradient is (G − 1)/G of the true one. In a tabular policy you can check this exactly, with no sampling at all, by enumerating every possible group of G answers, weighting each by its probability and summing the gradient estimator (1/G) ∑ Ai ∇ log π(oi):

def group_advantages(rewards, mode="std", eps=1e-4):
    """Advantages for one group of G sampled answers to the same question.

        "std"   (r_i - mean) / (std + eps)      GRPO as in the R1 paper; std is the POPULATION std (divide by G)
        "mean"  r_i - mean                      no std scaling (Dr. GRPO style)
        "rloo"  r_i - mean of the OTHER G-1 rewards (leave-one-out baseline); needs G >= 2
    """
    G = len(rewards)
    mean = sum(rewards) / G
    if mode == "mean":
        return [r - mean for r in rewards]
    if mode == "rloo":
        return [r - (sum(rewards) - r) / (G - 1) for r in rewards]
    std = math.sqrt(sum((r - mean) ** 2 for r in rewards) / G)
    return [(r - mean) / (std + eps) for r in rewards]

def zero_signal(rewards):
    """True if every reward in the group is the same, so the group has nothing to teach."""
    return max(rewards) == min(rewards)

Milestone 5: the GRPO objective for one group

With advantages in hand, GRPO uses PPO's machinery from chapter 8. Freeze a copy of the policy as πold and sample the group from it. The probability ratio of a sampled answer a is ρ = πθ(a) / πold(a), which is exactly 1 on the first gradient step and drifts away as the policy changes. Each sample contributes the clipped surrogate min(ρ A, clip(ρ, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) A), to be maximised, and a KL penalty to the reference. Written as a loss to minimise, averaged over the group (a one-token answer has no inner sum over tokens):

L = −(1/G) ∑i min( ρi Ai, clip(ρi, 1−ε, 1+ε) Ai )  +  β (1/G) ∑i k3i

The penalty uses the k3 estimator of chapter 8, computed from the one sampled answer: with r = πref(a) / πθ(a), k3 = r − log r − 1. It is never negative, it is zero when the two policies agree on a, and its expectation under samples from πθ is the exact KL, which is what the sampled penalty is estimating. You can compare the two on the trained policies below.

The plot shows why the clip is a one-sided safety rail. For a good answer (A > 0), the objective grows with ρ until ρ = 1 + ε and is flat after: the policy has already raised this answer's probability by 20%, and the objective offers nothing for more. For a bad answer (A < 0) it mirrors that below 1 − ε. On the other side nothing is clipped, because the min picks the more pessimistic of the two terms: a good answer whose probability has fallen (ρ < 1 − ε) still has the full gradient to push it back up.

The gradient, by hand

For a softmax, ∂ log π(a) / ∂z = ea − p (the one-hot vector of the sampled answer minus the probabilities), so ∂ρ/∂z = ρ (ea − p). A sample's policy term has gradient −ρ A (ea − p) while the clip is inactive, and exactly zero while it is active: when A > 0 and ρ > 1 + ε, or A < 0 and ρ < 1 − ε. For the KL term, ∂k3/∂ log π = 1 − r, so its gradient is (1 − πref(a) / πθ(a)) (ea − p), which pulls an answer the policy has made likelier than the reference back down, and an answer it has made less likely back up. At πθ = πold = πref the whole thing collapses to the plain advantage-weighted REINFORCE gradient of chapter 7, −(1/G) ∑ Ai (eai − p), which is also the hand case in the check.

def ratio(zq, zold_q, a):
    """rho = pi_theta(a) / pi_old(a) for the sampled candidate a, computed through log-probabilities."""
    return math.exp(log_softmax(zq)[a] - log_softmax(zold_q)[a])

def clipped_objective(rho, adv, eps):
    """PPO's per-sample term min(rho * A, clip(rho, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * A). This is a quantity to MAXIMISE."""
    clipped = min(max(rho, 1.0 - eps), 1.0 + eps)
    return min(rho * adv, clipped * adv)

def k3(zq, zref_q, a):
    """The k3 estimator of KL(pi_theta || pi_ref) from one sample a: r - log r - 1 with r = pi_ref(a) / pi_theta(a)."""
    log_r = log_softmax(zref_q)[a] - log_softmax(zq)[a]
    return math.exp(log_r) - log_r - 1.0

def grpo_loss_and_grad(zq, zold_q, zref_q, actions, advs, eps, beta):
    """(loss, dz) for ONE group of samples `actions` (candidate indices) with advantages `advs`.

        loss = -(1/G) sum_i clipped_objective(rho_i, A_i, eps)  +  beta * (1/G) sum_i k3_i

    dz is the gradient of that loss with respect to the K logits zq. Per sample, with p = softmax(zq)
    and e_a the one-hot vector of the sampled candidate:
        policy term:  d(-rho A)/dz = -rho * A * (e_a - p), but ZERO when the clip is active, i.e.
                      when (A > 0 and rho > 1 + eps) or (A < 0 and rho < 1 - eps)   (strict inequalities)
        KL term:      d(k3)/dz = (1 - pi_ref(a) / pi_theta(a)) * (e_a - p)
    """
    G = len(actions)
    p = softmax(zq)
    loss, dz = 0.0, [0.0] * len(zq)
    for a, adv in zip(actions, advs):
        rho = ratio(zq, zold_q, a)
        loss += -clipped_objective(rho, adv, eps) / G + beta * k3(zq, zref_q, a) / G
        active = not ((adv > 0 and rho > 1 + eps) or (adv < 0 and rho < 1 - eps))
        coef = (-rho * adv if active else 0.0) + beta * (1.0 - math.exp(log_softmax(zref_q)[a] - log_softmax(zq)[a]))
        for k in range(len(zq)):
            dz[k] += coef * ((1.0 if k == a else 0.0) - p[k]) / G
    return loss, dz

Milestone 6: the GRPO training loop and the comparison

Now the loop that chapter 14 draws as a stepper. Each iteration of train_grpo picks six of the twelve questions, and for each one: freezes πold, samples a group of eight answers from it, scores them with the verifier, standardises the rewards into advantages, then takes inner = 4 gradient steps on the same group (with πold held fixed, so the ratios drift and the clip can act). Reusing a batch is what the clip is for: generating the group is the expensive part of a real run, and the clip makes it safe to extract several steps from it. Sixty iterations, six groups each, is 2,880 sampled answers.

  • Both methods work. The expected reward rises from 0.197 to 0.806 (GRPO, 60 steps) and 0.817 (DPO, 1,000 steps), with almost identical drift from the reference (KL 1.43 and 1.51).   • They need similar amounts of generation. The 400 pairs cost 3,074 sampled answers from the reference, because most pairs tie and are discarded; GRPO drew 2,880. Offline data is not free data.

Both methods inherit the same ceiling: RL with a verifier can only sharpen what the reference sometimes does, a point chapter 14 returns to when it discusses what RLVR adds to a model.

def train_grpo(task, steps, group_size=8, batch_questions=6, inner=4, lr=0.5, eps=0.2, beta=0.04,
               mode="std", seed=0):
    """Train from the reference with GRPO. Returns (z, log) where log is a list of dicts, one per step:
    {"reward": expected_reward after the step, "zero_groups": number of groups with no signal this step}.

    Each step:
      1. pick batch_questions distinct questions (rng.sample(range(n_questions), batch_questions))
      2. for each, freeze z_old = copy of the current logits and sample group_size candidates from
         softmax(z_old) with sample_index; score them with the verifier and compute advantages
      3. repeat `inner` times: for every group take grpo_loss_and_grad at the CURRENT logits (z_old stays
         frozen) and make ONE plain gradient step on the question's logits: z -= lr * dz
    Groups with zero signal still go through step 3 (their policy term is zero, only the KL term acts).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    z = copy_logits(task.ref)
    log = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        batch = rng.sample(range(task.n_questions), batch_questions)
        groups, zero = [], 0
        for q in batch:
            zold = z[q][:]
            probs = softmax(zold)
            actions = [sample_index(probs, rng) for _ in range(group_size)]
            rs = [reward(task.questions[q].candidates[a], task.questions[q].answer) for a in actions]
            zero += zero_signal(rs)
            groups.append((q, zold, actions, group_advantages(rs, mode)))
        for _ in range(inner):
            for q, zold, actions, advs in groups:
                _, dz = grpo_loss_and_grad(z[q], zold, task.ref[q], actions, advs, eps, beta)
                z[q] = [v - lr * g for v, g in zip(z[q], dz)]
        log.append({"reward": expected_reward(task, z), "zero_groups": zero})
    return z, log

Look under the hood

  • What a real sequence adds. In a language model an answer is many tokens, so log π(y | x) in the DPO margin is a sum of token log-probabilities, the DPO gradient is the weighted difference of two sequence gradients (chapter 12), and GRPO's ratio is computed per token with the whole answer sharing one advantage (chapter 14). The weights and the clip work the same way; what changes is that the logsumexp terms no longer cancel as neatly, and that long answers dominate unless the loss is normalised, a choice on which libraries differ.
  • Why the verifier has a second correct form. Half the questions list the right answer twice ("576" and "576.0") and the reference splits its probability between them. A string-equality verifier would call one of them wrong and teach the policy to prefer a particular spelling. Real tasks are full of this: 1/2 and 0.5, x=3 and 3. The other half has a hedge. Delete the hedge's zero from the verifier (give it credit for mentioning the number) and watch what train_grpo learns: it is a short experiment and a vivid one.
  • Why DPO ends up near the reference-weighted optimum. With enough steps and pairs, the DPO policy approaches the reference re-weighted by exp(r/β) for the reward that explains the labels. Here the labels are deterministic (the verifier never errs), so there is no Bradley-Terry noise: the best-fitting reward is as sharp as the data allow and the policy concentrates on the correct answers, as the table shows. With human labels the same loss produces a gentler tilt.
  • Where the toy is generous. The policy has a parameter for every answer to every question, so improving question 3 cannot hurt question 4. In a network, updates on one prompt generalise to others in both good and bad ways, which is where over-optimisation, length drift and format collapse come from. Chapter 13's reward-hacking material is about what the toy leaves out.

Common misconception

"GRPO is DPO with sampling" or "DPO is GRPO with a fixed dataset." They optimise the same objective (reward under a KL leash) by different routes, and the toy shows the routes differ in what they need. DPO needs comparisons and a reference forward pass, and its gradient is one sigmoid weight on two logits. GRPO needs a scalar verifier and a group per question, and its gradient is an advantage-weighted score function with a clip. A pair carries no information about how good the winner was; a group does. A silent group is cheap to detect and expensive to have generated, while a missing pair is simply absent from the dataset. And neither can learn what the reference never does: a verifier makes the reward trustworthy, not the exploration adequate.

Try it yourself: four experiments

  1. Reward the hedge. Change reward so that any string containing the true answer's digits scores 1.0 (chapter 14's lazy verifier). Run train_grpo and look at which candidate strings gain probability on the odd-numbered questions. Does the policy learn arithmetic or hedging, and how would you detect it from the reward curve alone?
  2. Break the clip. Set eps to 100 in train_grpo with inner=8 and lr=1.0. In this run the logits diverge and k3 raises an OverflowError. Find the step at which the largest ratio first exceeds 1000, and then find the smallest eps in {0.05, 0.2, 1.0} that still trains stably.
  3. More pairs, fewer steps. Give DPO 1,000 and 4,000 pairs at 300 and 1,000 steps (the numbers in exp_compare.py show where it sits at 400). Is the limit the number of pairs or the number of steps? Which questions improve?
  4. Noisy labels. In make_pairs, flip the chosen and rejected answers of 20% of the pairs. How does DPO's expected reward at 1,000 steps change, and what happens to the loss at the end of training? Compare with chapter 12's discussion of labeller agreement.

Stretch goals

  • The std-or-not question. Run train_grpo with mode set to "std", "mean" and "rloo" over ten seeds each and report expected reward and KL with a spread. Is the difference between the modes larger than the difference between seeds?
  • Dynamic sampling. Modify train_grpo to resample a question until its group has non-zero spread (up to a limit) and compare reward per sampled answer with the plain loop. This is the idea in DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), cited in chapter 14.
  • An online DPO. Build pairs from the current policy each iteration instead of the reference and train DPO on them. How does it compare with GRPO at an equal sampling budget, and what does it do on question 10?
  • Sequences. Replace the six-way choice with a two-token answer (tens digit, then units digit), each a softmax conditioned on nothing, so a sequence log-probability is a sum of two terms. Re-derive the DPO gradient and the GRPO ratio, and check them by finite differences.
  • Estimate KL by samples. For the trained GRPO policy, compare the exact mean_kl with the average of k3 over many samples from the policy, and with the cruder k1 = log(πθ/πref). Chapter 8 explains why k3 has lower variance.

Systems connection

The verifier is the part of this lab that becomes infrastructure. In a real run it is a service called tens of thousands of times per step, with a deadline, and for code or SQL it executes untrusted output from a model you are deliberately training to be inventive: the controls are those of container isolation and least privilege, not those of a function call. The quantities you measured here (silent groups, pairs per sample, samples per update) are capacity planning: they set how many generations a step costs, which sets how many serving replicas a trainer needs, which is the territory of serving engines and token economics. And the checks in this lab (finite differences, exact enumeration, a seed printed on every failure) are the habit that makes a loss function reviewable, in the same way that observability makes a service debuggable.

What you learned

  • A verifier is a specification with loopholes. Exact numeric matching accepts "391.0" and "0391", rejects hedges, and returns 0.0 on junk instead of raising. Everything the policy can exploit, it will.
  • The DPO loss starts at ln 2. For a softmax policy its gradient moves exactly two logits by β σ(−m) in opposite directions, and the weight falls as the pair is learned: each pair is self-limiting. β sets how far from the reference the optimum sits.
  • Verified preference pairs are cheap to build and wasteful to sample: only pairs whose rewards differ count, so hard questions yield almost none.
  • The group-relative advantage replaces the critic with the other answers to the same question. Standardising boosts rare successes; the group mean is a baseline biased by exactly (G − 1)/G, and leave-one-out removes the bias. A tied group has exactly zero advantage.
  • The clip switches a sample's gradient off once its probability has moved by more than ε in the direction the advantage favours; it is what makes reusing a batch safe. The k3 penalty is a sampled, non-negative estimate of KL to the reference.
  • Both methods improve a policy on a verifiable task to a similar level at similar drift; both are bounded by what the reference sometimes gets right. Groups with no signal are generated and wasted, which is why prompts should be matched to the model.

Check your understanding

At the very start of DPO training the policy equals the reference. What is the mean loss, and why?

  1. 0, because the policy already matches the reference exactly
  2. 1, because the loss is a log-likelihood normalised to one nat
  3. ln 2 = 0.693, because every implicit reward is 0, so the margin is 0 and −log σ(0) = log 2
  4. It depends on β, which scales the loss
The implicit reward is β times the log-ratio of the policy to the reference, which is zero for every answer when the two are equal. A zero margin gives a 50% preference probability and a loss of log 2, whatever β is. The first option confuses "no drift" with "no loss": the loss measures how well the pairs are explained, and an untrained policy explains none of them.

For a tabular softmax policy, which logits does the gradient of the DPO loss for one pair (q, w, l) touch?

  1. Only the logits of the chosen and rejected answers, in opposite directions, each by β σ(−m)
  2. All six logits of the question, in proportion to the policy's probabilities
  3. Only the chosen answer's logit, because the rejected answer is a negative example
  4. The logits of the chosen and rejected answers by the same amount in the same direction
The log-sum-exp in each log-probability is the same for both answers and cancels in the margin, so only the two logits remain. The first alternative describes the gradient of a single log-probability (which has a softmax term), not of a difference. The third drops the rejected side, which DPO needs: without it the model could raise the chosen answer without limit.

A group of eight answers has exactly one correct answer. With the standardised GRPO advantage, what do the correct and wrong answers receive?

  1. +1 for the correct answer and −1 for each wrong one
  2. +0.875 for the correct answer and −0.125 for each wrong one
  3. +7 for the correct answer and −1 for each wrong one
  4. About +2.65 for the correct answer and −0.38 for each wrong one
The mean is 1/8 and the population standard deviation is √(1/8 × 7/8) = 0.331, so the advantages are 0.875 / 0.331 = 2.65 and −0.125 / 0.331 = −0.38. The second option is the mean-only (Dr. GRPO style) rule without the division; the first is what a 50-50 group gives.

Every one of the eight answers to a question gets reward 1.0. What does GRPO do with this group?

  1. It reinforces all eight answers, because all were correct
  2. Every advantage is 0, so the group contributes no policy gradient (the KL term, if any, still acts) although it cost eight generations
  3. It raises an error, because the standard deviation is zero
  4. It pushes the policy towards the average answer
The advantage is relative to the group, and the group has no spread. A guard on the standard deviation (the ε in the denominator) turns 0/0 into 0. The same happens when all eight are wrong. This is why a prompt set should contain mostly questions the model solves sometimes.

With the group mean as the baseline (including the sample itself), the expected GRPO gradient for G = 4 is what fraction of the true policy gradient?

  1. 1, because any baseline leaves the gradient unbiased
  2. 1/4
  3. 3/4 = (G − 1)/G
  4. 4/3
A baseline leaves the gradient unbiased only if it is independent of the sampled action. The group mean contains the sample's own reward, which shrinks its advantage by a factor (1 − 1/G). Excluding the sample (leave-one-out) restores the exact gradient, as the enumeration in the lab shows.

In a GRPO update, a sample has A > 0 and its probability ratio has already reached 1.5 with ε = 0.2. What is its policy gradient?

  1. Exactly zero: the clipped term is flat above 1 + ε, so this sample no longer pushes its probability up
  2. Reduced by 20%, because the ratio is clipped to 1.2
  3. Reversed, to pull the probability back below 1.2
  4. Unchanged: the clip only matters for negative advantages
For A > 0, min(ρA, clip(ρ)A) equals 1.2A once ρ > 1.2, a constant, so its derivative is zero. It does not reverse (the clip is a flat wall, not a restoring force; the KL term is what pulls back) and it is not scaled down. The clip acts on both signs, in the direction the advantage favours.
