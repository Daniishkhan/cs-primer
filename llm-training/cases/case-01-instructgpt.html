InstructGPT: RLHF in Practice

In early 2022 OpenAI published "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (Ouyang et al., 2022). Its headline: on OpenAI's own prompt distribution, labellers preferred the outputs of a 1.3 billion parameter model, fine-tuned with human feedback, to the outputs of the 175 billion parameter GPT-3 it started from. This case study reads the paper as an engineering recipe: what data was collected, which three models were trained, what the numbers show, what they cost, and what the authors themselves said was still wrong.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the full text of the arXiv version (2203.02155v1, 4 March 2022), including the appendices on data, labellers and training details. Every number below is quoted from it and attributed to the paper. Some of its results appear only as bar charts (for example the win rate of each model size, Figures 1 and 3); where the text gives no number I describe the pattern and do not invent one. I tried to fetch OpenAI's own blog post about the work and the server refused the request (HTTP 403), so I have not read it. The earlier papers the authors build on (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020) are known to me only through the way this paper describes them. Nothing here tests the claims independently: the evaluation was run by the authors, with their own labellers, on their own prompts.

The pattern in one sentence

When "what we want" is hard to write as a formula, collect human judgements of the model's own outputs, distil them into a learned reward, and optimise the model against that reward while a penalty keeps it close to where it started.

Context: a model that completes text is not a model that helps

GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020, "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners") was trained to predict the next token on a large body of internet text. The InstructGPT paper opens with the observation that this objective differs from "follow the user's instructions helpfully and safely", so the authors call the language-modelling objective misaligned with what users want. They use the framing of Askell et al. (2021): a model should be helpful, honest and harmless. The paper's contribution is not a new algorithm. Reinforcement learning from human feedback had already been applied to summarisation by Stiennon et al. (2020). What this paper shows is the method applied to a general-purpose model and a broad, real prompt distribution, with numbers that a product team could act on.

If you have read chapter 10 (supervised fine-tuning) and chapter 12 (preference optimisation), this page is the original of both: step 1 below is SFT, steps 2 and 3 are the RLHF pipeline that chapter 12 later contrasts with DPO.

Demonstration
A prompt paired with an answer written by a person to show the desired behaviour; the training data for supervised fine-tuning.
Comparison (ranking)
A human judgement that one model output is better than another for the same prompt. The paper shows labellers 4 to 9 outputs at once and asks for a ranking.
Reward model (RM)
A model that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs one number, trained so that answers people prefer get higher numbers. See chapter 13.
PPO
Proximal policy optimisation, the reinforcement-learning algorithm used for step 3; chapter 8 derives it.
KL penalty
A term subtracted from the reward that grows as the tuned model's token probabilities drift from the SFT model's, so it cannot wander far to chase reward.
PPO-ptx
The paper's variant that mixes ordinary pretraining-loss updates into the PPO updates; "InstructGPT" in the paper means this variant.
Alignment tax
The loss in performance on other tasks that an alignment procedure can cause.

The mechanism: three models, three datasets

Where the prompts came from

The prompt set (section 3.2) consists mainly of text submitted to the OpenAI API through the Playground interface to earlier versions of InstructGPT that had been trained on demonstration data only; users were told that their data could be used to train further models. The authors deduplicated prompts that share a long prefix, capped the number at 200 per user, split train, validation and test by user so that test users are not in training, and filtered training prompts for personally identifiable information. They also had labellers write three kinds of prompt themselves (plain tasks, few-shot instructions with example pairs, and prompts based on use cases stated in API waitlist applications) to bootstrap the first model. From these prompts they made three datasets: about 13 thousand prompts for SFT (with demonstrations), about 33 thousand for the reward model (with rankings), and about 31 thousand for PPO (prompts only, from the API). Section 5 notes that most training prompts are English and that customers on a waitlist, seeded with OpenAI employees' contacts, are not representative of all users.

Step 1: supervised fine-tuning

The authors fine-tune GPT-3 on labeller demonstrations for 16 epochs with cosine learning-rate decay. They note something that looks wrong: validation loss starts to rise after one epoch, yet training for more epochs improves both the reward-model score and human preference ratings, so they select the final SFT model by reward-model score on the validation set. Overfitting on the loss and improving on the thing you care about can coexist when the metric you care about is not the training loss.

Step 2: the reward model

The reward model starts from the SFT model with the final unembedding layer removed, and outputs a scalar for a (prompt, answer) pair. The paper only uses 6 billion parameter reward models, for all policy sizes, because a 175B reward model was found to be unstable and a poor value function for RL.

Labellers rank between K = 4 and K = 9 outputs for a prompt, which yields K(K − 1)/2 pairwise comparisons from only K forward passes. The authors found that shuffling all comparisons into one dataset made the model overfit within a single pass, because comparisons from one prompt are strongly correlated. Their fix is to treat all pairs from one prompt as a single batch element. The loss (equation 1 of the paper) is:

loss(θ) = − (1 / C(K,2)) · E(x, yw, yl) [ log σ( rθ(x, yw) − rθ(x, yl) ) ]

where rθ(x, y) is the scalar score, yw is the preferred answer and yl the rejected one. The reward difference is treated as the log-odds that a labeller prefers one answer; this is the Bradley-Terry model derived in chapter 12. Because the loss only depends on differences, the authors add a bias so that labeller demonstrations score 0 on average before RL starts.

Step 3: PPO against the reward

The RL environment (section 3.5) is a bandit: it shows a random prompt, the policy writes a whole answer, the reward model scores it and the episode ends. The value function is initialised from the reward model. A per-token KL penalty from the SFT model discourages over-optimising the reward model. The paper's combined objective (equation 2) is:

objective(φ) = E(x,y)∼DπRL [ rθ(x, y) − β log( πRLφ(y|x) / πSFT(y|x) ) ] + γ Ex∼Dpretrain [ log πRLφ(x) ]

β sets the strength of the KL penalty and γ the weight of the pretraining term; setting γ = 0 gives plain "PPO" and a positive γ gives "PPO-ptx". The pretraining term exists to repair a side effect described below. The paper also notes that comparison data is mostly collected from the SFT policies, with some from PPO policies, and that steps 2 and 3 can be iterated.

The labellers

The data is only as meaningful as the people who produced it. The authors hired about 40 contractors, selected through a screening test that measured how well they could identify and respond to sensitive prompts and how often they agreed with researchers on a labelling task. Section 5.2 describes them as mostly English-speaking people living in the United States or Southeast Asia, hired through Upwork or Scale AI. The team collaborated closely with them: onboarding, written instructions per task, and a shared chat room for questions.

Agreement is the number to look at (section 3.4). Training labellers agreed with each other on 72.6 ± 1.5% of comparisons; held-out labellers, who did not produce training data and took no screening test, agreed on 77.3 ± 1.3%. For comparison, the paper cites researcher-to-researcher agreement of 73 ± 4% in the summarisation work of Stiennon et al. (2020). So roughly one comparison in four is a disagreement between two careful people, which sets a ceiling on what any reward model fitted to a single labeller's choice can learn about "the" correct ranking. Section 5.3 adds that most comparisons were labelled by only one contractor, for cost. Chapter 5 of the evals course, Human Labels, explains how to measure and manage this.

What the paper reports

  • Preference. Outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model were preferred to outputs of the 175B GPT-3, "despite having 100x fewer parameters" (abstract). The 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time and to few-shot GPT-3 71 ± 4% of the time. Held-out labellers preferred it at about the same rate as training labellers, in a preliminary experiment.
  • Instruction following. Compared with GPT-3, labellers rated InstructGPT as more appropriate for a customer assistant, better at following explicit constraints, less likely to ignore the instruction, and less prone to hallucination in closed-domain tasks, where the answer must not contain information absent from the input (a 21% rate versus 41% for GPT-3).
  • Truthfulness. On TruthfulQA the paper says InstructGPT gives truthful and informative answers about twice as often as GPT-3.
  • Toxicity and bias. About 25% fewer toxic outputs than GPT-3 on RealToxicityPrompts when the model is prompted to be respectful; no significant improvement on the Winogender and CrowS-Pairs bias datasets.
  • Public benchmarks as a baseline. GPT-3 fine-tuned on the FLAN and T0 collections of public NLP tasks performed slightly worse than the authors' SFT baseline on their prompt distribution, and InstructGPT beat them (73.4 ± 2% win rate against the SFT baseline, compared with 26.8 ± 2% for T0 and 29.8 ± 2% for FLAN). The authors' explanation: classification and question answering are only about 18% of what API customers asked for, while open-ended generation and brainstorming make up about 57%.
  • Generalisation. Qualitatively, the model sometimes followed instructions in other languages and answered questions about code, though such prompts were very rare in the fine-tuning data. The paper presents this as a probe, not a measured result.

The alignment tax and the pretraining mix

Plain PPO lowered performance relative to GPT-3 on several public datasets, among them SQuAD, DROP, HellaSwag and a French-to-English translation task. The authors call this an "alignment tax" and argue it matters because it gives people a reason to use an unaligned but more capable model. Mixing a pretraining log-likelihood term into the PPO update (PPO-ptx) reduced the regressions without lowering labeller preference scores, though the paper reports that the 175B PPO-ptx model "still lags behind GPT-3 on DROP, SQuADv2, and translation", so the tax was reduced rather than removed.

What it cost

Section 5.1 gives the compute: 4.9 petaflop/s-days for the 175B SFT model and 60 for the 175B PPO-ptx model, against 3,640 for GPT-3's pretraining (Brown et al., 2020). The authors conclude that the cost of increasing alignment is modest relative to pretraining, and that "increasing investments in alignment of existing language models is more cost-effective than training larger models", at least for their customers' task distribution. Two cautions on reading this number. It excludes the human side: the paper says only that collecting data and running experiments was a fraction of GPT-3's cost and does not give a figure, so I cannot give one. The 4.9 and 60 figures are for training those two models; the sentence around them says that data collection plus compute, including experimental runs, was a fraction of GPT-3's cost, and gives no figure for that total.

What the authors said was still wrong

  • Whose preferences? Section 5.2 states that the model is aligned to a particular group: the labellers, the researchers who wrote the instructions, and the API customers who supplied prompts, not to "human values" in general. Aligning to the average labeller may be wrong where disagreement reflects real differences; the authors suggest weighting affected groups more heavily in some cases.
  • Obedience is not safety. "Perhaps the greatest limitation" (section 5.3) is that the models mostly follow the user's instruction even when that causes harm. Given a prompt asking it to be maximally biased, InstructGPT generated more toxic output than GPT-3 of the same size.
  • Simple mistakes. Section 4.3 lists a false premise accepted as true, needless hedging on simple questions, and degraded performance when an instruction contains several constraints.
  • Not fully aligned or safe. The models still produce toxic or biased text, make up facts, and produce sexual or violent content without being asked.

Later work addressed several of these, and the course covers it: reward over-optimisation and sycophancy, DPO (which removes the separate reward model), and verifiable rewards, the subject of the next case.

Try it yourself: a reward model from noisy rankings

The toy below is my own, not OpenAI's code. A hidden "quality" direction defines the true preference over 4-feature "responses". Simulated labellers rank 6 responses per prompt with Gaussian noise, tuned so that two labellers agree about 74% of the time (the paper reports 72.6% to 77.3%). I train a linear reward on the pairwise loss from equation 1, one prompt per batch element and a single pass, then test it against fresh noisy labels and against the noise-free truth. The final lines compute a KL-penalised reward for one sample with an assumed β = 0.02 and made-up log-probabilities.

"""Toy reward model trained on ranked lists, InstructGPT style (stdlib only).
Illustrative assumptions: 4 features per response, a hidden 'true' reward, labellers who rank with noise."""
import math, random
from itertools import combinations

rng = random.Random(0)
D = 4
TRUE_W = [1.0, -0.5, 0.8, 0.0]            # hidden quality direction (assumption)

def feats(): return [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(D)]
def dot(w, x): return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(w, x))

def rank_prompt(K, noise):
    """A labeller ranks K responses by true reward + Gaussian noise; return list best-first."""
    xs = [feats() for _ in range(K)]
    scored = sorted(xs, key=lambda x: -(dot(TRUE_W, x) + rng.gauss(0, noise)))
    return scored

def pairs(ranking):
    return [(ranking[i], ranking[j]) for i, j in combinations(range(len(ranking)), 2)]  # winner first

# 1. How many comparisons does one ranking give?
for K in (4, 9):
    print(f"K={K} responses -> {K*(K-1)//2} comparisons from {K} forward passes")

# 2. Labeller agreement: two labellers rank the same pair with independent noise.
def agree(noise, n=20000):
    hit = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        a, b = feats(), feats()
        l1 = dot(TRUE_W, a) + rng.gauss(0, noise) > dot(TRUE_W, b) + rng.gauss(0, noise)
        l2 = dot(TRUE_W, a) + rng.gauss(0, noise) > dot(TRUE_W, b) + rng.gauss(0, noise)
        hit += (l1 == l2)
    return hit / n
NOISE = 0.9
print(f"labeller-labeller agreement at noise {NOISE}: {agree(NOISE):.1%}  (paper: about 73%)")

# 3. Train r(x) = w.x with loss = -mean over pairs of log sigmoid(r(yw) - r(yl)), one prompt = one batch element.
train = [rank_prompt(6, NOISE) for _ in range(400)]
w = [0.0] * D
lr = 0.5
for epoch in range(1):                      # one pass: the paper notes RMs overfit after one epoch
    for ranking in train:
        ps = pairs(ranking)
        g = [0.0] * D
        for yw, yl in ps:
            m = dot(w, yw) - dot(w, yl)
            s = 1 / (1 + math.exp(m))        # = 1 - sigmoid(m)
            for k in range(D): g[k] += s * (yw[k] - yl[k]) / len(ps)
        for k in range(D): w[k] += lr * g[k]

# 4. Evaluate against the noise-free truth and against fresh noisy labels.
def acc(noise_eval, n=20000):
    hit = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        a, b = feats(), feats()
        label = dot(TRUE_W, a) + rng.gauss(0, noise_eval) > dot(TRUE_W, b) + rng.gauss(0, noise_eval)
        hit += ((dot(w, a) > dot(w, b)) == label)
    return hit / n
print("learned w (normalised):", [round(v / math.sqrt(dot(w, w)), 2) for v in w])
print("true    w (normalised):", [round(v / math.sqrt(dot(TRUE_W, TRUE_W)), 2) for v in TRUE_W])
print(f"RM agrees with a fresh noisy labeller: {acc(NOISE):.1%}")
print(f"RM agrees with the noise-free truth:   {acc(0.0):.1%}")

# 5. KL-penalised reward for one sampled response: r - beta * log(pi_rl/pi_sft)
beta, r, logp_rl, logp_sft = 0.02, 1.4, -35.0, -41.5
print(f"reward {r} - {beta} * ({logp_rl} - ({logp_sft})) = {r - beta*(logp_rl - logp_sft):.3f}")

Output when run:

K=4 responses -> 6 comparisons from 4 forward passes
K=9 responses -> 36 comparisons from 9 forward passes
labeller-labeller agreement at noise 0.9: 74.3%  (paper: about 73%)
learned w (normalised): [0.77, -0.26, 0.58, 0.0]
true    w (normalised): [0.73, -0.36, 0.58, 0.0]
RM agrees with a fresh noisy labeller: 81.4%
RM agrees with the noise-free truth:   96.3%
reward 1.4 - 0.02 * (-35.0 - (-41.5)) = 1.270

Reading the output: a 6-answer ranking gives 15 pairs, a 9-answer ranking 36, which is why the paper ranks K answers at once. The learned weights point nearly the same way as the hidden ones even though labellers disagree about a quarter of the time, and the model agrees with the noise-free ordering 96.3% of the time, well above its 81.4% agreement with a single fresh noisy label. Noise in individual labels averages out when there are enough of them; this toy cannot show the harder case, where labellers disagree systematically (for example, by culture or by what they were told), because averaging cannot remove a shared bias. Change NOISE or the number of prompts (400) and watch both accuracies move.

Try it yourself

Make half the simulated labellers systematically prefer the opposite sign for the second feature (a second group with different values) and train on the mixed data. What does the reward model learn about that feature, and what does the 74% agreement figure now hide?

The lessons

  • Judge the outputs, not the inputs. People are poor at writing a perfect answer but fairly good at picking the better of two. That is why steps 2 and 3 use comparisons, and why the preference-based tuning could add behaviour that demonstrations alone (the SFT baseline) did not: the paper's results put SFT between prompted GPT-3 and PPO.
  • Regularise toward where you started. The KL penalty and the pretraining mix are two answers to the same worry, that optimising a learned proxy for long enough finds its flaws. Chapter 13 shows that worry measured.
  • A reward model is a measurement, with error bars. About 73% agreement between labellers means the training signal is noisy by construction; evaluation needs held-out labellers and honest intervals, the habits of the evals course.
  • Evaluate on your own distribution. The strongest argument in the paper is that public NLP benchmarks do not reflect how customers use a model, which is why FLAN and T0 fine-tuning lost. It is also the paper's weakness: the evaluation prompts, labellers and instructions all come from the same organisation.
  • Mind the tax. Any post-training step can erase something. Measure other capabilities before and after (chapter 23).

Analogy: a restaurant critic panel

A new chef cannot write a formula for "delicious", but a panel of forty tasters can say which of two dishes they prefer. You first train a junior critic to predict the panel's choices (the reward model), then let the chef cook thousands of variations and keep what the junior critic likes, with a rule that the menu may not change too far from what the restaurant already serves (the KL penalty).

Where it breaks down: the junior critic is not the panel. A chef who studies the critic long enough will find dishes it overrates, such as very salty or very large ones, that the real panel would not like. The tasters also share a background, and a diner from elsewhere may disagree.

State of play, October 2026

The three-step recipe is still visible in newer pipelines, but its pieces have been rearranged. The January 2026 version of the DeepSeek-R1 paper (see the next case) uses rule-based rewards for mathematics and code, and a learned reward model only for general helpfulness: a reward model trained on 66,000 preference pairs and a separate safety reward model trained on 106,000 prompts, used in the final stage for only the last 400 of its 1,700 steps. The authors report that more steps with the model-based reward risk reward hacking. This is one lab's report about one model, not a survey. Which post-training method is "standard" changes quickly; treat the structure (learned reward for what cannot be checked, program for what can) as the durable idea.

Key takeaways

  • InstructGPT is three models: an SFT model on about 13k demonstrations, a 6B reward model on about 33k ranked prompts, and a PPO policy on about 31k prompts.
  • Rank K answers per prompt, train on all pairs as one batch element, and use a single pass to avoid overfitting the reward model.
  • A KL penalty to the SFT model limits reward over-optimisation; mixing in the pretraining loss (PPO-ptx) reduces the alignment tax but does not remove it.
  • Reported: 1.3B InstructGPT preferred to 175B GPT-3; 85 ± 3% preference at 175B; alignment cost 60 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 for pretraining.
  • The paper's own caveats matter: about 40 labellers, about 73% agreement, English-centred, and a model that obeys harmful instructions.

Check your understanding

Why does the paper train the reward model on all K(K − 1)/2 pairs from one prompt as a single batch element, rather than shuffling pairs into one big dataset?

  1. To make the labellers' work cheaper
  2. Comparisons from one prompt are strongly correlated, shuffled training overfitted after one pass, and one batch element needs only K forward passes
  3. Because PPO requires pairs to be adjacent
  4. To keep the reward model at 6 billion parameters
The paper reports that shuffling the correlated comparisons caused overfitting within a single pass, and that grouping them is more efficient because each completion needs only one forward pass. The model size (6B) was chosen for stability and compute, a separate reason.

Training labellers agreed with each other on 72.6% of comparisons. What does that imply for how you should read the reward model's accuracy?

  1. The reward model must be wrong about 27% of the time on any input
  2. The labellers were poorly trained and the data should be discarded
  3. Agreement of 100% is the realistic goal for a better reward model
  4. The label noise sets a ceiling on measured agreement with a single labeller; the model can still learn the consistent underlying preference
Disagreement among careful people is a property of the task (the paper cites 73% for researchers in earlier work). A model can agree with the shared signal far more often than a single noisy label does, as the toy shows. The tempting first option confuses agreement with a noisy label for correctness against a hidden truth.

What problem does the pretraining term in PPO-ptx address?

  1. Regressions on public NLP datasets (the alignment tax) caused by plain PPO fine-tuning
  2. The reward model being too small
  3. The KL penalty being too weak to stop reward hacking
  4. Labellers disagreeing with each other
PPO alone lowered performance on datasets such as SQuAD, DROP and HellaSwag; adding pretraining log-likelihood updates greatly reduced this without lowering labeller preference. The KL penalty is a separate mechanism, tied to the SFT model rather than the pretraining distribution.

A colleague says: "InstructGPT proves RLHF makes models safe, since 1.3B beat 175B." Which response matches the paper?

  1. Agreed: the paper shows the models no longer produce toxic or false text
  2. Disagree: the 1.3B result was measured with automated metrics only
  3. The preference result was measured by the authors' labellers on their prompts, and the paper itself says the models are neither fully aligned nor safe and can follow harmful instructions
  4. Disagree: the paper shows RLHF made models more toxic on every benchmark
The preference result used human labellers on a held-out customer prompt set, not automated metrics. The limitations section states that the models still generate toxic or biased text and can follow harmful instructions; toxicity fell on one benchmark under a respectful prompt but bias did not improve.
