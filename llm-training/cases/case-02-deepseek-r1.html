DeepSeek-R1

In January 2025 DeepSeek-AI published "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning". It describes a model, R1-Zero, trained with reinforcement learning directly on a pretrained base model and rewarded only for correct, well-formatted answers, whose answers grew longer and more self-checking as training went on. It then describes R1, a four-stage pipeline that fixes R1-Zero's rough edges, and six small models taught by R1's own outputs. This case follows the mechanism, the numbers the authors report, and the reservations later work and the paper itself raise.

What I could and could not read

I fetched the arXiv paper in both its first version (v1, January 2025) and its second (v2, dated 4 January 2026). I read v2 closely, including the supplementary material (data recipe, hyper-parameters, training cost, distillation, unsuccessful attempts); from v1 I checked only the abstract-level claims and the AIME figures. The two versions differ: v1 reports R1-Zero's AIME 2024 pass@1 rising from 15.6% to 71.0%; v2 says 15.6% to 77.9%, and its pipeline description and tables are reorganised. I use v2 throughout and say so when a number would differ in v1. All benchmark numbers are the authors' own, run by them; I have not reproduced any. Search results indicate a peer-reviewed version appeared in Nature in September 2025; I did not fetch it, and what I say about its coverage of training cost comes from secondary coverage and is marked as such. For the critical perspective I read only the abstract page of Liu et al. (2025), not the full paper.

The pattern in one sentence

When a program can check an answer, use the check itself as the reward, sample many attempts per question, push the model towards the attempts that beat the group's average, and then copy the resulting long reasoning into smaller models with ordinary supervised learning.

Context: reasoning without reasoning examples

By 2024, large language models could be made to reason step by step by prompting (chain-of-thought) or by fine-tuning on human-written reasoning (chapter 10). The R1 paper's introduction names the limit of that route: it depends on human-annotated reasoning traces, which are expensive, and "constraining models to replicate human thought processes" caps performance at the quality of the examples. Its alternative is to let the model explore with reinforcement learning, where the only supervision is whether the final answer is right. The earlier case, InstructGPT, used reinforcement learning against a learned reward standing in for human taste. R1 uses a programmatic reward wherever it can.

Verifiable reward
A reward computed by a program from the model's output: does the boxed answer equal the reference, do the unit tests pass. See chapter 14.
GRPO
Group Relative Policy Optimization (the paper's spelling): sample a group of answers to one question, score each, and use each answer's score relative to the group as its advantage, with no separate value network.
Chain of thought (CoT)
The intermediate reasoning text a model writes before its final answer.
Cold start
A small supervised fine-tuning set used before RL so that the RL model starts with readable, consistent output.
Rejection sampling
Generating many answers, keeping only those that pass a check, and training on the survivors.
Distillation
Training a smaller student model on outputs of a larger teacher (chapter 15).
pass@1 and cons@N
pass@1 is the average probability a single sampled answer is correct; cons@N is the accuracy of the majority answer among N samples.

The mechanism, part 1: R1-Zero, RL straight on the base model

R1-Zero starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base, the pretrained model, with no supervised fine-tuning phase. The prompt is a short template (Table 1 of the paper): a conversation between a user and an assistant, where the assistant first thinks inside <think> tags and then answers inside <answer> tags. The authors deliberately restrict constraints to this structure so that they can observe the model's natural progression.

The reward: two rules, no neural reward model

The reward (section 2.2) is the sum, with equal weight, of an accuracy reward and a format reward. For mathematics the answer must be in a specified format (for example boxed) so that a rule can compare it with the reference; for code, a compiler runs the answer against predefined test cases; the format reward checks that the reasoning sits inside the tags. The data recipe (Supplementary B.3.1) gives reasoning RL data of four kinds: mathematics (26k questions, reward 1 if the answer matches the reference, otherwise 0; proofs are excluded because correctness is hard to judge), coding (17k algorithm problems and 8k bug-fixing problems from real GitHub issues, judged by hidden tests), STEM multiple choice (22k) and logic (15k, including synthetic puzzles that can be checked automatically).

The paper explains why it avoids neural reward models for reasoning: they "are susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale reinforcement learning", and retraining them adds cost and complexity. This is the central design decision. Compare the InstructGPT reward model, which must be trusted to stand in for human preference.

The optimiser: GRPO

For each question, GRPO samples a group of G answers from the current policy and scores them, giving rewards r1 to rG. The advantage of answer i is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group's standard deviation (equation 3):

Ai = ( ri − mean(r1..G) ) / std(r1..G)

The update is a clipped, PPO-style objective with a KL penalty to a reference policy (equations 1 and 2). The group replaces the learned critic that PPO uses to estimate a baseline; chapter 14 derives why the group mean is a legitimate baseline. Settings reported for R1-Zero (section 2.1): learning rate 3e-6, KL coefficient 0.001, temperature 1, 16 sampled outputs per question, 32 unique questions per step (a batch of 512), a maximum output of 32,768 tokens before step 8.2k and 65,536 afterwards, and 10,400 steps in total, about 1.6 epochs. Each rollout generates 8,192 outputs, split into 16 mini-batches with a single inner epoch, and the reference model is replaced with the latest policy every 400 steps.

What R1-Zero did

The paper reports (section 2.3) that R1-Zero's AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (71.0% in v1), and to 86.7% with self-consistency decoding. Its average response length grew steadily during training, which the authors read as the model learning "to solve reasoning tasks with more thinking time". They report the emergence of reflective behaviour and show an "aha moment" (Table 2), characterised by a sudden increase in the word "wait" during reflection, where an intermediate checkpoint writes "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here" and restarts its solution. This is the part of the paper most often quoted, so read it carefully: it is a single excerpt from an intermediate version of the model, and the paper's quantitative evidence for the shift is the frequency of the word "wait" (Figure 9) and the accuracy and length curves (Figure 1).

R1-Zero also had problems. The paper lists poor readability and language mixing (the base model is trained mostly on Chinese and English) and says the rule-based RL was narrowly focused on reasoning, leaving writing and open-domain question answering weak.

The mechanism, part 2: the four-stage R1 pipeline

Section 3 of v2 describes how R1 repairs those problems. In order:

  1. Cold start. Collect thousands of long chain-of-thought examples with a conversational, first-person thinking style (Supplementary B.3.2: human annotators converted and verified outputs), and fine-tune V3-Base on them. The authors say the motivation is "primarily product-driven": users find the first-person reasoning more natural. They also warn that vivid human-like reasoning patterns "primarily reflect DeepSeek-engineered heuristics" and may elicit unwarranted trust.
  2. Reasoning RL. Run GRPO as for R1-Zero, now adding a language-consistency reward: the proportion of target-language words in the chain of thought. An ablation in the supplement says this slightly degrades performance, but makes the output more readable.
  3. Rejection sampling and SFT. Sample many answers from the RL checkpoint and keep the correct, readable ones, filtering mixed languages, long paragraphs and code blocks; the supplement counts about 600k reasoning samples. Some prompts, whose answers cannot be checked by a rule, are judged by DeepSeek-V3 against the reference. Add about 200k non-reasoning samples (writing, factual question answering, translation, software engineering) drawn largely from the DeepSeek-V3 pipeline. Fine-tune again on this 800k set.
  4. Final RL. Train on reasoning data with rule-based rewards and on general data with learned reward models, at a lowered temperature of 0.7. It runs 1,700 steps, with the general preference rewards used in only the last 400, because "more training steps with the model based preference reward signal may lead to reward hacking" (Supplementary B.5).

The helpfulness reward model was trained on 66,000 preference pairs generated by prompting DeepSeek-V3 as a judge (each pair scored four times with positions swapped, and kept only if the average score gap exceeded 1); the safety reward model on 106,000 prompts with responses labelled safe or unsafe. So R1 is not "pure RL": the supervised stages and learned reward models come back wherever the task has no cheap checker.

Table 3 reports the intermediate checkpoints. The dip at Dev1 is worth noticing: after the small cold-start fine-tune, AIME 2024 pass@1 fell to 59.0 from R1-Zero's 77.9, which the authors attribute to the limited size of the cold-start set. General-purpose scores moved the other way: IF-Eval (prompt strict) rose from 46.6 for R1-Zero to 83.3 for the final R1, and ArenaHard from 53.6 to 92.3. The final stage mostly bought instruction-following and preference scores rather than further maths, since, in the authors' words, "substantial reasoning-specific RL was done in prior stages".

What it cost

Supplementary B.4.4 and Table 7 give the training cost of the R1 work as 147 thousand H800 GPU-hours, or $294 thousand at an assumed rental price of $2 per GPU-hour: 101 thousand hours ($202 thousand) for R1-Zero, 5 thousand ($10 thousand) to create SFT data and 41 thousand ($82 thousand) for R1. The hours match the stated hardware: 64 × 8 = 512 H800 GPUs for about 198 hours for R1-Zero (512 × 198 is about 101 thousand) and about 80 hours for R1. Read it for what it is. The table covers these RL and SFT stages on top of an already pretrained V3-Base; it does not include pretraining that base model, the preliminary experiments (the paper says it used A100 GPUs for experiments with a 30B-parameter model), or salaries. Secondary coverage of the Nature version describes the same figure as excluding the base-model cost; I did not fetch that article.

The mechanism, part 3: distilling R1 into small models

To give broader access "at a lower energy cost", the authors fine-tuned open-source Qwen and Llama base models on the 800k samples generated with R1, for 2 to 3 epochs (Supplementary B.4.3), using only supervised fine-tuning, no RL, and released the results.

Table 15 reports, for example, a 1.5B Qwen student scoring 28.9 on AIME 2024 pass@1 (GPT-4o-0513 scores 9.3 and Claude-3.5-Sonnet-1022 16.0 in the same table), a 7B student 55.5, and a 32B student 72.6. The paper calls it "remarkable" that a 1.5B model beats the best closed-source models on mathematical benchmarks; note that this is on mathematics benchmarks against non-reasoning models, and the 1.5B model's Codeforces rating in the same table is 954 against R1's 2029 in Table 8.

The paper then asks the natural question: would a small model reach the same level by running R1's large-scale RL itself? Supplementary F.1 compares three 32B models (Table 16): QwQ-32B-Preview at 50.0 on AIME 2024 pass@1, Qwen2.5-32B-Zero (trained with over 10 thousand RL steps) at 47.0, and R1-Distill-Qwen-32B at 72.6; LiveCodeBench is 41.9, 40.2 and 57.2. The authors conclude that distillation is economical and effective, that small models doing RL alone "require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation", and that going beyond the teacher's abilities "may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning". This is the link to chapter 15: RL creates the capability in the large model; supervised learning transfers it more cheaply.

What did not work, and what the paper cautions

  • Small bases failed. Early RL experiments on a 7B dense model and a 16B mixture-of-experts model did not improve on AIME; they repeated themselves as answers grew longer. Gains appeared only with 32B dense, 230B MoE and 671B MoE bases (Supplementary G.1). The recipe depends on a strong base checkpoint.
  • Process reward models and tree search. The authors report trying a process reward model (hard to define a step, hard to label steps, and a learned PRM invites reward hacking) and Monte Carlo tree search (the token search space is exponentially larger than in chess, and a fine-grained value model is hard to train), and say neither paid off in their experiments (G.2). They stress this "does not imply that these approaches are incapable".
  • Verifiers decide where this works. The conclusion says reliable rule-based verifiers made R1-Zero possible and that tasks without them, such as writing, remain an open challenge; R1 falls back on human annotation and only hundreds of RL steps there.
  • Stated limitations. Weak structured output and no tool use; "overthinking" on simple questions; language mixing outside Chinese and English; sensitivity to prompts (few-shot prompting consistently degrades performance); and little improvement over V3 on software engineering benchmarks.
  • Safety. The paper's safety evaluation concludes the model's safety level is generally "moderate", comparable to GPT-4o (2024-05-13), that it can be jailbroken, and that an open model can be fine-tuned to remove protections.

What later work said

Liu et al. (2025), "Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training: A Critical Perspective", raised two points in its abstract (the only part I read). First, base models may already hold the behaviours: they report that DeepSeek-V3-Base already shows an "Aha moment" and that Qwen2.5 base models reason strongly even without a prompt template, which they link to possible pretraining bias. That qualifies the reading of the "aha moment" as something RL created; it may be a behaviour RL selected and amplified. Second, they identify an optimisation bias in GRPO that "artificially increases response length", especially for incorrect answers, and propose Dr. GRPO to remove it, reporting 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B base. Chapter 14 discusses both. The R1 authors' own v2 includes an experiment designed for the first concern: they trained Qwen2-Math-7B (released before the first reasoning model, OpenAI's o1) with about 10,000 policy-gradient steps to check that RL alone can produce reasoning from a base model not exposed to such trajectories, and report that it beat non-reasoning models such as GPT-4o on AIME.

Try it yourself: a verifiable reward and group advantages

The toy below is my own, not DeepSeek's code. It does three things: scores answers with an accuracy-plus-format rule in the style the paper describes; computes GRPO's group-relative advantage for three groups of 16 samples; and trains a policy over four "strategies" with hidden pass rates (0.15, 0.30, 0.55, 0.80, an assumption standing for "how much the model checks its work") using only the group-normalised reward. Lab 4 builds the same ideas properly.

"""Toy: rule-based reward + group-relative advantage (GRPO's Eq. 3) + a 4-strategy policy trained with it. Stdlib only."""
import math, random, re
rng = random.Random(1)

# --- 1. A rule-based reward: accuracy + format, equal weights (as the R1 paper describes) ---
def reward(text, gold):
    fmt = 1.0 if re.fullmatch(r"<think>.+</think><answer>.+</answer>", text, re.S) else 0.0
    m = re.search(r"<answer>(.+?)</answer>", text, re.S)
    acc = 1.0 if m and m.group(1).strip() == gold else 0.0
    return acc + fmt

print(reward("<think>7*8=56</think><answer>56</answer>", "56"))   # right, well formed
print(reward("<think>7*8=54</think><answer>54</answer>", "56"))   # wrong, well formed
print(reward("The answer is 56", "56"))                            # right, but no tags to extract it from

# --- 2. Group-relative advantage: A_i = (r_i - mean) / std ---
def advantages(rs):
    mu = sum(rs) / len(rs); sd = math.sqrt(sum((r - mu) ** 2 for r in rs) / len(rs))
    return [0.0 if sd == 0 else (r - mu) / sd for r in rs]
for name, rs in [("4 of 16 correct", [1]*4 + [0]*12), ("all 16 correct", [1]*16), ("none correct", [0]*16)]:
    a = advantages(rs)
    print(f"{name:16s} largest advantage: {max(a):+.2f}   smallest advantage: {min(a):+.2f}")

# --- 3. Tiny policy: softmax over 4 'strategies' with hidden pass rates; update with group advantages ---
PASS = [0.15, 0.30, 0.55, 0.80]       # assumption: chance each strategy solves a problem (e.g. more checking = better)
theta = [0.0] * 4
def probs():
    z = [math.exp(t) for t in theta]; s = sum(z); return [v / s for v in z]
G, LR = 16, 0.5
for step in range(1, 61):
    p = probs()
    picks = [rng.choices(range(4), p)[0] for _ in range(G)]
    rs = [1.0 if rng.random() < PASS[k] else 0.0 for k in picks]
    adv = advantages(rs)
    grad = [0.0] * 4
    for k, a in zip(picks, adv):
        for j in range(4): grad[j] += a * ((1.0 if j == k else 0.0) - p[j]) / G   # REINFORCE with group baseline
    theta = [t + LR * g for t, g in zip(theta, grad)]
    if step in (1, 10, 20, 40, 60):
        print(f"step {step:2d}: P(strategy)= {[round(v, 2) for v in probs()]}  expected pass rate = {sum(a*b for a, b in zip(probs(), PASS)):.2f}")

Reading the output: a rule gives 2.0 for a correct, well-formed answer, 1.0 for a wrong but well-formed one, and 0 for an answer without the tags, even if its text is right, so the format reward does shape behaviour. When 4 of 16 samples are correct, each correct one gets a large positive advantage (+1.73) and each wrong one a small negative one (−0.58). When all 16 are right or all 16 are wrong, every advantage is zero and the question teaches nothing, which is why the training questions must be neither too easy nor too hard for the current model. Over 60 steps the policy moves almost all of its probability to the strategy with the highest pass rate, with no value network and no human labels.

Try it yourself

Change G to 4 and rerun the training loop with several seeds. How often does the policy converge to the best strategy, and how often does a group with no variance produce no update at all? Then change the pass rates to [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.05] and watch how long learning takes when the reward is rarely non-zero.

The lessons

  • The verifier is the product. The paper's whole argument rests on rewards that cannot be hacked cheaply; where it uses a learned reward it limits it to 400 steps. Building a good checker is often harder than choosing the algorithm (evals chapter 2).
  • RL needs a base that can already sometimes succeed. No success in a group means no advantage and no signal; the paper's failure of small bases shows this in practice.
  • Pure RL gives capability, not manners. R1-Zero reasoned but mixed languages and read poorly; the supervised and preference stages exist for the user, not the benchmark.
  • Distil rather than re-discover. For small models the paper's own comparison favours learning from the large model's traces over running RL again.
  • Treat anecdotes as anecdotes. The "aha moment" is a vivid excerpt. The evidence is the curves and the counts, and the base model's role is still being worked out.

Analogy: a driving instructor with an answer key

Imagine learning to solve puzzles where the only feedback is "right" or "wrong" on the final answer. You attempt each puzzle sixteen times, compare your attempts, and repeat more of whatever the better ones did. Over many puzzles you learn to double-check, because double-checking attempts win more often. Afterwards a student can read your worked solutions and learn the habit far faster than by repeating your trial and error.

Where it breaks down: an answer key only exists for puzzles with checkable answers. Teaching a good essay, a kind reply or a safe refusal has no key, so the pipeline needs human-shaped data and learned judges there, with the reward-hacking risks that brings.

Key takeaways

  • R1-Zero applied GRPO to a pretrained base with accuracy and format rewards and no SFT: AIME 2024 pass@1 went from 15.6% to 77.9% (v2), with longer answers and visible reflection.
  • GRPO's advantage is (reward − group mean) / group std; groups with no variance give no gradient.
  • R1 adds four stages (cold start, reasoning RL, rejection-sampling SFT on about 800k samples, final RL with some learned rewards) to fix readability, language mixing and general ability.
  • The paper reports $294 thousand and 147 thousand H800 GPU-hours for the RL and SFT stages, excluding the pretrained base.
  • SFT on 800k R1 samples gave small models strong maths scores; RL alone on a 32B base did worse than distillation in the authors' comparison.
  • Strong base models, checkable rewards and care with learned rewards were the preconditions; the "aha moment" may be selection, not creation.

Check your understanding

In a GRPO group of 16 sampled answers to one question, all 16 are correct. What gradient signal does that question contribute?

  1. A large positive signal, because every answer was right
  2. A small positive signal proportional to the reward
  3. None: every advantage is zero, because each reward equals the group mean
  4. A negative signal, because the question is too easy
The advantage is the reward minus the group mean, divided by the group's standard deviation. With identical rewards the numerator is zero for every sample, and the toy (like common implementations) treats a zero standard deviation as a zero advantage, so nothing is learned from that question. The same holds if all 16 are wrong.

The R1 authors avoid neural reward models for reasoning RL. What is their stated reason?

  1. Neural reward models are susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale RL, and retraining them adds cost and complexity
  2. Neural reward models cannot read mathematics
  3. GRPO cannot be used with a learned reward
  4. Rule-based rewards are always more accurate than human judgement
The paper gives the reward-hacking and pipeline-complexity reasons. GRPO works with any scalar reward, and R1 does use learned rewards for general helpfulness and safety, but only in the final stage and, for preferences, only the last 400 of 1,700 steps. Rules are reliable for checkable tasks, not for all tasks.

On the paper's Table 16 comparison of three 32B models, what does the result for Qwen2.5-32B-Zero versus R1-Distill-Qwen-32B suggest?

  1. RL on a small model always beats distillation
  2. Distillation only works if the student also does RL afterwards
  3. The two methods performed the same on AIME 2024
  4. For a 32B student, distilling R1's outputs (72.6 on AIME 2024) beat large-scale RL on the same size of base (47.0) in the authors' experiment
The authors conclude that distillation is both cheaper and stronger for smaller models, while noting that pushing beyond current teachers may need larger bases and more RL. The distilled models in the paper received SFT only, with no RL stage.

A blog post claims "R1-Zero proves that reasoning can be created from nothing by RL." Which caveat is best supported by the sources in this case?

  1. None: the paper shows RL created reasoning in every model size tried
  2. The RL started from a strong pretrained base, small bases failed to improve in the authors' own tests, and Liu et al. report that the base model already shows "aha"-like behaviour
  3. R1-Zero used human-written reasoning traces for SFT first
  4. R1-Zero reported no benchmark improvement
R1-Zero had no SFT stage, which rules out the third option, and its AIME score rose substantially. But the paper's Supplementary G.1 reports failures on 7B and 16B models, and the critical perspective suggests RL may amplify behaviours present in the base.
