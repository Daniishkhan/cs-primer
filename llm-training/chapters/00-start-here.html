The model you talk to in a chat window is the last of several models, each made from the one before by a different kind of training. This course follows that life from raw text to a served, evaluated model, and this page gives you the map: the three kinds of model, the stages that make each, and how to study the rest.

Why this matters

Most people who use language models meet only the end product and treat the rest as a black box. An applied AI engineer cannot afford that. Deciding whether to fine-tune or prompt, why a model refuses, why a change made it worse at something else, what a million tokens costs, or whether a small model can do the job: all of these depend on knowing which stage of training put a behaviour there. After this page you will know the vocabulary, the order of the stages, and where each is taught.

Three kinds of model

The same architecture, the decoder-only Transformer from A2, shows up as three different products. What separates them is not the network but the training that came after.

Base model

Trained only to predict the next token over a huge amount of text (the language-modelling objective). It is a very good continuer of text. Give it a question and it may answer, or continue with another question, or write a forum thread, because all of those occur in its training data.

Chat (instruction-tuned) model

A base model trained further on examples of conversations and on human preferences, so that it treats your text as a request and replies helpfully, in a consistent role, and declines some things. This is what a chat assistant is.

Reasoning model

A model further trained, mostly by reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked, to produce a long chain of intermediate reasoning before its answer. It spends more tokens, and so more compute, per question.

Three terms recur in the course, so fix them now. Pretraining is the first, most expensive stage: next-token prediction on trillions of tokens, producing the base model. Post-training is everything after: fine-tuning on demonstrations, learning from preferences, reinforcement learning, distillation. Inference or serving is running the finished model to produce answers for users.

The life of a model

A taste of the core idea

The surprising fact about this pipeline is how little changes between stages. Pretraining and fine-tuning optimise the same loss: predict the next token. What changes is the text. Below is a deliberately tiny "language model": a table of which word follows each three-word context, with greedy completion. It is nothing like a Transformer, but it shows how the data alone changes what a model does.

from collections import Counter, defaultdict

def train(text, n=4):
    """A word-level n-gram 'language model': counts which word follows each (n-1)-word context."""
    words = text.split()
    table = defaultdict(Counter)
    for i in range(len(words) - n + 1):
        table[tuple(words[i:i + n - 1])][words[i + n - 1]] += 1
    return table

def complete(table, prompt, n=4, max_new=14):
    words = prompt.split()
    for _ in range(max_new):
        ctx = tuple(words[-(n - 1):])
        if ctx not in table: break
        nxt = table[ctx].most_common(1)[0][0]          # greedy: most likely next word
        if nxt == "<end>": break
        words.append(nxt)
    return " ".join(words)

# Stage 1, "pretraining": the model sees ordinary text, here a page of quiz questions
pretrain = """what is the capital of france ? what is the capital of spain ? what is the capital of italy ?
what is the capital of norway ? what is the capital of japan ?"""
base = train(pretrain)
print("BASE  :", complete(base, "what is the capital of france ?"))

# Stage 2, "supervised fine-tuning": same objective, but the text is now written as conversations
sft = """<user> what is the capital of france ? <assistant> paris . <end>
<user> what is the capital of spain ? <assistant> madrid . <end>
<user> what is the capital of italy ? <assistant> rome . <end>"""
chat = train(pretrain + " " + sft)
prompt = "<user> what is the capital of spain ? <assistant>"
print("CHAT  :", complete(chat, prompt))
print("unseen:", complete(chat, "<user> what is the capital of japan ? <assistant>"))
BASE  : what is the capital of france ? what is the capital of france ? what is the capital of france ?
CHAT  : <user> what is the capital of spain ? <assistant> madrid .
unseen: <user> what is the capital of japan ? <assistant>

The base model, asked a question, does what its training text did after a question: it produces another question (and, being greedy, loops). After seeing a few conversation-shaped examples with a marker such as <assistant>, the same mechanism answers. That is supervised fine-tuning (SFT), covered in chapter 10, and the markers are the chat template.

The last line is the honest part. The toy answers only questions it has seen: it has no way to generalise, so for "japan" it stops. A real model does generalise from a few thousand or million examples, because it has already built rich internal representations during pretraining, and fine-tuning mostly teaches it which behaviour to select. Why that works, and where it fails, is a thread running through Part 4.

Common misconception

"Chat models are trained from scratch on conversations." Almost never. Pretraining takes the overwhelming majority of the compute; the post-training stages that make a model a helpful assistant use a tiny fraction of the data and compute. This is why post-training is accessible to small teams (you can fine-tune on one GPU), and why a model's knowledge and its biases come mainly from pretraining, while its manners and format come from post-training.

Key takeaways

  • There are three kinds of model: base (continues text), chat (follows requests) and reasoning (thinks at length before answering). The architecture is the same; the training differs.
  • Pretraining makes the base model and uses almost all the compute. Post-training (fine-tuning, preferences, RL, distillation) shapes behaviour with far less.
  • Every stage optimises something like next-token prediction or a reward on generated tokens; the data decides the behaviour.
  • The course runs: pretraining systems, RL foundations, post-training, cheap adaptation, inference, evaluation.
  • Labs are offline and deterministic; estimates with numbers come before experiments.

Check your understanding

In the toy example, why does the base model respond to a question with another question?

  1. It does not understand English
  2. Its training text had questions followed by questions, and next-token prediction reproduces the pattern in its data
  3. Greedy decoding always repeats the prompt
  4. It was trained with a different loss from the chat model
Both models use the same counting objective; the difference is the data. A base model continues text in the style it was trained on. Greedy decoding loops here only because the context points back to itself.

Which stage uses the largest share of the compute in building a typical chat assistant?

  1. Supervised fine-tuning
  2. Preference optimisation
  3. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
  4. Pretraining
Pretraining processes trillions of tokens; post-training stages use orders of magnitude fewer. That is why a small team can post-train an open base model but rarely pretrain a frontier one.

A colleague says: "The chat model knows facts about chemistry because we fine-tuned it on chat data." What is the most likely truth?

  1. Most factual knowledge came from pretraining; fine-tuning mainly taught the format and behaviour for using it
  2. Fine-tuning stores each fact as a new rule
  3. The facts came from the reward model
  4. The model looks the facts up at run time
Fine-tuning on a modest set of conversations cannot supply the breadth of knowledge a model shows. It selects and shapes behaviour from what pretraining built. Retrieval at run time is a different design, not what the base network does.

What distinguishes a reasoning model from a chat model, as the course uses the terms?

  1. It has a different architecture with recurrent layers
  2. It is always larger
  3. It is trained, largely with RL on checkable problems, to produce long intermediate reasoning before the answer, spending more tokens per question
  4. It is a base model without any post-training
The architecture is the same Transformer. The difference lies in the post-training stage (chapter 14) and shows up in serving cost: more generated tokens per answer.
