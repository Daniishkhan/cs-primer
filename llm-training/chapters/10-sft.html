Supervised Fine-Tuning

A pretrained model continues text; it does not answer questions. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the first and simplest post-training step: show the model examples of the behaviour you want and train it to reproduce them. This chapter covers what the data looks like, how a conversation becomes tokens, which tokens the loss may see, how to avoid wasting compute, and why a model can get better at your task while getting worse at everything else.

Why this matters

SFT is the stage almost every deployed model passes through, the starting point for the preference and reinforcement-learning stages of chapters 12 to 14, and the cheapest way to adapt a model to your own domain. It is also where careless engineering does the most silent damage: a template that differs between training and serving, a loss that includes the user's words, a normalisation that quietly favours long answers, a learning rate that erases what the model knew. None of these crash the run. After this chapter you will be able to read an SFT pipeline and name where each of those bugs could hide, estimate what a run costs, and choose a recipe that adds a skill without removing others.

What SFT is

Supervised fine-tuning continues training a pretrained language model on a dataset of demonstrations: prompts paired with the responses you want. The objective is the same one used in pretraining (chapter 14 of A2): maximise the probability of the next token given the previous ones. For a prompt x and a target response y = (y1, …, yT), the loss is the negative log-likelihood, a sum of cross-entropies over the response tokens:

LSFT(θ) = − ∑t=1T log πθ( yt | x, y1, …, yt−1 )

Nothing about the maths is new. What changes is the data (curated demonstrations instead of web text) and the mask (loss only on the response). In reinforcement-learning terms (chapter 6), SFT is behavioural cloning: the policy is trained to imitate an expert's actions, with no reward and no exploration. That is both its strength and its limit. It is stable, cheap and easy to debug. But a model trained only by imitation can never be better than its demonstrations, and it learns to copy the *surface* of good answers, including their confident tone when it does not know the facts, a point we return to under misconceptions.

What SFT changes and what it does not

A base model already contains most of its knowledge and skills, learned from trillions of tokens. What it lacks is a format: asked "What is the capital of France?" it may continue with more quiz questions, because that is what such text is followed by on the web. SFT teaches the format: take the role of an assistant, answer, stop. Zhou et al. (2023), in "LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment", fine-tuned a 65B-parameter model on only 1,000 carefully curated prompts and responses, with no reinforcement learning and no human-preference modelling, and report that its responses were equivalent to or preferred over GPT-4's in 43% of cases in their human study. They conclude that almost all knowledge is learned in pretraining and that little instruction data is needed to produce high-quality output, a view often called the superficial alignment hypothesis.

That hypothesis is true of style and format and incomplete as a statement about capability. Larger and more varied SFT sets measurably add skills such as coding, mathematics and tool use; Lambert et al. (2024) release the full open recipe of this kind in "Tulu 3: Pushing Frontiers in Open Language Model Post-Training", in which SFT is the first of three stages, followed by DPO and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (chapters 12 and 14). A practical reading: a small, clean dataset is enough to change how a model behaves, and a larger, broader one is needed to change what it can do.

The data

An SFT example is a conversation: an optional system message, one or more user turns, and the assistant turns to be learned. Four sources dominate, usually mixed.

Quality beats quantity, up to a point

Chen et al. (2023, "AlpaGasus") started from the 52,000-example Alpaca dataset, scored each example with a strong model, kept about 9,000, and report that a model trained on the filtered subset outperformed the original Alpaca under GPT-4 judging and trained 5.7 times faster. Together with LIMA this gave the field a rule of thumb: a few thousand clean, diverse examples beat tens of thousands of noisy ones. Two caveats keep it honest. Both studies judged outputs largely with a strong model, which has biases of its own (chapter 23 and A6 return to them), and "quality" was defined by the filter, so you inherit its taste. And the rule has a ceiling: when the aim is a skill (a new language, a specialist domain, long-form reasoning) rather than a style, volume and coverage start to matter again.

Concrete checks that pay for themselves on any SFT dataset:

  • Duplicates. Exact and near-duplicate prompts inflate some behaviours and let eval leakage through; use the hashing methods of the chapter 1 lab.
  • Decontamination. Remove examples that overlap your evaluation sets, or your scores measure memorisation (chapter 23).
  • Response length and style. If 90% of answers begin "Certainly!", the model will. Look at 50 random examples by eye; no metric substitutes.
  • Refusals and sycophancy. Over-refusal and flattery in the demonstrations become behaviour (see case study C6 for what flattery at scale did).
  • Privacy and licences. Customer tickets, medical notes and scraped forum text contain personal data (data protection), and outputs of some hosted models are covered by terms that restrict training competitors.

From conversation to tokens: chat templates

The model sees a flat sequence of tokens, not a list of messages. A chat template is the fixed recipe that turns a message list into that sequence, using special tokens that mark where each turn starts and ends. A widely copied format is ChatML, used here as an illustration; other families use different markers. The template is a contract: the exact characters at training time must match the exact characters at serving time. We render a five-message conversation and show which tokens the loss will count.

import re
# Render a conversation with a ChatML-style template, then mark which tokens the loss will see.
MESSAGES = [
    {"role": "system", "content": "You are a concise assistant."},
    {"role": "user", "content": "Capital of France?"},
    {"role": "assistant", "content": "Paris."},
    {"role": "user", "content": "And Italy?"},
    {"role": "assistant", "content": "Rome."},
]

def render(messages):
    """Return the text and the character spans of assistant replies (reply plus <|im_end|>)."""
    text, spans = "", []
    for m in messages:
        text += f"<|im_start|>{m['role']}\n"
        start = len(text)
        text += f"{m['content']}<|im_end|>"
        if m["role"] == "assistant":
            spans.append((start, len(text)))
        text += "\n"
    return text, spans

text, spans = render(MESSAGES)
print(text)

# Toy tokeniser: special tokens, words and punctuation (a real one uses subwords).
toks = [(m.group(), m.start()) for m in re.finditer(r"<\|[a-z_]+\|>|\w+|[^\w\s]", text)]
trained = [any(s <= pos < e for s, e in spans) for _, pos in toks]
IGNORE = -100
labels = [tok if t else IGNORE for (tok, _), t in zip(toks, trained)]

print(" ".join(f"[{tok}]" if t else tok for (tok, _), t in zip(toks, trained)))
print(f"\n{len(toks)} tokens, {sum(trained)} in the loss ({100 * sum(trained) / len(toks):.0f}%)")
i = [k for k, t in enumerate(trained) if t][0]
print(f"first trained position {i}: the model sees ... {toks[i-2][0]} {toks[i-1][0]} and must predict {labels[i]!r}")

first trained position 18: the model sees ... <|im_start|> assistant and must predict 'Paris'

Three details in that output decide whether the model works in production.

  1. The turn terminator is trained. The final <|im_end|> of each assistant reply is inside the loss (the bracketed tokens). That is how the model learns to stop. If the template or the mask omits it, the model never learns to emit it, and generation runs on until the length limit. For some base models the end-of-sequence token of the tokeniser differs from the template's turn terminator, and TRL's documentation warns to align them (for example eos_token="<|im_end|>" for a Qwen base model with ChatML).
  2. The role header is not trained. At serving time the system appends the header (<|im_start|>assistant) itself as the "generation prompt" and the model continues from there, so the header is context, not something the model must produce. In the output, assistant sits just before [Paris] and is unbracketed.
  3. Special tokens may be new. A base model whose vocabulary has no ChatML tokens must learn their embeddings from scratch; the embedding matrix is resized and those rows start random. This is why TRL's chat_template_path option comes with a warning that tokens used by the template must be added to the tokeniser and the embeddings resized.

In practice you do not write the rendering by hand. Hugging Face tokenisers carry a template and apply it with apply_chat_template:

from transformers import AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("your-org/your-base-model")
messages = [{"role": "user", "content": "Capital of France?"}]

# Serving: end with the open assistant header so the model writes the reply.
prompt = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, tokenize=False, add_generation_prompt=True)

# Training: the full conversation, including the assistant reply and its terminator.
messages.append({"role": "assistant", "content": "Paris."})
full = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, tokenize=False)

Common misconception

"Fine-tuning teaches the model new facts." SFT mostly teaches how to use what pretraining stored. Gekhman et al. (2024, "Does Fine-Tuning LLMs on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations?") report that examples introducing facts the model did not already know are learned more slowly, and that once learned they linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate; they conclude that models acquire factual knowledge mainly in pretraining. If your product depends on facts the base model lacks (your price list, last week's policy), retrieval (A5) is the dependable route; SFT is for behaviour, format and style.

Template mismatch: the silent failure

Train with one template and serve with another and the model sees a prompt format it has never been rewarded on. The outputs usually still look like English, so nothing errors; quality simply drops. The classic variants: a double beginning-of-sequence token because both the template and the tokeniser add one; a missing newline after the role header; a system prompt placed in a different position; and serving through an API that applies its own template on top of a prompt you formatted yourself. The defence is mechanical: store the exact rendered training string for a few examples, then in the serving stack render the same conversation and compare strings byte for byte.

Loss masking: which tokens learn

In a training sequence the user's words are inputs, not behaviour to copy. If the loss includes them, part of the gradient goes to making the model better at writing user questions, which is wasted capacity at best and, at worst, trains the assistant to ask questions in the user's voice. The standard practice is therefore to compute the loss only on assistant tokens by setting the label of every other position to a reserved value, −100 in PyTorch and Hugging Face, which the cross-entropy function skips (its ignore_index).

The mechanics have one subtlety. Language-model training scores the prediction at position t against the token at position t+1. Libraries shift labels by one inside the loss, so labels is the same length as input_ids, with −100 where nothing should be learned. In the example, the model's output at the position of assistant is scored against the label Paris; the output at Paris against .; and so on. Step through one example, from messages to the gradient.

Step through

One SFT example, from messages to loss

  1. Messages. Your dataset stores structured turns, not text. Keeping them structured lets you change the template later without redoing the data.
  2. Template. The tokeniser's chat template turns the messages into one string. Both turns end with the terminator; the assistant header is plain text here.
  3. Tokenise. The string becomes token ids. Real tokenisers split words into subwords; we use whole words so the picture stays readable. The special markers are single tokens.
  4. Labels. A copy of the ids, with −100 on every position the loss must ignore. Only the reply and its terminator keep their values.
  5. Shift. The model reads the whole sequence in one forward pass, but the output at each position is compared with the next token's label. The output at assistant is scored against Paris., the output at Paris. against the terminator.
  6. Loss and update. Cross-entropy is averaged over the unmasked positions. The prompt still influences the loss by being read (attention), but nothing teaches the model to write it.

Masking is a default, not a law

Shi et al. (2024, "Instruction Tuning With Loss Over Instructions") report that also computing the loss over the instruction often improves results, mainly by reducing overfitting, and most clearly when instructions are long and outputs short or when the training set is small, across 21 benchmarks. So "mask the prompt" is the right default, and "test the alternative when your data is prompt-heavy" is the right habit.

In TRL, which is the usual high-level library for this, the choices map onto configuration (documentation fetched October 2026): for a prompt-completion dataset the loss is on the completion only by default (completion_only_loss); for a conversational dataset, assistant_only_loss=True restricts the loss to assistant messages, and requires the chat template to mark the assistant spans with {% generation %} and {% endgeneration %} keywords (TRL patches known model families automatically). Multi-turn data is normally trained on every assistant turn, as in the example above, which gives more learning signal per conversation.

Normalising the loss: a quiet choice with consequences

When a batch contains examples of different lengths, "average the loss" is ambiguous. Average over every unmasked token in the batch (the token-mean), or average each example's loss first and then average those (the example-mean)? They weight things differently, as a two-example calculation shows.

# Two examples in one batch: a short answer (5 trained tokens) and a long one (95 tokens).
# Suppose the model's mean per-token loss is 2.0 on the short one and 1.0 on the long one.
n = [5, 95]
mean_loss = [2.0, 1.0]
tokens_total = sum(n)

# (a) token-mean over the whole batch: every token counts equally
token_mean = sum(m * c for m, c in zip(mean_loss, n)) / tokens_total
# (b) mean of per-example means: every example counts equally
example_mean = sum(mean_loss) / len(n)
print(f"token-mean over the batch : {token_mean:.3f}")
print(f"mean of per-example means : {example_mean:.3f}")

# Weight each token's loss carries in the gradient, as a fraction of the total
for name, w in (("token-mean", [c / tokens_total for c in n]),
                ("example-mean", [0.5, 0.5])):
    per_tok = [wi / c for wi, c in zip(w, n)]
    print(f"{name:13}: one token of the short answer has weight {per_tok[0]:.4f}, "
          f"one token of the long answer {per_tok[1]:.4f}  (ratio {per_tok[0] / per_tok[1]:.0f}x)")

# Gradient accumulation over 2 micro-batches of 1 example: averaging the micro-batch means
# gives the example-mean, NOT the token-mean the single big batch would have used.
accum = (mean_loss[0] + mean_loss[1]) / 2
print(f"2 micro-batches averaged   : {accum:.3f}  (differs from {token_mean:.3f} by {accum - token_mean:+.3f})")
token-mean over the batch : 1.050
mean of per-example means : 1.500
token-mean   : one token of the short answer has weight 0.0100, one token of the long answer 0.0100  (ratio 1x)
example-mean : one token of the short answer has weight 0.1000, one token of the long answer 0.0053  (ratio 19x)
2 micro-batches averaged   : 1.500  (differs from 1.050 by +0.450)

With token-mean, every token matters equally, so long answers dominate the gradient. With example-mean, a token in a 5-token answer counts 19 times as much as a token in a 95-token one, so short answers dominate. Neither is wrong; they encode different preferences, and the choice shifts the length distribution of what the model writes. What *is* a bug is getting a different answer from the same data depending on how you split a batch. The last line shows it: averaging two single-example micro-batches gives 1.500 where the token-mean over the whole batch is 1.050. In October 2024 Unsloth reported that naive gradient accumulation was not equivalent to full-batch training in this way, and Hugging Face published "Fixing Gradient Accumulation" the next day, stating that the correct loss is the total over all micro-batches divided by the total number of non-padding tokens. Reports since then say regressions of this kind have recurred in the ecosystem, so when you change the number of devices or accumulation steps and your loss curve shifts, suspect normalisation first.

Packing: not computing on padding

Training examples have wildly different lengths. A GPU batch is a rectangle, so shorter examples are padded to the longest in the batch, and the padded positions cost compute and memory while teaching nothing. Real conversation lengths are long-tailed, so the waste is large. A simulation: 4,000 examples with a log-normal length distribution (mean 479 tokens, a few reaching the 2,048-token limit), batches of 8.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
MAXLEN, BATCH = 2048, 8
lengths = np.clip(rng.lognormal(mean=5.8, sigma=0.9, size=4000).astype(int), 16, MAXLEN)   # token counts
print(f"{len(lengths)} examples, mean length {lengths.mean():.0f}, median {np.median(lengths):.0f}, max {lengths.max()}")

# (a) pad every batch of 8 random examples to its longest member
order = rng.permutation(len(lengths))
padded = 0
for i in range(0, len(order), BATCH):
    b = lengths[order[i:i + BATCH]]
    padded += b.max() * len(b)
print(f"padding: {100 * (1 - lengths.sum() / padded):.0f}% of the positions computed are padding")

# (b) pack examples into 2048-token rows, first-fit decreasing
rows = []
for L in sorted(lengths, reverse=True):
    for r in rows:
        if r[0] + L <= MAXLEN:
            r[0] += L; break
    else:
        rows.append([L])
print(f"packing: {len(rows)} rows of {MAXLEN}, {100 * (1 - lengths.sum() / (len(rows) * MAXLEN)):.1f}% padding")
4000 examples, mean length 479, median 335, max 2048
padding: 61% of the positions computed are padding
packing: 936 rows of 2048, 0.1% padding

Roughly 61% of the positions computed in the padded scheme are padding. Packing concatenates several short examples into each fixed-length row until it is nearly full, here cutting the padding to a tenth of a percent. (Grouping similarly sized examples into the same batch recovers part of the waste without packing; the random batches here are the worst case.)

TRL's SFTConfig(packing=True) turns packing on, with strategies "bfd" (best-fit decreasing, the default, which truncates overflow), "bfd_split" (splits overflowing examples across rows) and "wrapped" (aggressive, cuts mid-sequence). Per the documentation, with "bfd" padding-free batching is enabled automatically, and padding-free mode is supported only with FlashAttention 2 or 3, which can process the flattened rows without cross-example attention. Check that your attention implementation actually honours boundaries before trusting a packed run; the failure is invisible in the loss.

Hyperparameters and cost

SFT hyperparameters are gentler than pretraining ones, because the model is already good and you want to nudge it.

  • Learning rate. Far lower than pretraining. TRL's SFT default is 2e-5 for full fine-tuning; adapter methods (chapter 11) train new, randomly initialised parameters and use around 1e-4 (TRL's documentation suggests about 1e-4 for adapters). Too high is the quickest route to forgetting (below).
  • Epochs. One to three passes. Beyond that, models memorise the demonstrations: training loss keeps falling while held-out loss rises and outputs become formulaic. TRL defaults to 3.
  • Schedule. A short warmup then linear or cosine decay (see optimisers in A0).
  • Batch size, in tokens. Effective batch is micro-batch × accumulation steps × devices. Because examples vary in length, think in tokens per update, and keep normalisation consistent (previous section).
  • Maximum length. Examples longer than the limit are truncated (TRL's default is 1,024 tokens and it keeps the start), which silently cuts off the end of long answers, including the terminator, so check the truncation rate.

Cost is easy to estimate with the 6ND rule from A2 chapter 16: about 6 FLOPs per parameter per training token. A worked case: fine-tune a 7-billion-parameter model on 20,000 examples of 600 tokens for 3 epochs, on an H100 whose dense bf16 peak is taken as 989 TFLOPS (half of the 1,979 TFLOPS "with sparsity" that NVIDIA lists) at an assumed 40% utilisation.

# How long does an SFT run take, and does it fit?  All hardware numbers are assumptions stated here.
N = 7e9                     # parameters
examples, avg_tokens, epochs = 20_000, 600, 3
D = examples * avg_tokens * epochs
flops = 6 * N * D           # the 6ND rule for forward + backward (A2, chapter 16)
peak = 989e12               # H100 SXM dense bf16: half of NVIDIA's 1,979 TFLOPS "with sparsity" figure
mfu = 0.40                  # assumed fraction of peak achieved
secs = flops / (peak * mfu)
print(f"training tokens D = {D/1e6:.0f} M, compute = {flops:.2e} FLOPs")
print(f"on one H100 at {mfu:.0%} utilisation: {secs/60:.0f} minutes ({secs/3600:.1f} GPU-hours)")
print(f"model states for full fine-tuning at 16 bytes/param: {16*N/1e9:.0f} GB  (one GPU holds 80 GB)")
print(f"minimum GPUs just to hold them, ignoring activations: {16*N/80e9:.2f} -> {int(-(-16*N//80e9))}")
training tokens D = 36 M, compute = 1.51e+18 FLOPs

The compute is modest, about an hour of one GPU. The memory is the obstacle: the 16 bytes per parameter of chapter 2 puts the model states alone at 112 GB, more than a single 80 GB card holds, before any activations. Full fine-tuning of a 7B model therefore needs sharding across at least two GPUs (chapter 3), or a method that does not train every weight, which is the subject of chapter 11. For a 24 GB consumer card, full fine-tuning is out of reach above roughly a billion parameters under this accounting (24 GB / 16 bytes is 1.5 billion parameters, before activations).

Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tuning on a narrow dataset can degrade abilities the dataset never mentioned. Catastrophic forgetting is the name for this loss of earlier capability when a network is trained on new data. The cause is plain: all tasks share the same weights, and gradient descent on the new data moves them wherever the new loss decreases fastest, with no regard for what the old behaviour needed. A neural network has no separate storage for "old skills".

A toy shows the whole phenomenon. A small network gets an extra input flag. Skill A (flag 0) is "the sign of x1". Skill B (flag 1) is an XOR-like rule, "x1 and x2 have the same sign". A network that could learn both exists, so there is no capacity conflict. We train on A, then fine-tune on B only, with different recipes.

import numpy as np
# Two "skills" share one small network. Input = (x1, x2, task flag).
# Skill A (flag 0): label = sign(x1).  Skill B (flag 1): label = sign(x1 * x2)  (an XOR-like rule).
def data(task, n, rng):
    x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (n, 2))
    y = (x[:, 0] > 0) if task == 0 else (x[:, 0] * x[:, 1] > 0)
    return np.c_[x, np.full(n, float(task))], y.astype(float)

def init(rng, h=24):
    return {"W1": rng.normal(0, 0.8, (3, h)), "b1": np.zeros(h), "W2": rng.normal(0, 0.8, (h, 1)), "b2": np.zeros(1)}

def forward(p, X):
    H = np.tanh(X @ p["W1"] + p["b1"])
    return H, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(H @ p["W2"] + p["b2"]).ravel()))

def train(p, X, y, lr, steps):
    p = {k: v.copy() for k, v in p.items()}
    for _ in range(steps):
        H, q = forward(p, X)
        g = (q - y)[:, None] / len(y)                       # d loss / d logit for cross-entropy
        gH = (g @ p["W2"].T) * (1 - H ** 2)
        p["W2"] -= lr * H.T @ g;  p["b2"] -= lr * g.sum(0)
        p["W1"] -= lr * X.T @ gH;  p["b1"] -= lr * gH.sum(0)
    return p

def acc(p, task, rng):
    X, y = data(task, 2000, rng)
    return float(np.mean((forward(p, X)[1] > 0.5) == y))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
XA, yA = data(0, 1000, rng); XB, yB = data(1, 1000, rng)
base = train(init(rng), XA, yA, lr=1.0, steps=800)         # "pretrained": skill A only
print(f"{'recipe':36} {'skill A':>8} {'skill B':>8}")
print(f"{'after training on A':36} {acc(base,0,rng):8.3f} {acc(base,1,rng):8.3f}")
mix = np.r_[np.c_[XB, yB][:900], np.c_[XA, yA][:100]]      # 10% replay of A
recipes = {
    "tune on B, lr 1.0":                (XB, yB, 1.0),
    "tune on B, lr 0.2":                (XB, yB, 0.2),
    "tune on B, lr 0.05":               (XB, yB, 0.05),
    "tune on B + 10% A replay, lr 1.0": (mix[:, :3], mix[:, 3], 1.0),
}
for name, (X, y, lr) in recipes.items():
    p = train(base, X, y, lr, 800)
    print(f"{name:36} {acc(p,0,rng):8.3f} {acc(p,1,rng):8.3f}")
recipe                                skill A  skill B
after training on A                     0.996    0.497
tune on B, lr 1.0                       0.600    0.988
tune on B, lr 0.2                       0.705    0.982
tune on B, lr 0.05                      0.943    0.957
tune on B + 10% A replay, lr 1.0        0.980    0.987

Three patterns, each of which recurs at scale.

  1. Step size trades learning against forgetting. At learning rate 1.0, skill B reaches 0.988 and skill A drops from 0.996 to 0.600. At 0.05, A stays at 0.943 but B reaches only 0.957 (and would need more steps to match).
  2. Replay is the most reliable fix. Mixing 10% of the old data into the new restores both skills (0.980 and 0.987). In LLM practice this means including a slice of general instruction data, or the original SFT mixture, in every domain fine-tune.
  3. Where the update lands matters. Methods that restrict how far or in how many directions the weights move forget less. We test this directly in the next chapter.

What the research reports for LLMs, briefly. Luo et al. (2023) studied continual fine-tuning of models from 1B to 7B parameters and found that forgetting got more severe with scale across that range, perhaps because larger models start stronger and so have more to lose. Biderman et al. (2024, "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less") compared LoRA with full fine-tuning on code and mathematics and report that LoRA usually learns less on the target domain but preserves performance outside it better, and forgets less than the usual regularisers weight decay and dropout. Shenfeld, Pari and Agrawal (2025, "RL's Razor") argue that forgetting tracks how far the fine-tuned policy moves from the base policy in KL divergence, and that on-policy reinforcement learning, which stays close to the base model, forgets less than SFT at similar target-task performance. This is the same KL quantity as the leash of chapter 8, now explaining why that leash protects more than the reward model.

A mitigation checklist

  • Lower the learning rate and shorten the run before anything cleverer, and watch held-out loss and a general benchmark, not only the training loss.
  • Replay or mix in general data. A few percent to a few tens of percent of broad instruction data; tune the share against your forgetting measurements.
  • Use a parameter-efficient method (chapter 11) when the new skill is narrow.
  • Interpolate back towards the base. Averaging fine-tuned and original weights often recovers general ability while keeping most of the new skill (model merging, chapter 16).
  • Measure it. Evaluate the old model and the new model on a fixed regression suite of skills you care about, before shipping (chapter 23). Forgetting that nobody measures is forgetting your users discover.

Try it yourself

Run the two scripts, then change them and predict before you look.

$ python3 forgetting.py
$ python3 chat_template.py

  2. Add a recipe that tunes on B with lr 1.0 for only 100 steps. Is a short, aggressive run safer than a long, gentle one?
  3. In chat_template.py, make the span for assistant replies stop before the terminator: change spans.append((start, len(text))) to spans.append((start, len(text) - len("<|im_end|>"))). Which tokens lose their label, and what would the model fail to learn?

Systems connection

An SFT dataset is a build artefact, and the failures above are the familiar failures of unversioned builds. The template contract is an API contract between a producer (training) and a consumer (serving), as in API design: version it, and test both ends against shared examples. The packing mask is an isolation problem like the tenant isolation of multi-tenancy: data from one request must not leak into another's computation. And the gradient-accumulation bug is a reminder from postmortems that the most dangerous bugs are the ones with no error message.

State of play, October 2026

Tooling. TRL's SFTTrainer accepts language-modelling and prompt-completion data, in standard or conversational form; defaults in the documentation fetched in October 2026 include a learning rate of 2e-5, 3 epochs, a maximum length of 1,024, packing=False and the options named above. It integrates PEFT adapters via peft_config and quantised loading via quantization_config (chapter 11). The library reported its 1.0 release on 31 March 2026 (secondary coverage). Practice. Open post-training recipes such as Tulu 3 (2024) publish their SFT data mixtures, which makes them useful baselines to copy and then ablate. Any specific dataset or model name will date quickly; pick data by the checks in this chapter, not by popularity.

Key takeaways

  • SFT is next-token cross-entropy on demonstrations, i.e. behavioural cloning. It mostly teaches format and behaviour; new facts are better supplied by retrieval, and can raise hallucination when forced in.
  • Data quality dominates: LIMA (1,000 curated examples) and AlpaGasus (about 9,000 of 52,000) show how far a small clean set goes. Dedupe, decontaminate, read samples and check licences.
  • The chat template is a contract between training and serving. Train the turn terminator so the model stops; do not train the role header; compare rendered strings byte for byte.
  • Mask the loss with −100 on non-assistant tokens, shift by one, and keep normalisation consistent: token-mean and example-mean disagree (1.050 versus 1.500 in our example), and accumulation must match the single big batch.
  • Packing removes padding (61% down to 0.1% in the simulation) but needs boundary-respecting attention. Full fine-tuning of 7B needs 112 GB for model states.
  • Forgetting is the price of shared weights. Use lower learning rates, replay, parameter-efficient methods and merging, and measure regression on skills you did not train.

Check your understanding

After SFT, a model answers correctly but never stops: it appends invented follow-up questions until the token limit. Which cause is the most likely?

  1. The learning rate was too high
  2. The turn terminator was not inside the loss (masked out, or missing from the template), so the model never learned to emit it
  3. The batch size was too small
  4. The model was trained for too many epochs
Stopping is behaviour learned from a trained terminator token. If it is masked or absent, the model has no reason to produce it. A high learning rate or too many epochs would degrade quality in other ways, not remove one specific token. Check the labels of the last token of each assistant turn.

Which statement about loss masking in a multi-turn SFT example is correct?

  1. Only the final assistant reply should have labels; earlier replies are context
  2. The role header <|im_start|>assistant must be trained so the model learns to speak as the assistant
  3. Masked tokens are removed from the input so the model never sees them
  4. Masked tokens are still read by attention in the forward pass; they just contribute no loss, and every assistant turn can be trained
Labels of −100 only remove positions from the loss; the model still attends to them as context. Training on all assistant turns gives more signal per conversation. The role header is supplied by the serving stack as the generation prompt, so training it is unnecessary.

You change from 1 GPU with batch 16 to 4 GPUs with 4 per GPU, and the loss curve shifts although the data and learning rate are unchanged. Examples vary a lot in length. What should you check first?

  1. Whether the loss is averaged per micro-batch and then averaged across devices, rather than normalised by the total count of unmasked tokens
  2. Whether the GPUs are the same model
  3. Whether the tokeniser changed
  4. Whether weight decay is applied twice
Averaging per-micro-batch means weights examples (or small groups) equally instead of tokens, which differs from the single-big-batch token-mean when lengths vary. This is the gradient accumulation issue Unsloth and Hugging Face described in October 2024. The other options are possible but would not make the result depend on how the batch is split.

A team wants its assistant to answer questions about a product catalogue that changes weekly. They plan to fine-tune on the catalogue. What is the best reasoning?

  1. SFT will store the catalogue reliably, so retrain weekly
  2. SFT cannot learn any facts, so do nothing
  3. Use retrieval for the changing facts and SFT for tone, format and tool behaviour; forcing unknown facts in through SFT is slow and can increase hallucination
  4. Fine-tune once with a very high learning rate so the facts stick
Gekhman et al. report that new-fact examples are learned slowly and then raise hallucination. A weekly-changing catalogue is retrieval's job (A5). Option 4 would also cause forgetting. Option 2 overstates: SFT can add some knowledge, just not dependably or cheaply.

In the forgetting experiment, which change protected skill A while still learning skill B well?

  1. Lowering the learning rate from 1.0 to 0.05
  2. Mixing 10% of the old skill's data into the fine-tuning set at the original learning rate
  3. Training for more steps on B alone
  4. Training only on skill A longer before starting
Replay kept A at 0.980 and B at 0.987. A lower learning rate also protected A (0.943) but B reached only 0.957 in the same number of steps, so it trades one skill for the other. More steps on B alone worsens forgetting; longer training on A does not change what B does to the weights.

Why is packing with a plain causal attention mask risky?

  1. It increases padding
  2. It makes the model see fewer tokens per step
  3. It makes the loss non-differentiable
  4. Tokens in a later example can attend to unrelated earlier examples in the same row, so the model trains on context that will never exist at serving time
Packing removes padding (the opposite of option 1) and increases useful tokens per step. The danger is cross-example attention, fixed by block-diagonal masks or variable-length attention kernels with position ids restarted per example. The leak does not show up as an error, only as slightly worse behaviour.
