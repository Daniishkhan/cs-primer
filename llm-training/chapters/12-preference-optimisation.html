Preference Optimisation: RLHF & DPO

A supervised fine-tuned model imitates the answers it was shown, but nobody can write down a loss for "helpful" or "polite" or "honest". People can, however, look at two answers and say which they prefer. This chapter shows how those comparisons become a training signal, first through the reward model and reinforcement learning pipeline (RLHF), then through Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), which reaches nearly the same place with a single classification loss.

Why this matters

Almost every chat model you have used went through some form of preference tuning after supervised fine-tuning, and the choice between the RL route and the DPO route decides what hardware you need, what can go wrong and how much labelled data you must buy. If you only know the recipes, a DPO run that "works" (loss falls, accuracy rises) while the model gets worse is baffling; if you know the derivation, you can see why. After this chapter you will be able to fit a Bradley-Terry reward from comparisons, derive the DPO loss from the KL-regularised objective of chapter 8, read DPO's logged metrics, say what a pair of preferences actually teaches the model, estimate whether a run fits on a 24 GB card, and choose among DPO's relatives.

Why compare answers instead of scoring them

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches a model by example: here is a prompt, here is a good answer, raise the probability of every token in it. That works when someone can write the good answer, and it has a ceiling: the model can at best learn to imitate its demonstrators. Writing a long, careful, correct answer is slow and expensive; judging one is faster; and for many qualities (tone, helpfulness, how well an explanation lands) nobody can say precisely what the target is.

The alternative is to ask a person to look at two candidate answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. People are more consistent at this than at assigning absolute scores (is this answer a 6 or a 7?), because the question is relative and the two answers share a context. A record of such a choice is a preference pair: a prompt x, a chosen response yw (the winner) and a rejected response yl (the loser). This idea predates language models: Christiano et al. (2017, "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences") trained agents on simulated robotics and Atari tasks from human comparisons of short video clips, and report using human feedback on less than one percent of the agent's interactions with its environment. Stiennon et al. (2020, "Learning to summarize from human feedback") applied the recipe to text, and Ouyang et al. (2022, "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback") made it the standard way to turn a base model into an assistant.

The family of methods that learn from such comparisons is called preference optimisation. The original member is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. DPO and its relatives are later ways of getting the same effect with less machinery. The rest of the chapter is the story of what the preferences are, what they are converted into, and why that conversion can be shortened.

The RLHF pipeline

RLHF has three stages, shown in the figure. Each uses something an earlier chapter built.

  1. Supervised fine-tuning. Train the base model on demonstrations (chapter 10). The result, written πSFT, already follows instructions passably and is the model comparisons are collected from.
  2. Reward modelling. Sample several answers per prompt from πSFT, have people rank them, and train a reward model rφ(x, y): a network that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs one number, higher meaning "people would prefer this". The next section derives how. Chapter 13 is about this component and how it fails.
  3. Reinforcement learning. Treat the reward model as the environment. Sample answers from the policy, score them with rφ, and update the policy with PPO while the KL penalty from chapter 8 keeps it near πSFT, which now plays the role of the reference policy πref.

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) put numbers on this. Its three datasets were about 13,000 training prompts for SFT, 33,000 for the reward model and 31,000 for PPO (the PPO prompts came only from the API, with no written answers needed). Labellers saw between K = 4 and K = 9 answers per prompt and ranked them; the reward model was a 6-billion-parameter network started from the SFT model with its output layer replaced by a scalar head, because the authors found a 175-billion-parameter reward model unstable to train and unsuitable as a value function. The headline result, in their abstract: outputs from the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model were preferred to outputs from the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model with about a hundred times more parameters. Case study C1 reads the paper in detail.

From comparisons to a reward: the Bradley-Terry model

The reward model is trained to predict human choices. To do that we need a model of how a score turns into a choice. The standard one is the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley and Terry, 1952), the same idea behind Elo ratings in chess: every item has a hidden strength, and the probability that item i beats item j depends only on the difference of strengths, passed through the logistic sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z):

P(yw ≻ yl | x) = σ( r(x, yw) − r(x, yl) )

A reward difference of 0 gives 50 percent; a difference of 1 gives 73 percent; a difference of 3 gives 95 percent. Only differences matter, so adding any constant to every reward changes nothing: a reward model's absolute values are meaningless, and that fact returns in the DPO derivation.

Analogy: chess ratings

A chess federation never asks anyone to score a game from 1 to 10. It records who beat whom and fits a rating to each player so that rating differences predict results; a player 200 points above another is expected to win about three games in four. A reward model plays the same role for answers: it is a rating, fitted from head-to-head results, that predicts which of two answers would win.

Where it breaks down: a chess player has one rating, estimated from many games against many opponents. An answer appears in one or two comparisons, so its reward must be predicted from its text by a network that has never seen it, and the network has to guess what the labellers valued from a few thousand to a few hundred thousand examples. Whatever it guesses wrong, the policy will find.

The loss

Given a dataset D of triples (x, yw, yl), the maximum-likelihood fit maximises the probability of the observed choices. Taking the negative log gives the loss InstructGPT used (their equation 1, averaged over the K-choose-2 pairs of a prompt):

LRM(φ) = − E(x, yw, yl) ∼ D [ log σ( rφ(x, yw) − rφ(x, yl) ) ]

This is ordinary logistic regression (see linear and logistic models in A1) in which the input is the reward difference and the label is always "the first one won". Its gradient with respect to the difference is −(1 − σ(Δ)): pairs the model already gets right with a wide margin contribute almost nothing, pairs it gets wrong contribute almost the full unit. We can check all of this on a problem small enough to see. Five candidate answers have hidden rewards 0, 0.5, 1, 2 and 3; we simulate Bradley-Terry choices between random pairs and recover the rewards by gradient descent on the loss above.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_r = np.array([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])          # hidden "human" reward of 5 candidate answers
K = len(true_r)

def sigmoid(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

# 1. what Bradley-Terry says: P(i preferred to j) = sigmoid(r_i - r_j)
print("P(answer 4 beats answer 3) =", round(float(sigmoid(true_r[4] - true_r[3])), 3))
print("P(answer 4 beats answer 0) =", round(float(sigmoid(true_r[4] - true_r[0])), 3))

# 2. simulate N comparisons, then recover rewards by gradient descent on the pairwise loss
def make(n):
    i = rng.integers(0, K, n); j = (i + rng.integers(1, K, n)) % K
    win_i = rng.random(n) < sigmoid(true_r[i] - true_r[j])
    return np.where(win_i, i, j), np.where(win_i, j, i)      # (winner, loser)

def fit(w, l, steps=3000, lr=0.5):
    r = np.zeros(K)
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = sigmoid(r[w] - r[l])                              # model's P(chosen wins)
        g = np.zeros(K)
        np.add.at(g, w, -(1 - p)); np.add.at(g, l, +(1 - p))  # d loss / d r
        r -= lr * g / len(w)
    return r - r.mean()

tw, tl = make(200); w2, l2 = make(20000)
ew, el = make(20000)                                           # held-out
print("\nrecovered rewards (centred), true rewards centred:", np.round(true_r - true_r.mean(), 2))
for n, (w, l) in ((200, (tw, tl)), (20000, (w2, l2))):
    r = fit(w, l)
    acc = np.mean(r[ew] > r[el])
    print(f"N={n:6d}  r_hat={np.round(r,2)}  held-out pair accuracy={acc:.3f}")
oracle = np.mean(true_r[ew] > true_r[el])
print(f"oracle (true rewards) accuracy on the same noisy labels = {oracle:.3f}")

Two things to read from the output. First, the recovered rewards sit close to the true ones once centred (a shift of every reward by the same constant is invisible to the data), and 200 comparisons already get the ordering and the rough gaps. Second, look at the accuracy: even the true reward function agrees with only 79.2 percent of the labels. The labels themselves are noisy, because Bradley-Terry says an answer with a modest advantage loses a real fraction of the time. This is not a flaw in the fit; it is the ceiling. InstructGPT measured the real-world equivalent: its training labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5 percent of the time and held-out labellers 77.3 ± 1.3 percent, close to the 73 percent researchers had seen on summaries. A reward model whose held-out accuracy is 70 to 75 percent is therefore not failing; a figure of 95 percent would be suspicious.

A reward model is a language model with a different head

In practice rφ is a Transformer, usually initialised from the SFT model. Remove the output layer that predicts a token over V words and put a linear layer from the hidden size C to one number. Feed it the prompt followed by the answer and read the scalar from the hidden state of the final token, where attention has seen everything. Training runs each of the K answers of a prompt through once and forms all K-choose-2 pairwise differences from the K scalars, which is cheaper than scoring each pair separately and, InstructGPT found, avoids the overfitting that comes from shuffling correlated pairs into one dataset and passing over it repeatedly. One epoch is the norm; the model overfits after that.

The RL stage, and what it costs

With a reward model in hand, stage 3 maximises, over the policy πθ, the objective from chapter 8:

maximise Ex∼D, y∼πθ(·|x) [ rφ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πθ(·|x) ‖ πref(x) )

InstructGPT added a third term, mixing in the original pretraining loss so that the tuned model did not lose ability on public benchmarks (their "PPO-ptx" variant, with a coefficient γ on the pretraining term). The method works, and it is demanding. Chapter 8's four models in memory are: the policy being trained, the frozen reference, the reward model and a value model (the critic of chapter 9), and every iteration needs fresh samples from the policy, which is slow generation. The script below prices the memory for a 7-billion-parameter shape (Llama 2 7B, 6.74 billion parameters) using chapter 2's 16 bytes per parameter for full training state and chapter 11's LoRA and NF4 accounting. Everything is an estimate and the assumptions are in the comments; the full-tuning rows count model state only, with no activations.

Full PPO needs 36 bytes per parameter across its four models (16 + 16 + 2 + 2), about 243 GB for a 7B model before activations, which means several GPUs and a sharded optimiser (chapter 3). Full DPO needs the policy and a reference (18 bytes per parameter, 121 GB), and drops to 108 GB if the reference log-probabilities are computed once in advance, but still does not fit on one 80 GB card. The LoRA rows are the interesting ones. With LoRA, the frozen base model is the reference: switch the adapter off and you get the reference's log-probabilities from the same weights, so there is no second copy. A 7B DPO run fits on a 24 GB card in bf16 (20 GB at four pairs of 1,024 tokens) and uses about 10 GB with a 4-bit base. GPU capacities here are the vendor's: 24 GB for the RTX 4090 and 80 GB for the A100 and H100 SXM (NVIDIA product pages, as cited in chapter 2).

Systems connection

Stage 3 is a pipeline with a generation half and a training half, and the generation half is a serving problem: it is memory-bound and bursty, exactly the workload chapter 18 and chapter 20 analyse. The labelling half is a data operation with the properties of any human labelling effort: guidelines, agreement rates, adjudication. The 72.6 percent agreement above is the figure you should expect to be defending when someone asks why the reward model is not more accurate.

DPO: skipping the reward model

Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning and Finn (2023, "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model") noticed that the first two RLHF stages followed by the third can be collapsed. The reward model is only an intermediate: its job is to define the objective that the policy then optimises. If the optimum of that objective can be written in closed form, we can substitute it back and train the policy directly on the comparisons. We already have the closed form from chapter 8. The derivation takes five moves; the stepper shows them in order and the text below spells out each.

Step through

Deriving the DPO loss

  1. The objective. Start from what RLHF is trying to do for a fixed prompt: find the policy with the highest expected reward, minus a KL charge for drifting from the reference.
  2. The optimum. Chapter 8 proved that this is solved exactly by the reference re-weighted by exp(r/β), normalised by Z(x) = ∑y πref(y|x) exp(r(x,y)/β). A response with one unit more reward becomes e1/β times more likely relative to another.
  3. Invert. Take logs of step 2 and rearrange. The reward of any response is β times how much the optimal policy has raised its probability over the reference, plus β log Z(x). The second term is the same for every response to the same prompt, and it is the only part that is hard to compute because Z sums over every possible response.
  4. Bradley-Terry cancels the hard part. The preference probability uses a difference of two rewards for the same prompt. Both contain β log Z(x), so it subtracts out. The probability that people prefer yw now depends only on the two log-ratios.
  5. Train on it. We do not know π*, but we are free to propose a policy πθ and ask it to explain the human choices through the formula in step 4. Maximum likelihood on the observed preferences gives the DPO loss. If a policy fits the data perfectly under this loss, it is, by construction, the optimal policy for the reward that best explains the comparisons.

Reading the loss

Define the implicit reward of a response under the current model as

r̂θ(x, y) = β log( πθ(y|x) / πref(y|x) ).

It is zero at the start of training (the policy equals the reference) and grows for responses the model has made more likely than the reference, shrinks for the ones it has made less likely. DPO's loss is the reward-model loss with this quantity in place of a learned network:

LDPO(θ) = − E [ log σ( r̂θ(x, yw) − r̂θ(x, yl) ) ]

So the language model is its own reward model, which is the paper's title. The log-probability of a whole response is the sum of its token log-probabilities, so the loss needs one forward pass of the policy and one of the reference over each of the two responses, and nothing else. The reference log-probabilities do not change during training, which is why they can be computed once and cached.

Go deeper the gradient, and why the weighting matters

Write m = r̂θ(yw) − r̂θ(yl) for the margin. Since d/dm [ −log σ(m) ] = −(1 − σ(m)) = −σ(−m), the chain rule gives

∇θ L = −β · σ(−m) · [ ∇θ log πθ(yw|x) − ∇θ log πθ(yl|x) ].

The bracket says: raise the log-probability of the chosen response and lower that of the rejected one. The scalar in front, σ(−m) = σ(r̂l − r̂w), says how hard: close to 1 when the implicit rewards rank the pair the wrong way, close to 0 when they already rank it correctly by a wide margin. The authors stress that this weighting is essential. A naive version that always pushes the chosen response up and the rejected one down, with weight 1, degenerates, because the model can lower the rejected probability without bound. Weighting by how wrong the model currently is makes each pair self-limiting: as soon as it is ranked correctly the pair stops pulling. This is the DPO counterpart of PPO's clip.

Note what the gradient does not say: it never says that the chosen response's probability must rise in absolute terms. Only the difference of the two log-ratios is constrained. We return to this in "Where DPO goes wrong".

A check you can run: DPO recovers the reward

The derivation makes a testable claim: after training on enough comparisons, the implicit reward β log(πθ/πref) should equal the reward that generated the preferences, up to a constant, and the policy should equal the reference re-weighted by exp(r/β). We test it on the five-answer problem from the Bradley-Terry script. The "policy" is a softmax over five logits for one prompt, the reference favours the weak answers, 4,000 preference pairs are sampled from the hidden reward, and we train with exact gradients of the DPO loss for two values of β.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
true_r = np.array([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])   # hidden human reward for 5 candidate answers to ONE prompt
K = 5
def sigmoid(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))
def softmax(z): e = np.exp(z - z.max()); return e / e.sum()

ref_logits = np.array([1.5, 1.0, 0.5, 0.0, -0.5])      # the SFT model's taste: favours the weak answers
pi_ref = softmax(ref_logits)

# preference data: pairs of answers, labelled by Bradley-Terry with the hidden reward
N = 4000
i = rng.integers(0, K, N); j = (i + rng.integers(1, K, N)) % K
win_i = rng.random(N) < sigmoid(true_r[i] - true_r[j])
w, l = np.where(win_i, i, j), np.where(win_i, j, i)

def dpo(beta, steps=4000, lr=2.0):
    z = ref_logits.copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        logp = z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum()); logref = ref_logits - np.log(np.exp(ref_logits).sum())
        m = beta * ((logp[w] - logref[w]) - (logp[l] - logref[l]))      # implicit-reward margin
        coef = -(1 - sigmoid(m)) * beta                                  # d loss / d (logp_w - logp_l)
        g_logp = np.zeros(K); np.add.at(g_logp, w, coef); np.add.at(g_logp, l, -coef)
        g_logp /= N
        z -= lr * (g_logp - np.exp(logp) * g_logp.sum())                 # chain rule through log-softmax
    return z, m

for beta in (0.1, 1.0):
    z, m = dpo(beta)
    pi = softmax(z)
    implicit = beta * np.log(pi / pi_ref)
    implicit -= implicit.mean()
    closed = pi_ref * np.exp(true_r / beta); closed /= closed.sum()      # tilt of the reference by the TRUE reward
    print(f"beta={beta}")
    print("  pi_ref         ", np.round(pi_ref, 3))
    print("  pi after DPO   ", np.round(pi, 3))
    print("  closed form    ", np.round(closed, 3), "(pi_ref * exp(r/beta), normalised)")
    print("  implicit reward", np.round(implicit, 2), " true (centred)", np.round(true_r - true_r.mean(), 2))
    print(f"  train pair accuracy {np.mean(m > 0):.3f}")

At β = 1 the implicit rewards, −1.28, −0.84, −0.37, 0.81 and 1.68, are within a few hundredths of the true centred rewards, −1.3, −0.8, −0.3, 0.7 and 1.7. The trained policy differs slightly from the closed form built from the true reward because the 4,000 labels are a finite, noisy sample: the closed form with the estimated reward matches exactly, by construction. At β = 0.1 the same rewards imply a much sharper tilt: all the probability goes to the best answer. A small β means a long leash. That is the whole role of β, and it is why the TRL default of 0.1 (see below) allows large departures from the reference while a value near 1 does not.

Common misconception

"DPO has no reward model, so it cannot be reward-hacked, and it is not reinforcement learning." DPO has a reward model: it is hidden in the policy, as the implicit reward above. What DPO removes is the separate network and the online sampling loop, not the idea of optimising a reward under a KL leash. The preferences still define what is rewarded, so biases in the data (a taste for long answers, say) are still learned and amplified. And because DPO trains on a fixed dataset rather than on the policy's own samples, it can exploit gaps in that dataset in ways PPO would not, as the next sections show.

Running DPO in practice

The mechanics are short. TRL's DPOTrainer expects a dataset with prompt, chosen and rejected columns (plain text or chat messages), and with no reference model passed it uses the model's initial state as the reference. The listing is not run here, because it needs PyTorch and a GPU; on a 24 GB card with a LoRA configuration like the memory estimate above it would download a model and print training logs, whose meaning we cover below.

from datasets import load_dataset
from peft import LoraConfig
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer

dataset = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="train")   # prompt / chosen / rejected

args = DPOConfig(
    output_dir="dpo-out",
    beta=0.1,                    # the leash: smaller lets the policy move further from the reference
    learning_rate=5e-6,          # with LoRA; full tuning uses far smaller values (the default is 1e-6)
    loss_type=["sigmoid"],       # the loss derived above
    per_device_train_batch_size=2,
    gradient_accumulation_steps=8,
    max_length=1024,
    num_train_epochs=1,
)
trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model="Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B",     # start from an SFT model; DPO assumes the reference is already sensible
    args=args,
    train_dataset=dataset,
    peft_config=LoraConfig(r=16, lora_alpha=32, target_modules="all-linear"),
)
trainer.train()

State of play, October 2026

TRL, Hugging Face's post-training library, published its v1.0.0 release on 31 March 2026. The documentation on the project's main branch, read in October 2026, lists these DPOConfig defaults: beta=0.1, learning_rate=1e-6, loss_type=["sigmoid"], max_length=1024, gradient checkpointing on, and a precompute_ref_log_probs switch that caches the reference's log-probabilities so that the reference need not stay in memory. It documents about fifteen alternative loss types (IPO, hinge, robust, length-normalised and others) that can be combined with weights. Secondary summaries of the release say SFT, DPO, reward-model, RLOO and GRPO trainers carry a stability promise while some 75 further methods sit in an experimental namespace; check the release notes for the current list, since defaults and the stable set can change between versions. Tülu 3 (Lambert et al., 2024), an open post-training recipe with released data and code, runs SFT, then DPO, then RLVR (chapter 14), which is a fair picture of how the stages are stacked in current open recipes.

What to watch while it trains

The trainer logs the implicit rewards directly, so the loss curve is not the only instrument. The documentation defines rewards/chosen and rewards/rejected as the average of β log(πθ/πref) over each side, rewards/margins as their difference and rewards/accuracies as the fraction of pairs where the chosen implicit reward is higher. Read them together.

  • Margins rise and accuracies climb towards the 70 to 80 percent range on real, noisy data: the model is fitting the preferences. Accuracy near 100 percent on the training set means memorisation, not alignment.
  • Check logps/chosen against logps/rejected. Both usually fall, the rejected one faster. If the chosen log-probability falls steeply, the margin is being won by pushing things down, not by making good answers likelier (next section).
  • Evaluate generations, not just the metrics: sample the same twenty prompts before and after, and watch average length. A model that wins its pairs by being longer is learning the labellers' taste for length, not quality.

Where DPO goes wrong

Likelihood displacement. Because the loss only constrains the difference of the two log-ratios, the chosen response's probability can fall as long as the rejected one's falls more. Razin et al. (2024, "Unintentional Unalignment: Likelihood Displacement in Direct Preference Optimization") study this and report that the probability mass can move to responses with the opposite meaning: training a model to prefer "No" over "Never" can raise the probability of "Yes". In a safety experiment they report Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate falling from 74.4 percent to 33.4 percent after training on pairs that were meant to teach refusal. They tie the effect to preference pairs whose two responses have very similar internal representations, and show that filtering the pairs that contribute most reduces it. The practical lesson is that pairs should differ in the way you intend and by enough to be learnable.

Offline data, stale distribution. The preferences were gathered on answers from some model (often a different one). DPO's loss is computed on those fixed answers, not on what the current policy would write, so the policy can drift into regions that no pair ever covered and nothing will tell it. RLHF's sampling loop keeps scoring the policy's own outputs. The remedy is online or iterative DPO: sample new answers from the current policy, label them with a reward model or a judge (see LLM-as-judge), train again.

Overfitting deterministic preferences. If every pair in the data is labelled with certainty, the Bradley-Terry fit wants an infinite margin, and the KL term is the only thing resisting it. Azar et al. (2023, "A General Theoretical Paradigm to Understand Learning from Human Preferences") analyse this and propose IPO, which replaces the logistic loss by a squared one with a fixed target margin so the regulariser stays effective; TRL describes it as the identity transform instead of the logit. In practice the cure is early stopping (one epoch is the norm), small learning rates and label smoothing.

Length. Both RLHF and DPO tend to make answers longer when labellers prefer longer answers. Singhal et al. (2023, "A Long Way to Go: Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF") report that in the three settings they studied most of the reward improvement came from longer responses, and that a purely length-based reward reproduced most of the downstream gains over the SFT model. Chapter 13 reproduces the mechanism in a simulation.

The relatives

Many methods vary DPO. They differ in what they remove, what they assume, and which of the failures above they target. The table states only what the authors' abstracts claim; results are self-reported and depend heavily on the setup, so treat them as hypotheses to test on your task.

Two observations help you choose among them. First, the methods that remove the reference model (ORPO, SimPO) save memory but also remove the explicit leash; you rely on the length normalisation, the margin and early stopping instead, so the failure analysis above applies with extra force. Second, RLOO shows the trend that leads to chapter 14: once you accept online sampling, the critic and the clip are optional extras, and a baseline built from the other samples for the same prompt is often enough.

RLHF or DPO?

The honest summary is that both work and neither dominates. DPO is the better default when you have a fixed preference dataset, limited hardware and want a stable first result: it is a classification loss on cached numbers, with one important hyperparameter (β). Online methods (PPO, RLOO, online DPO) win when the reward signal can be queried on new samples, because they correct the policy where it actually lives, and they are necessary when the reward is a program rather than a dataset, as in the next chapters. The pragmatic sequence in open recipes is SFT, then DPO on a broad preference mixture, then online RL where a verifier exists.

Choose DPO when

You hold a large preference dataset already, one or two GPUs, and no appetite for a PPO tuning project. Start with β = 0.1, one epoch, a small learning rate, and compare generations before and after.

Choose online RL when

The reward can be evaluated on fresh samples (a reward model you trust, a judge, a verifier), the preference data came from a very different model, or you need the policy to explore beyond the data.

Either way

Keep the SFT model as the reference, hold out preferences you never train on, and read samples. Metrics that improve while samples degrade are the signature of optimising the wrong thing.

Key takeaways

  • Preferences are cheaper and more consistent than demonstrations or absolute scores. The Bradley-Terry model turns a reward difference into a choice probability, P = σ(rw − rl), and fitting it is logistic regression.
  • Human labels are noisy: InstructGPT's labellers agreed 72.6 to 77.3 percent of the time, so a reward model at 70 to 75 percent held-out accuracy is near the ceiling.
  • RLHF = SFT, then a reward model, then PPO with a KL leash. It needs four models in memory and generation inside the loop.
  • DPO follows from the KL-regularised optimum π* ∝ πref exp(r/β): invert it for the reward, and Bradley-Terry cancels the intractable normaliser. The result is a classification loss on log-ratios, and the policy is its own implicit reward model.
  • The gradient is weighted by σ(−margin): wrongly ranked pairs pull hard, correctly ranked ones stop. β sets the leash length.
  • DPO constrains only a difference, so chosen log-probabilities can fall (likelihood displacement); it trains offline, so it can drift off the data; and it inherits the labellers' biases, such as length.
  • With LoRA the reference is the frozen base with the adapter off, so a 7B DPO run fits a 24 GB card.

Check your understanding

A reward model assigns reward 2.0 to the answer a labeller chose and 0.5 to the one they rejected. Under the Bradley-Terry model, what probability does the model give to that labeller's choice?

  1. About 0.50
  2. About 0.82
  3. About 0.95
  4. About 0.65
P = σ(2.0 − 0.5) = σ(1.5) = 1 / (1 + e−1.5) = 0.82. A difference of 0 would give 0.50 and a difference near 3 gives about 0.95. Only the difference matters, not the two values themselves.

In the DPO derivation, why can the intractable normaliser Z(x) be ignored?

  1. Because Z(x) equals 1 whenever the reference model is well trained
  2. Because the KL penalty makes Z(x) small
  3. Because it is estimated once from a sample and held fixed
  4. Because Bradley-Terry depends on the difference of the two rewards for the same prompt, and the β log Z(x) term appears in both and cancels
The reward is β log(π*/πref) + β log Z(x), and Z depends only on the prompt. Both responses share the prompt, so the term vanishes from the difference. Z is a sum over all possible responses and is not 1 in general, and nothing is estimated: the cancellation is exact.

You raise β in a DPO run from 0.1 to 0.5, keeping everything else fixed. What is the expected effect?

  1. The trained policy stays closer to the reference, because a larger β makes departures from it costlier
  2. The policy moves further from the reference, because the reward signal is amplified
  3. Nothing, because β only scales the loss
  4. The reference model is no longer needed
The optimum is πref exp(r/β): a larger β flattens the exponent and keeps the policy near the reference, as the β = 1 versus β = 0.1 rows of the toy experiment show. It does not just scale the loss, because it multiplies the log-ratios inside the sigmoid, and the reference is needed at every value.

During a DPO run, rewards/accuracies reaches 0.9 and rewards/margins keeps growing, but logps/chosen has fallen steadily and generated answers read worse. What is the most plausible diagnosis and first response?

  1. The learning rate is too low; raise it
  2. The reference model is too strong; replace it with the base model
  3. Likelihood displacement and over-fitting: the margin is being won by pushing probabilities down; train fewer steps or at a lower rate, and check the pairs for near-duplicate chosen and rejected responses
  4. The reward model has overfitted; retrain it
The loss only constrains the difference of log-ratios, so it can be satisfied by lowering both log-probabilities, the rejected one more. 90 percent accuracy on noisy human labels is also suspiciously high. A higher learning rate would make both worse, and there is no separate reward model in DPO to retrain.

Why can LoRA-based DPO on a 7B model run without a second copy of the model for the reference?

  1. The reference log-probabilities are never needed with LoRA
  2. Turning the adapter off gives the original weights, so the same frozen base serves as the reference
  3. LoRA compresses the reference model to a few megabytes
  4. The reference is replaced by the reward model
The base weights are frozen and the policy differs from them only by the adapter. Disabling the adapter yields πref from the same memory. The reference is still needed (option 1 is wrong), and the adapter is small but the base is not.

Two preference pairs have implicit-reward margins of −2 (ranked wrongly) and +4 (ranked correctly). Roughly how much larger is the DPO gradient weight on the first?

  1. About the same: both have one chosen and one rejected response
  2. About 2 times
  3. About 10 times
  4. About 50 times
The weight is σ(−m): σ(2) = 0.88 for the first and σ(−4) = 0.018 for the second, a ratio of about 49. This concentration of effort on pairs the model currently gets wrong is what makes DPO self-limiting.
