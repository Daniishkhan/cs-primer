Evaluating a Model You Trained

After weeks of work the loss went down and the samples look better. That tells you very little. This chapter is about the measurements that do: held-out sets you can trust, harnesses that make numbers reproducible, checks for contamination, honest error bars on small differences, and the regression suite that catches what fine-tuning quietly broke.

Why this matters

Training can improve exactly the thing you measured while damaging things you did not. A model tuned for a new skill may lose general knowledge, drop its refusals of unsafe requests, or start failing its own output format. And a score can be inflated by the test set having leaked into the data, or by choosing among many variants until one looks good. Teams that skip this step ship regressions and publish numbers that do not replicate. After this chapter you will be able to build an evaluation plan for a tuned model: what to hold out and how, how to run the model reproducibly, how to check for contamination, how to decide whether a difference is real, and which capabilities to re-test.

This chapter is about evaluating your own model, which differs from judging someone else's in a way that matters: you know what went into it, and you have the means to contaminate the test without noticing. The wider craft of evaluation (error analysis, judges, statistics, benchmarks, leaderboards) is the subject of the A6 course, Evals, Guardrails & Safe Operation; here we cover what is specific to the training loop and link out for depth.

What you are trying to learn

An evaluation answers a question, so start with the question. After training, you usually want three things, and each needs a different measurement.

  1. Did the model get better at the target? A task-specific held-out set, scored with a metric that matches the product.
  2. Did it get worse at anything else? A fixed regression suite of capabilities you want to keep, run on the base and the tuned model alike.
  3. Can we believe the numbers? Evidence that the test was not seen in training, that the comparison is fair, and that the difference is bigger than noise.

Training loss and validation loss (A2 chapter 14) are useful monitors but are not answers to these questions: loss measures how well the model predicts the next token of text like its training data, not whether its answers are correct, safe, or well formatted. A fine-tuned model's loss on its training distribution almost always falls.

Held-out sets: constructing a test you can trust

The basic rule is the oldest in machine learning (A1): data used to train must not be used to evaluate. Three practical points matter more for language models than the textbook suggests.

Split by the unit that leaks

Randomly splitting examples is not enough when examples are related. If a support-ticket dataset has ten tickets per customer, a random split puts the same customer in train and test, and the model can score well by recognising the customer. Split by the unit whose members resemble each other: by document, by user, by repository, by conversation, or by time. A temporal split (train on before a date, test on after) is the honest choice when the product will face the future, and it also catches models that memorised facts that have since changed.

Three sets with three jobs

  • Training set: what the model learns from.
  • Development (validation) set: used to choose checkpoints, prompts, learning rates, and merge coefficients. Because you look at it repeatedly, it stops being independent.
  • Test set: scored rarely, ideally once per decision that matters, and never used to choose anything. If you tune against it, it becomes a development set and you need a new test set.

The size matters because of noise, which a later section quantifies. A test set of 100 items cannot distinguish models that differ by 3 points.

Where leaks come from

Analogy: the exam paper that got out

A student scores 95% on an exam, and the teacher is delighted, until it turns out the paper had been circulating the week before. The score is real, but it no longer measures what the teacher wanted to know. Worse, nobody can tell by looking at the mark. The fix is not a better student; it is an exam that has been kept in a locked drawer, plus a check of whether any of the questions appeared in the revision materials.

Where it breaks down: a student who saw the questions knows she did. A model has no such awareness, and for web-scale training sets even its builders often cannot say which documents it saw, so contamination has to be detected from the outside.

Contamination checks

Contamination means that evaluation items, or close variants, appear in the training data. For pretraining on web crawls (chapter 1) it is close to inevitable for any benchmark that has been public for long. It also happens in post-training: synthetic fine-tuning data generated by prompting a stronger model (chapter 15) can reproduce or paraphrase test questions, especially if the prompts were seeded from public benchmarks.

The standard check is overlap of n-grams, runs of n consecutive words or tokens. Brown et al. (2020), in "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners", report checking benchmark items for 13-gram overlap with the training data (described in the paper's contamination analysis; I did not re-read that appendix for this chapter). Build an index of all n-grams in the training data, then for each evaluation item ask how many of its n-grams appear. Long n-grams give few false alarms but miss paraphrases; short ones catch rewording but flag common phrases. The script builds a toy case with known leaks and measures what each n finds.

"""Contamination check by n-gram overlap. Train corpus: 2,000 documents of random words. Eval items: 100 clean, 100 copied verbatim
into a training document, and light (15% of words swapped) and heavy (35% swapped) paraphrases of items that are in the corpus."""
import random
rng = random.Random(4)
VOCAB = [f"w{i}" for i in range(3000)]
def text(n): return [rng.choice(VOCAB) for _ in range(n)]

train = [text(200) for _ in range(2000)]
clean = [text(60) for _ in range(100)]
leaked = [text(60) for _ in range(300)]
for i, item in enumerate(leaked):                       # each leaked item sits inside a training document
    d = train[i]; train[i] = d[:70] + item + d[70:]
def paraphrase(item, rate):                             # swap a fraction of words for others (a crude stand-in for rewording)
    return [rng.choice(VOCAB) if rng.random() < rate else w for w in item]
verbatim, light, heavy = leaked[:100], [paraphrase(x, .15) for x in leaked[100:200]], [paraphrase(x, .35) for x in leaked[200:]]

def ngrams(toks, n): return {tuple(toks[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(toks) - n + 1)}
def index(n):
    s = set()
    for d in train: s |= ngrams(d, n)
    return s
print(f"{'n-gram size':>12}{'verbatim':>10}{'light':>8}{'heavy':>8}{'clean flagged':>15}   (flag = any shared n-gram)")
for n in (13, 8, 5, 3):
    idx = index(n)
    rate = lambda items: sum(any(g in idx for g in ngrams(x, n)) for x in items) / len(items)
    print(f"{n:>12}{rate(verbatim):>10.0%}{rate(light):>8.0%}{rate(heavy):>8.0%}{rate(clean):>15.0%}")
 n-gram size  verbatim   light   heavy  clean flagged   (flag = any shared n-gram)
          13      100%     69%      8%             0%
           8      100%    100%     49%             0%
           5      100%    100%     94%             0%
           3      100%    100%    100%             0%

Verbatim copies are caught at any n. A light paraphrase (15% of words changed) slips past 13-grams a third of the time, and a heavy one (35%) almost always, while 5-grams still catch 94% of those. The price of short n-grams does not show in this table, because the toy text is random words, so clean items never share even a 3-gram with the training data. Real text shares many short phrases, so in practice short n-grams need a threshold on the fraction of an item's n-grams found, and a manual look at what is flagged. Neither rule catches an item that has been translated or semantically rewritten; embedding similarity can, at the cost of more false positives, and a model's unusually high accuracy on the original but not on a freshly written equivalent is itself a signal.

What to do about it:

  • Decontaminate before training when you can: remove training documents that overlap with your test items, and keep the removal log.
  • Prefer private or fresh test sets for decisions that matter: items you wrote, or collected after the training cutoff, and not published.
  • Report it. A result with the contamination analysis attached is more useful than a clean-looking one without. A6 covers benchmark decay and audits in chapter 7, and how to build a private benchmark in chapter 6.

Common misconception

"A higher benchmark score means a better model." It means a higher score on that benchmark, run that way, on that day. It can reflect leakage, a favourable prompt format, luck in a small sample, or a model that has been tuned towards the benchmark's style. Sclar et al. (2023), in "Quantifying Language Models' Sensitivity to Spurious Features in Prompt Design", report accuracy differences of up to 76 points for LLaMA-2-13B from changes to prompt formatting that do not change the meaning. A score is a measurement with conditions, and the conditions belong next to the number.

Running the model: harnesses and reproducibility

An evaluation harness is a program that takes a model and a set of tasks, builds the prompts, runs the model, scores the outputs and records everything. Its main benefit is that the base model, the tuned model and the other team's model all go through identical code, so differences are due to the models. The widely used open-source example is EleutherAI's Language Model Evaluation Harness, whose repository describes it as a unified framework with over 60 standard academic benchmarks and hundreds of variants, supporting backends from Hugging Face Transformers to vLLM and SGLang and hosted APIs (README read in October 2026). Other tools exist, including Hugging Face's lighteval and the UK AI Security Institute's Inspect framework (named from memory here; check their documentation). A6 builds a small harness of your own in chapter 2.

$ pip install "lm_eval[hf]"
$ lm_eval --model hf \
    --model_args pretrained=./my-tuned-model \
    --tasks hellaswag \
    --device cuda:0 --batch_size 8

The command follows the harness's README example. Four details decide whether the number means what you think.

Scoring mode
For multiple-choice tasks, a harness can compute the model's log-probability of each answer option and pick the highest (log-likelihood scoring), or let the model generate an answer and parse it (generative scoring). The two can rank models differently, and chat-tuned models are often better judged generatively.
Prompt and template
Use the chat template the model was trained with (chapter 10) and a fixed few-shot setup. Evaluating a chat model without its template, or a base model with one, measures the mismatch rather than the model.
Decoding settings
Temperature, maximum length, stop sequences. For comparisons use greedy decoding or the same seeds; for sampled evaluation use enough samples to average out the randomness.
Record everything
Harness version, task version, prompt text, template, decoding settings, model revision, hardware and library versions. Two runs that differ in any of them are different experiments.

Is the difference real? Error bars on evaluations

An evaluation set is a sample of the questions you might ask, so its accuracy is an estimate with a margin of error. For a set of n items and accuracy p, the standard error is about √(p(1 − p)/n): for n = 100 and p = 0.6 that is about 5 points, so a 95% interval is roughly ±10 points. Miller (2024), in "Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations", argues for treating evaluations as experiments and reporting uncertainty; A6 covers the method in chapter 4.

Two models scored on the same items should be compared pairwise. Both find the same questions hard, so the difference per item has much less variance than the two accuracies separately. A paired bootstrap resamples the items (keeping each item's two outcomes together), recomputes the difference many times, and reads an interval off the spread. The script applies it to a before-and-after table for a fine-tuned model across five suites. The per-item results are simulated, seeded, so that the true effects are known.

"""Before/after table for a fine-tuned model. Per-item correctness is SIMULATED (seeded) so the true effect of each suite is known;
the point is how to read the table: paired bootstrap intervals on the difference, not two separate accuracies."""
import random
rng = random.Random(21)

# suite: (number of items, accuracy of base model, true accuracy of tuned model)
SUITES = {"target task (new skill)": (400, 0.52, 0.64), "general knowledge": (1000, 0.68, 0.665),
          "instruction following": (300, 0.81, 0.77), "JSON validity": (200, 0.90, 0.93), "refusal of unsafe requests": (150, 0.95, 0.87)}

def simulate(n, pb, pt):
    """Item difficulty is shared between models (some items are just harder), which is what makes paired comparison tighter."""
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        hard = rng.random()
        b = hard < pb + (rng.random() - .5) * .2
        t = hard < pt + (rng.random() - .5) * .2
        out.append((int(b), int(t)))
    return out

def paired_bootstrap(pairs, reps=2000):
    diffs = []
    n = len(pairs)
    for _ in range(reps):
        s = [pairs[rng.randrange(n)] for _ in range(n)]
        diffs.append((sum(t for _, t in s) - sum(b for b, _ in s)) / n)
    diffs.sort()
    return diffs[int(.025 * reps)], diffs[int(.975 * reps)]

print(f"{'suite':<30}{'items':>6}{'base':>7}{'tuned':>7}{'delta':>8}{'95% interval':>20}  verdict")
for name, (n, pb, pt) in SUITES.items():
    pairs = simulate(n, pb, pt)
    b = sum(x for x, _ in pairs) / n; t = sum(y for _, y in pairs) / n
    lo, hi = paired_bootstrap(pairs)
    verdict = "improved" if lo > 0 else "REGRESSED" if hi < 0 else "no clear change"
    print(f"{name:<30}{n:>6}{b:>7.1%}{t:>7.1%}{t-b:>+8.1%}{f'[{lo:+.1%}, {hi:+.1%}]':>20}  {verdict}")
suite                          items   base  tuned   delta        95% interval  verdict
target task (new skill)          400  50.0%  63.0%  +13.0%     [+9.8%, +16.2%]  improved
general knowledge               1000  68.0%  66.0%   -2.0%      [-3.6%, -0.4%]  REGRESSED
instruction following            300  76.0%  75.0%   -1.0%      [-3.7%, +1.7%]  no clear change
JSON validity                    200  90.0%  91.5%   +1.5%      [-1.5%, +4.5%]  no clear change
refusal of unsafe requests       150  93.3%  83.3%  -10.0%     [-16.0%, -4.0%]  REGRESSED

Read it suite by suite. The target skill improved by 13 points, and the interval is far above zero. General knowledge fell by 2 points on 1,000 items, an interval just below zero: a small but real regression. Instruction following and JSON validity moved by amounts well inside their noise: the honest verdict is "no clear change", not "stable" and not "improved". And refusal of unsafe requests fell by 10 points on only 150 items, an interval entirely below zero. A single headline number, "13 points better on the target", would have hidden the safety regression. The right decisions here are to ask whether a 10-point drop in refusals is acceptable (it is probably not), to gather more items for the unclear suites, and to look at the failures themselves (error analysis).

Generation tasks and pass@k

For tasks with checkable outputs, such as code with unit tests or maths with a numeric answer, a common metric is pass@k: the chance that at least one of k sampled attempts is correct. Chen et al. (2021), in "Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code", which introduced the HumanEval benchmark, estimate it without bias by generating n ≥ k samples per problem, counting the c that pass, and computing 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k): the probability that a random subset of k of the n samples contains no correct one, subtracted from 1. The simulation below checks it against the known truth for 100 problems of varied difficulty.

"""pass@k: the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021) versus naive alternatives. n samples per problem, c of them correct."""
import random
from math import comb
rng = random.Random(3)

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    """P(at least one of k samples, drawn without replacement from the n generated, is correct) = 1 - C(n-c, k) / C(n, k)."""
    if n - c < k: return 1.0
    return 1.0 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)

# 100 problems; each has its own true per-sample success probability (many easy, many hard)
probs = [rng.betavariate(0.6, 1.2) for _ in range(100)]
def true_pass(p, k): return 1 - (1 - p) ** k
for k in (1, 5, 20):
    print(f"true pass@{k:<2} over the 100 problems: {sum(true_pass(p, k) for p in probs)/100:.3f}")

print(f"\n{'n samples':>10}{'pass@1 est':>12}{'pass@5 est':>12}{'pass@20 est':>13}")
for n in (20, 50, 200):
    cs = [sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(n)) for p in probs]
    row = [sum(pass_at_k(n, c, k) for c in cs) / 100 for k in (1, 5, 20)]
    print(f"{n:>10}{row[0]:>12.3f}{row[1]:>12.3f}{row[2]:>13.3f}")

# the naive way to estimate pass@5 from 5 samples per problem is noisy: repeat it many times
naive = []
for _ in range(200):
    naive.append(sum(any(rng.random() < p for _ in range(5)) for p in probs) / 100)
m = sum(naive) / 200; sd = (sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in naive) / 199) ** .5
print(f"\nnaive pass@5 from exactly 5 samples: mean {m:.3f}, standard deviation across repeats {sd:.3f}")
true pass@1  over the 100 problems: 0.333
true pass@5  over the 100 problems: 0.653
true pass@20 over the 100 problems: 0.840

 n samples  pass@1 est  pass@5 est  pass@20 est
        20       0.335       0.656        0.830
        50       0.332       0.653        0.847
       200       0.333       0.654        0.848

naive pass@5 from exactly 5 samples: mean 0.655, standard deviation across repeats 0.034

With only 20 samples per problem the estimates are close to the truth for pass@1 and pass@5 (0.335 against 0.333, and 0.656 against 0.653), and pass@20 is a little off (0.830 against 0.840) because 20 samples is exactly the limit of what it can see. The naive method, sampling exactly 5 times and checking, is also unbiased (mean 0.655) but varies by 3.4 points between repeats, because it throws away information. Which k matters depends on the product: pass@1 is what a user who gets one answer experiences; pass@k for larger k reflects a system that can verify and retry, and is the quantity that reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards improves (chapter 14), which is a different thing from reliability of a single try.

Regressions on other skills

Fine-tuning moves all the weights it touches, and the changes that help the target can hurt elsewhere. This is called catastrophic forgetting (chapter 10). Biderman et al. (2024), in "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less", report that in standard settings LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning on programming and maths targets but better preserves the base model's abilities outside the target domain (chapter 11), a reminder that the cost of a method can lie in what it leaves unchanged as well as in what it learns. Reinforcement learning from preference data can shift behaviours in ways that are not obvious, such as refusal rates and flattery (chapter 13).

Build a regression suite once and run it every time you change the model. A reasonable starting point:

  • General knowledge and reasoning: a broad multiple-choice benchmark and a short set of reasoning problems, to catch broad capability loss.
  • Instruction following and format: the constraints your product relies on (length, JSON schema, tone), checked by code rather than by eye.
  • Safety and refusal: a set of requests the model should refuse and a set it should answer, to catch both unsafe drift and over-refusal (A6 guardrails).
  • Long context and multilingual ability, if the product uses them.
  • Your own failures: every bug found in production turned into a test case (the improvement flywheel).

Quantised or merged models need the same suite: chapter 19 measured the damage of rounding, and the merged models of chapter 16 are judged by the same before-and-after table. For open-ended output a model can serve as the judge, with the biases that implies (LLM-as-judge), and some qualities need human labels (human labels).

A checklist for a tuned model

  1. Write down the decision the evaluation supports, and the minimum improvement and maximum regression you would accept.
  2. Split by the right unit; freeze a private test set; keep a separate development set.
  3. Decontaminate against the training data and record the check.
  4. Run base and tuned models through the same harness, with the right template and recorded settings.
  5. Report paired differences with intervals on the target and the regression suite.
  6. Read failures, not just scores. A 2-point drop on a benchmark may be a bug in the answer parser.
  7. Score the test set once for the final decision, and keep the result with the model's record.

Systems connection

A regression suite is the model's counterpart of a test suite in continuous integration (testability), and a before-and-after table with intervals plays the role of the canary comparison in deployment strategies, where a bad version is usually a worse answer, not a crash. The discipline of recording versions and settings is the same reproducibility problem as in configuration management. The lab-scale version of this chapter is A6 Lab 1: An Eval Harness with Error Bars.

State of play, October 2026

Open evaluation tooling is mature and changes quickly: harnesses add tasks and backends every few months, and many older benchmarks are near saturation, with the best models scoring so high that differences are within noise and leaked items. Treat any public benchmark as a sanity check, not as a measure of your product, and check the version of both the harness and the task. This chapter's methods (private test sets, paired intervals, contamination checks, regression suites) are stable; which benchmarks are informative this year is not, so I have named none as current recommendations.

Key takeaways

  • Loss is a monitor, not a verdict. Evaluate the target task, the capabilities you must keep, and whether the numbers can be believed.
  • Split by the unit that leaks, keep development and test sets separate, and score the private test set rarely.
  • Contamination comes from the crawl, from synthetic data and from tuning against the dev set. N-gram overlap catches copies; short n-grams or embeddings are needed for paraphrase.
  • Run all models through one harness with the same template and decoding; record versions and settings.
  • Use paired differences with confidence intervals; "no clear change" is a valid result, and small sets cannot show small effects.
  • Re-test other skills, especially safety and format, every time the weights change.

Check your understanding

A dataset has twelve support tickets per customer. You split it randomly by ticket into train and test, and the tuned model scores very high on test. What is the most likely problem?

  1. The test set is too large
  2. The same customers appear in train and test, so the model can score by recognising them: split by customer instead
  3. The model needs a larger learning rate
  4. The metric should be perplexity
Related examples must stay on the same side of the split. Splitting by the unit whose members resemble each other (customer, document, user, time) measures generalisation to new ones. Test-set size affects noise, not this kind of leak.

An evaluation item was rewritten by changing about a third of its words. Which check is most likely to find it in the training data?

  1. Exact-match search on the whole item
  2. A 13-gram overlap test
  3. Checking training loss
  4. A short n-gram overlap (around 5) with a threshold on the fraction found
Heavy rewording breaks long n-grams, so 13-gram overlap flagged only 8% of heavy paraphrases in the chapter's experiment, while 5-grams flagged 94%. Short n-grams give more false alarms on real text, hence the threshold and the manual review.

Two models score 71% and 73% on the same 300 test questions. Why is a paired comparison better than comparing the two separate intervals?

  1. Both models find the same questions hard, so the per-item difference has less variance than either accuracy
  2. Paired tests always give a smaller p-value, whatever the data
  3. It removes the need for a test set
  4. It corrects for contamination
The shared difficulty of items cancels in the per-item difference. It does not always produce significance: if the models disagree in both directions it can be wide. It says nothing about contamination, and the test set is still needed.

After fine-tuning for a new task, the target score rises 13 points. The safety-refusal suite (150 items) falls 10 points with an interval entirely below zero, and JSON validity rises 1.5 points with an interval from −1.5 to +4.5. What should you conclude?

  1. Ship it: the target gain outweighs everything
  2. Both changes are noise because the sample sizes are small
  3. The refusal drop is a likely real regression to investigate and fix; the JSON change is inconclusive
  4. The JSON improvement is real, and the refusal drop is not
An interval that excludes zero supports a real change; one that includes zero does not. A 10-point drop in refusals is a serious regression, whatever the headline gain. Small samples widen intervals, but this one is still clearly below zero.

Why is the unbiased pass@k estimator computed from n > k samples preferable to sampling exactly k and checking?

  1. The naive method is biased high
  2. Both are unbiased, but the estimator uses all n samples and so has lower variance
  3. The naive method cannot compute pass@1
  4. Using more samples changes what pass@k means
In the chapter's simulation the naive pass@5 averaged 0.655 (unbiased) but varied by 3.4 points between repeats. The estimator averages over all subsets of size k, extracting more information from the same samples.

You evaluate your chat-tuned model with a harness but omit its chat template, and the score is lower than the base model's. What does the result most likely show?

  1. Fine-tuning damaged the model
  2. The test set was contaminated
  3. A mismatch between the prompt format and what the model was trained on, not necessarily a worse model
  4. The model needs more few-shot examples
Prompt formatting can move scores by large amounts without changing meaning. Chat-tuned models are trained to see a particular template; leaving it out measures the mismatch. Rerun with the template before concluding anything about the weights.
