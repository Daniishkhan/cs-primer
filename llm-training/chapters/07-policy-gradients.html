Policy Gradients

A language model's samples are discrete choices, so a reward for them cannot be differentiated through. The policy gradient is the way around that. It is a short derivation that turns "make good samples more likely" into an ordinary gradient you can estimate. This chapter derives the simplest version step by step and shows in code that it is correct. Then it shows that the real problem is noise, and that the real fix is to subtract a reference value from each reward.

Why this matters

Every reinforcement learning method used to train language models is a refinement of one formula. Proximal policy optimisation (PPO) in chapter 08 clips it. Group relative policy optimisation (GRPO) in chapter 14 feeds it a baseline computed from a group of answers. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) in chapter 12 runs it against a reward model. Suppose you understand where the formula comes from and why it is noisy. Then those methods are variations with obvious motives. If you do not, they look like arbitrary recipes. After this chapter you will be able to derive the policy gradient and prove that a baseline does not bias it. You will compute a leave-one-out advantage by hand. You will also predict from the reward scale whether a training run will be noisy. The chapter builds directly on chapter 06. The lab for Part 2 (Policy Gradients & PPO) implements the code below.

The obstacle: you cannot differentiate through a choice

Training a network normally means writing a loss that is a differentiable function of the weights θ and letting backpropagation (A2 chapter 02) deliver the gradient. Now try that for reward. The model computes a distribution over the next token, a token is sampled from it, and the sample is scored. The score depends on θ only through which token happened to be picked. "Which token" is a discrete jump. Nudge a weight slightly and the sampled token either stays the same or switches to a different one. There is no slope to follow. On top of that, the reward is often a black box, such as a unit test or a human rating, with no gradient to offer.

What is differentiable is the probability the model assigned to what it sampled. The policy gradient builds on that fact. Let us fix notation, following chapter 06:

  • πθ(a | s) is the model's probability of token a given the text s so far;
  • a trajectory τ = (s0, a0, a1, …, aT−1) is one prompt and one sampled response, with T response tokens;
  • R(τ) is the scalar reward for the whole response;
  • the objective is the average reward, J(θ) = Eτ~πθ[ R(τ) ], and training is gradient ascent on J.

The log-derivative trick, step by step

The derivation has four moves, shown in the stepper below. Write the expectation as a sum over every trajectory the policy could produce, weighted by its probability, πθ(τ). Differentiate. Apply one identity. Recognise an expectation again.

Step through

Deriving the policy gradient

  1. The objective. The expected reward is a sum over all trajectories. We cannot compute it for a real model, but we can differentiate it on paper.
  2. Differentiate. Only the probabilities depend on θ. The reward of a given trajectory is a fixed number, so it passes through the gradient untouched. This is why the reward may be any black box.
  3. The trick. The derivative of a log is the derivative divided by the value. Rearranged, ∇π = π ∇log π. Multiplying and dividing by π costs nothing and changes the shape of the sum.
  4. An expectation again. The sum now has πθ(τ) in front of it, which is exactly how an expectation under the policy is written. Sampling responses from the model and averaging estimates it.
  5. Factorise. A response's probability is the product of its token probabilities. The prompt's probability and the append-a-token transitions contain no θ, so they drop out of the gradient. The log of a product is a sum, and each term is a quantity backpropagation already computes.
  6. The estimator. Sample, score, and weight. Responses with high reward get their token probabilities pushed up. Responses with low reward get theirs pushed down, once rewards are centred (see below).

The result deserves a plain-language reading. The quantity ∇log πθ(a | s) is the direction in weight space that makes token a more likely in state s. The policy gradient says: for each sampled token, move along that direction, scaled by the reward of the response it belongs to. Nothing in the formula asks the reward to be differentiable, smooth or even deterministic. The sampling does the exploring. The reward only decides how hard to push.

A check you can run

Algebra is easy to get subtly wrong, so verify on a policy small enough to compute exactly. Take a softmax over four actions with logits z, so π(a) = eza / ∑b ezb, with fixed rewards. For this policy the score has a neat closed form, ∇z log π(a) = onehot(a) − π. The box below derives it. The script computes the true gradient of the expected reward in three ways. First it uses finite differences. Then it uses the policy gradient formula summed exactly. Finally it uses 200,000 samples.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
z = np.array([0.5, -0.2, 0.1, 0.9])                 # logits of a 4-action softmax policy
r = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.5, 2.0])                  # reward of each action (deterministic here)

def pi(z): e = np.exp(z - z.max()); return e / e.sum()
def J(z): return float(pi(z) @ r)                   # exact expected reward

# 1. exact gradient of J by central finite differences
eps = 1e-6
num = np.array([(J(z + eps * np.eye(4)[i]) - J(z - eps * np.eye(4)[i])) / (2 * eps) for i in range(4)])

# 2. closed form from the log-derivative trick: grad_z log pi(a) = onehot(a) - pi
p = pi(z)
exact = sum(p[a] * r[a] * (np.eye(4)[a] - p) for a in range(4))      # E[ R * grad log pi ], summed exactly

# 3. the same expectation estimated from samples
N = 200_000
acts = rng.choice(4, size=N, p=p)
est = (r[acts][:, None] * (np.eye(4)[acts] - p)).mean(axis=0)

np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)
print("finite differences  ", num)
print("E[R * grad log pi]  ", exact)
print(f"sampled, N={N:,}  ", est)
finite differences   [-0.0493 -0.1602 -0.1247  0.3342]
E[R * grad log pi]   [-0.0493 -0.1602 -0.1247  0.3342]
sampled, N=200,000   [-0.0493 -0.1599 -0.1238  0.3329]

All three agree. The sampled estimate matches the exact one to two or three decimal places with 200,000 samples. With a batch of 16 it will be far rougher, and the rest of this chapter is about that roughness.

Go deeper the softmax score, derived

Let log π(a) = za − log ∑b ezb. Differentiate with respect to a logit zk. The first term gives 1 if k = a and 0 otherwise. The second gives ezk / ∑b ezb = π(k). So ∂ log π(a) / ∂zk = [k = a] − π(k), which is the vector onehot(a) − π.

Read it this way. Gradient ascent along it raises the logit of the sampled token a by (1 − π(a)). It lowers every other logit by its own probability. A rare token that is sampled and rewarded moves a lot. A token that already had probability near 1 barely moves. In a transformer, the logits are the output of the last layer. The same vector flows back into the network through ordinary backpropagation.

From one choice to a whole response

For a language model the trajectory is a response of T tokens, and step 5 of the derivation says the gradient of its log-probability is the sum of per-token terms. Every token in a response is multiplied by the same number, the response's reward R. Suppose a 300-token answer is correct. Then all 300 tokens are made more likely, including the ones that were irrelevant to its correctness. Over many samples the irrelevant ones average out and the useful ones accumulate, but that takes samples. Deciding which tokens deserve credit for a reward is the credit assignment problem. The value functions of chapter 09 are one answer to it.

One refinement is free. A token at position t cannot have influenced rewards received before position t. In a setting with rewards at every step, the weight on ∇log π(at | st) can therefore be the reward-to-go Gt = ∑t'≥t rt' rather than the full return. This lowers variance without bias. The argument is as follows. Take an earlier reward rt' with t' < t, condition on everything up to st. Then the factor Eat[∇log π(at | st)] = ∑a ∇π(a | st) = ∇ 1 = 0, so that term has zero expectation and can be dropped. Most language-model training has a single reward at the end of the response. Then reward-to-go equals the final reward for every token, and the refinement changes nothing.

In code, the loss is short. Given a batch of sampled sequences, the loss is the negative of advantage times the sum of token log-probabilities. Minimising it performs gradient ascent on reward. This listing uses the A2 notation: B sequences, T positions and vocabulary V. It is a sketch to show the shape. It is not output from this machine, because PyTorch is not installed here.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def reinforce_loss(model, input_ids, response_mask, rewards):
    """input_ids: (B, T) prompt + sampled response, response_mask: (B, T) 1 on response tokens,
    rewards: (B,) one scalar per sequence (K responses per prompt would be grouped upstream)."""
    logits = model(input_ids).logits[:, :-1]                      # (B, T-1, V): predict token t+1
    logp = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
    tok_logp = logp.gather(-1, input_ids[:, 1:, None]).squeeze(-1) # (B, T-1) log pi of the sampled tokens
    seq_logp = (tok_logp * response_mask[:, 1:]).sum(dim=1)       # (B,) sum over response tokens
    B = rewards.shape[0]
    baseline = (rewards.sum() - rewards) / (B - 1)                # leave-one-out
    advantage = (rewards - baseline).detach()                     # a constant weight, not differentiated
    return -(advantage * seq_logp).mean()

Three details matter. First, the advantage is detached. It is a weight, and gradients flow only through the log-probabilities. Second, the sequences must be sampled from the current model. That is what on-policy means. Reusing old samples after the weights change gives a biased estimate, which is the problem PPO's ratio addresses in chapter 08. Third, summing token log-probabilities rather than averaging them changes how long and short responses are weighted. That choice bites in chapter 14.

Variance is the enemy

The estimator is unbiased. Averaged over infinitely many samples it equals the true gradient, as the check showed. But training uses a batch, and a single batch's estimate is a random vector scattered around the truth. The scatter is the variance of the estimator. Suppose the scatter is large relative to the true gradient. Then each update is mostly noise and progress is slow. A step size big enough to make progress also makes the run unstable.

Why is the scatter so large? Look at the weights. Suppose rewards lie between 0.8 and 1.0, as they might when a reward model scores answers that are all decent. Every sampled response gets a weight near 0.9. The formula then pushes up the probability of everything that was sampled, by nearly the same amount. The only information about which response is better is the small difference between 0.8 and 1.0, riding on a large constant. The constant contributes no signal. But it multiplies the random score vectors, so it contributes a great deal of noise.

The experiment below makes this measurable. The policy is uniform over four actions with mean rewards 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8, plus noise of standard deviation 0.3. We form 20,000 independent gradient estimates, each from a batch of 16 samples, under four weighting rules. Then we repeat after adding 10 to every reward. Adding a constant to all rewards does not change which action is best, so a good method should not care. The table reports three things. The first is the largest deviation of the average estimate from the true gradient, labelled "max bias". This is a bias check, and it includes Monte Carlo noise of about 0.001 for the low-variance rows. The second is the total spread across the four logit components. The third is how often the sign of the best action's gradient entry came out right.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
mu = np.array([0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8])                 # mean reward of each of 4 actions
sigma = 0.3                                         # reward noise
z = np.zeros(4)                                     # uniform policy
p = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()
B, TRIALS = 16, 20000                               # samples per gradient estimate, repeats

def true_grad(shift):                               # exact gradient of J w.r.t. the logits
    r = mu + shift
    return p * (r - p @ r)

def estimate(shift, baseline):
    a = rng.choice(4, size=(TRIALS, B), p=p)
    R = mu[a] + shift + sigma * rng.standard_normal((TRIALS, B))
    if baseline == "none": b = 0.0
    elif baseline == "batch mean": b = R.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    elif baseline == "leave-one-out": b = (R.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) - R) / (B - 1)
    else: b = (p @ (mu + shift))                    # the true expected reward V (an oracle)
    onehot = np.eye(4)[a]                           # shape (TRIALS, B, 4)
    return ((R - b)[..., None] * (onehot - p)).mean(axis=1)    # average over the batch

for shift in (0.0, 10.0):
    g = true_grad(shift)
    print(f"\nreward shift +{shift:g}   true gradient {np.round(g, 3)}")
    print(f"{'baseline':<15}{'max bias':>12}{'std (sum over logits)':>24}{'sign of best-arm entry correct':>32}")
    for name in ("none", "batch mean", "leave-one-out", "true value"):
        e = estimate(shift, name)
        bias = np.abs(e.mean(axis=0) - g).max()
        std = np.sqrt(e.var(axis=0).sum())
        ok = (e[:, 3] > 0).mean()
        print(f"{name:<15}{bias:>12.4f}{std:>24.3f}{100*ok:>31.1f}%")

With no baseline, shifting the rewards by 10 multiplies the spread from 0.133 to 2.271, while the true gradient is still 0.075 at its largest entry. The best action's gradient sign is then right only 59 percent of the time, barely better than a coin. Any baseline that tracks the typical reward removes the problem. The spread stays at about 0.08 whatever the shift, and the sign is right about 97 percent of the time. Reward scale is not a free parameter in policy-gradient training.

Baselines: subtract the typical reward

A baseline b is a number subtracted from the reward before it multiplies the score. The weight becomes the advantage, A = R − b: how much better than expected this response was. Responses better than the baseline are pushed up, worse ones are pushed down, and typical ones barely move.

The surprise is that this is free. Subtracting a baseline does not change the expected gradient, provided b does not depend on the action that was sampled. The proof is two lines. The extra term is Ea~π[ b ∇log π(a) ] = b ∑a π(a) ∇π(a)/π(a) = b ∑a ∇π(a) = b ∇ ∑a π(a) = b ∇ 1 = 0. Probabilities sum to 1 for every θ, so their total derivative is zero. Whatever constant you subtract contributes nothing on average, while it cancels noise sample by sample. The baseline can depend on the prompt (the state), because the argument holds for each state separately. It cannot depend on the sampled response.

Which baseline?

  • Constant or running average. Keep a moving average of recent rewards. Cheap and unbiased (it does not depend on the current samples), but it ignores that some prompts are harder than others.
  • Batch mean. Subtract the mean reward of the B samples in the batch. It adapts to the prompt. But it includes the sample being weighted, so it depends slightly on that sample's own action and is biased. The expected gradient is shrunk by a factor (B−1)/B. The table shows it: the batch-mean row has an error of about 0.005, which is 0.075/16. For B = 2 half the gradient is lost.
  • Leave-one-out (RLOO). For sample i, subtract the mean reward of the other B−1 samples. The baseline is independent of sample i's action, so it is unbiased, and it needs no extra model. The idea goes back to variance reduction for the estimator in this chapter, which is called REINFORCE (Kool, van Hoof and Welling, 2019, "Buy 4 REINFORCE Samples, Get a Baseline for Free!"). Ahmadian et al. (2024, "Back to Basics: Revisiting REINFORCE Style Optimization for Learning from Human Feedback in LLMs") brought it to language-model fine-tuning. It needs several samples per prompt.
  • Learned value function. Train a second network V(s) to predict expected reward and subtract it. Powerful because it can use the state. But it costs a second model's memory and training. This is the "critic" of PPO (chapters 08 and 09).
  • Group mean, divided by group standard deviation. The choice made by GRPO: normalise rewards within the group of samples for one prompt (chapter 14). It is the leave-one-out idea, plus a scale normalisation.
Go deeper the best possible baseline

The estimator's variance for a given baseline is, up to terms that do not depend on b, E[ (R − b)2 ∥∇log π∥2 ]. Differentiating with respect to b and setting to zero gives b* = E[ R ∥∇log π∥2 ] / E[ ∥∇log π∥2 ]: the average reward, with each sample weighted by the squared size of its score. In practice the plain expected reward V is used, because the weighted version is hard to estimate. It is close to optimal when the score sizes are similar across samples. The "true value" row in the table uses exactly that oracle V. It beats leave-one-out only slightly (0.076 against 0.080). That is why a cheap baseline gets most of the benefit.

Common misconception

"A baseline changes what the model learns, so it has to be justified like a design choice." A valid baseline changes nothing about what the gradient points to on average. It only changes how noisy each estimate is. That is also why you can use whichever baseline is cheapest. The misconception has a real cousin. A baseline that depends on the sampled action, such as a sample's own reward, does change the gradient. It would zero it out. A batch mean that includes the sample shrinks it. "Does not depend on the action" is the condition to check.

Training with it

An unbiased but noisy gradient estimate still works if you take many small steps. The script below trains the four-action softmax policy with REINFORCE. It uses batches of 8, a learning rate of 0.5 and 300 steps, over 200 random seeds. It runs with and without a leave-one-out baseline, at reward shift 0 and +5.

import numpy as np

mu = np.array([0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8]); sigma = 0.3
B, LR, STEPS, SEEDS = 8, 0.5, 300, 200

def train(shift, use_baseline, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    z = np.zeros(4)
    for _ in range(STEPS):
        p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum()
        a = rng.choice(4, size=B, p=p)
        R = mu[a] + shift + sigma * rng.standard_normal(B)
        b = (R.sum() - R) / (B - 1) if use_baseline else 0.0        # leave-one-out baseline
        z = z + LR * ((R - b)[:, None] * (np.eye(4)[a] - p)).mean(axis=0)
    p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum()
    return p

print(f"4 arms with means {mu.tolist()}, batch {B}, lr {LR}, {STEPS} steps, {SEEDS} seeds")
print(f"{'reward shift':<14}{'baseline':<10}{'mean P(best arm)':>18}{'runs with P(best) > 0.9':>26}")
for shift in (0.0, 5.0):
    for use in (False, True):
        ps = np.array([train(shift, use, s) for s in range(SEEDS)])
        print(f"+{shift:<13g}{'LOO' if use else 'none':<10}{ps[:, 3].mean():>18.3f}{100*(ps[:, 3] > 0.9).mean():>25.0f}%")

With centred rewards, both versions solve the problem. On average the policy puts 98 percent of its probability on the best arm. Add 5 to every reward and the no-baseline run falls to 47 percent, with fewer than half of the seeds reaching 90 percent. The baseline run is unaffected. It is the same problem with the same expected gradient. The only change is how large the noise is. The effect would be worse with more actions or a larger learning rate.

The last step is to leave bandits behind. The script below trains a tiny autoregressive policy. It is a table of logits for every prefix of a 3-token response over a 3-token vocabulary (13 contexts, 3 logits each), standing in for a network. The reward is the one from chapter 06. It is 1 if the three tokens are all different and the last is token 2, and it arrives only at the end. Each update samples 16 responses and applies the leave-one-out baseline. It then adds up the per-token score vectors exactly as in the loss above. The expected reward is computed by enumerating all 27 responses, so it is exact.

import itertools, math
import numpy as np

V, T = 3, 3
SEQS = list(itertools.product(range(V), repeat=T))
reward = lambda s: 1.0 if len(set(s)) == 3 and s[-1] == 2 else 0.0      # same toy reward as chapter 06

class TabularLM:
    """Logits for every prefix: 1 + 3 + 9 contexts, 3 logits each. A stand-in for a network."""
    def __init__(self): self.z = {pre: np.zeros(V) for t in range(T) for pre in itertools.product(range(V), repeat=t)}
    def probs(self, pre): e = np.exp(self.z[pre] - self.z[pre].max()); return e / e.sum()
    def expected_reward(self):
        return sum(math.prod(self.probs(s[:t])[s[t]] for t in range(T)) * reward(s) for s in SEQS)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
lm, K, LR = TabularLM(), 16, 1.0                                          # K responses per update
print(f"step   0  expected reward {lm.expected_reward():.3f}")
for step in range(1, 401):
    batch = []
    for _ in range(K):                                                   # sample responses token by token
        s = ()
        for t in range(T): s += (int(rng.choice(V, p=lm.probs(s))),)
        batch.append(s)
    R = np.array([reward(s) for s in batch])
    adv = R - (R.sum() - R) / (K - 1)                                    # leave-one-out baseline
    grads = {pre: np.zeros(V) for pre in lm.z}
    for s, A in zip(batch, adv):
        for t in range(T):                                               # grad of sum_t log pi(a_t | s_<t)
            pre = s[:t]; g = -lm.probs(pre); g[s[t]] += 1
            grads[pre] += A * g / K
    for pre in lm.z: lm.z[pre] += LR * grads[pre]
    if step in (50, 100, 200, 400): print(f"step {step:>3}  expected reward {lm.expected_reward():.3f}")
step   0  expected reward 0.074
step  50  expected reward 0.930
step 100  expected reward 0.968
step 200  expected reward 0.988
step 400  expected reward 0.994

From 7.4 percent (uniform random responses) the policy reaches 93 percent after 50 updates and 99.4 percent after 400, learning from nothing but a score at the end of each response. Scaling this to a real model needs two more ingredients, and the next chapters supply them. One is a stable way to take larger steps (PPO). The other is a better way to assign credit (value functions, group baselines).

Analogy: coaching a free-throw shooter by scoreboard

A coach watches 16 shots and can only tell the shooter "made it" or "missed", with no comment on form. Telling the shooter "do whatever you did on the makes" is the policy gradient. Suppose the shooter makes 90 percent of shots. Then nearly everything he did gets praise and the advice is useless. The useful signal is that this shot was better than his average. A baseline is the coach comparing each shot to the shooter's own average. It is leave-one-out when the average comes from the other shots in the session.

Where it breaks down: a coach can see the shot's form and say which part helped. The policy gradient has only the score. It must discover which of the 300 tokens mattered by seeing many samples. A model's "form" is also spread over billions of weights.

Systems connection

Estimating a gradient from a few samples is the same statistical problem as estimating a rate from sampled traffic or a mean from a handful of benchmark runs. The error shrinks like 1/√N, and a few extreme values dominate a small sample. The tools in Statistics for Experiments apply directly. Those tools are confidence intervals and effect size against noise. The "signal to noise of the gradient" is a diagnostic worth logging. In a long run, the same noise shows up as erratic reward curves. That is one more reason to watch several metrics at once, as in chapter 05.

What REINFORCE gets wrong

Three weaknesses drive the rest of Part 2.

  1. Step size. The gradient is a local statement. A noisy estimate multiplied by a learning rate that is a little too large can move the policy somewhere much worse. Unlike supervised learning, there is no fixed dataset to recover from, because the next batch is generated by the damaged policy. PPO bounds how far one update can move the policy (chapter 08).
  2. On-policy samples are used once. After one gradient step the samples came from an out-of-date policy. Generating responses is the expensive part of reinforcement learning (RL) for language models, so throwing them away after one step is wasteful. Importance ratios let a batch be reused for several steps, with limits.
  3. Credit assignment and the cost of baselines. A single reward at the end of a long response is a weak signal for the first token. Value functions and advantage estimates (chapter 09) learn a finer-grained one, at the cost of a critic.

Key takeaways

  • Sampling is not differentiable and the reward may be a black box, yet ∇J = E[ R(τ) ∇log πθ(τ) ] can be estimated from samples. This is the log-derivative trick.
  • REINFORCE: sample responses, score them, and scale each token's ∇log-probability by its response's reward. For a softmax, the score is onehot(a) − π.
  • The estimator is unbiased but noisy. A constant offset in the rewards can inflate its spread by more than an order of magnitude.
  • A baseline that does not depend on the sampled action leaves the expected gradient unchanged and cuts the variance. Leave-one-out is unbiased and needs no extra model; the batch mean is biased by a factor (B−1)/B.
  • REINFORCE is on-policy, step-size sensitive and has coarse credit assignment. PPO, value functions and group baselines address these in turn.

  • Sutton and Barto, "Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction" (2nd ed., 2018), chapter 13. The policy gradient theorem and baselines, free online.
  • Sutton, McAllester, Singh and Mansour, "Policy Gradient Methods for Reinforcement Learning with Function Approximation" (1999). The policy gradient theorem for general policies.
  • Ahmadian et al., "Back to Basics: Revisiting REINFORCE Style Optimization for Learning from Human Feedback in LLMs" (2024). REINFORCE and RLOO applied to language-model alignment, with the argument that PPO's machinery is more than needed there.
  • Kool, van Hoof and Welling, "Buy 4 REINFORCE Samples, Get a Baseline for Free!" (2019). The leave-one-out baseline.
  • OpenAI Spinning Up, "Part 3: Intro to Policy Optimization". A readable derivation of the same result, including the reward-to-go and baseline steps.

Check your understanding

Why can't we train a language model on reward by ordinary backpropagation through the sampled response?

  1. Because the model has too many parameters
  2. Because rewards are always negative
  3. Because the sampled token is a discrete choice with no gradient, and the reward may be a black box that offers none either
  4. Because the softmax is not differentiable
Sampling a token is a jump, not a smooth function of the weights, and the reward may come from a checker or a person. The softmax itself is differentiable (the policy gradient uses it), and the parameter count is irrelevant to the obstacle.

What does the log-derivative trick, ∇π = π ∇log π, accomplish in the derivation?

  1. It turns a sum over all trajectories into an expectation under the policy, which can be estimated by sampling
  2. It removes the reward from the gradient
  3. It makes the policy deterministic
  4. It guarantees that the gradient has low variance
Multiplying and dividing by π puts π in front of the sum, which is the form of an expectation. The reward stays in the formula (it is the weight), the policy remains stochastic, and variance is a separate problem addressed by baselines.

A prompt gets K = 4 sampled responses with binary rewards 1, 0, 0, 1. What is the leave-one-out advantage of the first response?

  1. +1.00
  2. +0.50
  3. +0.33
  4. +0.67
The baseline for response 1 is the mean of the other three rewards, (0 + 0 + 1) / 3 = 0.33, so A = 1 − 0.33 = 0.67. Using +1.00 means no baseline, +0.50 is the plain batch mean (0.5) subtracted, and +0.33 is the baseline itself rather than the advantage.

A reward model scores every answer between 8 and 10, and you run REINFORCE with no baseline. What do you expect?

  1. No problem, since the gradient is unbiased and only the ordering of rewards matters
  2. Very noisy updates: every sampled response is pushed up with nearly the same large weight, so the useful difference between answers is buried in noise
  3. A biased gradient that points the wrong way
  4. The policy becomes deterministic immediately
The expected gradient is still correct (so not the third option), but its variance grows with the square of the reward scale. The first option confuses "unbiased" with "usable": an unbiased estimate with huge variance can need far more samples than you have.

Why is subtracting the mean of all B rewards in the batch (including the sample being weighted) slightly biased, while the leave-one-out mean is not?

  1. The batch mean is a constant, and constants are always biased
  2. The batch mean has a larger variance than the leave-one-out mean
  3. The batch mean contains the sample's own reward, so it depends on the sampled action; this shrinks the expected gradient by a factor (B−1)/B
  4. Leave-one-out uses a learned model, so it is exact
The zero-expectation argument needs the baseline to be independent of the action it multiplies. The leave-one-out baseline uses only other samples, which satisfies that. The batch mean includes the sample itself: with B = 16 the bias is a 1/16 shrinkage, as the table showed, and it is severe for B = 2.

Which is the main practical weakness of plain REINFORCE that PPO is designed to address?

  1. Each batch of freshly generated samples supports only one cautious update, and a step that is too large can degrade the policy that generates the next batch
  2. It cannot be used with a reward that is not differentiable
  3. It needs a separate model to compute rewards
  4. It only works for bandits, not sequences
PPO adds a clipped ratio so that samples can be reused for several steps and no single step moves the policy too far. REINFORCE works with non-differentiable rewards, needs no critic and handles sequences, as the tabular language-model script showed.
