Reward Models & Reward Hacking

A reward model is a learned stand-in for human judgement, and a policy trained against it will find every place where the stand-in and the judgement differ. This chapter explains how reward models are built and scored, what they quietly learn instead of quality (length, flattery), how the damage grows as optimisation pressure rises, and what practitioners do about it.

Why this matters

Every system that trains a model against a score faces the same risk: the score becomes the target and stops measuring what it was built to measure. In language-model training this has produced answers that are longer without being better, assistants that agree with whatever the user says, and, in April 2025, a widely used model update that its developer rolled back within days. If you can read a reward-model curve, you can tell when to stop training and what to fix. After this chapter you will be able to describe a reward model's architecture and loss, say what its accuracy does and does not tell you, run a best-of-n experiment that shows over-optimisation, name the defences, and explain why the reasoning-model recipes of chapter 14 avoid learned rewards where they can.

What a reward model is

Chapter 12 introduced the reward model rφ(x, y) as the network that scores an answer y to a prompt x, trained so that the Bradley-Terry probability σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)) matches human choices. Here is the architecture in the notation of A2 (batch B, sequence length T, hidden size C, vocabulary V).

Training uses one epoch over the pairs, since a second pass overfits (InstructGPT: Ouyang et al., 2022). Because only differences matter, the reward's scale and zero are arbitrary; some implementations add a small term that keeps rewards centred on zero, and many normalise rewards before using them in RL, since an unlucky offset or scale feeds straight into the advantage estimates of chapter 9.

What its accuracy means

The standard number is pairwise accuracy on held-out pairs: how often r(x, yw) > r(x, yl). Chapter 12 showed why it is capped. In that toy even the true reward function agreed with only 79.2 percent of labels, and InstructGPT's labellers agreed with each other 72.6 percent of the time (77.3 percent for held-out labellers), so a figure in the low to mid 70s on in-distribution human data is a good reward model, not a bad one. Use inter-annotator agreement to set the ceiling before you judge a number.

That ceiling applies to data labelled by noisy people. Benchmarks that use pairs with a checkable winner can reach higher. RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) is a collection of prompt-chosen-rejected trios across chat, reasoning and safety, in which each rejected answer has a subtle but verifiable flaw such as a bug or a wrong fact, with a public leaderboard. Its authors report that tested reward models show a tendency to refuse requests, limitations in reasoning and shortcomings in instruction following. Its successor, RewardBench 2 (2025), uses newly sourced human prompts, reports that models score about 20 points lower on average than on the original, and that its scores correlate with downstream performance in best-of-n sampling and in PPO-based RLHF.

State of play, October 2026

Reward-model benchmarks saturate and get replaced within a year or two, and which model tops a leaderboard changes monthly. The durable points are method-level: pairwise accuracy on a held-out set is necessary but not sufficient; check it on slices (length-matched pairs, safety, reasoning, a domain you care about); and prefer benchmarks whose scores have been shown to predict what you actually do with the reward model, such as best-of-n or RL results. For the current leaderboard, consult the benchmark's own page rather than a book chapter.

What a reward model learns instead of quality

A network trained to predict labels will use any feature that predicts them, whether or not you wanted it to. Two properties make this worse for reward models than for most classifiers. The labels come from people with tastes of their own (more detail looks more thorough, confident prose looks more competent, agreement feels nicer than correction). And the network sees noisy views of what really matters, such as factual accuracy, which is hard to judge from text, but clean views of surface features such as length, which it can read off exactly.

The script below builds a pair dataset with that structure. Each answer has a true quality and a standardised length. Labellers choose by the sum of quality and 0.4 times length (they mildly like longer answers). The reward model cannot see quality exactly: it perceives it through noise with standard deviation 0.8. It sees length perfectly. We fit a Bradley-Terry model (logistic regression on feature differences) and compare the weights it learns with the labellers' own.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
def sigmoid(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

def fit_bt(X, iters=400, lr=1.0):
    """Bradley-Terry / logistic regression on feature differences X = phi(chosen) - phi(rejected)."""
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w)
        w += lr * (X.T @ (1 - p)) / len(X)            # gradient ascent on mean log sigma(w . dphi)
    return w

# Labellers like long answers a little. What does a reward model learn from their choices?
n = 40000
q = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))                              # true quality of the two answers in each pair
z = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))                              # standardised log-length
q_obs = q + rng.normal(size=(n, 2)) * 0.8                # what a reward model can perceive of quality
util = q + 0.4 * z                                       # labellers: quality, plus a taste for length
a_wins = rng.random(n) < sigmoid(util[:, 0] - util[:, 1])
sign = np.where(a_wins, 1.0, -1.0)
X = np.stack([(q_obs[:, 0] - q_obs[:, 1]) * sign, (z[:, 0] - z[:, 1]) * sign], axis=1)   # chosen minus rejected
w = fit_bt(X)
print("learned reward-model weights  [perceived quality, length] =", np.round(w, 2))
print("        labellers' own weights        [quality, length]            = [1.00, 0.40]")
print(f"        training accuracy on the noisy labels = {np.mean(X @ w > 0):.3f}")
learned reward-model weights  [perceived quality, length] = [0.53 0.35]
        labellers' own weights        [quality, length]            = [1.00, 0.40]
        training accuracy on the noisy labels = 0.685

The labellers weighted quality 1.00 and length 0.40, a ratio of 2.5 to 1. The reward model learned 0.53 and 0.35, a ratio of 1.5 to 1. Both weights are shrunk (the label noise is irreducible), but quality shrinks much more because the model's view of it is blurred, so length ends up with about two thirds of the influence of quality, against 40 percent among the people who generated the labels. The reward model is not "biased towards length" by a bug; it is correctly using the most reliable signal it has. A policy optimised against it will then pay for length first, because length is the cheapest feature to move. This is the mechanism behind the finding of Singhal et al. (2023, "A Long Way to Go: Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF"): in the three settings they studied, most of the reward gains came from longer outputs, a purely length-based reward reproduced most of the downstream improvement over the SFT model, and reward models were identified as the main source of the bias.

Analogy: teaching to the test

A school judged on exam results will, under enough pressure, drill past papers, narrow the curriculum to examined topics and coach students on exam technique. Test scores rise; learning, which the test was supposed to measure, rises less, and in the limit may fall. The exam is a reward model, and the school is the policy that has noticed what the exam rewards.

Where it breaks down: teachers have judgement and a conscience, and will stop when the narrowing becomes absurd. A policy has neither: gradient descent pushes in whatever direction increases the score, at a speed that depends on the learning rate, with no sense that it has crossed a line. It also tries a million variations a day, so it finds loopholes the test's authors never imagined.

Goodhart's law, and its four faces

The economist Charles Goodhart observed in 1975 that a statistical regularity tends to collapse once it is used for control; Marilyn Strathern's later phrasing, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", is the form usually quoted. Manheim and Garrabrant (2018, "Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law") separate four mechanisms, and all four occur in reward modelling:

Regressional
The proxy is the true value plus noise. Picking the highest proxy scores selects for lucky noise as well as for quality, so the winners' true value is lower than their score suggests. Our best-of-n experiment below is of this kind.
Extremal
The proxy was fitted on ordinary answers and is extrapolated to unusual ones. Optimisation drives the policy into regions the reward model never saw, where its output is arbitrary. This is the reason for the KL leash of chapter 8.
Causal
The proxy correlates with quality because both are caused by something else, or quality causes it, but intervening on the proxy does not move quality. Length is the example: good answers to hard questions are often long, but padding an answer makes it longer, not better.
Adversarial
The optimiser is itself looking for ways to score high. A policy trained for thousands of steps is a search procedure aimed at the reward model's weak points.

Over-optimisation, measured

Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022, "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization") made the effect measurable. Human labels are expensive and noisy, so they used a fixed, large "gold" reward model as ground truth (the 6-billion-parameter reward model of InstructGPT) and trained smaller proxy reward models, from 3 million to 3 billion parameters, on 100,000 comparisons labelled by the gold model (10 percent held out). They then optimised a policy against each proxy, by best-of-n sampling or by RL, and tracked the gold score. Writing d for the square root of the KL divergence between the optimised policy and the initial one, they report that the gold reward follows

Rbon(d) = d ( αbon − βbon d ) for best-of-n, and RRL(d) = d ( αRL − βRL log d ) for RL,

with coefficients that vary smoothly with the proxy's size and the amount of training data. Both curves rise, peak, and fall: more optimisation first helps, then hurts. They also find that RL is slower than best-of-n in KL terms, both at optimising and at over-optimising, and examine how the policy size and the KL coefficient affect the curves.

We can see the same shape in a ten-line experiment. The base policy produces candidates with true quality drawn from a standard normal distribution. The reward model sees the true quality plus an independent error with a heavy tail (a Student-t distribution with 3 degrees of freedom, scaled by 0.5), like a reward model that is usually right and occasionally wildly overrates something. Best-of-n picks the candidate with the highest proxy score. Its KL from the base policy is known exactly: log n − (n − 1)/n.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
# best-of-n against a proxy with heavy-tailed error (Gao et al.-style experiment).
def bon(nn, trials=3000, df=3, scale=0.5):
    qq = rng.normal(size=(trials, nn))                   # gold reward of each candidate
    proxy = qq + rng.standard_t(df, size=(trials, nn)) * scale
    pick = proxy.argmax(axis=1)
    rows = np.arange(trials)
    return qq[rows, pick].mean(), proxy[rows, pick].mean()

ns = [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096]
d_list, gold, prox = [], [], []
print("best-of-n against a noisy proxy (t-distributed error, 3 degrees of freedom)")
print(f"{'n':>6} {'KL':>6} {'d=sqrt(KL)':>10} {'proxy score':>12} {'gold score':>11}")
for nn in ns:
    g, p = bon(nn, trials=max(300, 400000 // nn))
    kl = np.log(nn) - (nn - 1) / nn                      # analytic KL of best-of-n from the base policy
    d = np.sqrt(kl); d_list.append(d); gold.append(g); prox.append(p)
    print(f"{nn:6d} {kl:6.2f} {d:10.2f} {p:12.2f} {g:11.2f}")
d = np.array(d_list); gold = np.array(gold)
# Gao et al.: R_bon(d) = d (alpha - beta d). Linear least squares on gold/d = alpha - beta d (d > 0)
m = d > 0
A = np.stack([np.ones(m.sum()), -d[m]], axis=1)
alpha, beta = np.linalg.lstsq(A, gold[m] / d[m], rcond=None)[0]
print(f"\nfit  gold(d) = d (alpha - beta d):  alpha = {alpha:.2f}, beta = {beta:.2f}; peak at d = alpha/(2 beta) = {alpha/(2*beta):.2f}, KL = {(alpha/(2*beta))**2:.2f} nats")

Read the table. At n = 64 (KL 3.2 nats) the gold score is 1.48, its maximum. Selecting from 4,096 candidates raises the proxy score from 3.4 to 11.3 while the gold score drops to 0.48, a third of the best. The fit puts the peak at d = α/(2β) = 1.71, a KL of about 2.9 nats, close to the table's peak. The lesson transfers even though the numbers do not: there is a finite amount of optimisation pressure a given reward model can take, and it is much less than the amount that maximises the reward model's own score. Larger and better-trained reward models push the peak outwards (Gao et al. report that the coefficients scale smoothly with proxy size), but they never remove it.

Common misconception

"If the reward keeps going up in the training log, the model is getting better." The training log shows the proxy. In the experiment above the proxy more than tripled over the same range in which the real quality fell by two thirds. The reward curve cannot tell you which regime you are in; only a measurement that does not come from the reward model can, such as held-out human or judge comparisons, a second independent reward model, or a spot check of generations.

Sycophancy

Sycophancy is the tendency of a model to tell users what they want to hear: agreeing with their stated opinion, praising weak work, changing a correct answer when challenged. Sharma et al. (2023, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models") found it consistently across five state-of-the-art assistants on four free-form generation tasks. In their analysis of existing human preference data, when a response matched the user's views it was more likely to be preferred, and both humans and preference models sometimes preferred convincingly written sycophantic answers to correct ones; optimising against preference models sometimes traded truthfulness for sycophancy.

This is a causal Goodhart failure with a clear source: agreeing correlates with being rated highly because raters are human, and neither raters nor reward models can always tell what is true. A simulation shows the dynamics. Each prompt has a user who states a belief that is right half the time. Candidate answers agree with the user or not; an answer is correct when it agrees with a right user or disagrees with a wrong one. How correct an answer looks is right only 65 percent of the time. Labellers (and so the reward model) judge from appearance and give a bonus of 0.6 to agreement. We fit the reward model by logistic regression and then run best-of-n.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
def sigmoid(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

# Each prompt: the user states a belief that is right half the time.
# Each candidate answer either agrees with the user (a=1) or disagrees (a=0).
# An answer is correct (c=1) when it agrees with a right user or disagrees with a wrong one.
def candidates(n_prompts, k):
    u = rng.random((n_prompts, 1)) < 0.5                  # user is right?
    a = rng.random((n_prompts, k)) < 0.5                  # answer agrees with user?
    c = (a == u)
    flip = rng.random(c.shape) < 0.35                      # how correct the answer *looks* is right only 65% of the time
    c_look = np.where(flip, ~c, c)
    return u[:, 0], a, c, c_look

# Labellers (and the reward model) judge from how correct an answer looks, and like being agreed with.
def label_pairs(m):
    u, a, c, c_look = candidates(m, 2)
    util = 1.0 * c_look + 0.6 * a
    win0 = rng.random(m) < sigmoid(util[:, 0] - util[:, 1])
    s = np.where(win0, 1.0, -1.0)
    d_c = (c_look[:, 0].astype(float) - c_look[:, 1]) * s; d_a = (a[:, 0].astype(float) - a[:, 1]) * s
    return np.stack([d_c, d_a], axis=1)

X = label_pairs(60000)
w = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(500):
    w += 1.0 * (X.T @ (1 - sigmoid(X @ w))) / len(X)
print("reward model weights  [looks correct, agrees with user] =", np.round(w, 2))

print(f"\n{'n':>4} {'P(agree)':>9} {'P(correct)':>11} {'P(correct | user wrong)':>24}")
for k in (1, 2, 4, 8, 32):
    u, a, c, c_look = candidates(100000, k)
    score = w[0] * c_look + w[1] * a
    pick = score.argmax(axis=1); r = np.arange(len(u))
    wrong = ~u
    print(f"{k:4d} {a[r, pick].mean():9.3f} {c[r, pick].mean():11.3f} {c[r, pick][wrong].mean():24.3f}")

Two lessons. First, a small preference becomes an extreme behaviour under enough pressure, just as length did. Second, aggregate accuracy hid the failure: overall correctness went up from 0.50 to 0.62 at n = 4 while correctness on the prompts where the user was wrong fell from 0.50 to 0.40. Slicing the evaluation by the condition that matters (user right, user wrong) is what exposes sycophancy; see error analysis in A6 for the habit of slicing.

Defences

No defence removes the problem; each moves the peak outwards or makes it visible earlier.

  • Limit the pressure. The KL leash of chapter 8 bounds how far the policy can move, the early-stopping point is read off a held-out measure, and a small learning rate means fewer steps through the dangerous region. In the best-of-n experiment the budget was a KL of roughly 3 nats.
  • Measure with something else. Hold out human comparisons, use an independent reward model trained on different data, or use a calibrated LLM judge. Whatever you optimise, do not use it as the only measure.
  • Ensembles and conservative objectives. Coste, Anwar, Kirk and Krueger (2023, "Reward Model Ensembles Help Mitigate Overoptimization") train several reward models and optimise a worst-case or uncertainty-penalised score. With 25 percent label noise added, they report that this practically eliminates over-optimisation for best-of-n, with gains of up to 70 percent, and reduces it for PPO.
  • Control the shortcuts you know about. Length-matched pairs in the training data, a length-normalised objective such as SimPO's, and evaluation that is controlled for length. For sycophancy, add pairs where the user is wrong and the correct answer disagrees.
  • Refresh the reward model. As the policy moves, collect new comparisons on its current outputs and retrain, so the reward model sees the region the policy now occupies. This is what iterative RLHF pipelines do.
  • Reward steps, not just endings. Lightman et al. (2023, "Let's Verify Step by Step") compare outcome supervision with process supervision, in which a reward model scores each reasoning step, on maths problems; they report that process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision, with the best process-supervised model solving 78 percent of a representative subset of the MATH test set, and they release PRM800K, 800,000 step-level human labels. Process rewards are more informative but cost more to label, and they are still a learned proxy.
  • Replace the proxy with a check. Where the answer can be verified by a program (a maths result, a unit test), use that. The DeepSeek-R1 paper (2025) reports that for R1-Zero the authors did not use an outcome or process neural reward model, because they found a neural reward model may suffer from reward hacking in large-scale RL, and retraining it adds cost and complexity. That is the subject of the next chapter.

Systems connection

Goodhart's law is the oldest problem in operations too. A team measured on tickets closed closes tickets, a service tuned to a latency percentile ignores the traffic outside it, and an SLO that measures the wrong thing turns an error budget into a licence to ship user-visible failures. The remedies are the same: measure what users feel, keep a second independent indicator, and watch for the behaviour the metric invites. In an LLM product, the ratings widget and the LLM judge in your ML system design are reward models in miniature; anything you later train on them inherits their blind spots.

Key takeaways

  • A reward model is a Transformer with a scalar head read at the last token, trained with the Bradley-Terry pairwise loss for one epoch. Its accuracy is capped by label noise: low-to-mid 70s percent is normal for human data.
  • It learns whatever predicts the labels. Where it sees quality through noise and length cleanly, it leans on length: here a weight ratio of 1.5 to 1 against the labellers' 2.5 to 1.
  • Goodhart's law has four faces (regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial) and all of them occur in RLHF.
  • Optimising against a proxy first helps and then hurts. Gao et al. fit gold reward as d(α − βd) for best-of-n and d(α − β log d) for RL, with d the square root of the KL. In the toy, the proxy more than tripled while real quality fell by two thirds.
  • The training reward curve cannot detect over-optimisation. Measure with something the optimiser cannot see.
  • Sycophancy is a mild rater preference amplified by optimisation; slice evaluations by whether the user is right.
  • Defences: KL limits, independent measurement, ensembles, shortcut controls, refreshed data, process rewards, and verifiers in place of learned rewards wherever the task allows.

Check your understanding

A team reports 95 percent held-out pairwise accuracy for a reward model trained on pairs labelled by crowd workers who agreed with each other about 73 percent of the time. What is the most reasonable reaction?

  1. Congratulate them: this is the expected performance of a strong reward model
  2. Ask them to train longer to close the last five points
  3. Be suspicious: the figure exceeds what noisy labels allow, so look for leakage between train and test, duplicated pairs or a shortcut feature
  4. Conclude that the labellers were unusually good
Label noise caps achievable accuracy on such data; even the true reward function in our toy scored 79 percent. A score far above the agreement rate usually means the test pairs overlap with training pairs or can be solved by a surface feature. Training longer would overfit; the labellers' agreement is known to be about 73 percent.

In the best-of-n experiment, going from n = 64 to n = 4,096 raises the proxy score from 3.4 to 11.3 but lowers the gold score from 1.48 to 0.48. What explains this?

  1. With more candidates, the highest proxy score increasingly comes from candidates with large positive proxy error, not high true quality
  2. The gold reward model is broken at large n
  3. The base policy gets worse as n grows
  4. Best-of-n changes the weights of the reward model
Selecting the maximum of many noisy scores selects noise (regressional Goodhart), and heavy tails make the extremes mostly error. Nothing about the base policy or the reward model changes: only the selection pressure does.

Labellers prefer longer answers mildly, and a trained reward model gives length relatively more weight than the labellers did. Which response addresses the cause?

  1. Train the reward model for more epochs so it fits the labels better
  2. Raise the KL coefficient to zero
  3. Use a larger learning rate during RL
  4. Add length-matched pairs to the data, or normalise or control for length in the objective and in the evaluation
The reward model leans on length because it sees length cleanly and quality noisily. More epochs overfit, and a zero KL coefficient removes the leash. Matching lengths in pairs removes the shortcut's predictive power, and length-controlled evaluation stops you from mistaking length for quality.

Using Gao et al.'s best-of-n form R(d) = d(α − βd) with α = 1.4 and β = 0.41, roughly what KL budget (in nats) maximises the gold reward?

  1. About 0.3
  2. About 3
  3. About 7
  4. About 17
The peak is at d = α/(2β) = 1.4 / 0.82 = 1.71, so KL = d2 = 2.9 nats. The values 7 and 17 are far beyond the peak, where the gold reward is falling; 0.3 would stop optimising almost immediately.

Why did the DeepSeek-R1 authors use rule-based rewards (answer correctness and format) rather than a neural reward model for R1-Zero?

  1. Neural reward models cannot read mathematics
  2. Rule-based rewards are always more accurate than human judgement
  3. They found a neural reward model may suffer from reward hacking in large-scale RL, and retraining it adds cost and complexity to the pipeline
  4. Rule-based rewards remove the need for a KL penalty
This is the reason given in the paper. A checkable reward cannot be exploited by flattery or length in the way a learned proxy can (though it has its own loopholes, see chapter 14). The other options overstate: rule-based rewards cover only checkable tasks and say nothing about the KL term.

In the sycophancy simulation, overall correctness rises from 0.50 to 0.62 at n = 4 while correctness on prompts where the user is wrong falls to 0.40. What does this teach about evaluation?

  1. An aggregate metric can improve while a critical subgroup gets worse, so evaluate on slices defined by the failure you worry about
  2. Sycophancy only appears at n above 100
  3. The overall metric is wrong and should never be reported
  4. Correctness on user-wrong prompts is irrelevant to users
Overall accuracy mixes prompts where agreeing is correct with prompts where it is wrong; agreement helps on the first and hurts on the second. The aggregate remains worth reporting, but it must be accompanied by the slice. The effect is already clear at n = 4.
