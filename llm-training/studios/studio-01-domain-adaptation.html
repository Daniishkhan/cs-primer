Adapt a Model for a Specialist Domain

Design the way a mid-sized insurer should give its claims handlers a specialist assistant: prompt a model, retrieve from the documents, fine-tune on past files, or combine them, within a budget of $25,000 and eight weeks. The core problems are diagnosing what the model gets wrong, pricing labels and serving honestly, and building the evaluation that decides.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the usual order for an AI system: clarify the problem and users, decide how success is measured, estimate tokens, cost and latency, design the data and the architecture, choose the model strategy, go deep on the risky parts, plan for failure, roll out, state the trade-offs and say it aloud. A rubric at the end separates mid-level from senior signals. All numbers in the worked answers are either computed by a script shown on the page or labelled as assumptions; GPU figures come from NVIDIA's datasheets as used in chapter 18 and chapter 22. For the general method see the design framework and Designing with ML & LLMs.

📐 The brief

A manager at a mid-sized commercial property insurer says: "Our claims handlers spend hours finding the right clause in 3,000 pages of policy wordings and a 600-page handling manual, then writing the same kinds of file notes. We want an assistant that answers wording questions with the clause it relied on, and drafts a structured triage note. We have 8 weeks, one engineer, a $25,000 budget for everything except salaries, and about 3,000 past claim files we can use. Should we fine-tune a model?"

  • Policy documents and claim files are confidential. They may not leave the company's own cloud tenancy, so the model is open-weights and self-hosted, or a hosted model running inside the tenancy under contract.
  • A wrong answer about cover can cost money or breach regulation, so a handler must be able to see the source clause for every claim the assistant makes.
  • Wordings change every year, and the handling manual changes every few months.
  • Builds on supervised fine-tuning, LoRA & QLoRA, evaluating a model you trained and RAG end to end. Estimating costs uses the economics of a token; the practice run is Lab 3: LoRA from scratch.
  • Time box: 60 to 90 minutes. Write your own answer to each stage before opening the worked one.

2Name what the model must learn

Split the assistant's job into facts, behaviours and format. For each, say which of prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning is the natural tool, and why.

Compare with a worked answer

A model can be wrong for three different reasons, and each has a different cure.

  • It does not know a fact. The 2026 wording of a flood exclusion was never in its pretraining data, or it is in there but blurred. The cure is to put the fact in the context: retrieval (RAG) or a long prompt. Fine-tuning on documents teaches a model to sound like them, but chapter 10 explains why facts absorbed this way are unreliable and cannot be cited or updated.
  • It does not behave the way you want. It answers when it should abstain, rambles, uses the wrong register, or ignores the escalation rules. The cure is instructions and examples first (prompting), then fine-tuning if the behaviour is consistent, learnable from examples and too long to fit in every prompt.
  • It cannot produce the format. The triage note must be valid JSON with eleven fields from a closed vocabulary. The cure is constrained decoding or a validator plus retry, with fine-tuning as the way to make the format cheap and reliable (a short prompt that never needs the schema pasted in).

Common misconception

"Fine-tuning teaches the model our documents." It changes the model's habits and style reliably and its recall of specific facts unreliably. Even when it does memorise a clause, the model cannot say where it came from, and the wording changes next quarter. Facts that change or need citing go in the context.

3Success metrics and the evaluation plan

What do you measure, on what data, labelled by whom, and how many items do you need before you can tell two systems apart? Decide this before you build anything.

Compare with a worked answer

The evaluation is the deliverable that decides everything else (chapter 23; building a domain benchmark; statistics for evals). Plan three layers.

  1. A gold set, built in week 1. 400 wording questions written or collected from real handler queries (search logs, help-desk tickets), each with the correct clause identifier, the correct answer in one or two sentences, and a label for "answerable from the documents" or "not answerable". Include about 20 percent unanswerable questions, because abstention is a core behaviour. Two senior handlers label each item independently; disagreements go to a third person. Record the agreement rate: if two experts disagree on 15 percent of items, no system can honestly score above that ceiling.
  2. Metrics that match the cost of errors. For retrieval: recall@5 of the gold clause (relevance metrics). For answers: correct and cited (the answer matches the gold answer, the cited clause supports it), unsupported-claim rate (statements not backed by the cited text), and correct-abstention rate on the unanswerable items. For triage notes: field-level exact match on closed-vocabulary fields, a rubric-graded quality score for the free-text summary, and JSON validity rate. Report each separately; one blended score hides the trade-off between answering and abstaining.
  3. A regression suite. 150 general items (instruction following, a short summarisation, a refusal case, a maths question) that the base model passes, so you notice if fine-tuning damages general skills (catastrophic forgetting).

The size question is not optional. If the two systems you compare differ by six points, how many questions do you need? The script below uses synthetic pass/fail outcomes (no real model) with a shared "difficulty" so that both systems tend to fail on the same hard items, which is how real systems behave, and runs a paired bootstrap on the difference.

"""How many test questions do we need to tell two systems apart? Paired bootstrap on SYNTHETIC outcomes (not real model results)."""
import random, math
random.seed(7)
def paired(n, pa, pb, corr=0.6):
    # correlated correctness: a shared "difficulty" latent makes systems fail on the same hard items
    out=[]
    for _ in range(n):
        hard = random.random()
        a = random.random() < pa*(1.25-0.5*hard) if corr else random.random()<pa
        b = random.random() < pb*(1.25-0.5*hard) if corr else random.random()<pb
        out.append((int(a),int(b)))
    return out
def boot(d, B=2000):
    n=len(d); diffs=[]
    for _ in range(B):
        s=0
        for _ in range(n):
            a,b=d[random.randrange(n)]; s+=b-a
        diffs.append(s/n)
    diffs.sort(); return diffs[int(.025*B)], diffs[int(.975*B)]
print("true gap about 6 points (RAG 0.62 vs RAG+fine-tune 0.68), synthetic")
for n in (100, 300, 1000):
    d = paired(n, 0.62, 0.68)
    ma = sum(a for a,_ in d)/n; mb = sum(b for _,b in d)/n
    lo,hi = boot(d, 1000)
    verdict = "distinguishable" if lo>0 else "cannot tell"
    print(f"n={n:5}: A {ma:.3f} B {mb:.3f} diff {mb-ma:+.3f}  95% CI [{lo:+.3f}, {hi:+.3f}]  {verdict}")
# unpaired normal approx for planning
p=.65; 
for n in (100,300,1000,3000):
    print(f"n={n:5}: half-width of 95% interval on ONE accuracy near 0.65 = +/-{1.96*math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n):.3f}")

  • With 100 questions you cannot see a six-point gain. The interval on the difference is about +/- 12 points wide, so a real improvement and no improvement look the same. With 300 you are still unsure; at 1,000 the interval excludes zero. A gold set of 400 can detect gaps of roughly 7 to 9 points, so decide beforehand which size of gain would change your decision and size the set to match.
  • Pairing helps. Both systems answer the same questions, so the comparison looks at items where they disagree. That is why the paired interval is narrower than two separate intervals would suggest.
  • Fix the rules before you see results. Write down the decision rule now: "adopt fine-tuning if it improves correct-and-cited by at least 5 points with the interval above zero, with no regression above 2 points on the general suite". Rules written after seeing numbers are rationalisations.
  • Keep a locked test set. Tune on 250 development items; open the 150 held-out ones once, at the end (leakage). Questions that were used to build prompts or synthetic training data do not count as held-out.

4Estimate cost and latency

What does each option cost to build and to run? Which cost is largest, and how many requests are needed before a cheaper-to-run model repays its build cost?

The four candidate designs differ in what they put in the prompt: A prompting only (policy text plus five examples in every request), B retrieval (the top four chunks), C a fine-tuned model with a short prompt, and D fine-tuning plus retrieval. Training is cheap on modern hardware; labels are not. The first script uses the memory arithmetic of chapter 2 and chapter 11 and the cost model of chapter 22. The H100 figures (989 TFLOPS dense bf16) are from NVIDIA's datasheet; the 30 percent utilisation, the $3 an hour, the $0.20 and $0.60 per million token prices and the label prices are assumptions.

  • At 2,000 requests a day, the serving saving never repays a $14,000 build. The case for fine-tuning at this scale must rest on quality (better abstention, better notes), not on cost. At 1,000,000 requests a day it repays in a month. The break-even volume is a number worth quoting early.

For a handler waiting at a desk, a first token within about two seconds is the target; all four options meet it on a single GPU at this volume.
  • Do not forget the quiet costs: the second index to maintain, a model registry and evaluation runs for every new wording release, and the on-call burden of one more GPU service.

5Data and context

What goes into the index, what goes into training, and what must stay out of both? How do you avoid leaking the test set into either?

Compare with a worked answer

There are two corpora and they must not be confused.

The retrieval corpus (changes monthly)

Policy wordings, endorsements, the handling manual and regulatory circulars. Chunk by clause, not by 500 tokens, because a clause is the unit a handler cites (chunking). Store with metadata: product, version, effective date, section number. Filter by the policy's version before searching, since the wrong year's wording is a more dangerous error than a missed one (filters and permissions). Use hybrid search because clause numbers and defined terms ("Escape of Water") are lexical (hybrid search).

The training set (changes rarely)

Examples of behaviour: a claim summary and the triage note a senior handler wrote; a question, retrieved clauses and a cited answer; a question with irrelevant clauses and the correct abstention. About 2,500 examples are plenty for style and format (chapter 10); quality beats quantity, so have a senior handler review a random 10 percent and measure the error rate.

Where the training data comes from. The 3,000 past notes are real but uneven: some are rushed, some wrong. Filter by handler seniority and outcome (no later reopened or overturned decision), strip personal data (privacy and data handling), and remove any claim that a gold question was derived from. A model can also draft extra examples from a stronger model's output, but that is a distillation with a licence question and a risk of copying its mistakes (chapter 15, the Alpaca case). Read the provider's terms first, and treat synthetic examples as unreviewed until sampled by an expert.

Where the leaks hide.

  • The same claim appearing in training notes and in a gold question (split by claim, and by policy where possible, not by row).
  • Retrieval chunks quoted in training answers, so the model learns to answer from its weights and stops reading the context. Mix in examples where the context contradicts what the model would otherwise say, and examples with distractor chunks.
  • Prompts tuned on the gold set (every tuning run on it spends part of its power to detect a difference).

Context management is also a design decision: how many chunks, in what order, with what instruction about untrusted text. Documents are data, not instructions; a wording PDF that says "ignore previous rules" must not be obeyed (prompt injection).

6High-level architecture

Sketch the system for the option you would build first. Which boxes change monthly, which change quarterly, and which must be outside the model?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Two independent change rates. The clause index is rebuilt when wordings change (monthly). The adapter changes only when the evaluation shows behaviour problems that prompting cannot fix (quarterly at most). Because the adapter is a separate 84 MB file on a frozen base (chapter 11), rolling back is replacing a file.
  • The checker is deterministic code, not another model. It verifies that every cited clause id was in the retrieved set, that quoted text appears in that clause, and that the triage JSON parses and uses the closed vocabulary. Cheap, testable, and independent of the generator's failure modes (assertions and eval harnesses).
  • Build order matters: (1) index and retrieval with a strong prompt; (2) the checker; (3) the evaluation harness; (4) only then, a fine-tuned adapter, if the error analysis asks for one. The arrow from "Audit log" to the evaluation set is how production traces become new gold items (the improvement flywheel).
  • Security: the retriever enforces the handler's permissions on documents, since an assistant that can read every claim file is a data leak waiting for a clever question (authorisation).

Notice what the ladder protects you from: spending three weeks on training data before finding out that the retriever never surfaced the right clause. The most common finding in domain-adaptation projects is that most of the error was in retrieval and context, and the fine-tune adds a few points on top. Do not decide that in advance; measure it with the gold set.

Analogy: hiring for a specialist desk

Giving the model the clause is like handing a capable new hire the policy binder open at the right page (retrieval). Fine-tuning is like sending them on a three-month course so they write notes the way the house does (behaviour). You would not send someone on a course to memorise the binder, and a course is no substitute for knowing which binder to open.

Where it breaks down: a new hire reads a clause; a model weighs it against whatever its weights say, and may ignore the binder entirely. That is why the checker and the abstention examples exist.

8Deep dive: the fine-tuning recipe and its risks

If you reach rung 3, what do you train, with what settings, and what do you check afterwards? Which risks come from the data and which from the method?

Compare with a worked answer

The recipe is conventional (SFT, LoRA); the judgement is in the data mix and the checks.

  1. Base model. An instruction-tuned open model of 7 to 8 billion parameters, chosen by running your dev set through two or three candidates, not by leaderboard rank (a dated note below). Larger is not obviously better: a 70B model needs at least 40 GB for its 4-bit weights alone, so two or more 24 GB cards, and the extra cost rarely pays for a formatting-and-abstention task.
  2. Method. QLoRA, rank 16, applied to all linear layers, learning rate around 2e-4 (LoRA wants a learning rate roughly ten times a full fine-tune's: see the findings in chapter 11), 2 to 3 epochs, cosine schedule, sequences up to 4,096 tokens, loss on the assistant tokens only (loss masking). Treat these as starting values to sweep, not recipes.
  3. Data mix. 60 percent triage-note examples, 25 percent cited answers with the retrieved clauses in the prompt, 10 percent abstention cases (irrelevant or missing clauses), and 5 percent general instruction data from an open set to hold onto general skills. The proportions are a starting guess; vary them in ablations.
  4. Check, in this order. Dev-set metrics from stage 3; the 150-item regression suite (a drop above 2 points is a stop); a read of 50 outputs by a senior handler; the unsupported-claim rate; the abstention rate on unanswerable items (it should go up, but not on answerable ones); and a test with the retrieval turned off to see whether the model recites from memory.
  5. Ablate, then freeze. Five runs at $4 to $15 each (rank, learning rate, data mix, epochs) cost less than an hour of anyone's time. Pick by dev score, then open the locked test set once.
Go deeper what a 24 GB card holds

For the same shapes as chapter 18, fit.py in the toolbox estimates that full fine-tuning of the 8B model needs about 132 GB (16 bytes per parameter plus activations), bf16 LoRA about 20 GB, and QLoRA about 8 GB. Only the last two fit in 24 GB, and the QLoRA run leaves room for longer sequences or a larger batch. These are estimates from the formulas, not measurements; measure peak memory on your own run with nvidia-smi.

The key safety property is that the assistant never decides: it drafts and cites, and the handler owns the decision. That product decision does more for safety than any model improvement, and it should be written into the brief (governance and regulation).

10Rollout and monitoring

How does it go live without surprising the handlers? What do you watch, and when do you stop?

Compare with a worked answer

Shadow first. For two weeks the assistant runs on live questions but handlers do not see its answers; a sample is scored against what the handler eventually wrote. Then a pilot with 15 volunteers who can mark each answer "useful", "wrong" or "unsafe" with one click, followed by staged expansion (deployment strategies).

  • Metrics in production: citation-valid rate (should be near 100 percent by construction), abstention rate (a sudden fall means the model is bluffing more), thumbs-down rate by product line, handler edit distance on the drafted note (a proxy for usefulness), p95 time to first token, and tokens per request.
  • SLOs: p95 first token under 2 seconds, 99.5 percent of requests get an answer or an explicit "cannot answer" (SLIs, SLOs and error budgets).
  • Change control: every new wording release triggers the gold-set run on the new index. Every adapter version is a candidate that runs the same evaluation, in shadow, against the live one. Old adapters stay available for rollback.
  • Stop conditions: unsupported-claim rate above 2 percent on the weekly sample, or any confirmed wrong-cover incident, pauses the assistant for that product line until the cause is found (AI incident response).

12Say it out loud: a five-minute summary

Say your design aloud in five minutes, then compare with the model summary.

Compare with a worked answer

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"Before choosing between prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning, I'd separate what the assistant gets wrong into facts, behaviour and format, because each has a different cure. Facts about wordings change monthly and must be cited, so they live in a versioned clause index. Behaviour and format, like abstaining and writing a triage note in house style, are what an adapter is good at. But I wouldn't decide that in advance. Week one is a 400-item gold set, labelled by two experts, with a pre-written rule such as 'adopt fine-tuning if it adds five points with an interval above zero and costs under two points on a general regression suite'. At 100 questions I can't see a six-point gain, so the set size is part of the plan.

Costs say the same thing. QLoRA on an 8B model fits one 24 GB card and trains in about an hour for a few dollars; the bill is labels, $12,000 or more for 3,000 reviewed examples. Serving savings from a shorter prompt are about 47 cents per thousand requests, so at 2,000 requests a day they never repay the build. At a million a day they repay it in a month. So at this volume the case for fine-tuning is quality, and I'd have to see it on the gold set.

I'd climb a ladder: strong prompt, then retrieval with a deterministic checker for citations and schema, then rewrite the prompt and add a reranker, and only then train an adapter on 2,500 reviewed examples including abstention and distractor cases. The assistant drafts and cites; the handler decides. I'd ship in shadow, then a pilot, watch citation validity, abstention rate and unsupported-claim rate, and re-run the evaluation for every new wording release."

Notice the shape: name the gap → build the test first → price every option, including labels → climb from cheap to costly with exit tests → checker outside the model → shadow, pilot, monitor.

Tick what your own design (before reading the answers) covered. The first items are what a solid mid-level answer has; the later ones separate a senior answer.

  • Asked what the model gets wrong today and split the job into facts, behaviour and format before picking a technique.
  • Put changing facts in retrieval and could explain why fine-tuning on documents gives unreliable, uncitable recall.
  • Made the evaluation the first deliverable: gold set, two labellers, agreement rate, locked test set and a regression suite.
  • Wrote metrics that match error costs: correct-and-cited, unsupported-claim rate and correct abstention, reported separately.
  • Sized the test set with an interval on the difference and knew 100 items cannot show a six-point gain.

  • Priced the options with labels, not GPU hours, as the main cost, and computed a break-even volume for cheaper serving.
  • Knew what fits on one 24 GB GPU (QLoRA of an 8B model) and gave the memory components.
  • Designed a deterministic checker outside the model for citations and schema, and a fail-closed path when retrieval is down.
  • Kept two change rates apart: monthly index, rarely changed versioned adapter with rollback.

Key takeaways

  • Diagnose the gap first: missing facts go to retrieval, consistent habits and format to fine-tuning, and anything that changes monthly stays out of the weights.
  • Build the evaluation before the system, size it for the difference you care about, and write the decision rule in advance.
  • On modern hardware training is cheap and labels are expensive; fine-tuning rarely repays itself in serving cost unless volume is very high.
  • Climb from cheap to costly with an exit test at each rung, and keep a deterministic checker outside the model.
  • The assistant drafts and cites; a person decides. That choice is the main safety mechanism.
