Design an LLM Serving Platform

Design one platform that serves many models and many tenants with token-streaming latency targets and a GPU bill that finance can live with. The core problems are sizing from memory traffic, routing for cache hits, scaling GPUs that take minutes to start, and keeping tenants out of each other's way.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the usual order for an AI system: clarify the problem and users, decide how success is measured, estimate tokens, cost and latency, design the data and the architecture, choose the model strategy, go deep on the risky parts, plan for failure, roll out, state the trade-offs and say it aloud. A rubric at the end separates mid-level from senior signals. For the general method see the design framework and Designing with ML & LLMs.

📐 The brief

A platform lead says: "We run an internal LLM service for 40 product teams and about 200 external customers with their own fine-tuned adapters. Today every team rents its own GPUs and most sit idle half the day. Build one serving platform. Interactive traffic needs a first token in under a second at p95 and fast streaming after that; there is a premium larger model for a few use cases, a stream of overnight batch jobs, and a monthly GPU bill that finance wants cut by a third without anyone noticing."

  • Traffic is lumpy: a diurnal curve plus occasional customer jobs that triple the load in minutes.
  • Tenants must not see each other's data, prompts, adapters or latency spikes.
  • GPUs are expensive and take minutes to start; they are not elastic like CPUs.
  • Builds on the KV cache, serving engines, the economics of a token, adapters on demand, queueing theory, overload and multi-tenancy. Practice: Lab 6: a serving simulator; case: vLLM and PagedAttention.
  • Time box: 60 to 90 minutes.

2SLOs and metrics

Define the service-level indicators for a token-streaming service. Why is 'requests per second' the wrong main metric?

Compare with a worked answer

A streaming response has no single latency, so split it (chapter 18; SLIs, SLOs and error budgets).

  • Measure what the client sees. Server-side timers that start after the queue hide the very delay you must control. Measure TTFT at the gateway; break it into queue, prefill and network (tracing).
  • Use percentiles, per tenant and per model. An average over 240 tenants hides the one with a long-prompt problem (metrics and percentiles).
  • Requests per second says nothing about work. A request with 8,000 input tokens costs about five times the prefill of a 1,500-token one. Track tokens in and tokens out per second, and the number of sequences in flight.
  • Error budget policy. 99.9 percent allows 43 minutes of failures a month. Spend it on deploys and experiments deliberately; if it is gone, stop risky rollouts.

3Estimate capacity, GPUs and the bill

How many requests per second does one GPU serve at your latency target? How many GPUs for each pool? What does it cost, and what does the flat-fleet number hide?

Compare with a worked answer

The script applies the decode model of chapter 18 (every step reads the weights once and each sequence's cache once) and the prefill FLOP count (about 2 × parameters × input tokens). H100 SXM figures (80 GB, 3.35 TB/s, 989 TFLOPS dense bf16) come from NVIDIA's datasheet; the 60 percent bandwidth efficiency, 50 percent arithmetic efficiency, 90 percent usable memory, request shapes and 60 percent prefix-cache hit are assumptions. Shapes are Llama-3-class (8B: 128 KiB of cache per token; 70B: 320 KiB).

"""Serving platform: per-GPU capacity from the chapter 18/22 decode model. Hardware: H100 SXM 80 GB, 3.35 TB/s, 989 TFLOPS dense bf16 (NVIDIA datasheet). Efficiencies, traffic and prices are ASSUMPTIONS."""
BW, PEAK, HBM = 3.35e12, 989e12, 80e9
BW_EFF, MFU, HBM_USE, GPU_HR = 0.6, 0.5, 0.90, 3.0
def cap(name, P, wbytes, kv_tok, gpus, inp, out, hit, itl_slo=0.05):
    """P params, wbytes per param, kv_tok bytes per token (kv bytes already per-GPU-shared), gpus per replica."""
    weights = P*wbytes
    free = gpus*HBM*HBM_USE - weights
    ctx = inp + out/2
    seq_bytes = ctx*kv_tok
    b_mem = int(free/seq_bytes)
    b_itl = int((itl_slo*gpus*BW*BW_EFF - weights)/seq_bytes)
    B = min(b_mem, b_itl)
    step = (weights + B*seq_bytes)/(gpus*BW*BW_EFF)
    prefill = 2*P*inp*(1-hit)/(gpus*PEAK*MFU)          # seconds of replica time per request
    decode = out*step/B                                  # this request's share of decode steps
    per_req = prefill+decode
    print(f"{name:26} weights {weights/1e9:5.0f} GB  batch limit: memory {b_mem:4}, ITL {b_itl:4} -> B={B:4}  step {step*1000:5.1f} ms")
    print(f"{'':26} per request: prefill {prefill*1000:6.1f} ms + decode share {decode*1000:6.1f} ms = {per_req*1000:6.1f} ms -> {1/per_req:5.1f} req/s per replica ({gpus} GPU)")
    return 1/per_req, gpus

  • The batch limit is memory first, not latency. The 8B model fits 258 sequences of 1,650 tokens (a 1,500-token prompt plus half the output) before HBM runs out; the 50 ms inter-token target would allow 390. A step at batch 258 takes 35.7 ms, which meets the target with room.
  • Prefill is a third to over a half of the time. At hit rate 0, 48.7 ms of the 90.3 ms per request is prefill; prefix caching at 60 percent cuts prefill to 19.5 ms and capacity from 11.1 to 16.4 requests per second per GPU, a 48 percent gain. Caching is capacity.
  • The 70B model is about 14 times dearer per request in GPU time: 0.43 s of a two-GPU replica against 0.061 s of a one-GPU replica, which is 7 times the time on twice the GPUs (and its requests are longer: 3,000 tokens in, 500 out). It earns its place only where quality justifies it.

4High-level architecture

Draw the platform. Where are the pools, where does the batch work wait, and what makes a request cross from one pool to another?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Gateway. Authenticates, applies per-tenant quotas on tokens (not requests), validates the model name, and attaches a priority class. It is the place to refuse work cheaply (load shedding; rate limiter).
  • Router. Two decisions: which model (the caller's choice, or a policy such as "try the 8B model first and escalate", stage 6) and which replica (stage 6). It keeps a live view of each replica's queue depth, in-flight sequences and free cache blocks.
  • Pools. Each replica runs one engine (vLLM or SGLang, chapter 20) with continuous batching and paged cache. The 8B pool's replicas hold the base weights and a handful of adapters on the GPU at a time, with the rest in host memory.
  • Batch. Overnight jobs go through a queue and run on idle capacity at lower priority. They are the way to buy utilisation: interactive load leaves holes, and batch fills them (durable logs and exactly-once for the queue's semantics).
  • Stores. Adapters and weights live in object storage with a local NVMe cache on each node, because loading 16 GB over the network at every cold start is minutes you do not have.

5The engine: batching, memory and adapters

Which settings on a replica control throughput and tail latency? What does continuous batching assume, and what can one bad request do to everyone else?

Compare with a worked answer

Inside a replica the engine is a scheduler (chapter 20). At every step it picks which sequences to advance, admits new ones if cache blocks are free, and either prefills a prompt or decodes tokens. Four dials matter.

  • Maximum concurrent sequences. More sequences raise throughput until the cache is full or step time exceeds the ITL target. The script put the knee at 258 sequences for the 8B model; set the limit slightly below the memory bound so a few long conversations cannot exhaust blocks and trigger preemption (evicting a sequence and recomputing it later).
  • Maximum context length. The cache is reserved per sequence as it grows, so a 128k-token request holds 17 GB (chapter 18) for as long as it decodes. Cap context per tier and charge long contexts more.
  • Chunked prefill. A 30,000-token prompt prefilled in one step stalls every other sequence's next token. Splitting prefill into chunks mixed into decode steps keeps ITL steady at some cost to the long prompt's TTFT (prefill versus decode inside one step).
  • Adapter slots. Per vLLM's documentation, --enable-lora turns serving on, --lora-modules registers adapters and the request's model field selects one; --max-loras bounds how many distinct adapters are active in one batch and --max-cpu-loras how many are cached in host memory. Set --max-lora-rank to the largest rank you serve. Adapters in a batch add a small computation per layer; mixed batches cost a bit more than base-only ones (chapter 17; measure it on your engine version).
# One replica of the 8B pool (illustrative; flags as documented for vLLM, check your version)
vllm serve <base-model> \
    --max-model-len 8192 \
    --max-num-seqs 256 \
    --gpu-memory-utilization 0.90 \
    --enable-prefix-caching \
    --enable-lora --max-loras 8 --max-lora-rank 16 --max-cpu-loras 64 \

curl http://localhost:8000/v1/chat/completions -H "Content-Type: application/json" \

I did not run this (no GPU or vLLM here); the flags are the ones in the vLLM documentation fetched in October 2026 plus standard engine options, and defaults change by version.

  • The blast radius of one bad request is the replica. A prompt that is too long, an adversarial request that maximises generated length, or a pathological adapter can raise ITL for hundreds of co-batched users. Enforce per-request limits at the gateway (max input, max output, max total) and a hard wall-clock cap on generation.
  • Cold adapters cost latency. The first request for an adapter not on the GPU waits for a load from host memory (84 MB, milliseconds over PCIe) or from disk (tens of milliseconds). Route a tenant's traffic to replicas that already hold the adapter (stage 6).

6Routing: which model, which replica

Write two routing policies, one choosing the model and one choosing the replica. What does each optimise and what can go wrong?

Compare with a worked answer

Model routing

Not every request needs the 70B model. A cascade tries the cheap model and escalates when a check fails (a confidence signal, a format error, a failed unit test); a router predicts difficulty from the request and chooses up front (cost, latency and quality trade-offs). The platform should expose this as a policy, not decide it for tenants: a tenant picks "fast", "balanced" or "best", the platform maps those to models and can change the mapping when a better model arrives. Validate the mapping on each tenant's own evaluation set, since a router trained on general data may be wrong for a legal-contract workload. Always keep an explicit model name option for tenants with regulated needs.

Replica routing

Within a pool, choose a replica. Least-loaded routing balances load. Cache-aware routing sends requests that share a prefix to a replica that holds it. The two conflict, and the script measures the conflict on a synthetic workload: 40 tenants with their own 1,000-token system prompts, 8 replicas each caching 6 tenants' prefixes, tenant popularity following a long tail.

"""Prefix-cache hit rate under two routing policies. SYNTHETIC workload: 40 tenants, each with its own 1,000-token system prompt; each replica's prefix cache holds 6 tenants' prefixes (LRU)."""
import random, hashlib
from collections import OrderedDict
random.seed(3)
TENANTS, REPL, SLOTS, N = 40, 8, 6, 60000
weights = [1/(i+1)**0.9 for i in range(TENANTS)]   # a few big tenants, long tail
def run(policy):
    caches = [OrderedDict() for _ in range(REPL)]
    hits = 0; load = [0]*REPL
    for _ in range(N):
        t = random.choices(range(TENANTS), weights)[0]
        if policy == "random": r = random.randrange(REPL)
        elif policy == "least-loaded": r = min(range(REPL), key=lambda i: (load[i], random.random()))
        else:  # tenant-affinity: hash tenant to a home replica (and the next one for the biggest tenants)
            h = int(hashlib.md5(str(t).encode()).hexdigest(), 16)
            r = (h + (random.randrange(2) if t < 4 else 0)) % REPL
        c = caches[r]; load[r] += 1
        if t in c: hits += 1; c.move_to_end(t)
        else:
            c[t] = 1
            if len(c) > SLOTS: c.popitem(last=False)
    spread = max(load)/(sum(load)/REPL)
    return hits/N, spread
for p in ("random", "least-loaded", "tenant-affinity"):
    h, s = run(p)
    print(f"{p:16} prefix hit rate {h:5.2f}   busiest replica carries {s:4.2f}x the mean load")
random           prefix hit rate  0.35   busiest replica carries 1.01x the mean load
least-loaded     prefix hit rate  0.35   busiest replica carries 1.00x the mean load
tenant-affinity  prefix hit rate  0.91   busiest replica carries 2.14x the mean load
  • Random and least-loaded routing find a cached prefix 35 percent of the time, because each replica sees all 40 tenants and its cache holds only 6.
  • Tenant-affinity routing lifts that to 91 percent, which by stage 3 is worth roughly half again as much capacity. The price is imbalance: the busiest replica carries 2.1 times the mean load, so the others idle while it queues.
  • The practical policy is affinity with a spill-over rule: send a tenant to its home replica unless that replica's queue exceeds a threshold, then to the next in a stable order. Spread large tenants over two or three home replicas. This is the same idea as consistent hashing with bounded loads for caches (distributed cache), applied to a cache whose misses cost GPU seconds rather than milliseconds.
  • Adapters add a second affinity key. A request for tenant X's adapter prefers replicas with that adapter loaded.

The cross-tenant danger is real. If tenants share a prefix cache, a request that hits the cache returns its first token sooner than one that misses. A tenant can therefore probe whether another tenant's system prompt, or a particular document, has recently been processed on that replica, by timing responses. The practical rules are to scope cache keys by tenant for external customers (accepting lower hit rates), to share only platform-owned prefixes, and to treat cache hits as sensitive in the same way as any timing side channel (multi-tenancy and isolation). Internal teams under shared trust can share caches by agreement. The rule is a policy decision to write down, with the cost in capacity measured, not a default to leave to the engine.

8Autoscaling GPUs

Why does reactive autoscaling fail on a sudden customer job? Design the scaling policy and say what each signal contributes.

Compare with a worked answer

GPU pools differ from web fleets in two ways: a new replica takes minutes (schedule, pull the image, load 16 GB of weights, warm up, assumed 240 s here), and a replica cannot be partly loaded. The script simulates a synthetic day: a diurnal curve (60 to 150 requests per second) plus a 15-minute tripling from 09:00 (a customer job), 16 requests per second per GPU, and four policies.

"""Autoscaling an 8B pool. SYNTHETIC day: diurnal load plus a 3x spike at 09:00 sharp (a customer batch job). Capacity 16 req/s per GPU (hit rate 0.6, s3_cap.py).
A request that arrives when demand exceeds ready capacity queues; we report the worst minute of overload, in multiples of ready capacity."""
import math
CAP, GPU_HR, COLD = 16.0, 3.0, 240          # req/s per GPU; $/GPU-h; cold start seconds: schedule, pull 16 GB image+weights, load, warm up (assumption)
def demand(t):                                # t in seconds since midnight
    h = t/3600
    base = 60 + 90*max(0, math.sin((h-6)/12*math.pi))      # 60 at night, 150 at 12:00
    spike = 2.0*base if 9*3600 <= t < 9*3600+900 else 0     # 15 minutes at 3x
    return base + spike
TL = {}
def sim(policy, target_util=0.6, floor=6):
    ready = [floor]*1; pending = []; gpus = floor
    cost = 0.0; worst = 0.0; bad_s = 0
    ready_n = floor
    for t in range(0, 86400):
        pending = [(due, n) for due, n in pending if due > t] if False else pending
        # arrivals of new GPUs
        for p in [p for p in pending if p[0] <= t]:
            ready_n += p[1]
        pending = [p for p in pending if p[0] > t]
        d = demand(t)
        if policy == "reactive":
            want = math.ceil(d/(CAP*target_util))
        elif policy == "reactive+headroom":
            want = math.ceil(d*1.5/(CAP*target_util))
        elif policy == "scheduled+reactive":
            sched = math.ceil(demand(9*3600+300)/(CAP*0.8)) if 8*3600+1800 <= t < 10*3600 else 0
            want = max(sched, math.ceil(d/(CAP*target_util)))
        elif policy == "static peak":
            want = math.ceil(max(demand(x) for x in range(0, 86400, 60))/(CAP*0.8))
        want = max(want, floor)
        total = ready_n + sum(n for _, n in pending)
        if want > total: pending.append((t+COLD, want-total))
        elif want < ready_n and t % 60 == 0:                 # scale down slowly: one GPU a minute
            ready_n -= 1
        if t % 60 == 0: TL.setdefault(policy, []).append((t, d, ready_n))
        ratio = d/(ready_n*CAP)
        if ratio > 1: bad_s += 1
        worst = max(worst, ratio)
        cost += (ready_n + sum(n for _, n in pending))*GPU_HR/3600
    return cost, worst, bad_s
print(f"{'policy':22} {'GPU-h cost/day':>15} {'worst demand/capacity':>22} {'seconds overloaded':>19}")
for pol in ("static peak", "reactive", "reactive+headroom", "scheduled+reactive"):
    c, w, b = sim(pol)
    print(f"{pol:22} {'$'+format(c,',.0f'):>15} {w:22.2f} {b:19,}")

  • Reactive scaling alone is cheap and fails: $746 a day, but for 240 seconds demand was up to 1.8 times capacity. For those four minutes, queues grow, TTFT blows through the 1 s target, and the load shedder has to refuse traffic (overload and metastability: a backed-up queue also holds on to cache and makes recovery slower).
  • Headroom costs 48 percent more and does not fix it: with a 1.5 times safety margin the day costs $1,105 and the worst moment is still 1.17 times capacity, because a margin is a ratio and the jump is 3 times.
  • A scheduled floor fixes what is predictable. If the customer tells you the job starts at 09:00, or history shows it, hold 30 GPUs from 08:30 to 10:00: $798 a day, zero overloaded seconds, about 7 percent above reactive. The flat peak fleet costs $2,160.
  • What does the autoscaler watch? Not CPU, which is meaningless for GPUs. Use queue depth and wait time (the earliest signal), free KV blocks (the capacity signal), and TTFT p95 (the outcome signal). Scale up on the first two; scale down slowly on utilisation with a cool-down, since flapping wastes the 4-minute load time and a cache that was warm.
  • For unpredictable spikes keep a small pool of warm spare replicas with weights already loaded (they can join in seconds), shed or defer batch and low-priority traffic immediately, and use per-tenant limits so that a spike from one customer cannot consume the whole spare capacity.

The arithmetic under all of this is queueing: when utilisation approaches 100 percent, waiting grows without bound (queueing theory for engineers). That is why the target utilisation in the autoscaler (60 percent here) is much lower than the fleet average you would like on the bill.

9Tenants, fairness and isolation

One tenant floods the service for 30 seconds. What happens to the others, and which mechanisms stop it? What must never be shared?

Compare with a worked answer

The script simulates a single replica that can serve 12 requests per second. Tenant A sends 4 per second steadily. Tenant B sends 20 per second between seconds 20 and 50. Arrivals are synthetic.

"""Noisy neighbour: one replica serving 12 req/s of work (SYNTHETIC arrivals). Tenant A sends 4 req/s steadily; tenant B floods at 20 req/s for 30 s.
Compare one FIFO queue with per-tenant fair queueing (round robin over tenants that have work) and with FIFO plus a per-tenant limit of 6 req/s."""
import random, statistics as st
random.seed(21)
RATE, T = 12.0, 90
def arrivals():
    ev = []
    t = 0
    while t < T:
        t += random.expovariate(4.0); ev.append((t, "A"))
    t = 20
    while t < 50:
        t += random.expovariate(20.0); ev.append((t, "B"))
    return sorted(ev)
def serve(policy, limit=None):
    ev = arrivals(); q = {"A": [], "B": []}; fifo = []; clock = 0.0; i = 0; waits = {"A": [], "B": []}; shed = {"A": 0, "B": 0}
    tokens = {"A": 6.0, "B": 6.0}; last = 0.0; turn = "A"
    while i < len(ev) or fifo or q["A"] or q["B"]:
        if not (fifo or q["A"] or q["B"]) and i < len(ev): clock = max(clock, ev[i][0])
        while i < len(ev) and ev[i][0] <= clock:
            t, who = ev[i]; i += 1
            if limit:
                tokens[who] = min(limit, tokens[who] + (t - last) * limit); last = t
                if tokens[who] < 1: shed[who] += 1; continue
                tokens[who] -= 1
            (fifo if policy != "fair" else q[who]).append((t, who))
        if policy != "fair":
            if not fifo: continue
            t, who = fifo.pop(0)
        else:
            order = [turn, "B" if turn == "A" else "A"]
            pick = next((w for w in order if q[w]), None)
            if pick is None: continue
            t, who = q[pick].pop(0); turn = "B" if pick == "A" else "A"
        waits[who].append(clock - t); clock += 1 / RATE
    return waits, shed
def p95(x): x = sorted(x); return x[int(0.95 * (len(x) - 1))]

  • In one shared queue, B's flood hurts A: A's p95 wait is 27.7 seconds, about as bad as B's own, although A did nothing wrong.
  • Fair queueing protects A (0.4 s) by alternating between tenants with work waiting. B pays: its p95 wait is 40 seconds, since it asked for more than its share. This is the behaviour you want.
  • A rate limit sheds B's excess (466 of B's requests were refused with a 429) and keeps everyone else's wait near zero. Which one to use is a product choice: queue and delay, or refuse early. Most platforms do both, with the limit in tokens per minute because a request's cost is its tokens, and a weighted fair queue across tenants inside the pool.
  • Priority classes. Interactive above batch above best-effort; batch work is preempted or paused whenever interactive queue wait approaches the SLO.

Common misconception

"Per-tenant rate limits on requests per second are enough." One request with a 100,000-token context and a 4,000-token output costs far more than a hundred small ones. Limit tokens in and out, concurrent sequences, and context length, not only request counts.

What must not be shared.

  • Prompts, outputs and logs. Encrypted at rest, retained for the contracted time, access-controlled, and never used for training without an agreement (data protection; privacy and data handling). Logging full prompts by default is the commonest breach.
  • Adapters. An adapter is derived from a customer's data and may encode it. Store per-tenant, authorise per request, and make sure routing cannot apply tenant X's adapter to tenant Y's request (a confused-deputy bug, authorisation).
  • Cache entries as stage 7 describes.
  • Hardware for the highest-sensitivity tenants. Siloed pools for customers who require them, priced accordingly: the silo-pool-bridge trade-off.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The cost of serving is memory traffic and utilisation, so I'd size from the decode model. One H100 serves a 1,500-token-in, 300-token-out request to an 8B model in about 90 milliseconds of GPU time, or 61 with a sixty percent prefix cache hit, which is 16 requests a second per GPU. Peak load of 150 a second needs 15 GPUs with 35 percent headroom; the 70B model costs about 14 times the GPU time per request and needs 18 GPUs for 12 a second. About 36 GPUs at peak is $79,000 a month flat and, if it follows load, in the low fifties. Adapters are 84 MB each, so 200 customers share one base model.

Three things make that number real. First, routing: sending a tenant's requests to the replica that holds its prefix lifts the cache hit rate from 35 to 91 percent but concentrates load, so I'd use affinity with spill-over. Second, autoscaling: a GPU takes four minutes to become useful, so a reactive scaler spent four minutes at 1.8 times capacity on a synthetic tripling; a scheduled floor ahead of known spikes plus a reactive scaler on queue depth cost seven percent more and had no overload. Third, tenants: a flood in a shared queue put the quiet tenant's p95 wait at 28 seconds; fair queueing and token limits brought it to under half a second.

I'd define SLOs on time to first token and inter-token latency measured at the gateway, scope caches by tenant for external customers because cache hits leak through timing, keep batch work as a yielding low-priority tenant that fills idle time, and watch quality continuously because infrastructure can be healthy while outputs drift."

Notice the shape: workload and SLOs → per-GPU capacity from the decode model → pools and adapters → cache-aware routing → autoscaling that respects cold start → fairness and isolation → quality watch.

Key takeaways

  • Serving cost is memory traffic and utilisation: size from the decode model, then raise utilisation with shared pools, caching and batch work that yields.
  • KV-cache memory sets the batch size; prefix caching and cache-aware routing convert repeated prompts into capacity.
  • GPUs take minutes to start, so autoscaling must be predictive or buffered; a scheduled floor plus a queue-based reactive scaler handles most spikes.
  • Fairness needs token-based limits and per-tenant queueing; isolation needs scoped caches, logs and adapters.
  • Healthy infrastructure can serve worse outputs; evaluate quality continuously.
