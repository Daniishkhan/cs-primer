Post-train a Small Reasoning Model

Plan the post-training of a small model that solves checkable arithmetic and coding tasks with reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards: choose the base model, the data, the reward and the compute, and design the monitors that would catch reward hacking before it ruins the run.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the usual order for an AI system: clarify the problem and users, decide how success is measured, estimate tokens, cost and latency, design the data and the architecture, choose the model strategy, go deep on the risky parts, plan for failure, roll out, state the trade-offs and say it aloud. A rubric at the end separates mid-level from senior signals. All numbers in the worked answers are either computed by a script shown on the page or labelled as assumptions; GPU figures come from NVIDIA's datasheets as used in chapter 18 and chapter 22. For the general method see the design framework and Designing with ML & LLMs.

📐 The brief

A lead says: "We want our own small reasoning model for two jobs: multi-step arithmetic and quantity problems that come up in invoices and spreadsheets, and short Python functions that must pass unit tests. We cannot send this to a frontier API at the volume we need, and we want to own the weights. We have four weeks, one engineer, and about $2,500 of GPU time on rented H100s. Plan the post-training run, and tell me how you will know the numbers are not lying."

  • Answers are checkable by a program: a final number, or a function that passes tests. That is the setting for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.
  • Whatever the model is rewarded for, it will try to get by the cheapest route. You are responsible for the route.
  • The output must be a model of 1.5 to 8 billion parameters that serves on one 24 GB GPU after quantisation.
  • Builds on policy gradients, PPO and KL penalties, reward models and reward hacking, RLVR and GRPO and distillation. Cases: DeepSeek-R1 and the sycophancy rollback. Practice: Lab 4: DPO and GRPO on a toy policy.
  • Time box: 60 to 90 minutes.

1Clarify the problem

What does 'reasoning model' mean for this team? List the questions you ask, and decide what is checkable and what is not.

Compare with a worked answer

"Reasoning" is an overloaded word. Here it means a model that writes a chain of intermediate steps before a final answer and is rewarded on whether the final answer is right (chapter 14), not a model that merely imitates worked solutions.

2Success metrics and the evaluation plan

Which sets and which numbers will you report? How do you keep the training reward from being your only evidence?

Compare with a worked answer

The training reward is the wrong thing to report, because the policy is optimised against it (Goodhart's law in chapter 13). Evidence must come from checks the policy never saw as a reward.

  1. Held-out in-distribution set. 500 prompts from the same sources as training, never used for training, tuning or filtering. Report pass@1 averaged over 8 samples per prompt (the sampling noise at temperature 1 is large) with a paired bootstrap interval against the base model (statistics for evals).
  2. Shifted set. 200 prompts with rephrased surface features: different names, units and layouts, or more steps. Performance that holds in-distribution and fails here has memorised patterns.
  3. Strict re-verification. The final scorer on all eval sets is stricter than the training reward: exact answer match with units, a second independent solver, unit tests that include hidden edge cases. The training verifier and the evaluation verifier should not share code.
  4. Behaviour statistics. Mean and distribution of response length, fraction of responses that contain visible reasoning, fraction that hit the length limit, language mix, and repetition rate. A change in these precedes a change in the score.
  5. Regression suite. A general instruction-following and knowledge set; a loss beyond 3 points fails the run (chapter 23).
  6. Contamination check. n-gram overlap of every training prompt with every eval item and with common public benchmarks, because many public maths and code problems circulate in pretraining data (contamination). Report the overlap found.

Report pass@1 for what users get, and pass@k (any of k samples correct) only as a diagnostic: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards often raises pass@1 while pass@k at large k changes little, meaning the model got better at finding answers it could already reach, not at reaching new ones. The research on this is contested; the safe wording is to measure both and not claim new capability from pass@1 alone.

3Pick the base model

Which model do you start from, and what are the three properties you check before committing compute?

Compare with a worked answer

The choice is made by measuring, in half a day, not by reputation. Three properties matter.

  • Starting pass rate. A group-relative method learns only from groups where some completions pass and some fail (chapter 14). If the model's per-problem pass rate is near 0 or 1, groups are all-equal, the advantage is zero, and nothing is learned. The table is computed from the binomial: with 8 samples per prompt, the chance that a group has no signal is p8 + (1−p)8.
"""Post-training a small reasoning model with GRPO: compute budget. Assumptions marked; H100 figures from NVIDIA datasheet (3.35 TB/s, 80 GB, 989 TFLOPS dense bf16)."""
BW, PEAK, HBM = 3.35e12, 989e12, 80e9
GPU_HR = 3.0
def plan(name, P, layers, kvh, dh, prompts=64, G=8, resp=1200, steps=400, bw_eff=0.6, mfu=0.30):
    kv_tok = 2*layers*kvh*dh*2                      # bytes per token, bf16
    rollouts = prompts*G
    # generation: all rollouts decoded together in waves limited by memory
    free = HBM*0.35 - 2*P                           # share of HBM for cache when colocated (assumption)
    seqs = int(min(rollouts, free/(kv_tok*(resp/2+300))))   # average context ~ prompt 300 + half the response
    step_s = (2*P + seqs*(resp/2+300)*kv_tok)/(BW*bw_eff)
    waves = rollouts/seqs
    gen_s = waves*resp*step_s
    toks = rollouts*(resp+300)
    train_s = 6*P*toks*1.33/(PEAK*mfu)
    per_step = gen_s+train_s
    hrs = per_step*steps/3600
    train_mem = 16*P/1e9
    print(f"{name}: KV {kv_tok/1024:.0f} KiB/token, rollouts/step {rollouts}, concurrent seqs {seqs}")
    print(f"   generate {gen_s:6.0f} s  train {train_s:5.0f} s  -> {per_step:6.0f} s/step; {steps} steps = {hrs:6.1f} H100-h = ${hrs*GPU_HR:7,.0f}")
    print(f"   full-parameter state 16 B/param = {train_mem:.0f} GB (80 GB card: {'fits with room for cache' if train_mem<45 else 'needs sharding or LoRA'})")

  • Read the last block. A problem the model solves 2 percent of the time wastes 85 percent of its groups; one it solves half the time wastes under 1 percent; one it solves 90 percent of the time wastes 43 percent. The useful band is a per-problem pass rate of roughly 20 to 80 percent, so filter the prompt set to that band using the starting model, and refresh it as the model improves (dynamic sampling).
  • Base or instruct? A base model that already shows step-by-step habits under a prompt template, or an instruct model that already reasons in text, both work. A base model that produces rambling or off-format text gives the verifier nothing to reward, so a short SFT warm-up on a few thousand worked examples (stage 5) may be needed first. Pick by measured pass rate band: you want 40 to 60 percent of your prompts inside it.
  • Size. The first block above gives the cost of 1.5B-shaped and 7B-shaped models. The 1.5B model trains in a day on one card; the 7B model's full-parameter state is 122 GB at 16 bytes per parameter (chapter 2), so it needs LoRA or sharding. Findings summarised in chapter 11 report that LoRA matches full fine-tuning for reinforcement learning, even at low rank, on the runs they report, which is a reason to use LoRA at 7B, with the caveat that generalisation beyond those tasks is not established.
  • Licence, tokeniser and serving. Check that the licence allows what you plan, that the tokeniser handles digits sensibly (digit-by-digit tokenisation helps arithmetic), and that your serving engine supports the architecture (chapter 20).

Decision for this brief: start with a 1.5B-class model for the first four weeks' ablations (cheap enough to run 15 times), and move one winning recipe to a 7B-class model with LoRA for the final run if the budget allows. 4Estimate the compute and the bill

How many rollouts per step, how long does one step take, how many steps can you afford, and what overheads does the arithmetic hide?

Compare with a worked answer

The first script (above) applies the decode model of chapter 18 to generation and the 6PD rule to the gradient step. H100 figures (80 GB, 3.35 TB/s, 989 TFLOPS dense bf16) are from NVIDIA's datasheet; the 60 percent bandwidth efficiency, 30 percent utilisation, the 35 percent of memory given to the generation cache, 1,200-token responses, 64 prompts of 8 samples, and $3 an hour are assumptions. Re-read its first block:

  • Per step, 512 rollouts of about 1,200 tokens: 1.5B-shaped model, 10 s to generate and 32 s to train, 42 s per step; 400 steps take 4.6 H100-hours or about $14. For the 7B-shaped model, 191 s per step, 21 H100-hours, about $64 for 400 steps (with LoRA, since full state does not fit).
  • Training, not generation, dominates in this model because rollouts are batched across 248 to 512 sequences and decode is cheap per token at that batch (chapter 18). In real systems generation often dominates, because of long-tail responses that hold a batch open, weight synchronisation between trainer and sampler, and idle time. Treat the figures as a lower bound.
  • Multiply by overhead. Take 3 times for synchronisation, stragglers and evaluation, 15 runs for ablations and failures, and another 2 times for the final long run. Even then the 1.5B plan is a few hundred dollars. The $2,500 does not constrain the number of GPU hours; it constrains the number of mistakes you can afford to find too late. That argues for cheap, short, heavily monitored pilot runs.
  • The binding constraint is people. Building and hardening the verifiers, curating prompts, and reading samples take the four weeks. If compute is cheap, spend your hours on the verifier.

5Data: prompts, difficulty and the warm-up

Where do the 20,000 prompts come from, how do you filter them, and do you need supervised examples first?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Prompts need answers or tests, not solutions. That is the advantage of this setting. For arithmetic problems, generate variants programmatically (templates over names, quantities, units) with a ground-truth computed by code. For code tasks, each prompt needs unit tests, preferably with both visible and hidden cases; hidden ones are what stop the policy from special-casing the visible examples.
  • Dedupe and decontaminate. Remove near-duplicates between prompts (MinHash, as in Lab 1) and against all evaluation sets.
  • Difficulty filter. Sample 8 completions per prompt from the starting model, keep prompts with pass rates between roughly 0.2 and 0.8, and keep a small share of harder ones for later. This turns the 20,000 prompts into perhaps 6,000 that give gradient signal at the start.
  • Curriculum or mixture. Easy-to-medium first, then add harder prompts as the model improves; keep both task families in every batch so that one does not forget the other.
  • Cold-start SFT or not? If the base model does not already produce step-by-step text in a parseable format, run a small SFT on 2,000 to 5,000 reasoning traces first. The traces can be your own model's best samples that pass the verifier (rejection sampling, which uses only your own compute), or distilled from a stronger model if its licence allows (chapter 15). The DeepSeek-R1 report describes both a pure-RL variant and a variant with a small cold-start dataset (case C2); the second was preferred for readability. Whether you need it depends on the base model's measured behaviour.

6Reward design and the verifier

Write the reward. For each rule, name the loophole it closes and the new loophole it might open.

Compare with a worked answer

The reward is a program, so it has bugs, and the policy will find them faster than a reviewer. The next script shows a first-draft "lenient" reward (gold answer appears anywhere) beside a strict one, on six hand-written completions standing in for model output.

"""A lenient reward and a strict verifier, side by side, on hand-written completions (a stand-in for sampled model output)."""
import re
GOLD = "42"
def lenient(text):
    # tempting first draft: reward 1 if the gold string appears anywhere
    return 1.0 if GOLD in text else 0.0
def strict(text):
    # needs reasoning of some length, exactly one final \boxed{...} at the end, and the number inside must equal the gold
    boxed = re.findall(r"\\boxed\{([^{}]*)\}", text)
    body = text.split("\\boxed")[0]
    if len(boxed) != 1 or not text.rstrip().endswith("}"):
        return 0.0
    if len(body.split()) < 15:
        return 0.0
    try:
        return 1.0 if abs(float(boxed[0].replace(",", "")) - float(GOLD)) < 1e-9 else 0.0
    except ValueError:
        return 0.0
cases = {
 "honest, correct": "Six boxes of seven pens is 6 * 7 = 42 pens. Then I check that no pens are damaged, so the total stays the same. \\boxed{42}",
 "honest, wrong": "Six boxes of seven pens: 6 + 7 = 13. Then I add the 5 spare pens to get 18 pens in total, after a quick check. \\boxed{18}",
 "answer first, no reasoning": "42 \\boxed{42}",
 "dumps many numbers": "Candidates: 12, 42, 17, 90, 3, 8, 77, 30, 2, 64, 100, 9, 15, 21. Final answer: \\boxed{9}",
 "guesses then boxes elsewhere": "It is probably 42 or maybe 40, I am not sure how to compute the pens in six boxes of seven. \\boxed{40}",
 "multiple boxed answers": "Try one: \\boxed{42}. Hmm, or maybe \\boxed{41} after counting the six boxes of seven again with some care.",
}

  • The lenient rule rewards four behaviours that are not solving the problem: giving the answer with no reasoning, listing many numbers, guessing in prose while boxing something else, and stating several answers. A policy needs only a few percent of lucky hits to start moving towards the cheapest of these.
  • The strict rule closes them with one parseable final answer, a minimum amount of reasoning text, and exact numeric comparison. Each rule has a cost: the minimum-length rule penalises a correct terse answer to an easy question, so set it from the length distribution of correct answers, not by taste; the single-box rule can punish a model that corrects itself mid-answer.
  • Prefer outcome rewards, add few shaping terms. A small format reward (+0.1 for one parseable answer) helps the first hundred steps, then can be switched off. Length penalties and process rewards are available but each is a new proxy to hack (chapter 13).
  • Code verifiers are the risky ones. Run model-written code in a sandbox without network or filesystem access, with CPU, memory, and wall-time limits (containers and VMs). Known hacks include printing the expected output by reading the test file, calling sys.exit(0) so the harness records success, patching the assertion function, and special-casing visible tests. Keep tests in a separate process the solution cannot import, check the test runner's own exit status and report, and use hidden tests the policy never sees.
  • Test the verifier before training. Write 50 adversarial completions by hand (and have a teammate write 50 more) and check that the reward gives zero. Treat the verifier as production code with its own test suite and version number; a verifier change mid-run changes the objective.

7Architecture of the training system

Draw the loops. Which parts are in the training loop and which must stay outside it?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Sampler and trainer. Generation uses an inference engine such as vLLM, which is far faster than the training framework's own generate; TRL's GRPOTrainer supports vLLM in a colocate mode (sharing the training GPUs) and a server mode (separate GPUs), per its documentation fetched in October 2026. The weights must be copied to the sampler every step or every few steps; the policy used to sample must be the policy being updated or the update is off-policy and needs correction.
  • Verifier service. Stateless, parallel, sandboxed, with a timeout. A hung test case must not stall 512 rollouts, so score with a deadline and give timeouts a zero reward.
  • The monitoring loop has no path back into the reward. If you later use audit results to patch the reward, that is a new reward version: restart the run or record the change as a new phase.
  • Logging. For every step: mean reward, reward distribution by task, fraction of all-equal groups, length mean and quantiles, truncation rate, KL to the reference (or entropy when no reference is loaded), clip fraction, gradient norm, and a dump of 20 random completions with their rewards. The dump is the most valuable log you will keep.

8The training recipe

Choose the algorithm settings. Which defaults would you trust, which would you sweep, and what would make you stop a run?

Compare with a worked answer

The mechanism is GRPO (chapter 14): sample a group of G completions for each prompt, score them, and use each completion's reward relative to its group's mean (and standard deviation) as its advantage, with a clipped policy-gradient update (chapter 8). No critic is trained.

from trl import GRPOConfig, GRPOTrainer

def reward_func(completions, ground_truth, **kwargs):
    # called with a batch of completions; dataset columns other than the prompt arrive as keyword arguments
    return [strict_verify(c, gt) for c, gt in zip(completions, ground_truth)]   # your strict verifier, one float per completion

args = GRPOConfig(
    num_generations=8,            # group size G
    max_completion_length=2048,   # longer than the 512 default so correct long reasoning is not truncated
    learning_rate=1e-6,           # the documented default; sweep around it
    beta=0.0,                     # no reference model loaded (the documented default); raise if the policy drifts
    use_vllm=True, vllm_mode="colocate",
    per_device_train_batch_size=8,
)
trainer = GRPOTrainer(model="<your base model>", reward_funcs=reward_func, args=args, train_dataset=train_ds)
trainer.train()

The listing follows the quick-start and the argument defaults in TRL's GRPO documentation as fetched in October 2026; I did not run it (no GPU here). In words, it would print a step-by-step training log of reward, completion lengths and loss, and write checkpoints; read the logging list from stage 7 into the callbacks.

  • Group size. With the binomial table above, G=8 loses little at mid-range pass rates and a lot at the extremes. G=16 halves the dead-group fraction at pass rate 0.1 (from 43 to 19 percent) at double the generation cost, which can pay on hard prompts.
  • KL and reference. Recent practice, and TRL's default, is no KL term (beta zero), relying on small learning rates and clipping; the chapter-14 box lists the current defaults and warns that library "GRPO" differs from the original paper's. A KL penalty is the first dial to turn if the audit shows drift (stage 9).
  • Dynamic sampling and overlong handling. Dropping all-equal groups and not punishing truncated responses as if they were wrong are two ideas from the DAPO work referenced in the chapter; check which your library version does, because they change the objective.
  • Stability. Watch entropy: a steady fall to near zero means the policy has stopped exploring, and a spike with exploding gradient norm means a bad update. Roll back to the last checkpoint rather than hoping.
  • Stop rules, written now: stop and inspect if the lenient-versus-strict gap exceeds 10 points, if mean length falls 30 percent from its start or doubles, if truncation exceeds 20 percent, or if the regression suite drops more than 3 points.

9Catching reward hacking

Design the monitors. What signals appear before the score collapses, and what do you do when one fires?

Compare with a worked answer

Reward hacking is the policy finding a way to score without doing the task (chapter 13); the verifiable setting makes it rarer than with a learned reward model, not impossible, as stage 6 showed. The monitoring plan has four layers.

  1. A second, stricter check on a sample. Score a 5 percent random sample of rollouts with a verifier that differs in implementation and strictness. The training reward minus this audit reward is the gap.
  2. Behaviour drift statistics. Length, share of very short responses, reasoning present, repetition, and the all-equal-group fraction. Hacks often shorten or simplify the output.
  3. Human reading. 30 completions per day, drawn from the top reward decile and from random, read by a person. The top decile is where hacks sit.
  4. Held-out evaluation with the strict scorer every 50 steps, plus the shifted set. The checkpoint selected for release is chosen on these, not on training reward.

The simulation below makes the layers concrete. It generates synthetic logs for a run in which 512 rollouts per step are scored by a lenient checker, with a hack that the model starts using at step 220 (answer-first, short responses). No real model was trained.

"""Catching reward hacking: SYNTHETIC training logs. The proxy reward is lenient; a strict held-out check is applied to a 5% audit sample.
Nothing here is a real training run; it shows what the monitoring signals look like if a hack takes hold at step 220."""
import random, statistics as st
random.seed(11)
def log_step(step):
    hack = 0.0 if step < 220 else min(0.7, (step-220)/200)   # fraction of completions exploiting the lenient parser
    rows=[]
    for _ in range(512):
        h = random.random() < hack
        honest = random.random() < min(0.75, 0.30 + step/900)  # true skill slowly improves
        proxy = 1 if (honest or h) else 0                      # lenient checker accepts "answer: N" anywhere, even unjustified
        strict = 1 if honest and not h else 0                  # strict checker needs the final boxed answer after reasoning
        length = int(random.gauss(900 if not h else 280, 120 if not h else 60))
        rows.append((proxy, strict, max(length, 20), h))
    return rows

Go deeper why the gap, not the level

The policy is optimised against the lenient reward RL. Let RS be the strict one. For honest solutions RL = RS; for hacks RL = 1 and RS = 0. The mean gap equals the hack fraction, so the gap measures how much of the reward is coming from loopholes, whatever the underlying skill. This also bounds your correction: if the gap is 0.4, at most 60 percent of the reward signal was informative, and the update was mostly learning the hack.

The same shape appears when the reward is learned: the sycophancy rollback is a case in which a signal that looked good in aggregate rewarded a side effect. The monitor that would have helped was a check the optimiser was not trained against.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The tasks are checkable, so this is reinforcement learning with a program as the reward: sample eight answers per prompt, reward the ones that pass, and push the policy towards them relative to the group. Compute is not the constraint; the arithmetic says a 1.5B-sized model needs a few dollars of GPU per 400 steps, a 7B-sized one tens of dollars with LoRA, and even with three-times overheads and fifteen runs it fits comfortably. What can go wrong is the reward. So the work is in the verifier and the monitoring.

I'd pick the base by measurement: filter prompts to a per-problem pass rate between about 20 and 80 percent, since a group where every sample passes or fails gives zero gradient and a problem solved 2 percent of the time wastes 85 percent of its groups. I'd write a strict verifier first, test it with a hundred adversarial completions, and run model-written code in a sandbox. Then a short pilot with a dozen logged numbers and a daily read of thirty samples.

To catch hacking, I'd never trust the training reward. A second stricter check on a five percent sample, held-out and shifted sets scored by a different verifier every fifty steps, and behaviour statistics like length and all-equal-group fraction. In a simulated run, the training reward climbs to 0.92 while the strict score falls to 0.22; the gap alarm fires at step 240 and the length alarm 120 steps later, so I'd use several signals and roll back to the last good checkpoint. I'd select the release by strict held-out score, quantise and re-evaluate, and shadow it before it takes traffic."

Notice the shape: checkable task → measure the base and filter prompts → strict, tested verifier → cheap pilots with many logs → independent hack monitors → release on held-out strict score.

Key takeaways

  • With a verifiable reward, compute is cheap and mistakes are expensive: put the effort into the verifier and the monitors.
  • Group-relative learning needs groups with mixed outcomes, so measure the base model and keep prompts in the 20 to 80 percent pass-rate band.
  • Never trust the training reward. Compare it with a stricter independent check; the gap between them is the alarm.
  • Use several signals together (gap, length, entropy, samples read by a person) and write stop and rollback rules before the run.
  • Choose the released checkpoint on held-out strict scores, and re-evaluate after quantisation.
