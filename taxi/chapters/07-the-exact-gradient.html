The Exact Gradient

Nudging measures a slope; calculus gives it. For the mean squared error of a linear model, every slope is one short formula that takes a single pass over the rides. You write it, and you trust it because it agrees with nudging.

Where we are

Step 6 gave the program a slope for any function and a step downhill: on the toy bowl, 200 nudged steps landed at (3.0000, −1.0000). The loss of step 4 is also a function of the knobs, so the same nudging works on it. Here is ride 4 alone, at step 4's knobs, with a count of how often the loss is asked for. Run it, then change 1e-4 to 1e-14 and run it again.

distance, minutes, passengers, fare = (7.70, 25.9, 1, 27.0)   # ride 4
x = [1.0, distance, minutes]
calls = 0

def loss(w):
    """The squared miss on ride 4 alone, for knobs w."""
    global calls
    calls += 1
    return (sum(a * b for a, b in zip(x, w)) - fare) ** 2

def nudge(f, w, eps):
    """The slope of f at w, one weight at a time."""
    slopes = []
    for i in range(len(w)):
        up, down = list(w), list(w)
        up[i] += eps
        down[i] -= eps
        slopes.append((f(up) - f(down)) / (2 * eps))
    return slopes

w = [2.5, 2.0, 0.0]
print(f"loss at w = {w}: {loss(w):.2f}")
calls = 0
for i, slope in enumerate(nudge(loss, w, 1e-4)):
    print(f"slope of weight {i}: {slope:9.3f}")
print(f"the loss was asked for {calls} times")

The wall

Three slopes took six calls to the loss, two for each weight. On the real rides each call is a pass over every ride, so on the check's 500 rides that is six passes for three numbers, and training will want a fresh slope at every step. The answer also depends on the nudge. At 1e-14 the printed slopes are visibly wrong, because the two losses being subtracted agree in all but their last few digits, and dividing that rounding noise by 2e-14 magnifies it. You need the slope itself, as a formula: exact, and in one pass over the rides.

The idea

Look at the three slopes beside ride 4's numbers. The ride is x = [1.0, 7.7, 25.9], and at these knobs the guess is $17.90 against a meter of $27.00, a miss of −9.10. Divide each slope by its x and you get −18.2 every time, which is 2 × (−9.10). So on this ride the slope of weight j is 2 × miss × xj.

The reason is the chain rule: the slope of a chain is the product of the slopes of its links. Nudge weight j and the prediction moves by xj times the nudge, because the weight is multiplied by xj. Nudge the prediction by δ and the squared miss goes from miss2 to miss2 + 2 · miss · δ + δ2, which is about 2 · miss · δ when δ is small. Multiply the two slopes and 2 × miss × xj appears.

With many rides the loss is the average of the squared misses, so its slope is the average of such slopes. That is the partial derivative of the loss with respect to wj: its slope with the other weights held still. Here f̂i is the prediction for ride i, fi its fare, xij the value of feature j in ride i, and n the number of rides:

∂L / ∂wj = (2 / n) ∑ (f̂i − fi) xij

Read it as a vote. Every ride pushes weight j in proportion to its miss and to how much of feature j it had. An undercharged ride (a negative miss) pushes the slope negative, so the step against it raises the weight. One pass over the rides gives every slope at once, however many weights there are. Step 8 puts it in a loop, and step 12 finds such gradients for any model built from simple operations.

Your move

One function: the gradient of the loss, written as a formula. It must agree with numeric_grad and cost a single pass over the rides.

def grad_mse_linear(X, y, w):
    """The exact gradient of mse(matvec(X, w), y) with respect to w:
    for weight j, (2/n) * sum over rides of (prediction - fare) * X[i][j].
    Agrees with numeric_grad to many decimal places, and is cheaper."""

The file holds numeric_grad and matvec, which you may use. Press ▶ Check to run check.py 7.

The check compares your gradient with numeric_grad (nudging by 1e-4) on the first 500 rides at w = [2.5, 2.0, 0.0], one weight at a time. It also tries one ride on paper: with X = [[1.0, 2.0]], y = [5.0] and w = [1.0, 1.0] the miss is −2, so the gradient is 2 × (−2) × [1, 2] = [−4, −8]. When it passes:

Solution spoiler 
def grad_mse_linear(X, y, w):
    n = len(y)
    pred = matvec(X, w)
    grad = [0.0] * len(w)
    for row, p, t in zip(X, pred, y):
        err = p - t
        for j, xj in enumerate(row):
            grad[j] += 2 * err * xj / n
    return grad

The library way

The same step in PyTorch run on the lab VM

PyTorch writes the formula for you. Mark w with requires_grad_() and every operation that touches it is recorded as the loss is computed. loss.backward() walks that record backwards, applying the chain rule at each operation, and leaves the slopes in w.grad. Steps 10 to 12 build that machinery yourself. The script ends with your formula as one tensor expression: X.T @ (X @ w - y) multiplies a (3, 500) matrix by the 500 misses and gives one slope per weight.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from common import design_matrix, load_rides, targets

rides = load_rides()[:500]                 # the same 500 rides as the check
X, y = design_matrix(rides), targets(rides)

w = torch.tensor([2.5, 2.0, 0.0], dtype=torch.float64)
w.requires_grad_()                         # ask the library to track w
loss = F.mse_loss(X @ w, y)                # recorded as it is computed
loss.backward()                            # walk the record backwards
print(f"loss {loss.item():.2f} at w = {w.tolist()}")
print("w.grad from backward():", [round(g, 3) for g in w.grad.tolist()])

n = len(y)
by_formula = 2 / n * X.T @ (X @ w.detach() - y)    # (2/n) sum (pred - fare) x_j
print("your formula, in one line:", [round(g, 3) for g in by_formula.tolist()])

The last two lines are the check's gradient again. Nudging, your formula and autograd are three routes to one answer, and they agree to the printed digits. The tensors are 64-bit floats, which is how gradient checks are always run. What the library did for you is the derivation. You wrote the formula; backward() found it by itself, from the record of how the loss was built. A formula is worth knowing once and rarely typing again.
