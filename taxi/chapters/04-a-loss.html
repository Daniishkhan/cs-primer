A Loss

Step 1 scored a guess by adding up the misses. For learning, a different score works better: square each miss, then average. This is the mean squared error, the loss that most of the leg drives down, and the reason is in its shape.

Where we are

With step 1's knobs the model predicts a mean fare of $8.60 against a real mean of $13.03. Step 1's total_error can score that over the whole file: $28,404.39 at $2.00 a mile, $13,297.45 at $3.00 a mile. True, and useless to read. The wall

Two things are wrong with summing absolute misses. First, the sum grows with the number of rides: the same knobs score $15.76 on five rides and $28,404 on the file, so the number says more about how many rides you looked at than about the knobs. Take the mean and that goes away. Second, and this one only bites in step 6: the absolute value has a corner at zero. Its slope is −1 on one side and +1 on the other and undefined in between, exactly where a guess is right. Step 6 finds better knobs by following the slope of the score, and a score with corners sends it in circles. The square has no corner. It is smooth everywhere, its slope at a perfect guess is zero, and it has one more property you can see in the output above: the $9.10 miss on ride 4 became 82.81 of the 95.21 total. Squaring makes big misses matter most, which is a choice. Mean squared error cares about the worst rides first.

The idea

The mean squared error, MSE, of a set of predictions is the average of the squared misses:

MSE = (1 / n) ∑ (f̂i − fi)2

Its units are dollars squared, which is why its square root, the root mean squared error, is the number to say out loud: a typical miss, in dollars. A loss is any score of this kind, a single number that is zero when the model is perfect and grows as it gets worse, chosen so that learning can push it down. From now on "the loss" of a set of knobs w on data (X, y) means mse(matvec(X, w), y), and that composition is given to you as loss(X, y, w).

def mse(pred, y):
    if len(pred) != len(y):
        raise ValueError(f"length mismatch: {len(pred)} vs {len(y)}")
    return sum((p - t) ** 2 for p, t in zip(pred, y)) / len(y)

The library way

The same step in PyTorch run on the lab VM

The library ships the loss as a function, F.mse_loss, next to a few dozen others. Written out with tensors it is one line, and the two agree to the printed digits.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from common import design_matrix, load_rides, targets

rides = load_rides()
X, y = design_matrix(rides), targets(rides)

for per_mile in (2.0, 2.5):
    w = torch.tensor([2.5, per_mile, 0.0], dtype=torch.float64)
    pred = X @ w
    by_hand = ((pred - y) ** 2).mean()
    library = F.mse_loss(pred, y)
    print(f"${per_mile:.2f} a mile: mse by hand {by_hand:.2f}, F.mse_loss {library:.2f}, "
          f"root {library.sqrt():.2f}")
$2.00 a mile: mse by hand 41.13, F.mse_loss 41.13, root 6.41
$2.50 a mile: mse by hand 21.84, F.mse_loss 21.84, root 4.67

Notice (pred - y) ** 2 with no loop: subtraction, squaring and the mean each act on the whole tensor. That is the vectorised style every library is built around, and it is your mse with the loop moved inside.

What you have now

  • mse(pred, y): the mean squared miss. Yours.
  • loss(X, y, w): the loss of knobs w on data (X, y), which is mse(matvec(X, w), y). Given.

The program can now take any knobs and return one number, and lower is better: 41.13 at $2.00 a mile, 21.84 at $2.50. That is enough to start turning knobs and watching.
