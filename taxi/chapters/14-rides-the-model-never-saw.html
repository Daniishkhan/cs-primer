Rides the Model Never Saw

Every loss printed so far was measured on the rides the weights were fitted to. Set some rides aside before the fitting starts, score the model only on those, and the number finally says something about rides it has not met.

Where we are

The program can fit a straight line to any rides it is given (steps 8 and 9), and step 13's autograd agrees with the hand gradient: on 200 rides at w = [2.5, 2.0, 0.0] both give [−8.2595, −39.1502, −178.327]. What the program cannot say is how that line will do on a ride it has not seen, because every loss it has printed was computed on the very rides the weights were fitted to. Here is the smallest case of the trap: learn the two knobs of step 1 from two of the five rides, then score the line on those two and on the other three.

RIDES5 = [(1.60, 6.2, 1, 7.0), (0.79, 7.1, 1, 5.0), (1.37, 7.4, 1, 7.5),
          (7.70, 25.9, 1, 27.0), (2.16, 9.5, 3, 9.0)]
LEARN_FROM = (0, 1)                  # the two rides the line is fitted to


def loss(rides, base, per_mile):
    """Mean squared miss of the guess base + per_mile * distance."""
    misses = [base + per_mile * d - fare for d, _, _, fare in rides]
    return sum(m * m for m in misses) / len(misses)


(d1, _, _, f1), (d2, _, _, f2) = (RIDES5[i] for i in LEARN_FROM)
per_mile = (f1 - f2) / (d1 - d2)     # the line through two points
base = f1 - per_mile * d1
seen = [RIDES5[i] for i in LEARN_FROM]
unseen = [r for i, r in enumerate(RIDES5) if i not in LEARN_FROM]

print(f"learnt from two rides: ${base:.2f} + ${per_mile:.2f} a mile")
print(f"loss on those two rides: {loss(seen, base, per_mile):5.2f}")
print(f"loss on the other three: {loss(unseen, base, per_mile):5.2f}")
learnt from two rides: $3.05 + $2.47 a mile
loss on those two rides:  0.00
loss on the other three:  8.64

Change (0, 1) to (2, 3) and run it again.

The wall

The line scores 0.00 on the two rides it learnt from. Two knobs can always pass through two points, so that zero tells you nothing: on the other three rides the same line scores 8.64. Learn from rides 3 and 4 instead and it still scores 0.00 on its two, but only 0.95 on the rest. A score on the rides a model learnt from is a promise, not a measurement. The real model has three knobs for thousands of rides, so it cannot memorise the file; the network of step 15 has 21 knobs, and the habit costs nothing to start now. You need a score from rides that played no part in the fitting.

The idea

Cut the rides in two before anything is fitted. The training rides are the ones the loop learns from. The held-out rides are kept out of the fitting completely: the weights never see them, and the loss on them is the score. It is step 4's loss with one change:

Lheld = (1 / nheld) ∑i in held (f̂i − fi)2

The sum runs over the held-out rides only, and the weights inside the guess f̂ were fitted without them. A model that scores well there has generalised: what it learnt carries over to rides it has not met. Three habits keep the score honest. Shuffle before cutting: files come in some order (by time, by area, by size), and the last fifth of a file sorted by time is the end of the period, not a sample of it. Seed the shuffle: a seed is the number a random generator starts from, so the same seed gives the same cut and a score you can reproduce. Learn nothing from the held-out rides, not even their averages: fit_linear standardises with the training rides' means and standard deviations, because computing them over all the rides would let the held-out rides leak into the fit (the courses' chapter on leakage lists more ways). From here on, the score that matters is a held-out score.

Your move

One function: the cut. fit_linear, residual_by and predict_std are given. fit_linear standardises on the training rides, trains the line with the loop of step 8 and returns the held-out loss with its model; residual_by averages fare minus prediction over the rides a test picks out.

def split(rows, frac, seed):
    """Shuffle rows with random.Random(seed) and cut them: the first
    `frac` share (rounded down) is the training set, the rest is held
    out. Returns (train, held). The same seed gives the same split."""

    rows = list(rows)
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(rows)
    cut = int(len(rows) * frac)
    return rows[:cut], rows[cut:]

The library way

The same step in PyTorch run on the lab VM

The library calls the cut random_split: give it a dataset, the two sizes and a seeded torch.Generator. The fit is least squares in one call, torch.linalg.lstsq, the bottom of the bowl that the loop of step 8 walks towards.

import torch
from torch.utils.data import TensorDataset, random_split
from common import design_matrix, load_rides, targets

rides = load_rides()
X, y = design_matrix(rides), targets(rides)
n_train = int(0.8 * len(y))                       # rounded down, as in split()
train, held = random_split(TensorDataset(X, y), [n_train, len(y) - n_train],
                           generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(0))
print(f"{len(train):,} rides to learn from, {len(held):,} held out")

Xt, yt = X[train.indices], y[train.indices]
Xh, yh = X[held.indices], y[held.indices]
w = torch.linalg.lstsq(Xt, yt.unsqueeze(1)).solution.squeeze(1)  # best line
print(f"line: ${w[0]:.2f} + ${w[1]:.2f} a mile + ${w[2]:.2f} a minute")
print(f"loss on the rides it learnt from: {((Xt @ w - yt) ** 2).mean():.2f}")
print(f"loss on the held-out rides:       {((Xh @ w - yh) ** 2).mean():.2f}")

miss = yh - Xh @ w                                # fare minus prediction
print(f"average of fare minus prediction: {miss[Xh[:, 2] > 30].mean():+.2f} "
      f"over 30 minutes, {miss[Xh[:, 2] < 5].mean():+.2f} under 5 minutes")
5,093 rides to learn from, 1,274 held out
line: $2.47 + $2.09 a mile + $0.29 a minute
loss on the rides it learnt from: 8.47
loss on the held-out rides:       7.92
average of fare minus prediction: +0.03 over 30 minutes, +0.42 under 5 minutes

The fitted line's base, $2.47, is close to the $2.50 flag-drop of step 1. The library's shuffle is not Python's random.Random(0), so it holds out different rides: the held-out loss is 7.92, not 9.32 (and below the 8.47 on the rides it learnt from, which a lucky cut can do), and the long-ride average flips from −0.20 to +0.03. Neither cut is the true one. Each is one draw of a score, and step 17 measures how far a draw moves. The library did the shuffle, the cut and the bookkeeping in one call.

What you have now

  • split(rows, frac, seed): a seeded shuffle, cut into training and held-out rows. Yours.
  • fit_linear(train_rides, held_rides): fits the line on the training rides, scores it on the held-out ones. Given.
  • residual_by(rides, model, key): the average of fare minus prediction over picked rides. Given.
  • predict_std(ride, model): a standardised model's fare for one ride. Given.

The program has its first honest score: 9.32 on 1,274 rides that took no part in the fitting, a typical miss of about $3. On them the line charged the short rides too little by about 42 cents on average and the long ones too much by about 20: small, and one cut cannot say whether it is a pattern or luck (the library's cut gave +0.03 for the long rides). The long version is the courses' chapter on generalisation, with cross-validation for repeating the cut. Next step: a model that can bend, because a line can only lean.
