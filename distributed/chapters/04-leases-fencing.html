Leases, Locks & Fencing Tokens

Taking a lock on one machine is easy because the machine knows if its holder is running. Across machines, "I hold the lock" is only a belief about the past, and the holder may be frozen, delayed or confused about the time. This chapter shows exactly how a correct-looking distributed lock corrupts data, how leases bound the problem with clocks, why even good leases are not enough, and how a plain increasing number, a fencing token, checked by the storage itself makes the whole scheme safe.

Why this matters

"Only one worker may do X at a time" is among the most common requirements in systems: one leader per shard, one process rebuilding an index, one writer per file, one cron job instance across a fleet. Engineers reach for a lock, often one built on Redis, ZooKeeper, a database row or a cloud lock service, and the code looks right. It works for months. Then a long garbage-collection pause, a delayed packet or a clock correction lets two workers believe they hold the lock simultaneously, and the data is silently damaged. Knowing why this happens, and the one extra mechanism that prevents it, is a classic senior-level distinction. It is also the key to understanding terms in Raft, epochs in Kafka and sequencers in Chubby: they are all the same idea.

A lock is a belief about the past

On a single machine, a mutex (systems chapter 12) is state in memory that the kernel enforces. If a thread holding the lock is descheduled for a second, nobody else can enter the critical section in the meantime, because the lock word still says "taken". The holder's pause is harmless to mutual exclusion.

Move the lock to another machine and two things change. First, the holder can crash while holding it, so the lock would be stuck forever. The standard fix is to make the lock expire. Second, once it expires, the holder's pause is no longer harmless. A lock with an expiry is a lease: a grant that is valid for a fixed term and then lapses unless renewed. Cary Gray and David Cheriton introduced the idea in 1989 for keeping distributed file caches consistent, and it has been the foundation of lock services ever since.

Analogy: a library book with a due date

You borrow a book for three weeks. The library promises not to lend it to anyone else until the due date, and if you vanish, it is automatically free again afterwards. That's what makes leases robust against crashes: nobody needs to know you are dead, the library just waits for the date. But the library cannot reach into your house. If you lose track of time and keep reading after the due date, nothing stops you; and if someone else borrowed the book on day 22, you are now both annotating "the" copy.

Where it breaks down: a library book can only be in one place, so two readers are at most a nuisance. A shared resource such as a file or a database row has one copy, and two writers corrupt it.

The scenario every lock service must survive

Here it is, drawn as a space-time diagram. Step through it slowly; it is the central picture of this chapter.

Step through

The pause that outlives the lease

  1. Client 1 takes the lock. It asks the lock service, which grants a 10-second lease and hands back token 33 (we will need the token later). Client 1 starts working.
  2. Client 1 freezes. A stop-the-world garbage collection, a virtual-machine stall or a swapped-out process stops it for far longer than the lease. It cannot renew the lease and, crucially, cannot know any time has passed.
  3. The lease expires. The lock service did exactly what it promised: after 10 s without a renewal it considers the lock free.
  4. Client 2 takes the lock. It is granted the lease with token 34.
  5. Client 2 writes. The storage accepts the write, which is correct: Client 2 does hold the lock.
  6. Client 1 wakes up. Its code is exactly where it left off, a line after "I hold the lock". It sends its write. Without any further protection, the storage accepts it and overwrites Client 2's newer data. Both clients behaved correctly according to everything they could see. The corruption lives in the gap between "I checked" and "my write arrived".
  7. The fix, previewed. If the storage remembers the highest token it has seen (34) and compares each write's token, Client 1's write carrying 33 is refused. No clocks are needed, only a number.

Where unbounded gaps come from

The "gap" in step 6 is the interval between the moment the client last believed it held the lease and the moment its action takes effect at the resource. In the asynchronous model of chapter 1 that interval has no upper bound. Three everyday causes:

  • Process pauses. Garbage collection (systems chapter 3; a real case), the operating system descheduling a busy process (chapter 7), page faults and swapping (chapter 6), a container being throttled, a virtual machine paused for live migration, a laptop lid closing, an operator sending SIGSTOP. Pauses of many seconds do occur.
  • Network delay. The client checked, sent its write immediately, and the packet sat in a congested queue, was retransmitted, or took a long route. By the time it reaches the storage, the lease is gone. The sender is not even running anything slowly; the message is.
  • Clock errors. The client decides whether its lease is valid by reading a clock. If the clock jumps (an NTP step, a leap second, a virtual machine resuming with a stale clock, networking chapter 17) it may believe it has a minute left when none remains. Reading a monotonic clock avoids jumps but not pauses or drift.

"Just check the lease right before writing" does not work

The check and the write are two separate steps, and between them an arbitrary amount of time can pass: the pause or delay can strike after the check. It's a time-of-check to time-of-use race (systems chapter 11) stretched across a network: you cannot make the check atomic with the action from the client's side. The widget below lets you confirm this by switching the client-side check on.

Leases with bounded clock drift

Before we fix the pause problem, it is worth seeing what a lease does guarantee, and what it assumes. A lease involves two parties, a granter (the lock service) and a holder, each measuring time with its own clock. They don't need synchronised clocks: they need only that their clocks run at nearly the same rate. Define the drift bound ρ as the largest fraction by which a clock's rate may differ from true time: over a real interval of Δ seconds, a clock measures between Δ(1−ρ) and Δ(1+ρ). Ordinary quartz oscillators are specified within tens to a couple of hundred parts per million, so a round figure of 100 ppm, ρ = 10-4, is a common planning number (measure your own hardware before relying on it).

The protocol and its safety margin

  1. The holder notes its local clock when it sends the request (not when the reply arrives: the grant happened somewhere in between, and sending is the latest time that is certainly earlier).
  2. The server grants a lease of term T and starts its own timer at the moment of the grant.
  3. The holder considers the lease valid until its own timer, started at the send time, reads T. (Better, until T minus the time its actions need.)
  4. The server refuses to give the lease to anyone else until its own timer reads T·(1+ρ)/(1−ρ), slightly more than T.

Why this padding? Consider the worst case. The holder's clock may run slow, so its T seconds last up to T/(1−ρ) of real time: it may keep acting that long. The server's clock may run fast, so its timer covers only 1/(1+ρ) real seconds per second. The server must count enough seconds on its own clock that, even if fast, it has waited at least T/(1−ρ) of real time: Tserver/(1+ρ) ≥ T/(1−ρ), which gives the formula. The padding is about 2ρT, and the holder's early start (at the send time) covers the network delay of the grant for free.

Check it by simulation. For each trial, give both clocks random rates within ±ρ, give the grant a random travel time, and ask whether the holder is still acting when the server frees the lease.

import random

T = 10.0                                   # lease term, seconds

def server_wait(term: float, rho: float) -> float:
    """How long (on the server's clock) to keep a lease reserved so that the holder,
    whose clock may run slow while the server's runs fast, has certainly stopped."""
    return term * (1 + rho) / (1 - rho)

def violations(rho: float, wait: float, trials: int = 100_000, seed: int = 1) -> int:
    rng, bad = random.Random(seed), 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        holder_rate = 1 + rng.uniform(-rho, rho)     # holder clock seconds per real second
        server_rate = 1 + rng.uniform(-rho, rho)
        grant_delay = rng.uniform(0.0, 0.05)         # request travel time (holder starts its timer at SEND)
        holder_stops = T / holder_rate               # real time at which holder's timer hits T
        server_frees = grant_delay + wait / server_rate
        bad += holder_stops > server_frees           # overlap: both believe they hold the lease
    return bad

print(f"{'drift rho':>10}{'server waits':>14}{'overlaps (naive T)':>20}{'overlaps (padded)':>19}")
for rho in (1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 5e-2):
    w = server_wait(T, rho)
    print(f"{rho:>10.4f}{w:>13.3f}s{violations(rho, T):>20}{violations(rho, w):>19}")

Without padding, even 0.01% drift (ρ = 10-4) produces overlap in 658 of 100,000 trials, and at 1% drift nearly 39% overlap. With the padded wait there are no overlaps at any drift. Also notice the cost: at ρ = 0.0001 the padding is only 2 ms on a 10 s lease. Clock rate drift is cheap to account for. What the padding cannot cover is an arbitrary pause, or a clock that jumps instead of drifting, because those break the assumption itself.

Renewal, term length and what "stop" means

  • Renew early. A holder that wants to keep the lease renews well before expiry (a common choice is after about one third of the term) so that a lost or slow renewal can be retried twice before the lease lapses. Each renewal is again timed from the moment the request was sent.
  • On failure, stop. If renewals fail, the holder must stop acting on the lease when its own timer (minus a margin) runs out. Stopping means aborting work in flight, not just refusing to start new work. But a packet already handed to the network cannot be recalled, which is exactly the problem the next section solves.
  • Term length is a trade-off. A long term means few renewals but slow failover after a real crash (the lock stays blocked until it expires). A short term means fast failover but more renewal traffic and more false expiries when a holder pauses briefly. A lease is, in effect, a failure detector with a contract (chapter 3): the server suspects the holder dead when the lease lapses.
  • Sessions. Lock services usually attach leases to a client session kept alive by a stream of keep-alive requests, with the locks and ephemeral nodes it owns disappearing when the session expires. Chubby, ZooKeeper and etcd all work this way (chapter 11 and the Chubby case).

What have we established? If clock rates stay within the drift bound, a correct lease protocol never lets the server give the lease to a second party while the first still believes (by its own timer) that it holds it. That is a real guarantee, and it rests on a timing assumption, so by the design rule of chapter 1 it is a statement about liveness-friendly timing being used for safety. The remaining hole is that "believes it holds the lease" and "is acting right now" can come apart by an arbitrary time.

Fencing tokens

Here's the key move, and it's an instance of the design rule from chapter 1: take the safety property out of the timing model. Instead of trying to guarantee that the old holder has stopped, accept that it might not have, and make its stale actions harmless. The mechanism is a fencing token (the name was popularised by Martin Kleppmann; Chubby calls its version a sequencer).

Analogy: the numbered ticket at the deli counter

Every customer who takes a turn is handed a ticket with a higher number than the last. The counter serves whoever's number it calls, and once it has served number 34 it will refuse anyone waving number 33, however firmly they insist it is their turn. It doesn't matter why they are late. The counter needs no clock and no knowledge of what the customer has been doing, only the numbers it has seen.

Where it breaks down: at a deli the numbers come from a single machine. In a distributed system the ticket dispenser is itself a service that can fail over, and its numbers must still never go backwards, which requires consensus (below).

The mechanism

  1. Every time the lock service grants the lock (or a new lease), it hands out a token that is strictly greater than any token it has issued before: 33, then 34, and so on.
  2. The client attaches its token to every request to the protected resource, reads included.
  3. The resource remembers the highest token it has seen. If a request arrives with a smaller token, it is rejected. Otherwise the resource updates its highest-seen value and proceeds.

Walk through step 6 of the diagram again with this in place. Client 2's request carries 34, so the storage records 34 and accepts. Client 1's later request carries 33 < 34 and is rejected. Client 1 may be paused, delayed, or have a wrong clock; none of that matters. The safety of the system no longer depends on timing at all. Timing remains important for liveness (a new holder can only get the lock after the old lease expires) but it has left the safety argument.

The check must live at the storage

Where must the comparison happen? The answer is forced by the same logic as before: a check is only meaningful if it is atomic with the effect, so it must live at the component that applies the effect.

This is why "the lock service checks that the client still holds the lock" (as a proxy in front of the storage) is not enough: the request can still sit between proxy and storage when the lease expires. It is also why, in practice, the storage must offer a primitive that couples the token comparison to the write. In a SQL database that is a conditional update; in a key-value store it is compare-and-set or a conditional put with a version precondition; in a file or object store it is a conditional write. Here it is with SQLite, runnable as-is:

import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("CREATE TABLE doc (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, body TEXT, fence INTEGER NOT NULL)")
db.execute("INSERT INTO doc VALUES (1, 'v0', 0)")

def fenced_write(token: int, body: str) -> bool:
    """The check and the write are ONE atomic statement inside the storage engine."""
    cur = db.execute("UPDATE doc SET body = ?, fence = ? WHERE id = 1 AND fence <= ?",
                     (body, token, token))
    db.commit()
    return cur.rowcount == 1

print("B (token 34) writes   :", fenced_write(34, "B-data"))
print("A (token 33) writes   :", fenced_write(33, "A-data"))
print("B (token 34) again    :", fenced_write(34, "B-data-2"))
print("row now               :", db.execute("SELECT body, fence FROM doc").fetchone())
B (token 34) writes   : True
A (token 33) writes   : False
B (token 34) again    : True
row now               : ('B-data-2', 34)

The statement UPDATE ... WHERE fence <= ? compares and writes in one atomic step inside the database engine. B's write succeeds, A's stale write affects zero rows and so fails, and B's next write still works because its token equals the stored one (hence <=, not <). The same shape works in any system that supports an atomic conditional write. You have met its cousin before: it is the optimistic version check of MVCC and optimistic concurrency in the data course.

The same story in code, with and without fencing

Here is the chapter's scenario as a small deterministic simulation: a lock service handing out leases and tokens, a storage that optionally checks tokens, and a client frozen at the worst moment.

import heapq

class LockService:
    """Grants a lease for `ttl` seconds; every grant carries a larger token."""
    def __init__(self, ttl: float):
        self.ttl, self.expires, self.token = ttl, 0.0, 32
    def acquire(self, now: float):
        if now < self.expires:
            return None                         # someone else holds a valid lease
        self.token += 1
        self.expires = now + self.ttl
        return self.token

class Storage:
    def __init__(self, fencing: bool):
        self.fencing, self.highest, self.content = fencing, 0, "v0"
    def write(self, token: int, data: str) -> str:
        if self.fencing and token < self.highest:
            return f"REJECTED (token {token} < highest seen {self.highest})"
        self.highest = max(self.highest, token)
        self.content = data
        return "accepted"

def scenario(fencing: bool) -> None:
    lock, disk = LockService(ttl=10), Storage(fencing)
    log, queue, tokens = [], [], {}
    def say(t, msg): log.append(f"  t={t:>4.1f}s  {msg}")
    def at(t, fn): heapq.heappush(queue, (t, len(queue), fn))

    def a_acquire(t):
        tokens["A"] = lock.acquire(t); say(t, f"A acquires the lease, token {tokens['A']}, valid until t={lock.expires:.0f}")
    def a_check(t):
        say(t, f"A checks its lease: {'valid' if t < lock.expires else 'EXPIRED'}; about to write")
    def a_pause(t):
        say(t, "A freezes in a long GC pause before the write leaves (it cannot know)")
    def b_acquire(t):
        tokens["B"] = lock.acquire(t); say(t, f"B acquires the lease, token {tokens['B']}")
    def b_write(t):
        say(t, f"B writes 'B-data' with token {tokens['B']}: {disk.write(tokens['B'], 'B-data')}")
    def a_write(t):
        say(t, f"A wakes and its write arrives with token {tokens['A']}: {disk.write(tokens['A'], 'A-data')}")

    at(0.0, a_acquire); at(9.0, a_check); at(9.1, a_pause)
    at(10.5, b_acquire); at(11.0, b_write); at(15.0, a_write)
    while queue:
        t, _, fn = heapq.heappop(queue); fn(t)
    print(f"fencing {'ON ' if fencing else 'OFF'}")
    print("\n".join(log))
    print(f"  final content of the file: {disk.content!r}\n")

The two runs are identical until the last line. Without fencing, the file ends as A's stale data. With it, A's late write is refused and B's data survives. The storage didn't need to know why A was late.

Details that matter

  • The new holder's first request announces its token. The storage learns "34 exists" only when a request carrying 34 arrives. A stale write that lands before that is indistinguishable and is accepted. That is fine: the new holder has not acted yet, so its first read will simply see the stale write, and nothing is lost. (Have the new holder start with a read or a no-op write carrying its token; the widget below models exactly that.)
  • Token generation must be monotonic across failures. If the lock service itself fails over and the new primary hands out 34 a second time, two holders have the same token and the storage cannot tell them apart. Tokens must come from a linearizable source, which in practice means a consensus-replicated service (chapter 11): Chubby sequencers, ZooKeeper's transaction ids or node version numbers, etcd's revisions. A lock built on an asynchronously replicated single node with a counter can hand out the same or a smaller token after a failover.
  • Not every resource can be fenced. A third-party API that sends an email or charges a card does not compare your tokens. There, fencing is impossible, and you fall back on idempotency keys (networking chapter 16) or on accepting occasional duplicates.
  • Multiple resources. A token only protects a resource that checks it. If an operation touches three stores, each must enforce the token independently. Anything that doesn't leaves a hole.
  • "Fencing" has an older meaning. In high-availability clusters, fencing (sometimes STONITH, "shoot the other node in the head") means physically cutting a suspected-dead node off from shared storage or powering it down, so it cannot misbehave. That is a hammer applied to the client; tokens are the logical version applied at the resource, and need no special hardware.

Terms, epochs and ballots are fencing tokens

Once you see the pattern, it appears everywhere. A Raft term (networking chapter 20) is a fencing token for leadership: a deposed leader that wakes up from a pause sends append requests with its old term, and followers reject them because they have seen a higher one. Paxos ballot numbers do the same for proposers (chapter 9). Kafka's partition leader epoch and producer epoch, HDFS-style journal epochs and ZooKeeper's epochs are the same mechanism for the same reason. Wherever a role can pass from one machine to another while the old one might still be alive, an increasing number and a rejecting receiver make it safe.

Efficiency locks and correctness locks

Not every lock needs fencing, and over-engineering has a cost too. A helpful distinction, drawn by Kleppmann in his 2016 essay on distributed locks, is the purpose of the lock:

Efficiency lock

"Only one worker should do this expensive job (send this nightly report, rebuild this cache entry)." If two workers occasionally both run, the cost is wasted compute or a duplicate email. A best-effort lease, even a simple one, is fine.

Correctness lock

"Only one writer may modify this record, or it will be corrupted." Two holders mean lost updates, double spends or broken invariants. This needs a linearizable lock service and a fencing check at the resource, or better, no lock at all.

Ask of every lock in a design: what happens if, for a few seconds, two nodes both hold it? If the honest answer is "we waste some work", a lease will do. If it's "we corrupt data", you need fencing, and you should also ask whether the lock is needed. Often the guarded operation can be re-expressed as an optimistic version check: "update this row only if its version is still the one I read", which is fencing in the data itself, with no lock service to run. Other alternatives are making the operation idempotent so duplicates are harmless, or assigning each partition of the data a single owner through a replicated log (chapter 8) so that ownership changes are ordered.

A lock service does not make your code correct by itself

Using ZooKeeper, etcd or a database advisory lock tells you who holds the lock, using consensus to avoid disputes about that. It cannot tell you whether the holder is still executing code at the moment its action reaches the world. Only a fencing check at the resource (or an idempotent operation) closes that gap.

A note on the Redlock debate

Redis's documentation describes an algorithm called Redlock for taking a lock across several independent Redis instances, requiring a majority and a time-based validity check. It prompted a well-known public disagreement in 2016 between Martin Kleppmann, who argued that it depends on timing assumptions (bounded pauses and drift) and issues no fencing tokens, so it is unsuitable where correctness matters, and Salvatore Sanfilippo, Redis's creator, who replied that the time checks it performs make the practical risk small and that the design targets different needs. The disagreement is partly about facts and partly about how much failure a design should be asked to survive. Both positions are instructive after this chapter: they differ on which timing assumptions are acceptable and what the lock protects. The Redlock case study sets out both sides with sources so you can judge.

Putting it together: a checklist for a safe lock

  1. Decide whether you need a lock for efficiency or correctness.
  2. Prefer eliminating the lock: optimistic version checks, idempotency, single-owner partitions.
  3. If a lock is needed, get it from a consensus-replicated service (chapter 11), not from a single node or an eventually consistent store.
  4. Use leases with renewals timed from the send time on a monotonic clock, with a margin for drift (the 2ρT padding) and for the time your actions need.
  5. For correctness: have the service issue a monotonically increasing token with every grant, send it with every request, and make the resource compare it atomically with the effect.
  6. Have the new holder touch the resource with its token before doing anything irreversible.
  7. Test the pause: inject a delay between "acquire" and "write" (chapter 19's tools and Lab 2 do this) and see what happens.

You will build a lease-based lock service with a fenced store, and a client that pauses at the worst possible moment, in Lab 2. Chapter 11 and the lock-service design studio show how to build the service itself on a consensus group.

Go deeper: leader leases, read leases and why they are different optional

Some systems use leases to let a leader answer reads without contacting a quorum: while its lease is valid no other leader can exist, so its local data is current. The same drift-and-padding argument applies, and so does the same limit: a paused leader can answer a read after its lease has lapsed, so the lease is safe only as long as pauses and clock behaviour stay inside the assumed bounds. Designs differ in how they bound the risk (Spanner, for example, makes clock uncertainty explicit, chapter 7), and in whether they read through the log instead (chapter 10). This is the safety-versus-liveness design rule once more: if the assumption fails, a read may be stale, but a write through consensus still cannot be corrupted, since it must be accepted by a quorum that checks terms.

Distributed systems connection

Leases turn failure detection (chapter 3) into a contract; fencing turns a fragile timing assumption into a safety property that survives asynchrony (chapter 1). The same two-part answer, "time out the old holder, and make the resource reject it", recurs in Raft's terms and election timeouts (chapter 10), in ZooKeeper and etcd sessions (chapter 11), in two-phase commit's coordinator recovery (chapter 13) and in Kafka's producer epochs (chapter 17). The Redlock debate and Chubby case study explore the same questions in real systems.

Key takeaways

  • A distributed lock must expire, otherwise a crashed holder blocks everyone: that makes it a lease. But an expiring lock means the old holder can still be running when the new one arrives.
  • The holder's belief "I hold the lease" can be arbitrarily stale: process pauses (GC, VM stalls), network delay and clock jumps all open a gap between check and action. Checking the lease just before the write cannot close it.
  • A lease with bounded clock drift is safe against rate drift if the server pads the term by about 2ρT, and the holder times from the moment it sends the request. It still assumes bounded pauses and no clock jumps.
  • A fencing token is a number that increases with every grant. The client sends it with every request and the storage rejects any token lower than the highest it has seen. The comparison must be atomic with the write; a check in the client or in a proxy leaves the gap open.
  • Tokens must come from a linearizable source (a consensus-backed service), or failover can issue duplicates. Resources that cannot compare tokens (external APIs) cannot be fenced; use idempotency there.
  • Distinguish efficiency locks (a duplicate costs some work) from correctness locks (a duplicate corrupts data). Prefer optimistic version checks and idempotent operations to correctness locks.
  • Raft terms, Paxos ballots, Kafka epochs and Chubby sequencers are all fencing tokens for roles that can move between machines.

Check your understanding

A client checks that its lease is valid and, immediately afterwards, is frozen for 15 seconds by a garbage-collection pause before sending its write. The lease was 10 seconds long. Why does the client-side check not protect the storage?

  1. The check used the wrong clock
  2. The check and the write are separate steps; the pause occurs after the check, so the write is sent on an expired lease the client no longer knows about
  3. Leases cannot be checked by clients
  4. The lock service should have extended the lease automatically
This is a time-of-check to time-of-use race across a network. No amount of care on the client can make the check atomic with the effect at the storage, because arbitrary time can pass between them. Only a check performed by the storage itself, at the moment of the write, closes the gap.

Lease term T = 10 s, drift bound ρ = 0.01 (1%). Roughly how long should the server keep the lease reserved, measured on its own clock, to be safe against the worst-case drift?

  1. Exactly 10 s
  2. 9.9 s, to be conservative
  3. Forever, to be safe
  4. About 10.2 s: T(1+ρ)/(1−ρ), roughly T(1+2ρ)
The holder's slow clock may keep it acting for up to T/(1−ρ) of real time, and the server's fast clock may cover only 1/(1+ρ) real seconds per second, so the server needs T(1+ρ)/(1−ρ) ≈ 10.2 s on its own clock. Waiting less than T is never safe.

A storage service receives write(token=33) after it has already processed write(token=34). What should a fenced storage do, and what does it not need to know?

  1. Reject the write; it does not need to know why the sender was late, nor anything about clocks
  2. Accept it, since it arrived last
  3. Ask the lock service whether 33 is still valid, then decide
  4. Accept it only if the sender's clock agrees with its own
Fencing needs only an increasing number and a comparison. Asking the lock service would reintroduce a gap between the check and the write; consulting clocks reintroduces timing assumptions. The whole point is that the comparison is local, atomic with the write, and independent of timing.

Which design lets a fencing token be defeated?

  1. A lock service replicated with a consensus protocol that persists the counter before replying
  2. A storage that does UPDATE ... WHERE fence <= :token as one statement
  3. A lock service that fails over to an asynchronous replica that missed the last increment, and so can hand out a previously used token
  4. A client that sends its token with every read and write
If the token source can repeat or go backwards, two holders may present equal or reversed tokens and the storage can no longer order them. Tokens must be strictly monotonic across failures, which requires a linearizable, durably replicated generator.

Which lock is a good candidate for a simple, unfenced lease?

  1. Ensuring a single writer to a customer's account balance row
  2. Ensuring that only one of several workers regenerates an expensive cache entry, where a duplicate merely wastes some compute
  3. Ensuring that only one node acts as the primary of a database
  4. Ensuring a bank transfer is applied once
Ask "what if two holders overlap for a few seconds?". For the cache entry, the cost is duplicated work; for balances, primaries and transfers, overlap corrupts data. Those need fencing (or a version check, or idempotency).

In Raft, a deposed leader that wakes from a long pause still sends append requests. Why can it not overwrite the cluster's log?

  1. Because it is not allowed to send messages after a pause
  2. Because the followers' clocks tell them its lease has expired
  3. Because its requests carry an old term, and followers reject any request with a term lower than the one they have seen, which is exactly fencing-token behaviour
  4. Because clients ignore it
The term is a monotonically increasing number attached to every message and checked by the receivers. The stale leader's term is lower than the current one, so its messages are refused, regardless of how long it slept and without any use of clocks.
