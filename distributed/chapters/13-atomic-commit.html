Atomic Commit: 2PC & Its Limits

A transaction that touches data on several machines must either commit everywhere or nowhere, even if machines crash halfway through deciding. This chapter takes that requirement apart: why it is a different problem from consensus, how two-phase commit solves it step by step (and what goes wrong if you drop any step), what each crash point leaves behind, why the protocol can leave honest participants stuck holding locks, and how real systems get out of the corner by putting the coordinator itself on a replicated log.

Why this matters

The moment data is split across machines (sharding, or several services each with their own database), a single business operation can need writes on more than one of them, and "some of them happened" is a corrupt state. Two-phase commit is the oldest answer, it is still inside many databases and message brokers, and it is the protocol senior engineers are asked to explain, including its famous flaw. Everything in the next chapter (Percolator, Spanner, Calvin) is a different way of paying, or avoiding, the cost you will see here. And when someone says "just use a saga", you will know exactly what that trades away.

The problem: everyone commits, or no one does

You met the single-machine version in Transactions & ACID: atomicity means a transaction's writes become visible together or not at all, and a database achieves it with a write-ahead log (WAL): a record on disk saying what was about to happen, written before the change itself, so that after a crash the database can finish or undo the work. On one machine there is a single log and a single "commit record"; the instant that record reaches disk, the transaction has committed.

Now split the data. A transfer moves 50 from an account on shard 1 to an account on shard 2. Each shard has its own log and its own disk. Nothing can write to both logs at the same instant, and either shard (or the network between them) can fail at any moment. We want what is called atomic commitment: a group of participants (also called resource managers, the machines holding the data), each of which has done its part of the work, must reach one common outcome, commit or abort. The usual statement of the requirements is:

  • Agreement. No two participants decide differently.
  • Irreversibility. A decision, once made, never changes.
  • Validity (commit). The outcome may be commit only if every participant voted yes. A single no forces abort.
  • Validity (abort). If every participant votes yes and nothing fails, the outcome must be commit. (Otherwise "always abort" would be a correct but useless protocol.)
  • Termination. Once failures are repaired, every participant eventually decides.

Why this is not "just consensus"

The previous course ended with consensus: a group agrees on one value, and any majority can make progress even if the rest are dead. It is tempting to say atomic commit is consensus on the value "commit or abort". It is close, and the two are related (we will use consensus to repair 2PC below), but they differ in two ways that matter.

  1. Every participant has a veto. In consensus, any proposed value may win and the proposers are interchangeable. In atomic commit the outcome is constrained by the votes: one no from anyone, for any local reason (a constraint violated, a deadlock victim, a full disk), must produce abort.
  2. Participants are not replaceable. Consensus replicas all hold the same state, so any majority will do and a dead minority is simply ignored. In atomic commit, shard 2 holds data that no other machine has. Its vote cannot be guessed or substituted, so the protocol has to wait for every participant (or learn that one voted no). A single dead participant stops a commit, which is the opposite of the "tolerate a minority" property of a quorum.

Analogy: the group trip

An organiser wants to book a trip for five friends, but only if all five can come. She phones each one: "Can you hold next weekend? I will book only if everyone says yes." A friend who says yes now declines every other invitation for that weekend, because she has promised. When all five have said yes, the organiser books and phones everyone to confirm. If any one says no, she phones the rest to release them. That is two-phase commit: ask and promise, then decide and announce. Notice what a veto is: Dan saying no cancels the trip for everyone, and notice that nobody can stand in for Priya's answer.

Where it breaks down: friends can phone each other and compare notes if the organiser disappears. Machines can too (cooperative termination, below), but it only helps in some of the cases. And friends who promised and then hear nothing can eventually give up and make other plans; a database participant that has promised cannot, because the promise protects data integrity, not a weekend.

Two-phase commit, step by step

Jim Gray described the protocol in his 1978 notes on operating-system and database transaction support, and it has been refined ever since. There is one coordinator (sometimes called the transaction manager) and a set of participants. The transaction's work has already been done; what remains is deciding to make it permanent. It runs in two phases:

  1. Phase 1, prepare and vote. The coordinator sends PREPARE to every participant. Each participant checks whether it can commit: constraints hold, the locks it needs are held, the writes are staged. If it can, it makes that promise durable by force-writing a prepared record to its log, and only then replies YES. If it cannot, it replies NO (and may abort locally immediately).
  2. Phase 2, decide and announce. If the coordinator has collected YES from everyone, it force-writes a COMMIT record to its own log and sends COMMIT to all. If anyone said NO, or did not answer in time, the decision is ABORT. Each participant logs the decision, applies it, releases its locks, and sends an ACK. When all acknowledgements are in, the coordinator writes a non-forced end record and can forget the transaction.

A forced write (or "force") means the record is on stable storage before the code proceeds: write followed by fsync, with all the caveats from Durability: Written Is Not Saved. "Logged" in this chapter always means forced unless it says otherwise.

Two moments deserve names. A participant that has voted yes is prepared (the paper literature also says uncertain or in doubt while it has not yet heard the decision): it has surrendered the right to decide alone. And the commit point is the coordinator's durable COMMIT record. This is the key move of the whole protocol: the decision is not "made" when messages are sent, it is made when one record reaches one disk, and every later action is just spreading news of a decision that already exists.

What must be logged before replying, and why

The log writes are the protocol. Message-passing alone cannot make it correct, because machines forget everything in memory when they crash. A good way to see why each rule exists is to remove it and build the failure. Every rule below is a scar.

The ruleIf you break it, here is the disaster
A participant force-logs prepared before replying YES.P2 replies YES, then crashes before the record is on disk. On restart it has no memory of the promise; its staged writes may be gone and its locks are gone, so it behaves as if the transaction never reached it. The coordinator has meanwhile collected YES from everyone and committed. P1 applied the transfer; P2 lost it. Atomicity is violated, and nothing in the protocol will notice.
A prepared participant keeps its locks (and its staged data) until it learns the decision.Another transaction reads or modifies the same row while P2 is in doubt. If the decision turns out to be COMMIT, that transaction has seen (or overwritten) an old version of data that is about to change, which is an isolation anomaly (lost update, read skew). The ACK is a promise: "you may forget; I will never ask again."

Deciding abort on a timeout is always safe, because no one can have committed before the coordinator logs COMMIT. This asymmetry (abort is always safe until the commit point) is why coordinators can time out in phase 1 but participants cannot in phase 2.

Notice the pattern: each rule says "make the fact durable before any other node can act on it". The same discipline is the heart of a write-ahead log on one machine; 2PC is that discipline applied twice, once per participant and once for the coordinator.

Presumed abort

As described, the coordinator must also log ABORT and wait for ACKs of aborts, because otherwise a participant that asks about an old transaction might find no record and not know what that means. The R* research project at IBM in the 1980s (Mohan, Lindsay and colleagues) noticed that the protocol can be made cheaper with a convention. Under presumed abort, if the coordinator has no record of a transaction, the answer is "abort". Consequences:

  • On abort the coordinator writes no forced record and expects no ACKs. Aborts, which are common, get cheaper.
  • If the coordinator crashes before the commit point, its restart finds nothing, and every inquirer is correctly told "abort". That is safe because nobody can have committed without a COMMIT record.
  • Only commits need the forced coordinator record, and the coordinator may forget a transaction only after all ACKs of a commit are in.

The Python below (the full listing is in the next section) counts forced writes at the coordinator for a three-participant transaction, with and without the convention. There is a mirror-image variant, presumed commit, that optimises the other case; production systems pick whichever outcome they expect to be commoner. Most textbook treatments and the code here use presumed abort.

Recovery, for every crash point

A protocol is only as good as its answer to "what happens if the power goes out right here?" for every here. The listing below is a deterministic simulation of 2PC with a coordinator C, three participants, a write-ahead log per node (a Python list that survives "crashes", while in-memory state such as lock flags does not), and a Plan object that injects a crash at a named point. Read it once through; the comments carry the argument.

"""Two-phase commit with a write-ahead log and crash injection (deterministic)."""
from __future__ import annotations


class Crash(Exception):
    """Raised when the node named in `plan` reaches the crash point."""


class Plan:
    def __init__(self, node: str | None = None, point: str | None = None):
        self.node, self.point = node, point

    def hit(self, node: str, point: str) -> None:
        if (node, point) == (self.node, self.point):
            raise Crash(node)


class Participant:
    def __init__(self, name: str, votes_yes: bool = True):
        self.name, self.votes_yes = name, votes_yes
        self.log: list[str] = []       # durable: survives a crash
        self.up = True                 # volatile
        self.locked = False            # volatile; rebuilt from the log on restart

    def on_prepare(self, plan: Plan) -> str | None:
        if not self.up:
            return None                               # silence
        try:
            plan.hit(self.name, "before-vote-log")
            if not self.votes_yes:
                self.log.append("abort")              # unilateral abort is always safe
                return "NO"
            self.log.append("prepared")               # FORCED to disk before replying
            self.locked = True
            plan.hit(self.name, "after-vote-log")     # voted, but the reply is lost
            return "YES"
        except Crash:
            self.up, self.locked = False, False
            return None

    def on_decision(self, decision: str, plan: Plan) -> bool:
        if not self.up:
            return False
        try:
            plan.hit(self.name, "after-decision-received")   # before logging it
            self.log.append(decision)
            self.locked = False                       # locks released only now
            return True
        except Crash:
            self.up, self.locked = False, False
            return False

    def in_doubt(self) -> bool:
        return "prepared" in self.log and not {"commit", "abort"} & set(self.log)

    def restart(self) -> None:
        self.up = True
        self.locked = self.in_doubt()                 # in-doubt: must re-take its locks


class Coordinator:
    def __init__(self, parts: list[Participant], presumed_abort: bool = True):
        self.parts, self.pa = parts, presumed_abort
        self.log: list[str] = []
        self.up = True
        self.forced_writes = 0

    def force(self, rec: str) -> None:
        self.log.append(rec)
        self.forced_writes += 1

    def run(self, plan: Plan) -> None:
        try:
            plan.hit("C", "before-prepare")
            votes = []
            for i, p in enumerate(self.parts):
                votes.append(p.on_prepare(plan))
                if i == 0:
                    plan.hit("C", "mid-prepare")      # PREPARE sent to P1 only
            plan.hit("C", "votes-in")
            decision = "commit" if all(v == "YES" for v in votes) else "abort"
            if decision == "commit" or not self.pa:
                self.force(decision)                  # the commit point
            plan.hit("C", "decision-logged")
            acks = []
            for i, p in enumerate(self.parts):
                acks.append(p.on_decision(decision, plan))
                if i == 0:
                    plan.hit("C", "mid-decision")     # DECISION sent to P1 only
            plan.hit("C", "all-sent")
            if all(acks):
                self.log.append("end")                # forget only when every ACK is in; not forced
        except Crash:
            self.up = False

    def answer(self) -> str:
        """What a restarted coordinator tells an in-doubt participant."""
        if "commit" in self.log:
            return "commit"
        if "abort" in self.log:
            return "abort"
        return "abort"        # presumed abort: no record means no commit happened

    def restart(self) -> None:
        self.up = True


def scenario(node: str | None, point: str | None, p3_yes: bool = True, leave_down: bool = False):
    parts = [Participant("P1"), Participant("P2"), Participant("P3", p3_yes)]
    coord = Coordinator(parts)
    coord.run(Plan(node, point))
    blocked = [p.name for p in parts if p.up and p.in_doubt() and not coord.up]
    if not leave_down:
        coord.restart()
        for p in parts:
            if not p.up:
                p.restart()
        for p in parts:                                # termination: ask the coordinator
            if p.in_doubt():
                d = coord.answer()
                p.log.append(d)
                p.locked = False
        if "commit" in coord.log and "end" not in coord.log:
            for p in parts:                            # coordinator re-sends the decision
                if "commit" not in p.log and "abort" not in p.log:
                    p.log.append("commit")
    final = {p.name: ("commit" if "commit" in p.log else
                      "?" if p.in_doubt() else "abort")      # never prepared: nothing to commit
             for p in parts}
    assert len({v for v in final.values() if v != "?"}) <= 1, final     # atomicity
    return final, blocked, [p.name for p in parts if p.locked]


CASES = [
    ("no crash",                                 None, None),
    ("C before sending PREPARE",                 "C",  "before-prepare"),
    ("C after PREPARE to P1 only",               "C",  "mid-prepare"),
    ("C has all votes, nothing logged",          "C",  "votes-in"),
    ("C logged COMMIT, nothing sent",            "C",  "decision-logged"),
    ("C sent COMMIT to P1 only",                 "C",  "mid-decision"),
    ("P2 crashes before logging its vote",       "P2", "before-vote-log"),
    ("P2 logged 'prepared', reply lost",         "P2", "after-vote-log"),
    ("P2 got COMMIT, died before logging",       "P2", "after-decision-received"),
]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{'crash point':42} {'blocked while C is down':24} outcome after recovery")
    for label, node, point in CASES:
        _, blocked, _ = scenario(node, point, leave_down=True)
        final, _, _ = scenario(node, point)
        print(f"{label:42} {','.join(blocked) or '-':24} {'/'.join(sorted(set(final.values())))}")
    print()
    final, blocked, locked = scenario("C", "votes-in", leave_down=True)
    print("coordinator stays down after votes-in:", final, "locks held by", locked)
    final, blocked, locked = scenario("C", "votes-in")
    print("coordinator restarts, presumed abort: ", final, "locks held by", locked)
    final, blocked, locked = scenario(None, None, p3_yes=False)
    print("P3 votes NO:                          ", final)
    for pa in (False, True):
        for yes in (True, False):
            ps = [Participant("P1"), Participant("P2"), Participant("P3", yes)]
            c = Coordinator(ps, presumed_abort=pa)
            c.run(Plan())
            print(f"presumed_abort={pa!s:5}  P3 votes {'YES' if yes else 'NO ':3}"
                  f" -> coordinator forced log writes: {c.forced_writes}")

The last four lines count the coordinator's forced log writes: with the presumed-abort convention switched on, an aborted transaction costs it none. Now walk down the table. Every row ends in a single outcome, and the assert in scenario() checks atomicity on each, so the simulation itself is evidence, not just the argument. The rows fall into four families:

  • Coordinator crashes before the commit point (before PREPARE, mid-PREPARE, votes in but nothing logged). On restart the coordinator has no COMMIT record, so it presumes abort. Participants that had prepared are blocked until it returns, then told ABORT. Participants that never saw PREPARE were never bound and are never stuck.
  • Coordinator crashes after the commit point (COMMIT logged, none or one sent). The transaction is committed; the decision is on disk. On restart the coordinator finds COMMIT without an end record, and answers COMMIT to every inquiry. The window in which some participants are blocked is the same, but the outcome is the opposite. This is why participants cannot guess: from where they sit, the two cases are indistinguishable.
  • A participant crashes before voting, or after logging prepared but before the reply leaves. The coordinator times out and aborts (silence is NO). On restart the participant either has no prepared record (it never promised, so it may abort alone) or has one and is in doubt, so it asks the coordinator, who has no commit record and answers abort. Nobody else is blocked.
  • A participant crashes after learning the outcome but before logging it (the last row). Its log still says prepared, so it restarts in doubt, re-takes its locks and inquires; the coordinator, which has not yet seen an ACK from it and therefore still remembers, answers COMMIT.

Real systems add one refinement worth knowing: cooperative termination. A blocked participant can also ask the other participants. If any of them has already learned COMMIT or ABORT, that settles it. If any has not yet voted yes (it is in its initial or aborted state), the asker may safely abort, because the transaction can no longer commit. Only when every reachable participant is prepared and none knows the outcome is the question genuinely open, and then no amount of asking helps.

Common misconception: "2PC has a timeout, so it can't hang"

Timeouts help in phase 1: a coordinator that does not hear a vote may abort, and a participant that has not yet voted may abort unilaterally. After a participant has voted yes there is no safe timeout, because the only two outcomes it could pick are both sometimes wrong. A timeout converts "blocked" into "possibly inconsistent", which is worse. (Real products do offer an operator command to force a decision on an in-doubt transaction. That is a heuristic decision, and it can violate atomicity; see XA below.)

The blocking window

Now we can state precisely the flaw that 2PC is famous for. Suppose the coordinator crashes after every participant has voted yes and before any participant has heard the decision. Look at the situation from P1's chair:

  • It voted yes and logged prepared. It may not commit on its own: the coordinator might have decided abort (say, P2 voted no, or the coordinator timed out on it).
  • It may not abort on its own: the coordinator might have logged COMMIT, and for all P1 knows P3 has already heard it, committed and made the change visible.
  • Asking P2 and P3 does not help if they are in the same state.

So P1 must wait, holding its locks, until the coordinator comes back. This is the blocking problem: 2PC is a safe protocol (it never produces disagreement, even in this case) but not a live one (it can make no progress while one particular machine is down). The period during which a participant is prepared and has not heard the outcome is its window of uncertainty, and a transaction stuck in it is called in doubt.

The cost is not the stuck transaction itself, it is what the locks do to everyone else. Every other transaction that wants one of those rows queues behind them, and (as with any lock convoy) the queue grows while the coordinator is down. On a database that keeps prepared transactions across restarts, the locks even survive a restart of the participant: PostgreSQL's PREPARE TRANSACTION leaves an entry in pg_prepared_xacts that holds its locks until someone runs COMMIT PREPARED or ROLLBACK PREPARED, and forgotten prepared transactions are a classic cause of tables that mysteriously stay locked.

Two further costs are worth stating in numbers you can compute. Latency: a commit needs two message round trips (prepare/vote, commit/ack, although the client can be told "committed" as soon as the commit point is durable) and at least three forced log writes on the critical path (participant prepared, coordinator COMMIT, participant commit). Locks are held for the entire duration, so lock hold time is dominated by round trips. Across data centres that is the cost the next chapter measures. Availability: a transaction over n participants commits only if all n and the coordinator are up. If each is up 99.9% of the time, independent failures give a commit availability of roughly 0.999n+1; for ten participants about 98.9%, roughly eleven times the downtime of a single node.

Three-phase commit, in a paragraph

If blocking is the problem, can we add a phase to remove it? Dale Skeen's three-phase commit (1981) inserts a pre-commit step between voting and commit: the coordinator first tells everyone "everyone voted yes" (pre-commit) and only then "commit". The invariant is that no participant commits until all have reached the pre-committed state, so a prepared-but-not-pre-committed participant knows nobody has committed and can abort, while a pre-committed one knows everyone voted yes and can commit. With these rules the survivors can run a recovery election and finish without waiting for the dead coordinator. The catch is the assumptions: 3PC is non-blocking only if the network has bounded delay and a failed node can be reliably told apart from a slow one, that is, a synchronous system with no partitions (system models). Under a network partition, one side can time out and decide to abort while the other side, which saw the pre-commit, decides to commit, and you have disagreement. Since real networks do partition and clocks and timeouts do lie, 3PC buys liveness by giving up safety in exactly the situation where you need safety most. It is mostly a teaching device; real systems fix blocking with consensus instead.

Paxos Commit and the practical answer: replicate the coordinator

The honest root cause of blocking is that the decision lives on one machine's disk. If the decision were stored somewhere that survives a minority of failures, a replacement coordinator could read it and carry on. That is exactly what a replicated log (chapter 8) provides.

Jim Gray and Leslie Lamport worked this out in Consensus on Transaction Commit (2006), the protocol called Paxos Commit. Sketch: each participant's vote is not sent to a single coordinator but is the proposed value of its own instance of Paxos, run among 2F+1 acceptors. The transaction commits if and only if every participant's instance has chosen "prepared"; if some participant is silent, any recovery leader may propose "aborted" for that participant's instance (and if it loses to an already chosen "prepared", it simply learns that). Because the choice of each vote is a consensus decision stored on a majority, no single crash can hide it: the outcome is a function of values that survive up to F acceptor failures. With F = 0 (one acceptor) the protocol reduces to ordinary 2PC, which is a nice sanity check on the design. The price is more messages and a bit more latency in the failure-free case; the gain is that the commit is non-blocking as long as a majority of acceptors is alive, with no 3PC-style synchrony assumption.

Most real systems do not implement Paxos Commit literally. They reach the same effect with the building blocks they already have: make each participant a replica group and make the coordinator's state a record in a replicated store. Spanner runs 2PC across Paxos groups, so that each "participant" is a group whose leader can fail over and whose prepared record is replicated; Percolator-style systems keep the commit decision in a single replicated row (the "primary"); CockroachDB-style systems keep a replicated transaction record. The next chapter shows all three. The shared pattern: 2PC is still the protocol, but nobody ever depends on one disk. The coordinator can still be temporarily unavailable while a new leader is elected, but it is a seconds-long pause, not an indefinite one.

Go deeper (optional): why presumed abort needs care with replicated coordinators advanced

Presumed abort answers "abort" when no record is found. With a replicated coordinator, "no record" must mean "no record on a majority", read through the log (a linearizable read, chapter 5), not "my local replica has not caught up". An answer from a stale follower would be a silent atomicity bug. Systems avoid it by routing in-doubt inquiries through the leader, or by writing an explicit aborted record so absence never has to be interpreted.

XA in practice

Most engineers meet 2PC through XA, the X/Open standard (early 1990s) for how a transaction manager talks to resource managers: databases, message brokers, anything that can prepare. The calls map directly onto this chapter: xa_start/xa_end bracket the work, xa_prepare is phase 1, xa_commit/xa_rollback is phase 2, and xa_recover lists in-doubt transactions after a crash. Java's JTA exposes it, application servers embed a transaction manager, and PostgreSQL (PREPARE TRANSACTION) and MySQL (XA START … XA PREPARE … XA COMMIT) support the participant side.

The experience of running it matches the theory:

  • The transaction manager's own log is the coordinator's log. If it is lost or replaced, in-doubt transactions on participants have no one to ask.
  • After a crash the operator (or a recovery process) must resolve in-doubt transactions; until then locks are held, which shows up as mysterious blocking.
  • When resolution is impossible, tools let an administrator force a decision. These are the heuristic outcomes (heuristic commit, heuristic rollback, heuristic mixed): they unblock the system and may break atomicity, and the standard even has an error code for "I forced this and it was wrong".
  • Throughput suffers from lock hold time over two round trips plus forced log writes, and every resource in the transaction must support XA (many hosted services, REST APIs and most message queues do not).

Contrast with sagas

In Sagas & Distributed Workflows you saw the architect's answer to the same problem: don't ask for atomicity. Each step is a local transaction that commits immediately and publishes its result; if a later step fails, compensating transactions undo the earlier ones semantically (refund the card, cancel the booking). Now we can say precisely what each approach trades.

Two-phase commitSaga
AtomicityReal: no outside observer sees a partial result (given isolation).Eventual: the system passes through visible partial states, and may need compensations to return.
IsolationParticipants hold locks from prepare to decision.None across steps: other transactions see intermediate results (needs semantic locks, versioning).
Failure behaviourCan block: stuck participants hold locks while the coordinator is unreachable.Never blocks other work; failures leave a workflow to retry or compensate.
What each participant must offerA prepare operation that holds resources durably (XA, or an engine's own).Just a local transaction and, for compensable steps, an undo.
Fits bestShort transactions inside a system you control, where partial states must never be visible (shards of one database).Long-lived business processes across independently owned services, where holding locks across the network is unacceptable.

Neither is "right". A bank moving money between two shards of the same ledger wants atomicity and isolation, and 2PC over replicated participants gives it (chapter 14). A travel booking spanning a hotel, an airline and a card processor run by three companies cannot ask them all to hold locks; it wants a saga and a plan for compensation. The senior-engineer skill is choosing based on what a visible half-finished state costs, and on who owns the participants.

Distributed systems connection

Atomic commit is where the three big ideas of this course meet. Failure models (chapter 1): 2PC is safe under any crash and any message loss, but its liveness needs the coordinator back. Consensus (chapters 8 to 10): putting the coordinator on a replicated state machine turns "blocked until it returns" into "blocked until a new leader is elected". Time and order: once participants can commit, when they commit relative to other transactions becomes the question, which the next chapter answers with timestamps, commit-wait and deterministic ordering. The Kafka-style transactions in chapter 17 use a coordinator with a log of its own too: the same shape, applied to a stream.

Key takeaways

  • Atomic commit is not consensus: every participant has a veto and no participant is replaceable, so the protocol must hear from all of them. It can be built from consensus, which is how blocking is fixed.
  • 2PC: phase 1 asks and collects durable promises (prepared logged before the YES); phase 2 spreads a decision that already exists. The decision exists the moment the coordinator's COMMIT is on disk (the commit point).
  • Every log-before-send rule has a counterexample: drop it and a crash produces disagreement. Log first, then speak.
  • Presumed abort: no record means abort. It makes aborts free and makes a coordinator crash before the commit point safe.
  • If the coordinator dies after everyone voted yes, participants cannot choose either outcome and hold their locks until it returns. 2PC is safe but not live; the stuck window is the cost.
  • 3PC removes blocking only in a synchronous, partition-free world; Paxos Commit (Gray and Lamport) removes it by running consensus on each vote. In practice: replicate the coordinator and the participants.
  • XA is 2PC as a standard interface; its operational pain is in-doubt transactions and forced heuristic decisions. Sagas avoid the blocking by giving up atomicity and isolation.

Check your understanding

Why is atomic commit not simply consensus on "commit or abort"?

  1. Consensus cannot decide between two values
  2. In atomic commit every participant holds data no other node has and can veto the outcome, so the protocol must hear from all of them, whereas consensus replicas are interchangeable and a majority suffices
  3. Atomic commit needs a leader and consensus does not
  4. Consensus requires synchronized clocks and atomic commit does not
Two differences: a single NO must force abort (validity ties the outcome to the votes), and participants are not replaceable (shard 2's vote cannot be guessed by shard 1). That is why one dead participant stops a commit, while a dead minority of consensus replicas does not stop agreement. The two problems are related: atomic commit can be solved using consensus, as Paxos Commit does.

A participant replies YES to PREPARE but has not yet forced a prepared record to disk when it crashes. What can go wrong?

  1. Nothing, because the coordinator will resend PREPARE
  2. The coordinator will crash as well
  3. The transaction will simply take longer
  4. On restart the participant has forgotten its promise, while the coordinator may already have committed, so the participants end up disagreeing
The YES is a promise that the participant can commit even after a crash. If the promise is only in memory, the crash cancels it, but the coordinator has already counted it. The log write must come before the reply. Resending PREPARE does not help once the coordinator has the vote and has moved to the decision.

The coordinator crashes after every participant voted YES and before sending any decision. A prepared participant cannot ask anyone useful. What is its safe behaviour?

  1. Keep holding its locks and wait for the coordinator to return (or for cooperative termination to find out the outcome)
  2. Abort after a timeout, since abort is always safe
  3. Commit after a timeout, since all votes were yes
  4. Release its locks but remember the transaction
Aborting is wrong because the coordinator may have logged COMMIT and other participants may already have committed. Committing is wrong because the coordinator may have decided abort (for instance after a missing vote). Releasing locks lets other transactions see state that is about to change. Only waiting is safe, which is exactly why 2PC blocks.

What does presumed abort let a coordinator skip, and why is that safe?

  1. The participants' prepared records, because abort needs no promise
  2. The commit record, because commit is the default
  3. Forcing a log record and collecting ACKs when it decides abort, because "no record" is interpreted as abort and no one can have committed without a COMMIT record
  4. Phase 1 entirely when it expects the transaction to abort
An abort decision needs no durable record because absence of a COMMIT record already means abort to anyone who asks. Participants still force prepared and the coordinator still forces COMMIT; only the abort path gets cheaper. It is safe because a commit cannot have happened without the coordinator's COMMIT record being on disk first.

Why does three-phase commit not solve blocking in practice?

  1. It needs more than three phases to be correct
  2. Its non-blocking recovery assumes bounded delays and reliable failure detection; under a network partition two sides can decide differently
  3. It is slower than 2PC by an order of magnitude on every transaction
  4. It cannot abort a transaction once started
3PC (Skeen, 1981) is non-blocking in a synchronous system without partitions. In an asynchronous network with partitions a node cannot tell "dead" from "slow", so the recovery rules can produce a commit on one side and an abort on the other, which breaks agreement. Consensus-based approaches keep safety under partitions and give up liveness only while a majority is unreachable.

A hotel, an airline and a payment provider run by three different companies must be coordinated for a booking. Which is usually the better fit?

  1. XA across all three, so the booking is atomic and isolated
  2. Three-phase commit across the internet
  3. A saga with compensations, because the parties will not hold each other's locks and may not support prepare at all
  4. A single global lock held for the whole booking
2PC needs each party to hold resources durably while in doubt and to expose a prepare operation, which independent organisations rarely allow, and a stuck coordinator would freeze other companies' data. A saga gives up isolation and atomicity (partial results are visible and compensated) in exchange for never blocking.
