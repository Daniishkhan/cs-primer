Paxos, Step by Step

Paxos is the protocol that taught the field how to agree on one value with nothing but unreliable messages and machines that can stop at any moment. It has a reputation for being impossible to understand, and the reputation is mostly undeserved: every step exists to block one specific failure. In this chapter we derive single-decree Paxos by removing each step and watching it break, prove why it is safe, break it with duelling proposers, and then grow it into the replicated log that real systems run (Multi-Paxos), before comparing it with Raft. You will also run a seeded simulation of it on a network that drops and reorders messages.

Why this matters

Raft is easy to learn precisely because it makes design choices that Paxos leaves open. If you only know Raft, you know one point in a design space, and you may not see why the rules are what they are. Paxos shows the bare skeleton: the least you must do to make agreement safe. That skeleton shows up everywhere. Chubby, Spanner, many cloud databases and the "consensus" boxes in countless architecture diagrams are Paxos or a close relative, and when engineers say "it's just Multi-Paxos" or "that is Paxos with a stable leader" they expect you to follow. It is also the best exercise in the course for the habit this whole subject rewards: for every rule in a protocol, be able to name the execution that goes wrong without it.

Where we are

You met the consensus problem, majorities and Raft's elections and log replication in networking chapter 20, and the previous chapter, Consensus & Replicated State Machines, set out the formal problem: agreement, validity and termination, the role of quorum intersection, and how a log of agreed values runs any service. We take all of that as given. This chapter asks the question underneath it: what is the minimum protocol that is guaranteed never to let two different values be decided?

Analogy: a committee that talks only by unreliable post

Five committee members must settle one question, say the date of the annual dinner, but they can only send each other letters. Letters can be lost, arrive late, or arrive out of order, and any member may fall asleep for an unknown time (and wake up believing the world is as they left it). There is no phone and no clock everyone trusts. They want one property above all: once the committee has decided "the 14th", nobody may ever be told "the 21st", however many letters go astray. Everything in Paxos is a rule for how members write, answer and remember letters so that this property survives.

Where it breaks down: real members would simply notice that most others are still awake and call a meeting. The committee is also allowed to forget everything between letters, which computers cannot do: an acceptor's promises must be written to disk before it replies (systems chapter 9), or a restart erases its memory and the guarantees with it.

The setting and the goal

Single-decree Paxos solves one instance of consensus: the system as a whole picks one value, once. (A "decree" is one decision of the parliament in Lamport's original Part-Time Parliament story, and single-decree is the name later writers give to running one instance; "Paxos Made Simple" presents the same thing as choosing a single value.) The processes play three roles, and one machine often plays all three:

Proposer
Suggests a value and drives the protocol to get it accepted. Several proposers may be active at once, and they do not coordinate with each other. (Purple in our diagrams.)
Acceptor
The voters and the memory of the system. Each acceptor stores what it has promised and what it has accepted. A majority of acceptors is what makes a decision. (Blue.)
Learner
Finds out what was decided, in order to act on it, for example a replica applying a command. Learners take no part in the decision itself.

The messages may be lost, duplicated, delayed or reordered; processes may crash and later recover (with their stable storage intact). Messages are not forged (that is the Byzantine world of chapter 12). The promises are:

  • Validity. Only a value that some proposer actually proposed can be chosen.
  • Agreement (safety). At most one value is ever chosen. A value is chosen when a majority of acceptors have accepted it with the same ballot (defined below).
  • Learners learn only chosen values.

Termination is not on that list, and that is deliberate. By the FLP result (chapter 2) no deterministic protocol in the asynchronous model can promise to always finish, so Paxos separates concerns: it is always safe, and it is live only when the network and processes behave well enough for long enough. Keep that split in mind; the whole chapter is about the safety half until the section on livelock.

Ballots: a way to say "later"

Suppose acceptors simply accepted the first value they saw. Three proposers each reach a different acceptor first, A1 accepts X, A2 accepts Y, A3 accepts Z. Nobody has a majority, and since an acceptor that has accepted must never change its mind (otherwise a chosen value could vanish), the system is stuck for good. So acceptors must be allowed to accept more than one proposal over time. But the moment they may accept several, we need a rule that stops a later acceptance from undoing a decision. To reason about "earlier" and "later" without trusting clocks (networking chapter 17), every attempt carries a ballot number, also called a proposal number:

  • Ballots are totally ordered, so any two can be compared.
  • Different proposers use different ballots, so two attempts never tie. A common scheme is the pair (round, proposer id), compared as round first, and the id to break ties. P2's first ballot is (1, 2), which is higher than P1's (1, 1) and lower than P1's (2, 1).
  • A proposer that learns of a higher ballot can always jump over it by using a larger round. Nothing needs to be synchronized for this to work.

A ballot is a logical clock of exactly the kind Raft calls a term. It does not say when something happened. It says whose attempt supersedes whose. A proposal is a pair (ballot, value). The aim of the protocol is to ensure: once a proposal with value v is chosen, every proposal with a higher ballot also has value v. If that holds, nothing can ever be chosen that differs from v. Everything below is machinery to make that sentence true.

The two phases

Here is the protocol in the shape of the paper, with the reason for each step to follow.

Phase 1 (prepare / promise).

  1. 1a, prepare. A proposer picks a fresh ballot n higher than any it has used and sends prepare(n) to the acceptors (to all of them, or at least a majority).
  2. 1b, promise. An acceptor that receives prepare(n) with n larger than any ballot it has promised so far records promised = n and replies promise(n, accepted), where accepted is the highest-numbered proposal it has accepted so far, or "none". From now on it will refuse to accept anything with a ballot below n. If n is not higher than its current promise, it ignores the message (or sends a refusal, which only speeds up the proposer).

Phase 2 (accept / accepted).

  1. 2a, accept. When the proposer has promises from a majority, it chooses a value: if any promise reported an accepted proposal, it must use the value of the one with the highest ballot; only if none did may it use its own value. It sends accept(n, v) to the acceptors.
  2. 2b, accepted. An acceptor that receives accept(n, v) accepts it, unless it has since promised a ballot higher than n. It records accepted = (n, v) and replies accepted(n, v).

A value is chosen as soon as a majority of acceptors have accepted the same proposal. Note that this moment is not visible to any single process: it is a fact about the state of the system, and the learners and proposers discover it after the fact. The picture of a smooth run is the first diagram, drawn the way distributed-systems people draw everything, as a space-time diagram: one horizontal line per process, time running left to right, and each message a slanted arrow from sender to receiver.

Both acceptor rules compare against the same variable, promised, and the two rules differ by one character: promise needs n > promised, accept needs n >= promised. The equality in the second rule is the case where this very proposer's own prepare set the promise. The acceptor's durable state is exactly three things: promised, and the pair accepted = (ballot, value). The proposer's state is soft: if it crashes it can start again with a higher ballot.

An acceptor must write before it replies

If an acceptor promises ballot 5, crashes, restarts with an empty memory and then accepts a stale ballot-3 proposal, its promise was a lie, and the argument below collapses. Real implementations flush promised and accepted to stable storage (an fsync, with all the caveats of systems chapter 9) before sending the reply. This durable write is also why consensus latency is bounded below by disk latency, not just network latency.

Every step exists for a reason: remove one and watch it break

The fastest way to understand Paxos is to treat each rule as a patch and to see the bug it patches. There are three patches that matter.

Remove the majority: the split brain

If a "decision" needed only one acceptor, or any fixed minority, two proposers could talk to two disjoint groups and each get their value chosen. We need any two decision groups to share a member, which is exactly the quorum-intersection property from networking chapter 18: any two majorities of the same set of acceptors overlap in at least one acceptor. That overlap is the memory that links one ballot to the next, and every argument below uses it.

Remove "promise not to accept lower ballots": the late accept

Suppose acceptors answer a prepare but then keep accepting any accept they receive, whatever its ballot. Take three acceptors:

  1. P1 runs phase 1 with ballot 1 on A1 and A2. Neither has accepted anything. P1 is slow; its accept(1, X) messages are delayed in the network.
  2. P2 runs phase 1 with ballot 2 on A2 and A3. Neither reports anything accepted, so P2 is free to propose Y. It sends accept(2, Y), and A2 and A3 accept it. Y is chosen.
  3. Now P1's delayed accept(1, X) messages arrive at A1 and A2. With no promise to stop them, both accept. A1 and A2 now both hold X, a majority. X is chosen too.

The promise is what rules this out: A2 promised not to accept anything below ballot 2, so it rejects ballot 1. A promise is a way of making a past statement binding on the future, and that is the heart of phase 1. It "closes" every lower ballot at that acceptor: after replying to prepare(n), the acceptor's answer about what it has accepted below n can never go out of date.

Remove "adopt the highest accepted value": the overwrite

This is the subtle one and the one most worth internalizing. Suppose phase 1 only gave the proposer permission to go ahead, and the proposer then simply proposed its own value. Here is a concrete execution with three acceptors, P1 wanting X and P2 wanting Y:

  1. P1 sends prepare(1). A1 and A2 answer with promises and no accepted value (A3 is slow or the message is lost; the protocol only needs a majority).
  2. P1 sends accept(1, X). A1 and A2 accept it. A majority holds (1, X): X is chosen. The accepted replies are lost, so P1 and everybody else are still unaware.
  3. P2 wakes up with ballot 2 and sends prepare(2) to A2 and A3. A3 reports nothing. A2 reports "I have accepted (1, X)". P2 now holds a majority of promises, and one of them says X is already accepted somewhere.
  4. If P2 ignores that report and sends accept(2, Y), A2 will accept it (ballot 2 is not below its promise of 2) and so will A3. Now (2, Y) is accepted by a majority: Y is chosen too. A2 has overwritten X.

The fix is the rule in step 2a: the proposer must propose the value of the highest-ballot accepted proposal reported in its majority of promises. Why does that suffice, and why the highest one? Because P2's majority overlaps the majority that chose X in at least one acceptor (here A2), and that acceptor, having promised ballot 2, can no longer accept anything lower, so what it reports is its final state at all ballots below 2. If X was chosen at ballot 1, A2 reports ballot 1 or something higher. If something higher is reported, that proposal was itself made by a proposer that followed the same rule, so by induction it carries X as well. Adopt the highest and you inherit X, whatever happened in between.

Go deeper: the safety proof in six lines optional

Claim. If a proposal (n, v) is chosen, then every proposal (m, w) with m > n that is ever sent in an accept message has w = v.

Proof by strong induction on m. Assume the claim holds for every ballot in [n, m). A proposer sends accept(m, w) only after receiving promises from some majority Q. Let C be a majority that accepted (n, v). Q and C share an acceptor a. If a had promised m before accepting (n, v), it would have rejected (n, v), since n < m; so a accepted (n, v) first, and its promise to m therefore reports some accepted proposal (k, u) with n ≤ k < m (the highest it had, which is at least ballot n). The proposer takes the report with the highest ballot k' among Q, so k' ≥ k ≥ n and k' < m (nobody in Q has accepted a ballot at or above m, because they all promised m and an accept at m has not been sent yet). By the induction hypothesis the proposal with ballot k' carries v, so w = v. □

Notice what was used: quorum intersection, the promise that makes an acceptor's report final for lower ballots, and the "highest ballot" rule. Remove any one and a step in the proof fails, which is the same as saying each of the counterexamples above exists. Lamport's own formulation in "Paxos Made Simple" builds the proof backwards from an invariant ("P2c") that says a proposal with value v and ballot n may be issued only if some majority has either accepted nothing below n or has v as its highest accepted value below n; phases 1 and 2 are simply the cheapest way to establish that invariant.

Learners and the "who knows it is chosen?" question

Being chosen is a property of acceptor state, but acting on a decision requires a learner to know. There are several designs. Acceptors can send each accepted message to every learner, who count: when a learner sees a majority of acceptors accepted the same ballot, the value is chosen. Or the acceptors tell one distinguished learner, who tells the rest, at the cost of one more hop and a failure mode if it crashes. Or a learner that is unsure can ask the acceptors, but notice the trap: what an acceptor has accepted is only a snapshot, and acceptors may hold proposals from different ballots, some of them abandoned. Seeing the same value in two replies does not prove it was chosen (they may have accepted it at different ballots, with no majority at any one ballot), and a majority of replies that look inconsistent does not prove that nothing was chosen. The reliable way to learn the outcome of an uncertain instance is to run the protocol (a new proposer with a higher ballot is forced to adopt any chosen value, and will tell you which). In the replicated-log use of Paxos this is exactly what a new leader does for every slot it is unsure about.

Duelling proposers and livelock

Safety is settled; what about progress? Look again at the rules: phase 1 of a higher ballot invalidates the phase 2 of every lower ballot, because the acceptors have promised not to accept it. Now give two proposers bad luck. P1 completes phase 1 with ballot 1. P2 completes phase 1 with ballot 2, so P1's accepts are rejected. P1, discovering that, restarts with ballot 3, which invalidates P2's accepts. P2 restarts with ballot 4... Each proposer's progress destroys the other's, and nobody is crashed or even slow. This is livelock: activity without progress.

Is this a flaw? It cannot be avoided in principle, since FLP says that no deterministic asynchronous protocol can promise termination, and an adversarial schedule like this one is exactly the kind of schedule that proof builds. Practical systems accept this and make livelock improbable and short. There are two standard moves, and they are normally combined:

  • A distinguished proposer (a leader). If only one proposer is active, there is nobody to duel with. Paxos tolerates several, so electing a leader is only a performance optimization: if the election misfires and two proposers briefly both think they lead, safety still holds and the worst result is a bout of duelling. The leader is chosen by a failure detector or a lease (chapter 3, chapter 4), which is the weaker kind of agreement that a timeout-based mechanism can provide, with no safety burden on it.
  • Randomized backoff. A proposer whose ballot is rejected waits a random time before retrying, longer after each failure (exponential backoff with jitter, the same idea as in networking chapter 16 and Raft's randomized election timeouts). Two duellists then rarely retry at the same instant, so one gets to finish both phases before the other returns.

In the simulation below, both mechanisms are in play in miniature: retries are scheduled after a randomized delay, and in practice you would also let one proposer back off for a longer period when it sees a ballot from a rival.

Run it: Paxos on a lossy, reordering network

This is single-decree Paxos in about 100 lines of Python (standard library only). The "network" is a priority queue of messages, each given a random delivery time (so messages overtake one another), and each message is dropped with probability 0.25. All randomness comes from a seeded random.Random, so every run is reproducible. First the protocol itself: the network, the acceptor, and the proposer with a switch for the adoption rule.

"""Single-decree Paxos in a seeded, lossy, reordering simulated network."""
import heapq
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Optional

Ballot = tuple[int, int]            # (round, proposer id): totally ordered, unique per proposer
NO_BALLOT: Ballot = (0, 0)


class Net:
    """Message queue with simulated time. Drops and random delays reorder messages."""
    def __init__(self, rng: random.Random, drop: float, max_delay: int):
        self.rng, self.drop, self.max_delay = rng, drop, max_delay
        self.q: list = []
        self.now = 0
        self.seq = 0
        self.sent = self.dropped = 0

    def send(self, dst: str, msg: dict) -> None:
        self.sent += 1
        if self.rng.random() < self.drop:
            self.dropped += 1
            return
        self.seq += 1
        heapq.heappush(self.q, (self.now + self.rng.randint(1, self.max_delay), self.seq, dst, msg))

    def next(self):
        if not self.q:
            return None
        t, _, dst, msg = heapq.heappop(self.q)
        self.now = max(self.now, t)
        return dst, msg


@dataclass
class Acceptor:
    name: str
    net: Net
    promised: Ballot = NO_BALLOT
    accepted: Optional[tuple[Ballot, str]] = None   # (ballot, value)

    def on(self, m: dict) -> None:
        if m["type"] == "prepare":
            if m["b"] > self.promised:                      # promise never to accept lower ballots
                self.promised = m["b"]
                self.net.send(m["from"], {"type": "promise", "from": self.name, "b": m["b"],
                                          "accepted": self.accepted})
        elif m["type"] == "accept":
            if m["b"] >= self.promised:                     # not promised to anyone higher
                self.promised = m["b"]
                self.accepted = (m["b"], m["v"])
                self.net.send(m["from"], {"type": "accepted", "from": self.name, "b": m["b"], "v": m["v"]})
                self.net.observe_accept(self.name, m["b"], m["v"])


@dataclass
class Proposer:
    name: str
    pid: int
    value: str
    net: Net
    acceptors: list
    adopt: bool = True              # the rule under test: adopt the highest-numbered accepted value
    round: int = 0
    ballot: Ballot = NO_BALLOT
    promises: dict = field(default_factory=dict)
    accepts: set = field(default_factory=set)
    sent_accept: bool = False
    decided: Optional[str] = None
    deadline: int = 0

    def start_round(self) -> None:
        self.round += 1
        self.ballot = (self.round, self.pid)
        self.promises, self.accepts, self.sent_accept = {}, set(), False
        for a in self.acceptors:
            self.net.send(a, {"type": "prepare", "from": self.name, "b": self.ballot})
        # randomized backoff before retrying with a higher ballot
        self.deadline = self.net.now + 20 + self.net.rng.randint(0, 20)

    def on(self, m: dict) -> None:
        if self.decided or m["b"] != self.ballot:
            return
        majority = len(self.acceptors) // 2 + 1
        if m["type"] == "promise":
            self.promises[m["from"]] = m["accepted"]
            if len(self.promises) >= majority and not self.sent_accept:
                self.sent_accept = True
                prior = [x for x in self.promises.values() if x is not None]
                v = self.value
                if self.adopt and prior:
                    v = max(prior)[1]                      # value of the highest-numbered accepted ballot
                for a in self.acceptors:
                    self.net.send(a, {"type": "accept", "from": self.name, "b": self.ballot, "v": v})
        elif m["type"] == "accepted":
            self.accepts.add(m["from"])
            if len(self.accepts) >= majority:
                self.decided = m["v"]

Then the harness. It runs two competing proposers (P1 wants "X", P2 wants "Y") against three acceptors, and uses an omniscient observer, something no real node has, to watch for the moment a value becomes chosen. That is how we test safety: whatever the network does, at most one value may ever be chosen.

def run(seed: int, adopt: bool = True, drop: float = 0.25, max_delay: int = 12, verbose: bool = False):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    net = Net(rng, drop, max_delay)
    chosen_by_ballot: dict = {}
    chosen_values: list = []

    def observe(name, b, v):                      # omniscient observer, used only to check safety
        s = chosen_by_ballot.setdefault((b, v), set())
        s.add(name)
        if len(s) == 2 and v not in chosen_values:
            chosen_values.append(v)
            if verbose:
                print(f"  t={net.now:3d}  *** value {v!r} is CHOSEN (ballot {b} accepted by a majority)")
    net.observe_accept = observe

    names = ["A1", "A2", "A3"]
    nodes: dict = {n: Acceptor(n, net) for n in names}
    p1 = Proposer("P1", 1, "X", net, names, adopt)
    p2 = Proposer("P2", 2, "Y", net, names, adopt)
    nodes.update(P1=p1, P2=p2)
    p1.start_round()
    p2.start_round()
    steps = 0
    while steps < 4000 and not (p1.decided and p2.decided):
        steps += 1
        for p in (p1, p2):                         # timeouts: retry with a higher ballot
            if not p.decided and net.now >= p.deadline and (not net.q or net.q[0][0] > p.deadline):
                p.start_round()
        ev = net.next()
        if ev is None:
            for p in (p1, p2):
                if not p.decided:
                    net.now = max(net.now, p.deadline)
                    p.start_round()
            continue
        dst, m = ev
        if verbose:
            extra = f" accepted={m['accepted']}" if m["type"] == "promise" else (f" v={m['v']!r}" if "v" in m else "")
            print(f"  t={net.now:3d}  {m['from']} -> {dst}: {m['type']}{m['b']}{extra}")
        nodes[dst].on(m)
    return chosen_values, (p1.decided, p2.decided), net, (p1.round, p2.round)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("One run, seed 4, with the adoption rule (drop rate 25%):")
    vals, dec, net, rounds = run(4, verbose=True)
    print(f"chosen={vals} decided by proposers={dec} rounds={rounds} "
          f"messages sent={net.sent} dropped={net.dropped}\n")

    for adopt in (True, False):
        bad = livelock = 0
        for seed in range(2000):
            vals, dec, net, _ = run(seed, adopt)
            if len(vals) > 1:
                bad += 1
                first_bad = seed if bad == 1 else first_bad
            if not (dec[0] and dec[1]):
                livelock += 1
        label = "adopt highest accepted value" if adopt else "SKIP the adoption step    "
        print(f"{label}: 2000 seeds, runs where two different values were chosen = {bad}"
              + (f" (first: seed {first_bad})" if bad else ""))

Read the trace for seed 4; it shows most of what this chapter says in one run. Both proposers start at time 0, but the network treats them differently: most of P1's first messages are lost, and the one that arrives (t=3, at A3) is ignored, because A3 has already promised P2's higher ballot (1, 2). P2 gets promises from A3 and A2 and no accepted value is reported, so it proposes its own Y; A1 accepts it at t=13 and A3 at t=19, and at that moment Y is chosen (the starred line), although neither P2 nor anybody else can see it: P2's accepted replies are still in flight. P1 keeps retrying with ballots (2,1), (3,1), (4,1)... as its randomized timer fires. At t=34 A1's promise to P1 already reports ((1, 2), 'Y'), but one promise is not a majority and P2's competing round (2, 2) overtakes it. Finally, at t=130 and t=133, promises for (5, 1) arrive from A1 and A3, reporting ((2, 2), 'Y') and ((1, 2), 'Y'); P1 must adopt the highest, so it proposes Y in ballot (5, 1) instead of its own X. P1 began wanting X and ends up deciding Y. That is the adoption rule at work, and it is why the summary line says chosen=['Y']: only one value was ever chosen, though two proposers began with different wishes. The run sent 47 messages and the network dropped 15 of them.

The last two lines are the experiment. With the adoption rule on, 2000 different seeds (each with a different pattern of drops and delays) produce zero runs where two values are chosen. With the rule switched off, every run breaks. That is not a coincidence of the seeds: in this harness both proposers keep trying until they believe they have succeeded, so as soon as each gets its own value accepted by a majority, two values have been chosen. In a real system the failure would need a rarer coincidence, which is exactly why such bugs survive in code for years and then strike at 3 a.m. A test that cannot fail tells you little, which is the point of building the broken variant: the checker is demonstrably capable of catching the bug. This style of testing (a seeded simulator, an invariant checked on every run, a deliberate bug to prove the checker works) comes back in chapter 19, and you will build the full Multi-Paxos version in Lab 4.

The widget: be the unreliable network

The simulator above decides randomly which messages to drop. In the widget below you are the network. Every message waits in a list until you deliver it or drop it. Three scripted stories are provided: a happy path, the counterexample from this chapter (a value is chosen, then a rival arrives, and you can switch the adoption rule off to see the overwrite), and the duel. Try your own orders too. A good challenge: can you find any sequence of deliveries and drops, with the adoption rule on, that makes the red banner appear? You will not, and the proof above says why.

From one decision to a log: Multi-Paxos

A real service needs a never-ending sequence of decisions: the replicated log of state machine replication (chapter 8). The obvious construction is to run an independent single-decree instance for each slot (log position): slot 1 decides the first command, slot 2 the second, and so on. Each instance is safe by itself. The cost is two round trips per command (prepare then accept), plus the duelling hazard on every slot. Multi-Paxos is the set of optimizations that make this efficient:

  • A stable leader skips phase 1. The key observation is that a promise names a ballot, not a slot. When the leader runs phase 1 with ballot n, the acceptors promise not to accept anything below n in any slot. One phase 1 therefore covers every future slot. As long as the same leader keeps its ballot, every new command needs only phase 2: one round trip from the leader to a majority, the same cost as Raft's AppendEntries. Phase 1 is repeated only when leadership changes.
  • Pipelining. The leader does not wait for slot k to be chosen before sending slot k+1, so many accepts are in flight at once.
  • Piggybacking. Learning "slot k is chosen" is attached to the next accept message rather than sent separately.

A new leader's phase 1 must cover all slots it does not already know to be chosen, so each promise reply carries the accepted proposal for every slot that has one. The new leader then applies the adoption rule slot by slot: where some reply reported an accepted value, it re-proposes the highest-ballot one; where none did, the slot is free. That is where gaps come from, and the next diagram shows a typical one.

Two details are worth stating explicitly. First, a no-op is a legitimate decision. The state machine applies slots in order and cannot skip a slot it does not know, so a gap would block everything after it. Filling gaps with no-ops lets the log advance. Second, command d being discarded is not a bug. d was accepted by one acceptor out of three, never chosen, and no client was told it succeeded (the leader would have replied only after a majority accepted). The client will time out and retry (see idempotency in networking chapter 16), which is why the state machine needs deduplication. Compare this with Raft, where a leader's log has no holes at all; we come to that now.

Multi-Paxos as described in papers is a family, not a single specification. Lamport has said that the basic protocol is simple and the full system, with leader election, reconfiguration, snapshots and holes, is left to the implementer. Google's description of building a production Paxos-based database, "Paxos Made Live" (Chandra, Griesemer and Redstone, 2007), is a well-known account of how much engineering lies between the algorithm and a working system, including disk corruption, membership change and testing. Many of these questions are exactly what Raft answered in the paper itself.

Paxos and Raft: the same idea, different choices

It is common to hear that Paxos and Raft are different algorithms. The better view, argued carefully by Heidi Howard and Richard Mortier in "Paxos vs Raft: Have we reached consensus on distributed consensus?" (2020), is that Raft is a specific, well-specified variant of Multi-Paxos: the same safety core (majority quorums, ballot numbers called terms, a promise not to go backwards, and the carrying-forward of the highest accepted value), with different choices for the parts that Paxos leaves open. If you keep the table below in mind, you can read a paper or a codebase for either and translate.

The trade-off is real. Raft's restriction on elections makes recovery simpler to reason about and to implement, at the price of leaders that must already have the most complete log. Paxos's openness allows more flexible designs (leaderless variants, per-slot parallelism, different quorums), at the price of more decisions for the implementer. Neither is "more correct". We study Raft's own subtleties in the next chapter.

Flexible Paxos: quorums need not be majorities

One more generalization, because it changes how you think about quorums. In Paxos, phase 1 and phase 2 each use a majority. Howard, Malkhi and Spiegelman (2016) observed that this is stronger than necessary. What the safety proof actually uses is only that every phase-1 quorum intersects every phase-2 quorum. Two phase-2 quorums need not intersect each other, and two phase-1 quorums need not either. For example, with 5 acceptors you could use phase-2 quorums of size 2 and phase-1 quorums of size 4 (2 + 4 > 5, so any pair intersects). Phase 2 is the hot path, executed on every command, so shrinking it makes the steady state faster and cheaper (a write waits for just 2 of 5 acceptors). The price is paid at leader change, which becomes harder: phase 1 must now reach 4 of 5, so only one crashed acceptor can be tolerated at that moment. It is a precise way of trading availability during elections for speed during normal operation, and it is the theoretical basis for placing replicas across regions so that commits stay local (chapter 18).

Common misconceptions

"Paxos picks a leader first." Basic Paxos has no leader; a leader is an optimization for liveness and throughput. "Phase 1 is a vote." It is not a vote on a value: it is a request for a binding promise and a report of the past. "A value is chosen when the proposer receives enough accepted replies." It is chosen when a majority of acceptors have accepted it, which can be earlier than any process learns. "Paxos solves FLP." It does not; it stays safe in the asynchronous model and gives up guaranteed termination, exactly as FLP says it must.

Distributed systems connection

Every consensus system you will meet in the rest of the course is built from the pieces in this chapter. Raft's terms are ballots; its log is Multi-Paxos with no gaps; ZooKeeper's Zab and the protocols inside Spanner and many cloud databases are close cousins (chapter 11, chapter 14). The commit protocol of chapter 13 can be made non-blocking by running its coordinator's decision through a Paxos instance (the idea behind Paxos Commit by Gray and Lamport). And the habit that makes Paxos understandable, asking what the world looks like if you delete the rule, is the habit behind every protocol review and every model checker run.

Key takeaways

  • Single-decree Paxos has proposers, acceptors and learners. A value is chosen when a majority of acceptors accept the same ballot; nobody sees that moment directly. It is always safe and only guaranteed to finish when the system is well behaved (FLP).
  • Ballot numbers are totally ordered and unique per proposer, a logical clock saying whose attempt supersedes whose. Raft's term is the same thing.
  • Phase 1 (prepare/promise) gets a binding promise not to accept lower ballots and a report of the acceptor's past. The promise stops late messages from old ballots; the report lets the proposer see any value that may already be chosen.
  • A proposer must adopt the value of the highest-ballot accepted proposal it hears about. Skipping this lets a second proposer overwrite a chosen value, as the concrete counterexample (X chosen at A1 and A2, then Y at A2 and A3) shows. Quorum intersection is what makes the evidence reach it.
  • Duelling proposers can livelock, since each higher prepare kills the other's phase 2. A distinguished leader and randomized backoff make this rare without affecting safety.
  • Multi-Paxos reuses one phase 1 across all slots under a stable leader, so each command costs a single round trip; a new leader recovers accepted values slot by slot and fills gaps with no-ops.
  • Raft is best seen as a particular Multi-Paxos with a strong leader, no log gaps and a restricted election. Flexible Paxos shows that only phase-1 and phase-2 quorums must intersect, not each quorum with itself.

Check your understanding

Three acceptors A1 to A3. A1 and A2 have accepted (ballot 1, X). A proposer P2 runs phase 1 with ballot 2 and hears from A2 and A3, A2 reporting (1, X) and A3 reporting nothing. What must P2 propose in phase 2?

  1. Its own value, since it has the higher ballot
  2. X, the value of the highest-ballot accepted proposal it heard about
  3. Either value; they are equally safe
  4. Nothing: it must abort, because the replies disagree
The adoption rule: P2 must use the value of the highest-numbered accepted proposal in its promises. X may already be chosen (here it is), and proposing Y would let two values be chosen. The higher ballot only gives P2 the right to propose, not the freedom to pick any value. The replies need not agree; only the highest-ballot one counts.

Why must an acceptor, after replying promise(5), refuse to accept any proposal with a ballot below 5?

  1. To make ballots increase in time order
  2. To save memory by not storing older proposals
  3. So that the proposer of ballot 5 can learn the outcome in a single round
  4. So that the report it just gave stays true: nothing lower can be accepted later and invalidate the proposer's picture of the past
Phase 1 only works as a snapshot of the past if the past stops changing. Without the promise, a delayed accept(1, X) could land after ballot 5's proposer had decided what to propose, and X and a different value could both be chosen (the "late accept" counterexample). The promise closes every lower ballot at that acceptor.

Two proposers keep starting higher ballots, each invalidating the other's phase 2, and nothing is ever chosen. What is this, and what is the standard remedy?

  1. Livelock; use a distinguished leader and/or randomized backoff before retrying
  2. A safety violation; add a third phase
  3. Deadlock; use a lock manager
  4. Split brain; require all acceptors to agree
It is livelock: processes keep working but make no progress. Paxos stays safe throughout. A single active leader removes the duel, and randomized backoff makes simultaneous retries unlikely; neither is needed for safety, only for liveness. Requiring all acceptors would make the system stop whenever any one crashes.

In Multi-Paxos, why can a stable leader skip phase 1 for new commands?

  1. Because the leader's commands are never contested
  2. Because acceptors promise per slot, and the leader reuses the promise for each slot
  3. Because a promise names a ballot and covers every slot: one phase 1 reserves the whole log for that ballot
  4. Because phase 1 is only needed when messages are lost
An acceptor's promise is "I will not accept any ballot below n" for all slots, not for one. So long as the leader keeps ballot n, every new slot needs only phase 2. A new leader must run phase 1 again, because it has a new ballot and needs to learn what the old leader left in the open slots.

A new Multi-Paxos leader finds that slot 4 has no accepted value in any promise it received, but slot 5 does. What should it do about slot 4?

  1. Leave it empty and carry on at slot 5; the state machine can skip it
  2. Propose a no-op in slot 4 so the log has no hole, and re-propose the reported value in slot 5
  3. Wait until the old leader returns and supplies the value
  4. Renumber slot 5 as slot 4
The state machine applies slots strictly in order, so a hole blocks everything after it. Slot 4 was not chosen (no majority has it, and the promises prove no majority holds it), so a no-op is a safe value to choose. Slot 5 must be re-proposed with the reported value because it might have been chosen. Waiting for a crashed node would stall the system; renumbering would change decided history.

Flexible Paxos says quorums need not be majorities. What is the exact requirement for safety?

  1. Every quorum must contain more than half the acceptors
  2. Every two phase-2 quorums must intersect each other
  3. Every two phase-1 quorums must intersect each other
  4. Every phase-1 quorum must intersect every phase-2 quorum
The proof only uses the overlap between the quorum that chose a value (phase 2) and the quorum that later reads the past (phase 1). Phase-2 quorums need not meet each other, nor phase-1 quorums. With 5 acceptors, phase-2 quorums of 2 and phase-1 quorums of 4 work, trading leader-change availability for cheaper commits.
