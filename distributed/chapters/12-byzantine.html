Byzantine Faults & Beyond

Everything so far assumed that a failed node simply stops. This short chapter asks what changes when a node can send wrong, inconsistent or even malicious messages: the Byzantine Generals result, the 3f+1 bound, PBFT in outline, where this machinery is used and where it is not, a neutral look at blockchains, and the Byzantine-flavoured failures that happen in ordinary datacenters.

Why this matters

Raft and Paxos tolerate machines that crash, stall or lose messages. They do not tolerate a machine that answers one peer "yes" and another "no". Knowing where that line sits tells you what your system really assumes, why datacenter designs get away with the cheaper model, and why open networks like blockchains need something different. It also gives a vocabulary for the strange failures that are not malicious but behave as if they were.

When nodes can lie

The fault models of chapter 1 run from gentle to harsh: crash faults (a node stops and stays stopped), omission faults (it drops some messages), and at the extreme Byzantine faults (a node may do anything: send contradictory messages, forge its state, collude with other faulty nodes). A Byzantine node might be hacked, running buggy software that corrupted its memory, or operated by a party with interests of its own. The name comes from Lamport, Shostak and Pease's 1982 paper, "The Byzantine Generals Problem": several divisions of an army surround a city, each commanded by a general, and they must agree on a common plan (attack or retreat) by messenger, although some generals may be traitors who send conflicting messages to different peers. A plan that only some divisions carry out is a disaster.

The problem: a commander sends an order; the loyal lieutenants must (1) all obey the same order and (2) if the commander is loyal, obey what the commander sent.

The 3f+1 bound

The central result, for the setting where messages are plain "oral" messages (no signatures), is that agreement among n parties with f traitors is possible only if n ≥ 3f + 1. Three generals cannot tolerate even one traitor. Here is why, by the smallest case: a commander C and two lieutenants L1 and L2, with one traitor.

L1 cannot distinguish A from B. If it follows the commander in A (correct) it also follows the commander in B, while L2 in B, hearing "retreat" from the commander, would follow it too, so loyal lieutenants disagree. If it decides by majority of what it hears, it is split one-one. With a fourth party, the extra witness breaks the tie, which is the general picture.

Two more intuitions for the general bound n ≥ 3f + 1:

  • Waiting. A node cannot wait for replies from everyone, since up to f may be silent forever, so it can wait for at most n − f. But up to f of the replies it receives could be lies. To outvote the liars with honest replies you need (n − f) − f > f, which simplifies to n > 3f.
  • Quorum overlap. In crash-fault protocols two majorities overlap in one node, which is enough because that node is honest. With Byzantine faults the overlap node might be a liar, so two quorums must overlap in at least f + 1 nodes (one of them honest). With quorums of size n − f the overlap is at least 2(n − f) − n, and requiring that to be at least f + 1 gives n ≥ 3f + 1 again.

Compare: crash tolerance costs 2f + 1 servers (three to survive one failure, as in networking chapter 20); Byzantine tolerance costs 3f + 1 (four for one traitor). Cryptographic signatures change the picture: if messages cannot be forged, a loyal node can show others exactly what it was told, and in the synchronous setting of the original paper agreement becomes possible with any number of traitors. Practical systems with signatures still need extra replicas for liveness and quorum overlap in asynchronous networks.

PBFT in outline

Lamport's paper settled what is possible; Castro and Liskov's PBFT (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, 1999) showed that Byzantine state machine replication could be fast enough to use. It keeps the structure you know from Paxos and Raft: a leader (called the primary), numbered views (like terms), and an ordered log. It needs 3f + 1 replicas and works in a partially synchronous network: it is always safe, and makes progress when the network behaves. The normal case has three phases for each request:

  1. Pre-prepare. The client sends its request to the primary, which assigns it a sequence number and sends a pre-prepare to all replicas.
  2. Prepare. Each replica that accepts the proposal broadcasts a prepare to everyone. A replica is "prepared" once it has the pre-prepare and matching prepares from 2f other replicas. This establishes that a quorum agrees on this order for this request in this view, so a lying primary cannot give two replicas different requests for the same slot.
  3. Commit. Prepared replicas broadcast a commit. With 2f + 1 matching commits a replica executes the request. The extra round ensures the order survives a change of primary: a request committed anywhere will be found by the next view.
  4. Reply. Each replica replies to the client, who accepts a result once f + 1 replicas give the same answer (at least one of them is honest).

The cost is visible in the picture. The prepare and commit phases are all-to-all, so a request needs on the order of n² messages (compared with about n for a Raft leader's AppendEntries and replies). The all-to-all pattern, message authentication on every message, and the complexity of the view-change protocol are what limit the cluster sizes of classic PBFT to tens of replicas. Later protocols reduce the message cost in various ways, at the price of other assumptions.

Where BFT is used, and where it is not

Almost every datacenter system, including the ones in this course (etcd, ZooKeeper, Spanner, Kafka's controller), assumes crash faults only. This is a reasoned choice rather than an oversight:

  • One organization operates all the machines and runs all the code, so there is no adversary in the replica group to defend against, and an attacker who controls enough machines to be Byzantine has usually won already.
  • BFT costs more: 3f + 1 replicas rather than 2f + 1, signatures or MACs, more messages, much more complex code. More complex code has more bugs, and a bug in a rarely run view-change path can hurt more than the faults it protects against.
  • Correlated bugs defeat replication anyway: if every replica runs the same buggy software, they all fail the same way. (Byzantine tolerance with diverse implementations exists, at great cost.)

BFT earns its place when the replicas are run by parties who do not fully trust each other, or where a single compromised node must not be able to corrupt a record: consortium and permissioned ledgers, some financial and infrastructure settings, safety-critical systems such as redundant flight-control computers (where the worry is also faulty hardware that gives different answers to different readers). Even there, designs are usually small clusters.

Blockchains and Nakamoto consensus

PBFT-style protocols need a known, fixed set of participants; they cannot work when anyone may join. The Bitcoin design (described in Satoshi Nakamoto's 2008 paper) targets exactly the open case: no membership list, no trust, participants who may come and go. In outline:

  • Transactions are grouped into blocks, each containing the hash of the previous block, forming a chain: changing an old block changes its hash and invalidates every later block.
  • To propose the next block, a participant (a miner) must find a number that makes the block's hash fall below a target, by trial and error. This proof of work costs real computation and is cheap to verify.
  • Honest participants build on the chain with the most accumulated work. A competing branch can only overtake it if its creators out-compute the rest of the network.

Two properties are worth understanding, with neutral eyes. First, proof of work is Sybil resistance. In an open system one actor can pretend to be a thousand nodes (a Sybil attack), so "one node, one vote" is meaningless. Weighting influence by computation, a resource that cannot be faked, ties it to something costly. (Proof of stake systems tie it to owned tokens instead; they make different trade-offs, which are still actively debated.) Second, finality is probabilistic. Two miners can find blocks at nearly the same time, producing a temporary fork; the network settles on the heavier branch, and the shorter one is discarded. A transaction in a block is therefore never absolutely final: it is more and more unlikely to be reversed as blocks pile on top of it, and the guarantee holds assuming that no attacker controls most of the computing power. This contrasts with Raft or PBFT, where a committed entry is final immediately and for good, in exchange for a known membership.

The honest summary of the trade: open membership and no trusted party, in exchange for waiting for confirmations, limited throughput and high resource use. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on whether you actually have the problem it solves: mutually distrusting parties with no common administrator. Inside one company, a Raft cluster is cheaper, faster and has instant finality; the combination of "I need Byzantine tolerance" and "I know exactly who the participants are" points to BFT protocols, not mining.

Byzantine-ish failures in real life

You do not need a malicious node to get Byzantine-looking behaviour. Real systems produce it constantly, which is why careful engineers do not rely on the crash-only model blindly:

  • Corrupted messages and data. Bits flip in memory, on disks and on wires. A flipped bit in a message turns an honest node into one that "lies". The first line of defence is detection: checksums and cyclic redundancy codes on network frames and storage blocks, ECC memory, and end-to-end checks in the application, since a lower layer's check does not cover every hop (the end-to-end argument of Saltzer, Reed and Clark). Weak checksums such as TCP's 16-bit sum miss some corruption, so many systems add their own stronger checksum on top. Detect the corruption and you have converted a Byzantine fault into the benign kind: a dropped message.
  • Software bugs. A node with a bug can send inconsistent state to different peers, such as a corrupted gossip entry that spreads, and nothing in a crash-fault protocol protects against it.
  • Partial and asymmetric partitions. S1 can talk to S2, S2 to S3, S1 cannot talk to S3. To S1, S3 is dead; to S2 it is alive; S3 may think the same about S1. The cluster ends up with nodes that hold inconsistent views of who is up, exactly as if some nodes were telling different peers different stories. The Cloudflare incident in the partial-partition case study is a good example, a faulty switch that dropped some traffic but not all, confusing a Raft-based cluster for a long time. Pre-vote and CheckQuorum (chapter 10) reduce the damage, but no crash-fault protocol is designed for non-transitive connectivity.
  • "Gray" failures. A node that answers health checks but is slow or broken on real work looks healthy to one observer and dead to another, the disagreement that failure detectors (chapter 3) cannot fully resolve.

The practical conclusion is a middle path: keep the crash-fault protocols, and surround them with defences that turn most Byzantine-looking failures into crashes. Checksum everything, make nodes fail-stop when internal invariants break (crash rather than continue with a corrupted state), authenticate peers, and test with fault injection that includes partitions and corruption, not only clean crashes (chapter 19).

"Byzantine" is not a synonym for "weird"

In the strict sense, Byzantine tolerance means a bounded fraction of nodes may behave arbitrarily, including adversarially, and the protocol still works. A system that tolerates crashes and happens to survive some corruption is not Byzantine fault tolerant. Be clear which claim you are making, and which fault model a system's documentation actually promises.

Distributed systems connection

Key takeaways

  • Byzantine faults let a node behave arbitrarily, including sending different messages to different peers. The Byzantine Generals paper (Lamport, Shostak and Pease, 1982) formalised the problem.
  • With unsigned messages, tolerating f Byzantine nodes needs at least 3f + 1 nodes: you can wait for only n − f replies, f of which may be lies, and two quorums must overlap in an honest node.
  • PBFT (Castro and Liskov, 1999) runs pre-prepare, prepare and commit phases with a primary and view changes. The all-to-all phases cost O(n²) messages per request.
  • Datacenter systems almost always assume crash faults: they control their machines, and BFT costs replicas, messages and complexity.
  • Nakamoto consensus achieves agreement in an open network using proof of work as Sybil resistance, with probabilistic finality that grows with confirmations.
  • Real systems see Byzantine-like failures from corruption, bugs and partial partitions. Defend with checksums, fail-stop behaviour, authentication and fault-injection testing.

How many replicas does a system need to tolerate 2 Byzantine nodes with unsigned messages?

  1. 5
  2. 7
  3. 6
  4. 4
n ≥ 3f + 1 = 7. Five would be the crash-fault answer (2f + 1) for f = 2. Intuitively you can wait for 5 replies, and 2 of those might be lies, leaving 3 honest ones to outvote 2.

In the three-generals scenario, why can lieutenant L1 not decide safely?

  1. Because messages might be lost
  2. Because it has no clock
  3. Because it does not know the number of generals
  4. Because its evidence is identical whether the commander or L2 is the traitor, and the right action differs
The traitor can arrange for L1 to see the same two conflicting messages in both worlds. Reliable messages and clocks do not help; the ambiguity is about who is lying. A fourth node supplies the extra independent witness.

Why does PBFT have both a prepare and a commit phase?

  1. Prepare fixes a request's order in a view; commit makes sure that order survives a change of primary
  2. Prepare is for reads and commit is for writes
  3. To reduce the number of messages
  4. Commit is only for the client's benefit
After prepare, a quorum agrees on the order within one view, but a new primary could still not know it. The commit phase makes enough replicas aware that a decision exists so the view change can find and keep it. It does not reduce messages; it costs another all-to-all round.

Why do datacenter systems like etcd or Kafka's controller not use BFT?

  1. BFT is mathematically impossible there
  2. They are too small for it
  3. One operator controls the machines and code, and BFT adds replicas, messages and complexity for a threat that is rarely the main risk
  4. BFT cannot handle crashes
The trust boundary matters. If the replicas are all yours, a hostile replica usually means a wider compromise, and the extra machinery adds bug surface. BFT does handle crashes (it is a superset of the crash model), and it is possible, just usually not worth it.

Which statement about proof-of-work blockchains is accurate?

  1. A block is final the instant it is mined
  2. Finality is probabilistic: reversal gets less likely as more blocks are added, assuming no attacker has most of the computing power
  3. They need a fixed, known list of participants
  4. They solve the Byzantine Generals problem with 2f + 1 nodes
Forks happen and are resolved in favour of the heaviest chain, so confidence grows with confirmations. Proof of work replaces a membership list by making influence cost computation (Sybil resistance). The honest-majority assumption is about computing power, not node count.
