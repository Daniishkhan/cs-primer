Multi-Region Systems

Putting copies of your data on several continents sounds like a deployment detail. It is really a collision between your consistency model and the speed of light, and every design in this chapter is a different way of paying that bill: in latency, in staleness, in conflicts to resolve, or in the risk of losing recent writes. You will leave knowing roughly what each option costs in milliseconds, which one to reach for, and how to fail over a region without making things worse.

Why this matters

Sooner or later a product has users on two continents, or a regulator who wants data to stay in one jurisdiction, or a customer who asks what happens if an entire cloud region goes down. "Just add a second region" hides several different architectures with different guarantees, and the wrong choice is expensive to undo. This chapter is the vocabulary for those conversations, and it ends with the practical question that separates paper designs from working ones: how do you actually move traffic, and how much data do you lose when you do?

The speed-of-light budget

Light in optical fibre travels at about 200,000 km/s, roughly two thirds of its speed in a vacuum (networking chapter 13 has the broader latency picture). That is 1 ms per 200 km one way, or 1 ms of round trip per 100 km of cable. No engineering will fix this; it is physics. Real round trips are slower than the floor, because cables do not run in straight lines, because of routers and amplifiers along the way, and because paths are chosen by business arrangements as much as geometry.

FIBER_KM_PER_S = 200_000            # light in glass travels at roughly two thirds of c

The distances are rough great-circle figures and the "typical" column is the order of magnitude you will see between public-cloud regions; real numbers vary by provider, route and day, so treat them as roughly right and measure your own (there is an experiment at the end of this chapter). The pattern is robust: same-continent pairs cost tens of milliseconds, across an ocean 70 to 150 ms, and the far side of the world 150 to 300 ms. Compare that with a round trip between availability zones in one region, which is typically a millisecond or two, and within a rack, well under a millisecond.

Now bring in what you know about consensus. A write that must be durable on a majority of replicas (Raft, Paxos, Spanner; chapter 8) cannot complete faster than the round trip from the coordinator to the nearest majority. A request that touches the database five times in sequence, with each touch a cross-region round trip of 75 ms, takes 375 ms before anything else happens. That is the budget logic: count the cross-region round trips on the critical path, and multiply. The art of multi-region design is minimising how many there are, and choosing which operations pay.

The options

The same four questions decide every design. Where does a write go first? How many regions must acknowledge before it is "done"? Can a stale read be tolerated? And if the write's region dies, what is lost? Four common architectures answer them differently. Try them in the explorer at the end of this section after you have read the descriptions.

Option 1: single leader in one region

The simplest design: one region holds the leader (the primary database, or the Raft leader), and other regions hold replicas. Reads are local if you accept replicas that lag (the staleness problem of data chapter 15); writes are remote, paying the round trip to the leader's region, plus that leader's own replication cost if it commits synchronously.

There are two sub-variants with very different safety. With asynchronous cross-region replicas, a write commits in the leader's region and is shipped later: fast (single-region latency plus the client's hop), but if the leader's region is lost, the most recent writes, whatever had not yet been shipped, are lost. That is a non-zero RPO (recovery point objective: how much data you can afford to lose, expressed as time). With synchronous replication to another region, a loss of the leader region loses nothing, but every write pays a cross-region round trip. Most single-leader deployments choose asynchronous replication and spend their effort making the loss window small and well understood.

Option 2: a leader per partition, with a home region

Instead of one leader for everything, partition the data (networking chapter 19, data chapter 16) and put each partition's leader in the region where it is used most. A European user's account lives in Ireland; an American user's in Virginia. Writes by a user to their own data are then local (or nearly), reads too, and only cross-partition operations or users who travel pay the long round trip. Variants include "follow the sun", where a partition's leader migrates through the day to be near the currently active users, and pinning by customer or tenant. The cost: you now need a directory of "which region owns this key", a way to move ownership safely (a leader hand-off is a small consensus problem), and a plan for the user who lives in Ireland but works in Singapore.

This is the shape of many large systems. Even when the user-visible API is one database, underneath it is often thousands of partition groups, each with its own leader placed to suit its traffic.

Option 3: a quorum spread across three regions

Run a consensus group whose replicas are in three different regions. Because a majority of three is two, losing any one entire region loses nothing and the group stays available, with automatic failover and no manual decision. This is the design behind globally replicated databases in the Spanner family (chapter 14). The price is that every write waits for the second-nearest region, as the diagram above showed: 65 to 75 ms if two of the regions are on one continent, and over 100 ms if they are spread across oceans. Reads can still be fast: the leader can serve linearizable reads locally using a lease (chapter 10), or any replica can serve a read at a slightly stale timestamp that it knows is safe. Placement matters enormously. The leader should be near the writers (so the client round trip is short) and the other two replicas should be as close to the leader as the failure-independence you want allows.

Two replicas are not enough: with majority commit, two regions tolerate no failure at all (a majority of 2 is 2). Four is wasteful: it needs three for a majority, so it survives the same single failure as three, at a higher cost in latency and money. Three regions is the sweet spot for failure tolerance, and five is used when you also want to survive a region failure during maintenance of another.

Option 4: active-active with conflict resolution

What if every region accepts writes locally, with no leader, and the regions exchange updates in the background? Latency is excellent: a write commits in the local region. Availability is excellent too: a region keeps working even if cut off from all the others. The price is that two regions can modify the same item concurrently, so the data is now eventually consistent, and somebody must define what a conflict resolves to. The options, from simplest and most dangerous to most work:

  • Last-writer-wins (LWW). Each write carries a timestamp, and the highest timestamp survives. Simple, always converges, and silently discards the other concurrent write. It also depends on clocks (chapter 7): a region with a fast clock wins every conflict, and a slow-clock region's genuine updates can disappear. Fine for data where "the latest wins" really is the semantics (a last-seen timestamp), dangerous for anything like a counter or a shopping cart.
  • CRDTs. Choose data types that merge deterministically and commutatively, so concurrent updates are combined rather than one dropped: counters that add, sets that union with removals tracked, registers, collaborative text (chapter 15). No coordination, no lost updates, but you must model your data with these types, and some invariants (a balance that must never go negative) cannot be maintained this way (chapter 16).
  • Application-level merges. Keep all the conflicting versions (Dynamo famously returned multiple "sibling" versions of a shopping cart) and let the application merge them with domain knowledge, perhaps by asking the user. Maximum control, maximum burden.

Common misconception

"Active-active" is often used to mean "both regions serve traffic", which a single-leader system with local reads already does. The hard part is both regions accepting writes to the same data. If your design says active-active but quietly routes each user's writes to a home region, you have built Option 2, which is much easier to reason about. That is not a criticism; it is usually the better design.

Play with it for a few minutes. Some things to find: put replicas in three regions on one continent and see how far the others are from a write; compare "one leader" with "home region" for a client far from the leader; spread five replicas across the world and watch the write cost grow; and notice that asynchronous replication gives the best write numbers precisely because it does not wait for anyone, and what that costs you on a region failure.

Analogy: the committee that only communicates by unreliable post

Take the committee from earlier chapters, now with members in different countries and letters taking days. To pass a motion with a majority, the chair must wait for replies, so the speed of the vote is the speed of the second-fastest post. A chair who may decide alone and mail the minutes later is fast, but if the chair vanishes, the unmailed decisions vanish too. Each member keeping their own copy of the minutes and merging whenever letters meet is also fast, but two members may have recorded different motions for the same agenda item.

Where it breaks down: a human committee can phone for clarification. In a distributed database the only phone is the very network that is slow.

Data residency

Sometimes the number of regions and their location is not yours to choose. Regulations in many jurisdictions (the European Union's GDPR is the best-known example) restrict where personal data about their residents may be stored or transferred, and some customers contractually require that their data stay in a country or a continent. The exact legal requirements vary and change, so this is a question for your lawyers, but the architectural consequences are common to all of them, and they are the same ones as Option 2:

  • The partition key must include the residency. Data is homed in its allowed region, and replicas of it must only go to allowed regions. A global "replicate everything to every region" design is simply off the table for regulated data.
  • Cross-region operations become restricted. A global query that joins European and American data may be illegal, or must be run as separate regional queries whose non-personal results are combined.
  • Leakage paths are everywhere. Backups, logs, analytics exports, caches, search indexes and support tools all carry copies. Designs that are careful about the primary database often fail on these.
  • Resilience shrinks. If data must stay inside one country, a region failure there cannot be recovered by failing over to another country. The recovery plan is within the permitted area (another region in the same jurisdiction, if one exists).

Failing over a region

An architecture is only as good as its failover. Failing over has four separate parts, and each is a place where real outages have happened.

1. Deciding the region is down. A region rarely goes down cleanly; it degrades, with a high error rate, partial connectivity, slow storage. A health check from one vantage point can say "down" when the region is fine and the check path is broken. A decision to fail over is a decision to lose or to risk some data (with async replication) and to take on a large traffic move, so most organisations keep a human in the loop for region-level failover and automate the mechanical steps. Automating the decision is possible, but tune it to avoid flapping, failing over and back repeatedly.

2. Steering traffic. Users must be sent to the surviving region. The tools, from coarse to fine: DNS steering (change the answer for your hostname, case study: steering traffic with DNS) is simple but slow to take full effect, because resolvers and clients cache answers for the TTL and some ignore it; a low TTL speeds up the next failover but increases DNS load all the time. Anycast (case study: anycast) announces the same IP address from several places and lets the routing system send each client to the nearest working one; withdrawing the announcement from a failed place redirects traffic in the time routing takes to converge, often much faster than DNS. Both are steering wheels for whole regions, not for individual requests, so you will want them to work with a global load balancer in front.

3. Promoting data. With a quorum across regions this is automatic (Option 3). With single-leader replication, a replica in another region must be promoted to leader. That means choosing the most up-to-date replica, making sure the old leader cannot accept writes if it comes back (fencing, chapter 4), and accepting the RPO that the replication lag implied at the moment of failure. The companion measure is the RTO, the recovery time objective: how long the service may be down. Together they are a contract: "we can lose up to 5 seconds of writes and be back within 10 minutes" describes an RPO of 5 seconds and an RTO of 10 minutes, and each tighter number costs more (synchronous replication buys RPO zero at the price of write latency; hot standbys buy a short RTO at the price of idle capacity).

4. Coming back. Failback is not the reverse of failover. The old region has writes the new leader never saw, or lacks writes the new one accepted. Reconciling them is exactly the split-brain problem.

Distributed systems connection: GitHub, 2018

A brief network partition between GitHub's data centres triggered an automatic failover of MySQL primaries to the other coast. The partition healed within seconds, but by then both sides had accepted writes, and cleanly reconciling them took many hours. The lesson is not "automatic failover is bad"; it is that failover has a data cost you must decide about in advance, and that "fail over and fail back" is a two-sided operation. The full story is in case study: 43 seconds that took a day, which this chapter deliberately does not repeat.

One more property of failover paths: they run rarely, so they rot. Credentials expire, capacity in the standby region was never raised, a dependency exists only in the primary region. The remedy is the discipline of the next chapter: exercise the failover regularly, in production-like conditions, until it is routine.

Cells and the blast radius

Everything above defends against a region failing. Most outages, though, are not hardware: they are a bad deploy, a poison input, a configuration change, an overloaded dependency. Those do not respect region boundaries, because the same software and the same configuration run in every region. A different structure limits the damage of such failures: cell-based architecture.

The idea is to split the system into several independent, identical cells, each a complete copy of the stack (service tier, database, queue, cache) serving a fixed subset of customers. A thin cell router maps each customer to a cell. If a deploy goes wrong or one customer's traffic triggers a bug, the damage stays in that cell: the blast radius is one cell's customers, say one in twenty, rather than everyone. Deploys roll out cell by cell, so a bad release is caught after hitting one cell. Cells also bound capacity problems: a cell has a known maximum size that you have tested, instead of one giant system whose scaling limits you meet in production. Regions and cells are complementary: you can place cells within regions, and fail a cell's traffic over to a partner cell in another region.

Cells do not come free. The router is a shared dependency whose own failure is global, so it must be simple and conservative. Anything shared between cells (a global control plane, one configuration service, a single deployment pipeline) is a hidden coupling that can turn a one-cell problem into an everything problem, and some cross-customer features (global search, cross-customer sharing) do not fit. The classic cautionary tale is the 2017 Amazon S3 outage, in which one mistyped command removed more servers than intended, and some of the subsystems involved had grown so large that restarting them took far longer than anyone had tested: see case study: small mistake, big blast radius. Partitioning a system into smaller, independently restartable units is the structural answer it prompted.

On a cloud VM

This is the one experiment in the course worth a few cents. You will measure real inter-region latency and feel what a cross-region quorum costs. Set up your lab machine first for the general approach to VMs; here you need three tiny cloud VMs in three different regions (for example Virginia, Ireland and Singapore in AWS, or the equivalents in Google Cloud or Azure). The smallest instance type is enough. You must destroy the VMs at the end; the last step is not optional.

1. Measure the round trips. From each VM, ping the other two (note ICMP may need allowing in the VM's firewall rules; allow it only from the other VMs' addresses):

$ ping -c 20 <other-vm-public-ip>
20 packets transmitted, 20 received, 0% packet loss
rtt min/avg/max/mdev = 74.1/74.6/76.9/0.5 ms    # sample; yours will differ

Compare the averages with the table above. Which pair is closest? Do they look like 1.4 to 1.7 times the physical floor?

2. Run a three-node etcd cluster across them. Install etcd on each VM (the release tarball from the project's GitHub releases page; the version you pick is up to you). Then start one member per VM, replacing the three IPn values with your VMs' addresses and using n1, n2, n3 as the name on the matching machine:

$ ./etcd --name n1 --data-dir ./n1.data \
    --listen-peer-urls http://0.0.0.0:2380 --listen-client-urls http://0.0.0.0:2379 \
    --initial-advertise-peer-urls http://IP1:2380 --advertise-client-urls http://IP1:2379 \
    --initial-cluster n1=http://IP1:2380,n2=http://IP2:2380,n3=http://IP3:2380 \
    --initial-cluster-state new

Security: this uses plain HTTP with no authentication, so the VM firewalls must allow ports 2379 and 2380 only from your three VMs' addresses and your own, never from the whole internet. For anything beyond a short experiment you would turn on TLS.

3. Time a write against a read. Find the leader, then from each VM time a write, a linearizable read and a local (serializable) read against the VM's own member:

$ export ETCDCTL_API=3 EP=http://127.0.0.1:2379
$ ./etcdctl --endpoints=$EP endpoint status -w table      # which member is the leader?
$ time ./etcdctl --endpoints=$EP put greeting hello         # write: goes to the leader, needs a majority
$ time ./etcdctl --endpoints=$EP get greeting               # linearizable read (the default)
$ time ./etcdctl --endpoints=$EP get greeting --consistency=s  # serializable read: answered by the local member

Expect the write to cost roughly a round trip to the leader plus the leader's round trip to its nearest peer, as in the space-time diagram. The linearizable read should cost a round trip to the leader's region (etcd confirms with the leader that it is still current), while the serializable read stays local and fast but may be stale. The timings include a little process start-up overhead, so look at the difference between the three rather than the absolute numbers. Then try moving leadership with etcdctl move-leader and watch the numbers change for each VM.

4. Destroy everything. Terminate all three VMs, delete their disks and any reserved public addresses, and check the cloud console's billing page the next day to confirm nothing is still running. A forgotten VM is the most common way this experiment costs more than a coffee.

Key takeaways

  • Light in fibre gives roughly 1 ms of round trip per 100 km. Cross-continent round trips are tens of milliseconds, intercontinental 70-300 ms, and real values run about 1.4 to 1.7 times the physical floor. Count the cross-region round trips on the critical path.
  • A synchronous write costs the round trip to the coordinator plus its round trip to the nearest majority. Placement of leader and replicas dominates the numbers.
  • The four designs: single leader (cheap reads, remote writes, non-zero RPO if async), leader per partition with a home region (local for most users), a three-region quorum (no loss, automatic failover, every write pays), and active-active (local everything, but you own conflict resolution: LWW, CRDTs, or app merges).
  • Data residency forces homing data by jurisdiction, which pushes you toward Option 2 and shrinks your failover options. Backups and logs leak copies.
  • Failover has four parts: decide, steer (DNS is slow, anycast faster), promote data (RPO, RTO, fencing) and fail back. Rarely run failover paths rot, so exercise them.
  • Cells limit the blast radius of failures that regions cannot: bad deploys, poison inputs, overload. They need a tiny router and no hidden shared dependencies.

Check your understanding

A three-replica Raft group has its leader in Virginia and followers in Oregon (about 65 ms away) and Singapore (about 220 ms away). Roughly how long does the leader take to commit a write, ignoring the client's own hop?

  1. About 220 ms, because it must hear from every replica
  2. About 0 ms, because the leader writes locally
  3. About 65 ms, because it needs only one follower besides itself for a majority of three
  4. About 285 ms, the two round trips added together
A majority of 3 is 2: the leader plus the first follower to acknowledge. The leader waits for the nearer one, Oregon. The Singapore acknowledgement arrives later and is not needed for the commit.

A system runs one leader in us-east and asynchronous replicas elsewhere. us-east is lost with 3 seconds of replication lag. What is the consequence?

  1. Up to about 3 seconds of acknowledged writes may be lost: the RPO is non-zero
  2. Nothing is lost; asynchronous replicas have everything
  3. All data is lost
  4. The recovery time is zero
Asynchronous replication acknowledges before the data reaches the other region, so whatever was not yet shipped is lost when the leader's region dies. RTO (how long recovery takes) is a separate question from RPO (how much data is lost).

Why is last-writer-wins a risky conflict policy for an active-active shopping cart?

  1. It never converges
  2. It requires a leader in every region
  3. It is too slow for local writes
  4. It keeps only one of two concurrent updates, so items added in the other region simply disappear, and clock skew decides which one wins
LWW always converges, which is its attraction, but it does so by discarding one of the concurrent writes. Two regions each adding an item leads to one item surviving. A merge-friendly structure (a CRDT set, or application-level sibling merging) keeps both.

Why do cells protect against a failure that multiple regions do not?

  1. Cells are placed on faster hardware
  2. A bad deploy or poison input affects every region that runs the same software, but affects only the one cell it reaches when customers are partitioned into independent cells and deploys roll out cell by cell
  3. Cells replicate data synchronously across the world
  4. Regions cannot be failed over, but cells can
Regions defend against infrastructure loss in one place. A software or configuration fault ships to all regions at once. Dividing customers into independent cells and deploying to one cell at a time bounds the number of customers any single fault can touch.

You lower your DNS record's TTL to 30 seconds so failover will be quick. After you change the record during an outage, some users still reach the old region for several minutes. Why?

  1. DNS never changes once cached
  2. The TTL is applied in minutes rather than seconds
  3. Some resolvers and clients cache longer than the TTL says, and existing connections stay open to the old address until they close
  4. Anycast overrides DNS
DNS steering is coarse: intermediate resolvers, operating systems, browsers and long-lived connections do not all honour a short TTL promptly. Anycast withdrawal reacts at routing speed, but also cannot move established TCP connections cleanly.
