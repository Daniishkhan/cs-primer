Where Next: On to Reliability

You started this course knowing that networks fail and replicas disagree. You now have precise models of time and failure, the exact meaning of the consistency models, Paxos and Raft down to the commitment rule, distributed transactions, CRDTs, logs and exactly-once, multi-region trade-offs, and three ways to test all of it. This last chapter takes stock, gives you a reading list of the classic papers in a sensible order, and points to what comes after design: running these systems for real.

Why this matters

The distributed-systems literature is unusual: a small number of short papers carry most of the ideas, and they are readable once you have the vocabulary you now have. Reading them in a good order, with a question in mind, turns you from someone who knows the folklore into someone who can check a claim at its source, which is how senior engineers argue about designs.

What you can now reason about

It is worth saying plainly what you can do now that you could not at the start of the course. Given a system design, you can ask and answer:

  • What does it assume? Synchronous, asynchronous or partially synchronous; crash or Byzantine faults; whether clocks are trusted (chapter 1, chapter 7). And what is impossible regardless: FLP, CAP and the latency price PACELC adds (chapter 2).
  • How does it notice failure? Heartbeats, phi-accrual, gossip, and what a false positive costs (chapter 3). Whether a lock or lease is safe when its holder pauses, and where the fencing token goes (chapter 4).
  • What exactly does it promise to readers? Linearizable, sequential, causal, session guarantees or eventual; how to tell from a history (chapters 5 and 6).
  • How does it agree? Quorum intersection, total-order broadcast, Paxos and Raft with their subtle cases, coordination services, and what changes when nodes lie (chapters 8 to 12).
  • What happens across machines? Where two-phase commit blocks, how Percolator, Spanner and Calvin each avoid or pay for coordination, and when a CRDT or an invariant-confluence argument removes the need for coordination altogether (chapters 13 to 16).
  • Where do duplicates and losses enter a pipeline? And what "exactly-once" really covers (chapter 17). What a design costs in milliseconds when it spans continents (chapter 18).
  • How would you gain confidence that it is right? Property tests, fault injection, Jepsen-style history checks, deterministic simulation, and a TLA+ specification (chapters 19 and 20).

That is a working toolkit for system-design interviews, for design reviews, and for reading a database's documentation sceptically. It is not mastery: mastery comes from building and operating things. The labs and studios of this course are the next step if you have not done them.

A reading list, in order

Read these with a pencil. The standard trick for a systems paper: first the abstract and conclusion; then the figures; then the body, looking for the one assumption the whole paper rests on. Most are short, and several have been explained in this course so you will recognise their diagrams.

Foundations

  1. Lamport, L. (1978). "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System." Communications of the ACM 21(7), 558–565. Read for: happened-before, logical clocks, and the state-machine idea in its last pages. Short and astonishingly modern. (Our chapters: networking 17, 7, 8.)
  2. Fischer, M. J., Lynch, N. A., & Paterson, M. S. (1985). "Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process." Journal of the ACM 32(2), 374–382. Read for: the precise statement (deterministic, asynchronous, one crash), and the "bivalent configuration" argument. It is hard; read the introduction and the discussion carefully and skim the proof. (Chapter 2.)

Agreement and coordination

  1. Lamport, L. (2001). "Paxos Made Simple." ACM SIGACT News 32(4), 51–58. Read for: the two phases and the one invariant (P2c in the paper) that drives them. Do it after chapter 9. The longer original, "The Part-Time Parliament" (ACM TOCS, 1998), is worth reading for fun and the committee metaphor.
  2. Ongaro, D., & Ousterhout, J. (2014). "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm." USENIX Annual Technical Conference (ATC '14). Read for: the figure that summarises the whole algorithm (Figure 2), and Section 5.4 on safety. Then Ongaro's 2014 PhD thesis for membership changes, snapshots and the rest. (Chapter 10.)
  3. Burrows, M. (2006). "The Chubby Lock Service for Loosely-Coupled Distributed Systems." OSDI '06. Read for: why a lock service rather than a consensus library, coarse-grained locks, sequencers, and honest lessons from operating it. (Case 1.)
  4. Hunt, P., Konar, M., Junqueira, F. P., & Reed, B. (2010). "ZooKeeper: Wait-free Coordination for Internet-scale Systems." USENIX ATC '10. Read for: the choice to expose a small, wait-free API with ordering guarantees instead of locks. (Chapter 11, Case 2.)

Data systems

  1. DeCandia, G., et al. (2007). "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store." SOSP '07. Read for: sloppy quorums, hinted handoff, vector clocks and application-level merging: a design that chose availability on purpose. It is the origin of much of the "eventually consistent" toolbox.
  2. Corbett, J. C., et al. (2012). "Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database." OSDI '12. Read for: TrueTime and commit wait, and how external consistency is obtained by waiting out clock uncertainty. (Chapter 7, chapter 14.)
  3. Peng, D., & Dabek, F. (2010). "Large-scale Incremental Processing Using Distributed Transactions and Notifications." OSDI '10. Read for: snapshot-isolation transactions built entirely in a client library on top of a plain key-value store, with lock cleanup by later transactions. (Case 5.)
  4. Thomson, A., Diamond, T., Weng, S.-C., Ren, K., Shao, P., & Abadi, D. J. (2012). "Calvin: Fast Distributed Transactions for Partitioned Database Systems." SIGMOD '12. Read for: the contrarian idea of agreeing on transaction order first, then executing deterministically without a commit protocol.

Avoiding coordination

  1. Shapiro, M., Preguiça, N., Baquero, C., & Zawirski, M. (2011). "Conflict-free Replicated Data Types." Stabilization, Safety, and Security of Distributed Systems (SSS 2011), LNCS 6976. A companion technical report, "A comprehensive study of Convergent and Commutative Replicated Data Types", has the full catalogue. Read for: why the merge function being a join makes convergence automatic. (Chapter 15.)
  2. Bailis, P., Fekete, A., Franklin, M. J., Ghodsi, A., Hellerstein, J. M., & Stoica, I. (2015). "Coordination Avoidance in Database Systems." Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment 8(3). Read for: invariant confluence: a test for which application invariants need coordination and which do not. (Chapter 16.)

Practice, books and lectures

  1. Kingsbury, K. Jepsen analyses. Published at jepsen.io/analyses. Read for: how an independent tester reads a database's documentation, turns its claims into tests, and reports exactly what held and what did not. Start with one for a system you use, and read the "what we tested" and "findings" sections. (Chapter 19, Case 4.)
  2. Kleppmann, M. (2017). Designing Data-Intensive Applications. O'Reilly Media. Read for: the single best book-length synthesis of this material (and the data course): storage engines, replication, partitioning, transactions, consistency and consensus, stream processing. It cites most of the papers above, so it doubles as a map.
  3. MIT 6.824 / 6.5840 Distributed Systems (MIT's graduate distributed-systems course; the course number changed from 6.824 to 6.5840). Free lecture videos, readings, and lab assignments are online. Use it for: a lecture series that goes through many of the papers above, in the way this list suggests, with labs in which you implement Raft and a fault-tolerant key-value service yourself. Reading the paper before the lecture is the intended way to use it.

If you only read three, read Lamport 1978 for how to think, the Raft paper for what a clear protocol looks like, and one Jepsen analysis for how real systems fail.

Try it yourself: a four-week plan

Week 1: Lamport 1978 and the FLP introduction, then re-do the clock simulations of chapter 7. Week 2: Paxos Made Simple and the Raft paper, then implement a toy of one (Lab 4). Week 3: Spanner and one of Percolator or Calvin, with the corresponding chapter 14 open beside you. Week 4: a Jepsen analysis of a database you actually use, and the first three Gossip Glomers challenges. After each, write five sentences: the problem, the idea, the one assumption it depends on, what it costs, and what you would still worry about.

What comes next: running these systems

This course was about designing and understanding systems that survive failure. The next question is what it takes to run them: to know, at 3 a.m., whether the cluster is healthy and what "healthy" even means. That is the territory of Course 5, Reliability & Operations:

  • Service level objectives. Turning "reliable" into numbers you can promise, measure and budget for.
  • Observability. Metrics, logs and traces that let you ask new questions of a system that is misbehaving, including the failure detectors and consistency checks you have met here, run continuously instead of in tests.
  • Incidents. How teams respond, communicate and learn: the process side of the outages in this course's case studies.
  • Capacity, change and recovery. Deploying safely (the cells and rollout ideas of chapter 18), rehearsing failover, and practising the recovery you designed.

That course is still being written. Follow the path from the course index, which lists what exists and what is coming next.

Distributed systems connection

Notice that the thread running through the whole course is the same sentence in different words: you cannot tell a slow node from a dead one, so every safe protocol must work without knowing. Timeouts are guesses, leases are bounded guesses, quorums are guesses that must overlap, and a test is a way to find the guess that was wrong. Operating a system is the long-running version: deciding, with incomplete information, what to do next.

Key takeaways

  • You can now state a system's model and assumptions, name what is impossible, and say precisely what a consistency or delivery guarantee does and does not cover.
  • You can follow the core protocols (Paxos, Raft, two-phase commit, Percolator, Spanner, Calvin), the coordination-free alternatives (CRDTs, invariant confluence), and the log-based systems built on them.
  • You have four ways to check a design: property tests and fault injection, Jepsen-style history checking, deterministic simulation, and model checking.
  • Read the classics in order: Lamport 1978, FLP, Paxos Made Simple, Raft, Chubby, ZooKeeper, Dynamo, Spanner, Percolator, Calvin, CRDTs, Bailis et al. Add Jepsen analyses, DDIA and MIT 6.5840 alongside.
  • Next is operating these systems: SLOs, observability and incidents, in Course 5.

Check your understanding

Which paper is the original source of the happened-before relation and logical clocks?

  1. Fischer, Lynch and Paterson, 1985
  2. DeCandia et al. (Dynamo), 2007
  3. Lamport, "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System", 1978
  4. Ongaro and Ousterhout (Raft), 2014
Lamport's 1978 paper defines happened-before, introduces logical clocks and ends with the state-machine idea that later consensus work builds on. FLP is about impossibility of consensus; Dynamo and Raft are system and protocol papers.

Which pair of papers presents two different answers to "how do we run transactions across machines": agreeing on order first versus coordinating with clocks and commit protocols?

  1. Calvin (deterministic ordering first) and Spanner (TrueTime and two-phase commit over Paxos groups)
  2. Dynamo and Chubby
  3. ZooKeeper and Paxos Made Simple
  4. FLP and Lamport 1978
Calvin sequences transactions before executing them deterministically, avoiding a distributed commit protocol; Spanner runs two-phase commit across Paxos-replicated groups and uses TrueTime to order transactions externally.

What is the best way to use a Jepsen analysis in your own work?

  1. As a ranking of which database is best
  2. As a model of how to turn a system's documented claims into tests, and a record of which claims held under which faults
  3. As proof that the system is correct if the report is clean
  4. As a replacement for reading the system's documentation
An analysis is a snapshot of one version under one set of tests. It teaches the method and records findings; a clean run is evidence of no violation seen, not a proof.
