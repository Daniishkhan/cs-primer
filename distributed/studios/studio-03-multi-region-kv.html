Design a Multi-Region Key-Value Store

Put replicas on three continents and the speed of light becomes your biggest design constraint: a round trip between two regions costs more than a thousand in-region ones. Every choice about consistency, leaders and failover is a choice about which users pay those milliseconds, and which failures you are willing to survive. This studio designs a store where that choice is explicit.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing a globally distributed key-value store that a company's product teams use as their primary database, in the family of Spanner, CockroachDB, DynamoDB global tables and Cassandra.

  • Deployed in four regions: US East, US West, EU West and Asia South. Users are spread across all of them.
  • About 500,000 reads and 50,000 writes per second globally; 100 TB of data; values around 1 KB.
  • Different tables need different consistency: money-like data must be linearizable; a product catalogue can be slightly stale; a shopping cart must stay writable from anywhere.
  • Losing a whole region must be survivable, with targets stated as RPO and RTO per table.
  • Some customers' data must stay inside the EU.

Prerequisites: consistency models (chapter 5), hybrid logical clocks (chapter 7), the speed-of-light budget and failover (chapter 18).

1Requirements: consistency is a per-table decision

Sort the data this store will hold into classes by what a wrong answer costs. For each class, choose a consistency model, and state RPO and RTO in numbers. Which promise do you refuse to make? What is out of scope?

Compare with a worked answer

A single global consistency setting would be either too slow for everything or too weak for money. So the first design decision is to make consistency a property of the table and to say which classes exist:

  • What we refuse to promise: low latency and linearizability for the same table across continents. That is the PACELC trade-off (chapter 2): even with no failures, strong consistency costs round trips.
  • Always, for every class: no corruption, no silently dropped acknowledged write in the strong class, and a clear, tested answer to "what happens when a region disappears?".
  • Residency: rows tagged as EU data are stored, replicated and failed over only inside EU locations (stage 6e).
  • Out of scope: SQL and secondary indexes, multi-key transactions across partitions (the atomic-commit machinery of chapter 13 and 14; say it is an extension), analytics, and the storage engine inside one node.

2Estimates: the speed-of-light budget

Take assumed round-trip times between regions. How long does a strongly consistent write take for a client near the leader and far from it? What does the choice of leader region cost? How much cross-region data do you move per day? How many consensus groups do you run?

Compare with a worked answer

Assumed round-trip times (illustrative; real values depend on routes and providers, so measure yours): us-east to us-west 65 ms, us-east to eu-west 75 ms, us-west to eu-west 140 ms, and Asia South is farther still (very roughly 120–250 ms to the others). Same region, between zones: about 1 ms. Light in fibre covers roughly 200 km per millisecond one way, so no engineering removes these (chapter 18).

# Assumed round-trip times in ms (illustrative, not measured). Same-region replicas: 1 ms.
rtt = {("us-east","us-west"):65, ("us-east","eu-west"):75, ("us-west","eu-west"):140}
def d(a, b):
    return 1 if a == b else rtt.get((a, b)) or rtt[(b, a)]

def commit_ms(leader, replicas):
    """Leader needs acks from a majority; it counts itself, so it waits for the
    (majority-1)-th nearest other replica."""
    need = len(replicas) // 2 + 1
    others = sorted(d(leader, r) for r in replicas)[1:]      # drop the leader itself (distance to itself sorts first)
    return others[need - 2]

layouts = {
  "3 replicas, one per region":      ["us-east", "us-west", "eu-west"],
  "5 replicas, 2 + 2 + 1":           ["us-east", "us-east", "us-west", "us-west", "eu-west"],
}
for name, reps in layouts.items():
    print(name)
    for leader in sorted(set(reps)):
        print(f"   leader in {leader:8}: commit waits {commit_ms(leader, reps):3} ms")
    # survive the loss of any whole region?
    need = len(reps) // 2 + 1
    ok = all(len(reps) - reps.count(r) >= need for r in set(reps))
    print(f"   majority = {need}; survives losing any one region: {ok}")
print()

    print(f"client in {client}, leader us-east, 3 replicas: strong write = {d(client,'us-east') if client!='us-east' else 0} + {commit_ms('us-east', layouts['3 replicas, one per region'])} ms")

  • A strong write costs one round trip to the leader plus one round trip from the leader to the nearest majority. With the leader in us-east and three replicas, a us-east client pays 65 ms, a us-west client 130 ms and an eu-west client 140 ms. The commit waits only for the nearest other replica (us-west, 65 ms), not the farthest: majority quorums ignore the slowest replica.
  • Reads: a strong read must be confirmed by the leader (a lease makes this local to the leader, chapter 10), so a far client pays the trip to the leader: 65–75 ms. A bounded-stale read from a nearby replica is ~1–5 ms. That asymmetry is why most of the 500,000 reads a second should be bounded-stale or follower reads when the table allows it.
  • Volume: 50,000 writes/s × 1 KB = 50 MB/s. Each write is sent to two remote regions: ≈ 100 MB/s cross-region ≈ 8.6 TB a day of inter-region traffic, which is a significant cost line as well as a capacity one.
  • Consensus groups: 100 TB in partitions of about 10 GB is on the order of 10,000 partitions, each its own replicated group. Heartbeats and elections per group would swamp the network, so real systems coalesce messages between each pair of nodes. Think of this as "multi-Raft".
  • Storage: 100 TB × 3 replicas = 300 TB, or × 5 = 500 TB for the strong tables if you choose five replicas (stage 6b). Decide per table.

What drives the design: where the leader of each partition lives, since it sets every writer's latency, and how many replicas are in how many regions, since that sets what failures are survivable.

3API: consistency is visible in every call

Design the client API. How does a table declare its class? How does a read ask for a freshness level? How does a client get read-your-writes when its next read lands on a different replica or region?

Compare with a worked answer 
CREATE TABLE accounts  (consistency = STRONG,        home = 'us-east',  replicas = 3 across regions)
CREATE TABLE catalogue (consistency = STRONG_WRITE,  stale_reads_ok = 10s)
CREATE TABLE carts     (consistency = ACTIVE_ACTIVE, merge = 'cart-crdt')
CREATE TABLE eu_users  (consistency = STRONG,        placement = EU_ONLY)

put(table, key, value, if_version=V | if_absent, idempotency_key=K)  -> {version, token}
get(table, key, read=STRONG | BOUNDED(10s) | LOCAL, after=token)    -> {value, version, token}

# token is an opaque causal timestamp (an HLC value, chapter 7): "I have seen up to here"
  • Per-request override, never silently weaker. A caller may ask for a weaker read than the table default (BOUNDED, LOCAL) to save latency; it may not get stronger consistency than the table can provide, and every response states which level it actually got.
  • Session guarantees through a token (chapter 6). A write returns a causal token (an HLC timestamp). A later read passes after=token; the replica serving it either already has data at least that new, or waits briefly, or forwards to a fresher replica. This gives read-your-writes and monotonic reads across regions without paying for linearizability.
  • Conditional writes (if_version, if_absent) are the building block of safe updates for strong tables. In active-active tables a conditional write is only checked locally, which is a weaker thing; the API must say so.
  • Idempotency keys make client retries after a timeout harmless: a timeout does not say whether the write happened (chapter 1).
  • Errors tell the truth: NOT_LEADER (with a hint), UNAVAILABLE_NO_QUORUM (strong class, minority side), STALE_BEYOND_BOUND, and COMMIT_UNKNOWN for a timeout after the proposal was sent.

5Architecture: where the leaders live

Draw three regions and two partitions with different home regions. For a client far from the leader, trace one write. Which replica's acknowledgement does the commit wait for, and why does the farthest one not matter?

Compare with a worked answer

Every partition is a replicated state machine (chapter 8) run by a consensus protocol such as Raft or Paxos (chapter 10). What makes it multi-region is only the placement of the replicas and the leader.

A write goes: client → local API server → (redirect if needed) → the partition's leader → majority → reply.

  • The leader commits when a majority has the entry. With three replicas that is the leader plus one follower, and the nearest follower wins the race. The far follower is up to date a moment later and does not slow the commit.
  • For the client, the cost is the extra leg to the leader. That is the argument for homing a partition near its writers, and for letting reads go to local replicas where the class allows.
  • Strong reads go to the leader (or to a follower that has confirmed it is current). Bounded-stale reads go to any replica whose applied timestamp is within the bound; the replica knows its own "safe time" from the leader's heartbeats.
  • Active-active tables do not follow this path at all: a write is accepted at the nearest replica group and replicated asynchronously to the others (stage 6c).

The runnable calculation in stage 2 shows the 3-replica and 5-replica layouts commit in the same time for a leader in us-east (65 ms) because the 5-replica layout's extra acknowledgement comes from a same-region peer at about 1 ms. What 5 replicas buys is not speed but resilience: any two simultaneous node failures, and one region failure, are survivable, at the price of five copies of the data and more replication traffic. The leader's location sets everyone's latency; place it near the majority of writers (follow the workload), keep it away from places with unreliable power or networks, and move it deliberately.

The best design usually avoids the question: most keys have an obvious home (a user's own settings), so give those a home region and use the strong or single-writer path; keep true multi-writer conflict resolution for the small set of tables (carts, counters) where it is natural, and use CRDT types there. Whatever you choose, state the rule in the table definition and test convergence after a partition heals.

(d) Region failover: RPO, RTO and the actual steps

  • Strong tables. No operator needed: followers in surviving regions notice the leader is gone (an election timeout, plus waiting out the old leader's read lease so a deposed leader cannot serve stale reads), elect a new leader, and continue. RPO is 0 because a committed entry exists on a majority, which includes at least one survivor. RTO is the detection and election time plus lease expiry: seconds to tens of seconds. Partitions whose majority was lost stay unavailable (the placement table above says which).
  • Async replicated tables. RPO is the replication lag at the moment of failure; promoting a replica loses the tail of writes. Record the lag, and when the old region returns, reconcile the lost tail rather than discarding it silently.
  • Active-active tables. Nothing to fail over: clients redirect to another region. The cost is entirely in what the failed region had accepted but not yet shipped (RPO = lag), and in the merge when it returns.
  • Client side. Traffic steering (DNS or anycast, health-checked) moves clients; clients must retry with backoff and jitter so the survivors are not stampeded (architecture chapter 17). Survivors need capacity headroom for the failed region's load, which is a cost decision (N+1 at region level).
  • Don't fail over on a flap. A flaky link can look like a region outage. Use hysteresis and prefer automatic failover only for mechanisms (like leader election) that are safe to repeat.
  • Failback is a migration, not a switch: bring the region back as followers, catch up, then transfer leaders gradually. Rehearse it.

(e) Data residency

  • Placement is a constraint, enforced by the system. A table or a row range tagged EU_ONLY may only have replicas in EU locations; the catalog rejects any change that would break it, and so does the rebalancer.
  • It must also survive failover. If EU data may only live in the EU, a quorum has to exist inside the EU, so you need at least three failure domains there (three zones for zone failures, three EU regions or two plus a witness for region failures). Residency trades directly against availability and cost; say so.
  • Everything derived counts: backups, logs, metrics with identifiers, caches, search indexes, CDC streams (data chapter 19), analytics exports and debugging dumps. A global secondary index that stores EU values elsewhere breaks the promise, so indexes follow the same placement.
  • Cross-border access is a routing question: a non-EU service reading EU data goes to the EU region (higher latency) or sees only what the policy allows. Whether a given legal rule is satisfied is a legal question; the system's job is to make the technical guarantee checkable and auditable.

(f) Testing it

  • Model the protocol. Specify replication and leadership in TLA+ and model-check small configurations (chapter 20, TLA+ card).
  • Deterministic simulation of the whole cluster (the approach of chapter 19): simulated network with region-sized delays, partitions, node crashes, clock skew, disk stalls; millions of seeded runs, each reproducible.
  • Black-box fault injection on a real cluster, Jepsen-style (Jepsen): partition regions (including asymmetric and partial partitions where A reaches C and B reaches C but A cannot reach B), kill leaders, skew clocks, and check histories with a linearizability checker for strong tables (Porcupine) and with convergence and invariant checks for active-active ones.
  • Game days. Drain a whole region in production-like conditions on a schedule, and measure the actual RTO against the target. A failover never rehearsed is a hypothesis.
  • Measure what users feel: p99 per operation type per region pair, not averages; staleness distribution of bounded reads; replication lag percentiles.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"I'd make consistency a property of the table, with three classes: strong for money-like data, strong writes with bounded-stale reads for catalogues, and active-active with a named conflict rule for carts and counters. Each gets a stated RPO and RTO. Every partition is a Raft or Paxos group with replicas spread across regions; the leader lives in the home region of most of its writers, which I put in the key. A strong write costs the client's trip to the leader plus the leader's trip to its nearest majority, about 65 to 140 ms with the assumed inter-region numbers, and the farthest replica is not on the critical path. So most of the 500,000 reads a second should be bounded-stale local reads, with causal tokens for read-your-writes. Placement is quorum arithmetic: three regions, or five replicas as 2+2+1, survive a region loss, while two regions alone cannot, so I'd add a witness. Region failure for strong data is a leader election with RPO zero and an RTO of seconds, and active-active tables just redirect clients, with RPO equal to the replication lag. For conflicts I'd prefer to avoid them with a home region per key and use CRDTs only where natural; last-writer-wins needs bounded hybrid clocks. Residency is an enforced placement constraint that also covers backups, indexes and the failover quorum. And I'd test with TLA+, deterministic simulation, partial partitions and scheduled region drains."

Notice the shape: classify the data → price each class in milliseconds → place replicas and leaders by quorum arithmetic → avoid or resolve conflicts → rehearse the failure → make residency and testing part of the design.

Key takeaways

  • Consistency is a per-table choice, and the contract must say what each class gives up.
  • Light speed sets the budget: strong write = leader leg + nearest-majority leg; the far replica does not matter.
  • Place leaders near writers, put the home region in the key, and move leaders deliberately.
  • Quorum arithmetic decides survivable failures: two regions cannot, three or 2+2+1 can, a witness fixes the trap.
  • Avoid conflicts with a home region per key; use CRDTs or siblings where multiple writers are natural; LWW loses writes.
  • Strong-table failover has RPO 0 and an RTO of an election; async copies have RPO equal to the lag.
  • Residency is a placement constraint that covers every derived copy and the failover quorum.
  • Test with model checking, simulation, partial partitions and rehearsed region drains.
