Design a Lock & Leader-Election Service

Every large system needs one piece of agreement: who is the leader of this job, who holds this lock. A coordination service gives it that, and it is also the place where one wrong assumption about time turns into corrupted data. This studio designs the service and, more importantly, the API that keeps its clients honest.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing an internal lock and leader-election service in the style of Chubby, etcd or ZooKeeper, which hundreds of other services use to coordinate.

  • Clients get sessions kept alive by heartbeats, take locks and leader roles as leases, and watch keys for changes.
  • The service itself is a five-node consensus group in one region (three availability zones).
  • Up to 10,000 client processes connect at once, and the count may grow tenfold.
  • A client that is paused, partitioned or slow must never be able to corrupt data because it still believes it holds a lock.
  • A leader failure inside the service should be invisible to most clients, and a failure of the whole service should be survivable.

You met leases and fencing in chapter 4, Raft in chapter 10 and the recipes in chapter 11. This studio puts them together, and asks what the API must do so that clients cannot get it wrong.

1Requirements and the safety contract

Before boxes: what exactly must never happen, and what may merely be slow? Write the safety property of a lock as a sentence you could check against a history. What do you promise under a partition, and what do you refuse to promise? What is out of scope?

Compare with a worked answer

Two kinds of lock. Chapter 4 separated efficiency locks (two holders waste work) from correctness locks (two holders corrupt data). The service must support both, but its API should push users toward the second, safe, use.

  • Safety (never): two clients are never granted the same lock with overlapping valid leases, and the order of grants is a single total order (a fencing token that only increases). A resource that checks tokens never accepts a write from an older grant after a newer one.
  • Liveness (eventually): if a majority of the group can talk to each other, and a lock's holder is dead, the lock is eventually granted to a waiter. Note the caveat from chapter 2: progress needs timing assumptions (here, bounded-ish delays and a majority), because consensus cannot guarantee termination in a purely asynchronous system.
  • Availability trade: it is a CP service. On the minority side of a partition it refuses writes and linearizable reads; it does not guess.
  • Behaviour a client can rely on: a session lasts as long as keepalives reach the group within its TTL; when a session ends, its ephemeral keys and locks are released; a watch delivers every change after a stated revision, in order, or tells the client it must re-read.

Functional requirements: sessions, ephemeral keys, mutual-exclusion locks, leader election, small configuration values, watches, compare-and-swap on a version. Non-functional: tolerate two of five node failures; leader failover in a couple of seconds; thousands of clients without per-client cost growing on the leader; no thundering herd on release. Out of scope: a general database (values stay tiny), cross-region operation (chapter 18 and the multi-region studio), Byzantine clients (chapter 12: we assume clients are buggy, not malicious).

2Estimates: where the load really is

Assume 10,000 clients, a 10-second session TTL, a keepalive every third of a TTL, 100 bytes per keepalive. Work out keepalive load, lock-acquisition load in a steady state and in a mass failover, the size of the data, and the cost of a thundering herd on one popular lock.

Compare with a worked answer

These are assumptions to reason with, not measurements.

  • Keepalives: 10,000 clients, one every TTL/3 ≈ 3.3 s: about 3,000 requests per second, at 100 B ≈ 300 KB/s. That is trivial for bandwidth. The cost is connections and per-session timers, not bytes. A Chubby-style long-poll keepalive (the server holds the request and replies near expiry, carrying notifications) cuts it to roughly one per client per TTL, about 1,000 per second.
  • Do keepalives go through consensus? They should not. If every renewal were a log entry, 3,000 writes a second would be paid for nothing. The leader keeps session timers in memory, and a new leader simply gives every session a fresh full TTL when it takes over (the safe direction, stage 6a).
  • Lock traffic: say 200 acquire/release operations a second in steady state. Each is a consensus write: leader append, one in-region round trip to a majority (well under a millisecond to a few milliseconds between zones) plus a disk sync, so a single group commits thousands of small writes a second when batched. Fine.
  • Mass failover: a network blip expires 5,000 sessions and every client retries at once: 5,000 writes in a burst (acquire) and 5,000 deletes (ephemeral cleanup). At a few thousand commits a second that is a couple of seconds of backlog. This is the burst to plan for, not the steady state.
  • Data: 1,000,000 small nodes × 1 KB ≈ 1 GB, held in memory, with periodic snapshots. The service stays small by design (chapter 11: what does not belong in a coordination service).
  • The herd: if 10,000 clients wait for one lock and all watch the lock key, each release wakes all 10,000, and draining the queue costs 10,000 × 10,000 = 108 notifications and as many retries. If each waiter watches only its predecessor, a release wakes exactly one: 104 in total. Four orders of magnitude, from a design choice.

Conclusion: the writes are not the problem. Sessions, watches, reconnection storms and herds are. Size the design around fan-out, and keep the leader's per-client work close to zero.

3The API: make the safe thing the only thing

Design the client API. A lazy programmer will write if lock.is_held(): do_work(). What does your API return, so that this mistake is hard to make? What must the library do on its own when the session is in trouble?

Compare with a worked answer

The central idea: a lock is a belief about the past (chapter 4). The API should never hand out a bare boolean. It hands out a grant: a fencing token and a local deadline, and the library enforces the deadline.

session = open_session(ttl=10s)                 # keepalives run in the library, not in app code
grant   = session.lock("/jobs/billing/leader")  # blocks; returns Grant

grant.token        # int, strictly increasing per lock name (a Chubby "sequencer")
grant.valid_until  # monotonic-clock deadline, already shortened for drift
grant.ctx          # a cancellation context: cancelled when the lease is at risk

storage.write(key, value, fencing_token=grant.token)   # every protected call carries it

session.on_event(JEOPARDY | EXPIRED | SAVED)    # mandatory handler: no default of ignoring
watch = session.watch("/config/", from_revision=r)     # ordered events, resumable, or RESYNC
  • No is_held(). There is only grant.ctx, which the library cancels before the server can expire the lease: the client's deadline is computed from the time it sent the keepalive, minus a drift allowance, so the client gives up first. A client that reads ctx before each step loses the lock safely; one that ignores it is exactly the case fencing exists for.
  • The token comes with the grant, and storage must check it. Chubby calls it a sequencer (lock name, mode, generation); in etcd the revision at which the lock key was created plays the same part. The service can verify a token on request; the protected system verifies it on every write (stage 6b).
  • Sessions are explicit, and failure events are mandatory. JEOPARDY (keepalives are not getting through; the lease might lapse), EXPIRED (it did; locks are gone) and SAVED (a new leader accepted the session within the grace period) are delivered to a handler the app must register.
  • Watches are resumable. Events carry a revision. After a reconnect, from_revision continues without a gap; if the history was compacted away, the client gets RESYNC and must re-read the subtree. Unlike ZooKeeper's one-shot watches, there is no window between "event fired" and "watch re-registered".
  • Compare-and-swap, not read-modify-write: writes take an expected version. Leader election is "create this ephemeral key if absent", and the result is a revision.
  • Backoff and jitter are in the library, not left to every team: retries on reconnect are randomised, so a blip is not followed by a synchronised stampede (architecture chapter 17).
  • Lock-delay (optional). After a session expires with a lock held, the service can withhold that lock for a short delay, so a still-running old holder has time to notice. Chubby offers this for servers that cannot check tokens. It narrows the window but is not a proof.

  • Why sessions' existence is replicated but their deadlines are not. Closing a session must be a decision all replicas agree on, because it releases locks. The timing of that decision can be local to the leader, as long as the leader never expires a session earlier than the client would. That is the whole safety argument for in-memory timers.

  • Size limits are an API feature. Values are capped at kilobytes and the number of keys per client is quota-limited. A coordination service that becomes a database is a service that cannot fail over quickly any more.

5Architecture and the shape of a failover

Draw the boxes: how do thousands of clients reach five nodes without the leader doing per-client work? Then draw a space-time diagram of a leader crash from one client's point of view. When does it keep its session, and when does it lose it?

Compare with a worked answer

Apply chapter 11: the group is a small kernel, and everything that scales with clients lives outside the log.

  1. Five nodes, three zones (2+2+1). Majority is three, so any two nodes can fail, including a whole zone (the zone with two). Adding nodes makes writes slower (a bigger quorum), not reads faster, so five is the usual ceiling.
  2. A proxy tier that terminates thousands of client connections, aggregates their keepalives into one stream per proxy, serves cached reads and fans out watch events. It is stateless in the sense that it holds no truth: a lost proxy means clients reconnect to another. Chubby's paper describes a similar proxy/cache layer that cut load on the master. Proxies must forward the client's session identity, since sessions belong to clients, not proxies.
  3. Reads. A linearizable read must be confirmed by the leader (a read index or a leader lease, chapter 10). A serializable (possibly stale) read can be served by any follower or proxy. Offer both, name them honestly, and default to the strong one.
  4. Client cache (Chubby-style): clients cache values and the leader invalidates them via the keepalive reply before a write completes. It makes reads nearly free at the cost of leader state and a stall when a client does not answer an invalidation. The alternative, used by etcd and ZooKeeper, is watches: the client keeps its own copy and the server notifies. Both turn polling into pushing.

Now the timeline.

  • Why the client gives up first. The client's deadline counts from when it sent the keepalive; the server's counts from when it received it. So the client's view of the lease always ends no later than the server's, and the client stops acting before anyone else can be granted the lock (drift aside, see 6a).
  • Why the session survives. The new leader restarts every timer at a full TTL. The client's local lease has ended, but the session has not; during JEOPARDY the client stops using its cache and pauses protected work. If it reaches the group within a grace period (Chubby's paper uses 45 s on a 12 s lease; treat your own numbers as a policy choice), it is SAVED. If not, the session expires and its locks go.
  • The price. Locks held by a really dead client are freed late by up to a full TTL after a failover, since timers restart. That is the deliberate safe direction.

6Deep dives

Five hard parts: (a) leases, drift and the TTL trade-off; (b) fencing end to end; (c) watches, the herd effect and the lock queue; (d) elections and stale leaders; (e) reconnection storms. Take each in turn, and state what you would measure.

Compare with a worked answer

(a) Leases, clock drift and the TTL trade-off

A lease is a promise the server makes on a timer, observed by a client on a different timer. Two things can go wrong: clocks that run at different speeds, and a client that stops without noticing. The first is handled by shortening the client's view by a drift allowance (if clocks differ by at most a fraction ε over the TTL, trust only TTL × (1 − ε)) and using a monotonic clock on both sides, never wall-clock time (chapter 7). The second cannot be handled by timing at all, which is what fencing is for.

Choosing the TTL is a trade-off with no free lunch:

The rule of thumb: the TTL must be several times larger than a leader election and a typical pause, and as short as your recovery time budget allows. Many teams set per-client TTLs, with the service enforcing a minimum.

(b) Fencing, end to end

The service can make sure that no two valid grants overlap. It cannot stop a paused client from waking up and writing. Only the resource can, if it remembers the highest token it has seen:

The same logic as a runnable simulation (simulated time, standard library only):

class Store:
    """Storage that rejects writes carrying a token older than the newest it has seen."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.max_token, self.value = 0, None
    def write(self, token, value):
        if token < self.max_token:
            return f"REJECT token {token} (saw {self.max_token})"
        self.max_token, self.value = token, value
        return f"ok     token {token}"

class LockService:
    """Hands out a lease plus a token that grows by one on every grant."""
    def __init__(self, ttl):
        self.ttl, self.token, self.holder, self.expires = ttl, 0, None, 0
    def acquire(self, client, now):
        if self.holder is None or now >= self.expires:
            self.token += 1
            self.holder, self.expires = client, now + self.ttl
            return self.token
        return None

lock, store = LockService(ttl=10), Store()
ta = lock.acquire("A", now=0);   print("A acquires at t=0  -> token", ta)
print("B tries at t=5     ->", lock.acquire("B", now=5), "(refused: A's lease is still live)")
tb = lock.acquire("B", now=12);  print("B acquires at t=12 -> token", tb, "(A's lease expired while A was paused)")
print("B writes :", store.write(tb, "from B"))
print("A wakes at t=20 and writes:", store.write(ta, "from A (stale)"))
print("store value:", store.value)

Practical points. The token is monotonic per lock name, so the resource must keep a high-water mark per protected object. If the resource cannot be changed (a third-party API), you are left with narrower tools: lock-delay, short leases with deadlines checked before each step, and making the operation idempotent. State plainly that these reduce the window and do not close it. The Redlock debate (chapter 4) is exactly about whether a lock without a fencing token is acceptable for correctness.

(c) Watches, herds and the successor queue

PatternWake-ups per releaseWake-ups to drain N waitersNotes
All waiters watch the lock key and retryN≈ N²/2 … N²The herd: a thousand waiters means roughly a million retries
Each waiter watches only the waiter just ahead of it (ordered keys)1NThe ZooKeeper lock recipe; also etcd's lock built on revisions

For leader election the picture differs: everyone must learn of the new leader, so a watch on one key is right, and the fan-out is N notifications per change, once. Make that cheap by serving it through the proxy tier (one event per proxy, then fan-out locally). Slow watchers are a hazard: if a client does not drain its events the server buffers without bound. Bound the buffer, drop the watch and send RESYNC.

(d) Failure of the group itself: elections, stalls, and who is leader

  • Election storms. If the leader stalls on a slow disk sync, followers time out and elect someone else; the old leader wakes and disrupts. Pre-vote and CheckQuorum (chapter 10) stop a node that was merely cut off from forcing new terms, and make a leader that cannot reach a majority step down.
  • Stale leaders. A deposed leader may still believe it is leader for a moment. It must not serve linearizable reads from memory without confirming with a quorum or holding an unexpired leader lease, and it must never extend session timers it is not sure it owns.
  • Membership changes (replace a failed node) go through the log, one node at a time, and the new node catches up from a snapshot before it counts toward the quorum.

(e) Reconnection storms and admission control

When the whole group restarts, 10,000 clients reconnect within the same few hundred milliseconds. Defend with: jittered reconnect backoff in the library; a connection and session-creation rate limit at the proxies; priority for keepalives over new work; and a queue length limit with a clear overload error instead of slowly dying. A keepalive that is late because the leader is overloaded looks exactly like a dead client, so overload expires sessions, which creates more load. Treat keepalives as the most precious traffic and shed everything else first.

Observability. Leader changes per hour (any burst is an incident); commit and fsync latency; pending proposals; number of sessions and the session-expiry rate (a spike with a flat client count means the service, not the clients, is the problem); watcher counts and the largest per-client event backlog; the age of the oldest uncommitted request; follower lag; snapshot duration. Test it with fault injection (chapter 19 and the etcd and Toxiproxy cards): kill the leader repeatedly under load, partition it, pause a client for longer than its TTL and check that fencing rejects it, and run a linearizability checker (Porcupine) on the recorded history.

Self-check rubric

Tick what your own design (before reading the answers) covered. An experienced engineer would hit most of these. Missing several is normal the first time, and worth a second try in a week.

  • Distinguished efficiency locks from correctness locks and stated the safety property: no two valid grants overlap, tokens only increase.
  • Did the estimates: about 3,000 keepalives a second at 10,000 clients, and recognised the load is sessions, watches and herds, not writes.
  • Kept session timers out of the log and explained why a new leader restarting them is the safe direction.
  • Designed an API with a grant (token and deadline), no bare is-held check, and mandatory jeopardy/expired events.
  • Made the client's deadline end before the server's, using monotonic clocks and a drift allowance.
  • Required the protected resource to check fencing tokens, and said what to do when it cannot.
  • Chose a 5-node, 3-zone group and explained why more nodes do not add read capacity.
  • Avoided the thundering herd (successor watch or server-side queue) and showed the N versus N2 arithmetic.
  • Planned failover: jeopardy, grace period, fresh TTL, and pre-vote/CheckQuorum against stale leaders.
  • Described watch semantics: ordered, resumable by revision, with a resync escape when history is compacted.
  • Handled reconnect storms with jitter, admission control and keepalive priority.
  • Covered failure modes and testing with fault injection and a linearizability check.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"A lock service is a small, strongly consistent kernel: five nodes across three zones running Raft, holding about a gigabyte of tiny keys, sessions and lock queues. Everything that scales with clients, such as connections, keepalives and watch fan-out, lives in a proxy tier, and keepalive timers stay in the leader's memory instead of the log. The thing I'd obsess over is the API. A lock is a belief about the past, so it never returns a boolean; it returns a grant with a fencing token and a local deadline that the library enforces, shorter than the server's lease so the client gives up first. But a paused client can still wake up and write, so the protected resource must remember the highest token it has seen and reject older ones; that is the only real defence. Failover restarts every session timer at a full TTL, which can only delay a lock release, never hasten it, and clients ride it out through a jeopardy period and a grace window. To avoid herds, waiters watch their predecessor, not the lock. Then I'd talk about overload, because late keepalives look like dead clients and expiry causes more load, and I'd test it by killing leaders, pausing clients beyond their TTL and checking histories for linearizability."

Notice the shape: safety property → what scales with clients → what must not go through the log → an API that forces fencing → failover direction → herd and storm control → test with faults.

Key takeaways

  • A lock service is a CP kernel: keep it small, and keep per-client work off the consensus path.
  • Never return a boolean. Return a grant with a fencing token and a deadline the library enforces.
  • The client must give up before the server does: count from the send time, use monotonic clocks, subtract drift.
  • Only the protected resource can stop a stale holder: it must reject tokens older than the highest seen.
  • Session timers can live in leader memory if a new leader only ever gives clients more time.
  • Waiting on your predecessor turns N2 wake-ups into N.
  • Plan for the herd, the reconnect storm and overload: late keepalives look like dead clients.
  • Test with leader kills, long client pauses and linearizability checks.
