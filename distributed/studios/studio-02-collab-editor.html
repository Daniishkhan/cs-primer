Design a Collaborative Editor

Two people type in the same sentence at the same moment. Both must see their own letters instantly, and both must end up with exactly the same text. Behind that magic is a small set of hard choices: who decides the order, how an edit is rewritten when others got there first, and what happens when someone is offline for an hour. This studio makes those choices.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing a real-time collaborative text editor in the style of Google Docs, as one product within a larger suite.

  • Up to 100 people can edit one document at once; typing from other people appears within a fraction of a second.
  • Everyone sees the same text once edits settle, and nobody's edit is silently lost.
  • People can work offline (a plane, a tunnel) and merge when they return.
  • You show presence (who is here) and live cursors; each person has their own undo.
  • Documents have permissions (owner, editor, commenter, viewer), a version history, and range from a shopping list to a 500-page manuscript.
  • Millions of documents are open at once, spread across many servers.

You met sequence CRDTs and the OT versus CRDT comparison in chapter 15, causal consistency in chapter 6 and coordination avoidance in chapter 16. This studio makes the choice for a product.

1Requirements: what does "everyone sees the same text" mean?

State the consistency guarantee in one precise sentence. Is it linearizability? Which anomalies are acceptable on the way (for example text appearing out of order for a moment), and which never? What is the cost of a lost keystroke versus a delayed one? What is out of scope?

Compare with a worked answer

The guarantee is convergence plus intention preservation. Not linearizability: nobody needs a global real-time order of keystrokes. The properties are:

  • Convergence (strong eventual consistency, chapter 15): any two clients that have received the same set of operations show identical text, regardless of arrival order.
  • Intention preservation: an operation has the effect its author intended even though it was applied in a changed context. If I insert a word after "dog" while you delete the first sentence, my word still lands after "dog".
  • Causality / session guarantees (chapter 6): you always see your own edits (read-your-writes) and an edit never appears before the edit it was a reply to.
  • Durability: once the server acknowledges an edit, it survives a server crash. Before the acknowledgement it may be lost, and the client keeps it and resends.

Acceptable on the way: a few hundred milliseconds of divergence; text that moves slightly as concurrent edits arrive; a cursor that jumps. Never: two clients permanently showing different text; a lost acknowledged edit; a duplicated edit after a retry; a revoked user still editing.

Functional: edit, presence, per-user undo, comments, history, sharing. Non-functional: local typing is instant (no network round trip before a keystroke appears), remote edits visible in roughly 100–300 ms, offline for hours, documents up to hundreds of thousands of characters. Out of scope: rich-text schema design, spreadsheets and drawings (different structures, same ideas), spell-check, search indexing.

2Estimates: documents, operations and fan-out

Estimate operations per second for the whole fleet and for one busy document, the log growth per day, the memory held per open document, and the cost of broadcasting cursors. Which number should drive the architecture: totals or the worst single document?

Compare with a worked answer

All numbers are assumptions.

  • Fleet: 1,000,000 concurrent editors. A person actively typing produces maybe 3 characters a second, but a client batches keystrokes into one operation every ~100 ms, so call it 2 operations a second while typing, typing 30% of the time: ≈ 0.6/s per editor. Total ≈ 600,000 operations a second, but spread over many documents.
  • Per document: most documents have 1–3 editors, so a document sees a handful of operations a second. A heavily shared one with 100 editors: 100 × 0.6 ≈ 60 ops/s in, and each op fanned out to 99 others ≈ 6,000 messages/s out. One server core can handle that for one document, with room to spare. The architectural consequence: documents are the unit of scale. A single document never needs to be split across servers; many documents need to be spread across many servers.
  • Log growth: an operation of ~50 bytes. 600,000/s × 50 B = 30 MB/s ≈ 2.6 TB/day if everyone were editing around the clock. Realistically about a third of that over a day, ≈ 1 TB/day. Hence compaction into snapshots, and cold documents moved to cheaper storage.
  • Memory per open document: text of 100 KB plus metadata (CRDT per-character ids could multiply this 10–30×; OT keeps almost none) plus a small tail of recent ops. With OT, ~1 MB per open doc; 1 million open docs ≈ 1 TB across a fleet of, say, 50 servers at 32 GB each. That is why idle documents are evicted from memory after a few minutes and re-loaded from snapshot plus log.
  • Presence: if each of N people sends a cursor update 5 times a second to the other N−1, that is N(N−1) × 5 messages/s. N = 10: 450/s (fine). N = 100: 49,500/s for one document. The cursor stream, not typing, is the largest fan-out; throttle it and cap how many cursors are shown (stage 6c).

What drives the design: the worst single document (100 editors; 500-page text) and the fan-out, not the fleet totals. Latency budget: local apply 0 ms; client to server 20–80 ms; server work < 5 ms; log append (replicated) ~5–10 ms; server to other clients 20–80 ms. Say an end-to-end 100–250 ms. The log append sits on the path to the acknowledgement, but not necessarily on the broadcast (6e discusses which order is safe).

3The protocol and API

Design the wire protocol between a client and the server. The connection will drop mid-edit and the client will resend. How do you make a resent operation not apply twice? What does the client do while it waits for the server's acknowledgement?

Compare with a worked answer

Use a persistent connection (WebSocket) for a document session, and ordinary HTTPS for document lifecycle, sharing and history. The editing protocol is a small state machine:

client -> server  JOIN   { doc, token, since_rev }            # since_rev = last revision the client has
server -> client  SYNC   { snapshot_rev, snapshot|null, ops[snapshot_rev+1 .. head], head }

client -> server  OP     { client_id, client_seq, base_rev, ops }   # client_seq increases by 1 per op
server -> client  ACK    { client_seq, rev }                         # the op got revision `rev`
server -> clients OP     { rev, author, ops }                        # transformed, in server order

client <-> server PRESENCE { cursor, selection, name, color }        # ephemeral, not stored
server -> client  REJECT { client_seq, reason }                      # no permission, invalid position

# The client holds: confirmed_text (at revision r) + `in_flight` op + `buffer` of later local ops.
# Only ONE op is in flight at a time (the Google Wave / ot.js variant); further typing is composed into `buffer`.
  • Idempotent retries. (client_id, client_seq) uniquely names an operation. If the connection drops and the client resends, the server checks the pair: already applied means re-send the ACK, do not apply again. Dedupe state per document is tiny (the last client_seq per client), the same idea as an idempotency key in the ledger studio.
  • One op in flight. The client sends one operation and waits for its acknowledgement before sending the next, composing whatever the user types meanwhile into a single buffered op. This limits the transformation work: the server only ever transforms a client's op against the server-side ops since base_rev, and the client only transforms incoming ops against its (at most two) pending ops. This one-in-flight rule is a simplification used by Google Wave’s protocol and libraries such as ot.js, descendants of the Jupiter system (Nichols et al., 1995). Jupiter itself sent each local change immediately and tracked every unacknowledged message with message counts; what it shared with this design is the single central server that lets each side transform against only one history.
  • Local-first typing. Keystrokes apply to the local text immediately; the network only affects when others see them.
  • Reconnect. The client rejoins with since_rev. The server sends the missing ops, or a fresh snapshot if the client is too far behind. The client then transforms its unacknowledged ops against what it missed and resends them.
  • Validation. The server checks that base_rev ≤ head, positions are within bounds, the author's role allows edits, and the op size is within limits. Never trust the client's document state.
  • Back-pressure. A slow client that cannot keep up gets disconnected and re-synced, rather than letting its queue grow without bound.

5Architecture: one owner per document

Draw the system and place the ordering point. If every document has exactly one server that orders its operations, what must be true to keep that promise as servers die? Then draw the timeline of two concurrent edits.

Compare with a worked answer

The key idea: give each document a single sequencer. Most of the difficulty of collaboration is concurrency; a single server that decides the order removes most of it for the price of routing all of a document's traffic to one place. As the estimates showed, one document easily fits on one server, so the price is small.

  1. Gateways terminate WebSockets, authenticate, check the ACL and route the connection to the document's owner (looked up in the coordination service, or computed by consistent hashing with a lease to confirm).
  2. The owner holds the document in memory (text, recent ops, connected sessions). For each incoming op it validates, transforms against ops since base_rev, assigns the next revision, appends it to the log, acknowledges, and broadcasts. It also relays presence, which it never persists.
  3. The owner role is a lease with an increasing epoch (chapter 4). Every log append carries the epoch and the log store rejects an append from an older epoch: the fencing token again. Without it, an old owner that was paused could keep appending operations after a new owner took over, and the log would fork.
  4. Log and snapshot stores are replicated storage, not part of the owner. The owner is soft state: kill it and a new one rebuilds from snapshot plus log tail (stage 6e).

On the other side, Bob transformed Alice's insert against his own delete (position 1 becomes 0). A step-by-step version is in the next stage.

6Deep dives

Seven hard parts: (a) OT versus CRDT; (b) server versus peer-to-peer and offline edits; (c) presence and cursors; (d) undo; (e) persistence and recovery; (f) spreading documents across servers; (g) large documents and permissions. Take each in turn and write the trade-off.

Compare with a worked answer

(a) OT or CRDT?

Both families converge; they differ in where the cleverness lives. Operational transformation (Ellis and Gibbs, 1989, and the Jupiter system) rewrites an operation so it applies correctly after concurrent ones. Sequence CRDTs (RGA, Logoot, and the structures behind libraries such as Yjs and Automerge) give every character a unique, ordered identity so operations commute without rewriting (chapter 15).

Needs a central order?Yes: the owner's revision numbers make the transform functions simple (one pair at a time)No: any delivery order converges; a server is optional

Garbage collectionTruncate the log behind snapshotsTombstones need all replicas to agree before removal (chapter 15)

Decision for this product: we already need a server for permissions, version history, search and durable storage, and every document has a single owner. That makes server-ordered OT a natural fit: it keeps the document small in memory and the transform logic bounded. If the product were local-first (works peer-to-peer, offline for weeks, no server required) the answer would flip to a CRDT. Real products have gone both ways, and reasonable engineers still argue about it; say that. A hybrid is common: a CRDT on the client for offline merging, with the server as an ordering and permission authority.

A small runnable OT, for one-character inserts and deletes, shows the idea and why naive application fails:

def apply(doc, op):
    kind, pos, ch = op
    return doc[:pos] + ch + doc[pos:] if kind == "ins" else doc[:pos] + doc[pos+1:]

def transform(a, b, a_wins_ties):
    """Rewrite op a so it applies after concurrent op b. Only insert/delete of one char."""
    ka, pa, ca = a; kb, pb, cb = b
    if kb == "ins":
        if pb < pa or (pb == pa and not a_wins_ties):
            pa += 1
    else:
        if pb < pa: pa -= 1
    return (ka, pa, ca)

doc = "cat"
alice = ("ins", 1, "h")      # c-h-at? -> "chat"
bob   = ("del", 0, None)     # delete 'c' -> "at"
a_then_b = apply(apply(doc, alice), transform(bob, alice, a_wins_ties=False))
b_then_a = apply(apply(doc, bob), transform(alice, bob, a_wins_ties=True))
print("start      :", repr(doc))
print("alice then bob':", repr(a_then_b))
print("bob then alice':", repr(b_then_a))
print("converged  :", a_then_b == b_then_a)
naive = apply(apply(doc, alice), bob)
print("naive (no transform) at alice's site:", repr(naive), "vs bob's site:", repr(apply(apply(doc, bob), alice)))
start      : 'cat'
alice then bob': 'hat'
bob then alice': 'hat'
converged  : True
naive (no transform) at alice's site: 'hat' vs bob's site: 'aht'

Without transformation, Alice's site ends with "hat" and Bob's with "aht": a permanent divergence. With it, both sites agree. Real transform functions also handle same-position ties (here: a deterministic a_wins_ties flag, which must be decided by something both sides agree on, such as the server order), ranges, and rich-text attributes, which is where the difficulty lives. The convergence test for any such code is randomised: generate thousands of random concurrent edit pairs and check both orders give the same text. This is a perfect fit for the deterministic simulation approach of chapter 19.

(b) Central server or peer-to-peer; and offline edits

Peer-to-peer (CRDTs over WebRTC or a relay) removes the server from the ordering path, which helps offline use and privacy. It does not remove the need for one: peers behind NATs need a relay, the document needs a durable home, a link-shared document needs someone who can say no, and a revoked collaborator already holds a full copy and cannot be forced to forget it (peers are not trusted, chapter 12). So: a central owner for ordering, permissions and durability, with clients written so they keep working without it.

Offline edits are the reconnect protocol stretched over hours:

  • The merge cost grows with the offline gap. A phone offline for a week against a document edited by ten people is transforming against thousands of operations, and the result may be semantically odd (paragraphs deleted around your edits). Beyond a threshold, offer an explicit "your offline changes" review, or fall back to a three-way text merge with a conflict view, the way version control does. CRDTs make the mechanics easier but do not make the meaning better.
  • Persist the pending ops locally (IndexedDB or similar) so closing the tab offline does not lose work. They carry stable (client_id, client_seq) ids, so a resend after any crash is harmless.

(c) Presence and cursors

  • Ephemeral by design. Presence is soft state: not logged, not persisted, rebuilt from the live connections. A heartbeat with a short TTL (a lease again) removes departed users even when a connection dies without closing.
  • Cursors are positions, so they must be transformed. A remote cursor at offset 40 moves to 41 when someone inserts before it. Represent it as an operation-like anchor (transformed with each incoming op), or in a CRDT as an element id. Late presence updates are stale by the time they arrive; that is fine.
  • The N² fan-out (stage 2: ~50,000 messages/s for 100 editors). Throttle to a few updates a second, coalesce (only the latest position matters), show detailed cursors for the nearest 10–20 people and only avatars for the rest, and send read-only viewers a coarse stream. Presence should travel on a separate channel and be droppable under load, so it can never delay a text op.

(d) Undo

Global undo (pop the last operation in the document) would undo someone else's typing. Undo must be per user and selective: undoing my last edit means applying the inverse of that operation, transformed over everything that happened after it. If I typed "red" and you then deleted a paragraph above it, my undo removes "red" at its current position, not at its original one. If someone has already edited inside my text, the inverse may only partly apply. Keep the undo stack on the client (my own ops only), send an undo as an ordinary new operation so the server and history need no special case, and decide a policy for conflicts (skip, or undo what still applies). In a CRDT the inverse of an insert is a deletion by id, and the inverse of a delete is resurrecting the element, which is why tombstones matter for undo as well as merge.

(e) Persistence and recovery

  • Order of events. Owner receives op → validates and transforms → assigns revision → appends to the replicated log → ACKs the author and broadcasts. Appending before acknowledging makes "ack means durable" true. A variant broadcasts optimistically before the append completes for speed, but then a crash can show other users text that never became durable; most products accept the extra ~10 ms and keep this simple.
  • Snapshots every ~500 ops or on idle, tagged with their revision. Loading a cold document is snapshot + replay of the tail: bounded work.
  • Recovery of an owner: a new owner acquires the lease (higher epoch), loads the latest snapshot, replays the tail from the log, and accepts connections. Clients reconnect with since_rev and resend unacknowledged ops, deduplicated by (client_id, client_seq). Typical gap: a lease expiry plus a few seconds.
  • Checksum guard. Periodically a client sends a hash of its text at a given revision. A mismatch means a transform bug or corruption: the client discards local state and reloads from the server's snapshot. A divergence detector that fixes itself is worth its small cost.

The robust choice combines the first and the second: hash for a preferred server, a lease with an epoch to make it exclusive. A gateway that routes to the wrong server gets redirected. A hot document (a company all-hands with 5,000 viewers) is handled by separating editors, which stay on the owner, from viewers, who subscribe to a fan-out tier that relays the owner's output stream. A read-only viewer needs no transformation and can tolerate more lag.

(g) Large documents and permissions

  • Large text: store it in a rope or piece-table structure so that an insert in the middle of 1 MB is O(log n), not a copy. Load by segments (a page or chapter) and let the client request ranges, while ops still use global positions or ids. Compose a burst of keystrokes into one operation. For CRDTs, compact tombstones when every replica has passed a point (the garbage-collection problem of chapter 15).
  • Permissions are checked twice: at the gateway when connecting (can this principal open the document at all?) and by the owner on every op (does this author's role allow edits?). The owner caches the ACL with its acl_version; a revocation publishes a new version and the owner drops or downgrades the session immediately. Commenters can only create comment anchors, not change text, and the op validator enforces this. A user revoked while offline can still have local ops pending: on reconnect they get REJECT and their copy is retained as a conflict copy, never merged.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The guarantee is convergence with intention preservation, not linearizability: everyone ends with the same text, and my edit means what I meant. Per-document load is small, a hundred editors is about sixty operations a second, so documents are the unit of scale and I give each one a single owner server that orders its operations. The owner role is a lease with an epoch, and every append to the replicated operation log carries it, so a paused old owner cannot fork history. The log is the source of truth, with snapshots every few hundred operations for fast loads. Clients type locally, send one operation in flight with a base revision and a client sequence number so retries are idempotent, and transform incoming operations over their pending ones. I'd pick server-ordered OT here because we need a server for permissions and history anyway; a CRDT is the right call if the product must work peer-to-peer or offline for weeks, and it's a real debate. Offline edits merge on return, with a review step for long gaps. Presence is ephemeral, throttled and droppable, undo is per-user and transformed, and permissions are checked at the gateway and on every operation. I'd test convergence with a seeded simulator that injects delays, drops and owner crashes."

Notice the shape: state the guarantee → find the unit of scale → pick the ordering point → make retries and failover safe → decide OT or CRDT with reasons → keep presence soft → test convergence by simulation.

Key takeaways

  • The guarantee is convergence and intention preservation; nobody needs linearizable keystrokes.
  • Documents, not the fleet, are the unit of scale: one owner per document keeps ordering simple.
  • The operation log is the truth; snapshots bound replay; ack after the durable append.
  • Make retries harmless with (client_id, client_seq), and fence the owner with an epoch.
  • OT with a central order is simpler when a server is needed anyway; CRDTs win for offline-first and peer-to-peer. Both are legitimate.
  • Presence is soft state and the biggest fan-out: throttle and drop it freely.
  • Undo is per user: a transformed inverse sent as a normal operation.
  • Test convergence with randomised, seeded simulations.
