Plan a Small Model on a Budget

An organisation has a pile of its own text, a rack of GPUs for three weeks and a deployment target that is one modest card. Choose the model's size, the number of training tokens, the context length and the architecture, and show on paper, with numbers, that the plan fits the money, the memory and the schedule before anyone presses run.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock planning session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong plan with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the order a planning review takes: clarify the problem, decide whether to train at all, turn the budget into FLOPs, choose size and tokens, choose context length, choose the architecture, check memory and time, de-risk with small runs, plan for failure, decide how success is judged, and say what changes if the requirements change. The arithmetic is the arithmetic of chapters 16, 18 and 19, run as code with real output. All inputs about the organisation (data volumes, document lengths, prices, utilisation) are assumptions to reason with, not measurements.

📐 The brief

A regional insurer asks: "We want our own language model for claims and policy documents: classifying claims, extracting fields, summarising long files and drafting replies. The text cannot leave our premises. We have eight H100-class GPUs for three weeks. Tell us what to train."

  • Data: 14 billion tokens of cleaned, deduplicated in-house text (claim notes, correspondence, adjuster reports, policy wordings). Open-licence general text is available too; assume 300 billion usable tokens.
  • Documents are long: median 3,000 tokens, 95th percentile 24,000 (policy wordings with schedules).
  • Compute: 8 GPUs for 21 days, nothing more. The team is three engineers and the pilot is due in six weeks.
  • Serving: one 24 GB card per regional office, up to 20 people using it at once.
  • Builds on parameters, FLOPs and memory (chapter 16), scaling laws (chapter 18), efficient attention (chapter 19), tokenisation (chapter 13) and training stability (chapter 17). Related reading in A3: GPU memory, long runs and token economics.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes.

2Should we train from scratch at all?

A senior reviewer's first question is whether this project needs to exist. What is the cheapest credible alternative, how much of the budget does it cost, and what would make you proceed anyway?

Compare with a worked answer

The alternative is to continue pretraining an open-weight model (one whose licence allows commercial use) on the in-house text, then fine-tune it (supervised fine-tuning, LoRA). It starts from a model that already reads, so the in-house data only has to teach the domain. What does it cost in this budget's own units? The first part of this script uses the 6ND rule of chapter 16.

"""Stages 2 and 8: what the yardstick run and the scaling ladder cost, in the budget's own units."""
from planlib import *
total_h = GPUS * DAYS * 24
C_main = gpu_seconds_to_flops(0.78 * total_h, 0.35)
def gpu_hours(flops, mfu): return flops / (PEAK * mfu) / 3600

print("A. the yardstick: continue pretraining an open ~1.5B model on 28B tokens (2 epochs of the 14B domain tokens)")
fl = 6 * 1.5e9 * 28e9
h = gpu_hours(fl, 0.35)
print(f"   6ND = {fl:.2e} FLOPs = {h:,.0f} GPU-hours = {h/GPUS:.1f} hours on {GPUS} GPUs = {h/total_h:.1%} of the budget, about ${h*PRICE:,.0f}")
print(f"   the main run is {C_main/fl:.0f} times as much compute\n")

print("B. the scaling ladder: small runs on the final recipe (MFU is lower for small models; assumed 25%)")
print(f"   {'N':>6}{'D':>7}{'FLOPs':>11}{'GPU-h':>7}")

Part A answers it: about 200 GPU-hours, 5% of the budget and roughly a day of the cluster, to adapt a 1.5B-parameter open model on two passes over the domain data. The from-scratch main run is 16 times as much compute. So the decision is not "scratch or adapt" but "adapt first, because it is cheap and it sets the bar". The yardstick run goes first, and the from-scratch model must beat it to be worth shipping.

Reasons to proceed with a from-scratch base anyway, each of which should be checked, not assumed:

  • A tokeniser that fits the data. Policy numbers, currency amounts and clause references may split badly in a general tokeniser (chapter 13), costing context and accuracy. A model trained from scratch can have its own. (Measure this on your own text before believing it.)
  • Licence and provenance. A regulated insurer may need to know every source of the training data, which an inherited model does not offer.
  • Size control. Open models come in fixed sizes. Training your own lets you pick a size for one 24 GB card.
  • Learning. A legitimate reason in a course, a poor one in a business case.

The rest of this studio plans the from-scratch model, with the yardstick as a gate (stage 10). If the yardstick wins, the right outcome of the planning exercise is to ship it and say so.

3From GPU-days to FLOPs

Turn "8 GPUs for 21 days" into the single number every later choice depends on. What fraction of the time is the main run, what utilisation do you assume, and how sensitive is the answer to it?

Compare with a worked answer

Chapter 16's formula is GPU-seconds = 6ND / (peak × MFU), read in reverse: the budget in GPU-seconds times the delivered speed is the FLOPs you can spend. Three allowances come off the top before the main run: the scaling ladder and ablations (stage 8), the yardstick run (stage 2), and a reserve for failures and restarts (stage 9). The planlib.py file below holds the formulas of chapters 16 and 18 and every assumption of this studio in one place; the first script uses it.

"""Stage 3: turn the budget into FLOPs."""

A small model on eight GPUs will not reach the 40% to 43% that Meta reported for a 405B model on 16,384 GPUs, so 35% is a deliberately modest planning figure.   • The answer moves with utilisation. At 25% the budget is 2.8×1021, at 45% it is 5.0×1021: a factor of 1.8 from one assumption. Planning with a range is more honest than a single number.
  • The money is small. 4,032 GPU-hours at an assumed $2.50 is about $10,000. Cloud prices vary and an owned cluster has no hourly price, so treat this only as a scale. The binding constraints are time and the team, not cash.
  • Chinchilla at this budget is 5.6B parameters on 117B tokens (21 tokens per parameter), from the Besiroglu et al. refit of chapter 18. That is the loss-optimal point for a pure training objective. The next stage asks whether we want it.

A sanity check from the last line of the output: a 70B-parameter Chinchilla-style run needs 150 times this budget. A frontier-scale ambition is not available; a competent small model is.

4Size and tokens

Pick the number of parameters and the number of training tokens. List the constraints that push the size up and the ones that push it down, put numbers on each for six candidate sizes, and choose.

Compare with a worked answer

For each candidate shape the script computes the parameters (chapter 16's counting function), then the tokens the main-run budget buys at that size, counting the attention-score FLOPs as well as 6N. It then reports six things for the choice: the tokens per parameter; the predicted loss above the compute-optimal model; how many times the 14B domain tokens would have to be repeated if they are 20% of the training mix; the bf16 weight size and a decode speed ceiling (bandwidth divided by weight bytes, with the card's 1 TB/s an assumption); and the 16 bytes per parameter of training state, in total and sharded over 8 GPUs.

          (2560, 20, 4, 32, 8960), (3072, 24, 6, 34, 10752), (4096, 32, 8, 28, 14336)]   # (C, heads, kv heads, layers, F)

  • Training loss alone says go big. The predicted penalty for a smaller model falls from 0.26 nats at 0.25B to 0.01 nats at 6.2B. The top rows are outside the range the fit was built on (a quarter-billion parameters on 1.7 trillion tokens is 6,700 tokens per parameter), so read them as warnings, not predictions. Note also that the losses come from a fit to DeepMind's corpus: use only the differences, as the chapter says.
  • The data pushes the size up, from below. The domain data is the scarce, valuable part. Chapter 18 cites Muennighoff et al. (2023): repeated data is nearly as good as fresh up to about four passes and quickly worse after. At a 20% share, a 1.6B model would need 4.5 passes and anything smaller would need more. This sets a floor of roughly 2B, unless the domain share is lowered, which weakens the point of the domain model.
  • Serving pushes the size down, from above. At 2.8B, bf16 weights are 5.6 GB, leaving about 18 GB of a 24 GB card for the KV cache and activations. At 6.2B the weights are 12.5 GB and leave far fewer concurrent long documents. The decode ceiling is 179 tokens per second for one stream at 2.8B and 80 at 6.2B (an upper bound under the assumed bandwidth; token economics turns it into a price).
  • The flat bottom makes the choice cheap. The 2.79B model is predicted to be 0.024 nats above the optimum on a budget where the optimum is twice the size. That small price buys half the serving memory, half the checkpoint size and roughly half the cost of every restart.

Decision: about 2.8B parameters. It satisfies all four constraints at once: at most 2.7 passes over the domain data in the 4k phase (2.3 after the long-context phase of stage 5), a penalty under 0.03 nats, weights under 6 GB, and per-GPU training state of 5.6 GB when sharded. The tokens follow from the budget: about 158B in total (57 per parameter, nearly three times Chinchilla's 20, the direction chapter 18 recommends for a model that will be used a lot). The mix is then 20% domain (about 32B tokens, 2.3 passes) and 80% general text (about 126B tokens, under half of what is available, so no repetition).

State of play, October 2026

Small models meant for wide use are trained far beyond the compute-optimal point. The SmolLM2 paper (Allal et al., 2025) describes a 1.7-billion-parameter model "overtrained" on about 11 trillion tokens, roughly 6,500 tokens per parameter. This plan sits at 57, because its constraint is a fixed 21-day budget and a small body of domain text, not a public-release goal. I have not checked releases after early 2025, and the Besiroglu constants are a fit to one family of models; refit on your own ladder (stage 8) before trusting a second decimal.

5Context length

Documents have a median of 3,000 tokens and a 95th percentile of 24,000. What context length do you train at, what does each length cost in FLOPs, memory and serving, and how do you get long-context ability without paying for it on every token?

Compare with a worked answer

The script uses the chosen 2.79B shape (stage 6 derives it). It models document lengths as a lognormal with the stated median and 95th percentile, which is an assumption about the shape of the tail. It then prices each context length three ways: the share of documents and tokens that fit, the KV cache per sequence (chapter 19), and the training FLOPs per token including the attention term (chapter 16).

"""Stage 5: how long must the context be, and what does each length cost? Document lengths are an assumed lognormal."""
import numpy as np
from planlib import *
C_, H_, KV_, L_, F_ = 2560, 20, 4, 32, 8960             # the chosen shape (stage 6 derives it)
N = params(C_, L_, H_, KV_, F_)[0]
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma = np.log(3000), np.log(24000 / 3000) / 1.645   # median 3,000 tokens, 95th percentile 24,000 (assumed)
docs = np.minimum(rng.lognormal(mu, sigma, 200_000), 400_000)
print(f"{'context':>8}{'docs that fit':>14}{'tokens in docs that fit':>24}{'KV GiB / sequence':>19}{'sequences in 18 GiB':>20}")
kv = kv_bytes_per_token(L_, KV_, C_ // H_)
for T in (2048, 4096, 8192, 32768, 131072):
    fit = docs <= T
    print(f"{T:>8,}{fit.mean():>14.0%}{docs[fit].sum()/docs.sum():>24.0%}{kv*T/2**30:>19.2f}{int(18*2**30 // (kv*T)):>20}")
print(f"\nKV cache: {kv/1024:.0f} KiB per token with {KV_} key/value heads; with full multi-head attention it would be {kv*H_//KV_/1024:.0f} KiB")

print(f"\ntraining cost per token (chapter 16: 6N + 12 L C T), N = {N/1e9:.2f}B")
base = flops_per_token(N, L_, C_, 4096)
for T in (4096, 8192, 32768, 131072):
    f = flops_per_token(N, L_, C_, T)
    print(f"  T = {T:>7,}: {f:.2e} FLOPs/token, {f/base:4.2f}x the 4k cost, attention-score share {12*L_*C_*T/f:5.1%}")

days = 0.78 * DAYS

  • Most of the tokens are in the long documents. Only 15% of the tokens lie in documents that fit in 4,096 tokens, and 73% lie in documents that fit in 32,768. A model trained only at 4k sees a long policy wording as unrelated chunks and never practises the cross-references that make it an insurer's document. Context length is a data question first.
  • Cost per token grows with length. At 32k each token costs 2.4 times the 4k price, and attention scores are two thirds of the arithmetic; at 128k it is 7 times, 89% attention. The 6N rule fails at long context, as chapter 16 warned.
  • The cache is manageable at 32k, not at 128k. With 4 key/value heads the cache is 64 KiB per token (320 KiB with 20, the full multi-head price), so one 32k sequence holds 2.0 GiB and nine fit in the 18 GiB left on the card. At 128k each sequence is 8 GiB and only two fit. That argues against 128k unless the tasks need it. The brief says they do not.
  • Do not train every token long. Extend at the end. The Llama 3 team trained at 4,096 tokens and then 8,192 for most of pre-training, then increased the context "gradually in six stages" up to 128K using about 800 billion tokens, and counted a stage as done when short-context evaluations had "recovered completely" and needle-in-a-haystack retrieval worked at that length (Llama Team, 2024, section 3.4.2). The reason they give is that attention compute grows quadratically. This plan does the same in two steps, 4k then 32k, and raises the rotary base frequency during the extension (chapter 11).
  • How much to spend on the 32k phase? The second half of the script splits the 16.4 days between a 4k phase and a 32k phase (with activation recomputation, assumed to lower utilisation to 28%). Spending 5% of tokens at 32k leaves 172B tokens in total; 10% leaves 158B; 0% leaves 189B. Because 85% of this organisation's tokens are in long documents, the plan takes 10% of tokens at 32k, about 16B, and pays for it with 31B fewer tokens overall. The last line shows why recomputation is mandatory at 32k: activations for one sequence are 85 GiB without it and 5 GiB with it.

For the long-context phase, up-weight long documents and keep the domain share high; the data of the extension phase is where the long policy wordings do their work. Gate: the phase starts only if short-context scores are intact (Gate 4 in stage 8) and ends when long-document retrieval from 32k passes.

6Architecture and tokeniser

The vocabulary is the one choice where the domain matters. The second table shows the trade. A larger vocabulary packs more characters into each token (the characters-per-token column is an assumption; measure it on your own text, chapter 13), so the FLOPs to read 1,000 characters fall from 5.7×1012 to 4.8×1012 across the range, but the embedding grows from 42M to 336M parameters (11% of the model at 131k), and the logits tensor for one 4k sequence grows from 0.27 GB to 2.15 GB in fp32. Beyond 32k to 64k the saving is a few per cent in FLOPs and the cost is in memory at the output. The plan picks 32,768, trained with byte-level fallback on a mixed sample of in-house and general text, with digits split individually so that amounts and policy numbers are consistent. The check is the fertility on held-out in-house documents: characters per token, and the rate of rare tokens, as chapter 13 and the glitch-token case study (case 6) describe.

Comparing across tokenisers. Loss per token is not comparable between different vocabularies. When you compare the from-scratch model with the yardstick (stage 10), compare bits per character of held-out text, or task scores, not perplexity per token.

8De-risk: the ladder, the yardstick and the gates

Before committing 78% of the budget to one run, what do you run, how much does it cost, and what decisions does each result unlock? Draw the schedule.

Compare with a worked answer

Part B of the stage 2 script costs the scaling ladder: nine small runs with the final recipe, three sizes (50M, 100M, 200M parameters) at three token counts (4B, 12B, 36B). They cost 123 GPU-hours, 3% of the budget, and leave 160 GPU-hours of the 7% allowance for ablations: learning rate, batch size, key/value heads, domain share and the tokeniser. Those ablations are small runs too; a ladder that cannot afford them is a ladder that will not be used.

  • What the ladder is for. It checks that the recipe trains stably at small scale (chapter 17 and the debugging studio before this one), fits the loss against N and D for this data (the published constants are for someone else's corpus), and measures utilisation on the real code. It also tells you how far to trust the extrapolation: the main run is 91 times the compute of the largest ladder run, two orders of magnitude, which is the edge of what published fits cover.
  • The gates are decisions, not reviews. Gate 1 (day 1.5): if the ladder points do not sit on a smooth curve, stop and find out why before spending more. Otherwise freeze the size. Gate 2 (day 2.5): the yardstick's scores on the domain evaluation set the target. Gate 3 (day 8): compare held-out loss and gradient statistics with the ladder's prediction; if loss is above it by more than the ladder's noise, investigate before step 100,000. Gate 4 (day 14.8): short-context scores unchanged, so the 32k phase may start.
  • Define the stop conditions in advance. The plan needs a rule such as "if the main run is not within the ladder's predicted band at Gate 3 and the cause is not found in 24 hours, redirect the remaining time to the yardstick model". Deciding in advance is the same discipline as a deployment rollback criterion (deployment strategies).

10Evaluation and the decision rule

How will you decide that the model is good enough to ship, and what would make you ship the yardstick instead? Which numbers are comparable across models with different tokenisers?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Held-out loss in bits per character. Measured on documents held out by policy and claim, not by random document, so the same claim file cannot sit on both sides of the split (leakage). Bits per character is comparable across tokenisers; loss per token is not.
  • Task suite. Few-shot or lightly fine-tuned accuracy on claim classification and field extraction; summaries scored against a rubric by people (judges need calibrating against people first); retrieval of a stated fact from the middle of a 32k document. The suite is written and frozen before the main run starts, with a data card (building a domain benchmark).
  • Contamination. The general text and the in-house text must not contain the evaluation items or their near duplicates (contamination, evaluating a model you trained).
  • Confidence intervals. With a few hundred items per task, differences of a couple of points are noise. Report intervals and compare paired (statistics for evals).
  • Decision rule, written down now. Ship the from-scratch model if it beats the yardstick on at least two of the three task families with non-overlapping intervals, and its short-context scores are intact. Otherwise ship the yardstick, and spend the remaining effort on data quality. The rule is a decision about the organisation's money, so it needs agreement before the result is known.
  • Cost per request, for context. At 2.79B parameters a 24,000-token claim bundle costs about 2 × 2.79×109 × 24,000 = 1.3×1014 FLOPs to read; on a card sustaining an assumed 100 TFLOP/s that is about 1.3 seconds. Reading is cheap; the 18 GiB of cache is what limits concurrency (KV cache, token economics).

The formulas and assumptions in one file planlib.py, imported by every script above

"""planlib.py: the formulas of chapters 16 and 18, and the assumptions of this studio, in one place."""
import numpy as np

# ---- assumptions (every one of these is a choice, not a measurement) ----
GPUS, DAYS = 8, 21                      # the budget: eight H100-class GPUs for three weeks
PEAK = 989e12                           # dense bf16 FLOP/s of an H100 SXM (vendor datasheet, as in chapter 16)
PRICE = 2.5                             # assumed dollars per GPU-hour, only to express the budget in money
MFU = {"phase 1 (4k context)": 0.35, "phase 2 (32k, recompute)": 0.28}
SPLIT = {"scaling ladder and ablations": 0.07, "yardstick run (adapt an open model)": 0.05, "reserve for failures and restarts": 0.10}   # shares of GPU-hours
V = 32_768                              # vocabulary of the domain tokeniser (assumed; stage 6 discusses the choice)

# ---- chapter 18: Besiroglu et al. (2024) refit of the Chinchilla loss, L = E + A/N^a + B/D^b ----
E_, A_, B_, a_, b_ = 1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658

def loss(N, D):
    return E_ + A_ / N ** a_ + B_ / D ** b_

def optimum(C):
    G = (a_ * A_ / (b_ * B_)) ** (1 / (a_ + b_))
    N = G * (C / 6) ** (b_ / (a_ + b_))
    return N, C / (6 * N)

# ---- chapter 16: parameters and FLOPs for a LLaMA-style decoder (GQA, SwiGLU, tied embeddings) ----
def params(C, L, H, KV, F, V=V, tied=True):
    d = C // H
    block = C * C + 2 * C * KV * d + C * C + 3 * C * F + 2 * C
    emb = V * C * (1 if tied else 2)
    return L * block + emb + C, L * block, emb

def flops_per_token(N, L, C, T):
    """Training FLOPs per token: 6N for the weights plus 12 L C T for the attention scores (full, not causal-halved)."""
    return 6 * N + 12 * L * C * T

def kv_bytes_per_token(L, KV, d, bytes_=2):
    return 2 * L * KV * d * bytes_

def gpu_seconds_to_flops(gpu_hours, mfu):
    return gpu_hours * 3600 * PEAK * mfu

  • Asked about tasks, users, context needs and how success is measured, and noticed that the document-length tail matters.
  • Raised adapting an open model as the baseline, priced it against the budget, and made it a gate rather than an afterthought.
  • Converted GPU-time into FLOPs with an explicit MFU range, and held back a reserve and a ladder allowance.
  • Found the compute-optimal size from a fit and then explained the departure from it in terms of data and serving.
  • Counted domain-data passes and used the roughly four-pass limit as a constraint on size.
  • Used the flat bottom of the loss curve to justify a smaller model with a numerical penalty.
  • Priced context length in FLOPs (the attention term), activation memory and KV cache, and chose staged extension over training long throughout.
  • Computed parameters from a shape and checked that the attention, MLP and embedding shares are sensible.
  • Chose grouped-query attention from the cache arithmetic, and justified the vocabulary by embedding cost and characters per token.
  • Checked memory (16 bytes per parameter, sharded; activations; the 32k case needing recomputation) and the time per phase.
  • Planned small runs and decision gates with written stop rules, and showed the extrapolation distance in orders of magnitude.
  • Listed failure modes with early signals and prepared actions, including bad data, overflow and utilisation shortfall, and tied them to the debugging studio.
  • Defined evaluation in advance: held-out by claim, bits per character across tokenisers, intervals, contamination, and a decision rule against the baseline.
  • Said what changes with twice or ten times the budget, a longer context, or a second language.

Key takeaways

  • Start from the budget in FLOPs, with a range for utilisation. Everything else is a choice made inside it.
  • The compute-optimal size is a starting point. Data supply pushes the size up, the serving target pushes it down, and the flat bottom of the loss curve makes the compromise cheap.
  • Context length is a data question and a cost question: look at where the tokens are, price the attention term and the KV cache, and extend context in a short final phase instead of training long throughout.
  • Choose standard components and check them with the accounting of chapter 16: parameters from a shape, 16 bytes per parameter of state, activations with and without recomputation.
  • Spend a small share of the budget on evidence first: a scaling ladder for this data and a yardstick run of an adapted open model. Set gates and written stop rules.
  • Plan for failure before the run: monitoring against the run's own baseline, rollback criteria, a reserve, and a preflight with an injected fault.
  • Decide in advance how success is judged and what you will ship if the plan loses to the baseline.
