Chinchilla: Compute-Optimal Training

By early 2022 the biggest language models had 175 to 530 billion parameters and had all seen roughly 300 billion training tokens. A DeepMind team asked: for a fixed amount of compute, is that the best split between model size and data? Their answer, in Hoffmann et al.'s "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models", was no. They trained over 400 models to measure the trade-off, predicted that a 70-billion-parameter model on 1.4 trillion tokens would beat their own 280-billion-parameter Gopher at the same compute, built it, and it did.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the Chinchilla paper in full (arXiv 2203.15556; the copy I extracted is v1 of 29 March 2022 with a 2023 copyright line) including its appendices on the loss fit and FLOP counting. I read only the abstracts of three related papers: Kaplan et al. (2020), the replication attempt by Besiroglu et al. (2024) and the inference-aware analysis by Sardana et al. (2023 to 2025). From the Llama 3 paper (July 2024) I read the abstract, introduction and scaling-law section. I did not read the Gopher paper (Rae et al., 2021), so every Gopher figure here is as reported in the Chinchilla paper. Three small inconsistencies within the Chinchilla paper: its abstract gives the MMLU average as 67.5% while its text and Table 6 say 67.6%; the abstract says models were trained on "5 to 500 billion tokens" while the introduction says "5B to over 400B"; and Gopher's training compute appears as 5.76 × 1023 FLOPs in the main text and 6.3 × 1023 under a more careful count in an appendix. I quote the main-text figure and flag the difference where it matters.

The pattern in one sentence

For a fixed compute budget there is a best split between how big the model is and how much data it sees, and a model that is too large for its data wastes compute twice: it costs more to train and more to run, for a worse result than a smaller model trained longer.

Context: bigger, but not longer

Kaplan and colleagues' 2020 paper "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" had shown that a language model's loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, dataset size and compute. It also drew a practical conclusion, in the words of its abstract: "the best use of compute is often to train very large models on a modest amount of data and stop well before convergence". The field followed. The Chinchilla paper's Table 1 lists the largest dense models of the time:

The question, stated precisely

Equation 1 of the paper asks for the model size and token count that minimise the final training loss L(N, D) subject to FLOPs(N, D) = C. If loss is a power law in each variable, the optimum is a power law in C as well: Nopt proportional to Ca and Dopt to Cb. Since C = 6ND, the two exponents must add up to 1. Kaplan's results correspond to a = 0.73 and b = 0.27: put most of any new budget into parameters. The question is whether that is right.

The authors answer with three independent methods on the same family of models, all trained on a Gopher-like Transformer and its data, with training runs of fewer than one pass over the data. In all three they train many models of different sizes for different numbers of tokens and fit the result.

  1. Approach 1: fix model sizes, vary training length. Models from 70 million to over 10 billion parameters were each trained with four different schedule lengths, spanning a 16-fold range of tokens. Then, at 1,500 FLOP values, the authors picked whichever run had the lowest loss and fit power laws. Result: a = 0.50, b = 0.50.
  2. Approach 2: IsoFLOP profiles. For nine FLOP budgets from 6 × 1018 to 3 × 1021, train models of many sizes (up to 16 billion parameters) so that each uses exactly that budget, plot the final loss against size, fit a parabola to find the minimum, then fit a power law to the minima. Result: a = 0.49, b = 0.51.
  3. Approach 3: fit a formula. Model all final losses with L(N, D) = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ and minimise under 6ND = C. The three terms are the loss of an ideal model of the data, the penalty for finite model size, and the penalty for finite training. Result: a = 0.46, b = 0.54.

Three roughly agreeing answers, each far from Kaplan's. The paper explains the disagreement. Kaplan et al. used one fixed token count and learning-rate schedule for all models, so a run's intermediate loss, measured part-way through a long cosine decay, over-states how well a shorter run would have done. That biases the comparison toward large models. Hoffmann et al. instead set the cosine learning-rate schedule to decay by 10× over exactly the number of tokens each run was trained on, which their Figure A1 shows gives the best final loss "regardless of model size". They also included models up to 16 billion parameters, while most of Kaplan's runs had fewer than 100 million. The practical rule from their Table 3, using Approach 1: a 1-billion-parameter model should see about 20 billion tokens (20.2), a 10-billion one about 205 billion, a 67-billion one 1.5 trillion, and a 175-billion one about 3.7 trillion. The text of Section 3.4, using a different approach, puts the 175-billion figure at "over 4.2 trillion tokens".

The loss-versus-size valley, from the paper's own formula

The Chinchilla paper prints its fitted constants for Approach 3: E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34 and β = 0.28. They can be used to draw an IsoFLOP curve, and to find the minimum numerically. The script below does that. It also tabulates tokens per parameter for the models in Table 1 and for Llama 3 405B (figures from its paper).

"""Chinchilla's parametric loss (Hoffmann et al. 2022, Eq. 10 in Appendix D.2): L(N,D) = E + A/N^0.34 + B/D^0.28,
minimised on the curve C = 6 N D. Constants as printed in the paper. Brute-force search over model size."""
import numpy as np
E, A, B, alpha, beta = 1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28
L = lambda N, D: E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta

def optimum(C):
    Ns = np.logspace(8, 13, 200001)           # 1e8 .. 1e13 parameters
    Ds = C / (6 * Ns)
    i = np.argmin(L(Ns, Ds))
    return Ns[i], Ds[i], L(Ns[i], Ds[i])

print(f"exponents from Eq.4: a = beta/(alpha+beta) = {beta/(alpha+beta):.3f}, b = alpha/(alpha+beta) = {alpha/(alpha+beta):.3f}  (paper's Approach 3: 0.46, 0.54)")
print(f"\n{'budget C (FLOPs)':>17} {'N_opt':>9} {'D_opt':>10} {'D/N':>6} {'loss':>6}")
for label, C in (("1e21", 1e21), ("GPT-3 3.14e23", 3.14e23), ("Gopher 5.76e23", 5.76e23), ("1e25", 1e25)):
    N, D, l = optimum(C)
    print(f"{label:>17} {N/1e9:8.1f}B {D/1e9:9.0f}B {D/N:6.1f} {l:6.3f}")

print("\nthe same loss formula applied to the two real models:")
for name, N, D in (("Gopher     280B x 300B tokens", 280e9, 300e9), ("Chinchilla  70B x 1.4T tokens", 70e9, 1.4e12)):
    print(f"  {name}: 6ND = {6*N*D:.2e} FLOPs, predicted loss {L(N, D):.3f}")

print("\ntokens per parameter, from the figures printed in each source:")
rows = [("MT-NLG 530B (Table 1)", 530e9, 270e9), ("Gopher 280B (Table 1)", 280e9, 300e9), ("GPT-3 175B (Table 1)", 175e9, 300e9),
        ("LaMDA 137B (Table 1)", 137e9, 168e9), ("Chinchilla 70B (Table 1)", 70e9, 1.4e12), ("Llama 3 405B (its paper)", 405e9, 15.6e12)]
for name, n, d in rows:
    print(f"  {name:26} {d/n:6.1f}   6ND = {6*n*d:.2e} FLOPs")
t3 = [(1e9, 20.2e9), (10e9, 205.1e9), (67e9, 1.5e12), (175e9, 3.7e12), (280e9, 5.9e12), (1e12, 21.2e12)]
print("Approach 1 optimum (Table 3):", ", ".join(f"{n/1e9:g}B -> {d/n:.1f}" for n, d in t3))

Read this output with care, because it shows two things. First, the exponents derived from the printed constants (0.452 and 0.548) match the paper's Approach 3 (0.46 and 0.54), so the constants are consistent with the method. Second, the optimum those constants give at the Gopher budget is about 32 billion parameters and nearly 3 trillion tokens, smaller than the 70 billion that Chinchilla actually used, and about 93 tokens per parameter against the roughly 20 that Approaches 1 and 2 give. The paper says as much: "The third approach predicts even smaller models being optimal at larger compute budgets", and its Figure 4 projects 40 billion parameters from the full fit. The authors chose 70 billion, at the larger end of the 40 to 70 billion range their approaches suggested, "due to both dataset and computational efficiency considerations". This is an honest example of why the three methods are shown together. I come back to this in the replication paragraph below.

Chinchilla itself

To test the prediction the authors trained Chinchilla, a 70-billion-parameter model on 1.4 trillion tokens, "the same compute budget as Gopher" but "4× more data". It used the same architecture and dataset family as Gopher (a Gopher-style Transformer trained on DeepMind's MassiveText, with a different subset mix to accommodate the extra tokens). Their Table 4 gives the differences: Chinchilla has 80 layers, 64 heads and width 8,192 against Gopher's 80 layers, 128 heads and width 16,384; it used a maximum learning rate of 1 × 10-4 (Gopher 4 × 10-5) and a batch of 1.5 million tokens rising to 3 million. There were also minor changes: AdamW instead of Adam, a slightly different SentencePiece tokeniser without NFKC normalisation (94.15% of tokens shared with Gopher's), and bfloat16 forward and backward passes with a float32 copy of the weights in the optimiser state. The runs used TPU v3 and v4 hardware with JAX. See Mixed Precision & Training Stability and Gradient Descent & Optimisers for those choices.

The headline results all come from the paper's Section 4:

On MMLU, Chinchilla beat Gopher on 51 of 57 tasks, matched it on 2 and lost on 4 (college mathematics, econometrics, moral scenarios and formal logic). The paper notes that Chinchilla exceeded the June 2023 expert forecast for state-of-the-art MMLU accuracy (63.4%), and was above 90% on four individual subjects. The authors place more weight on MMLU, BIG-bench and question-answering than on language-modelling perplexity, because a model trained on four times as much web text is more likely to have seen the test sets: "train/test set leakage may artificially enhance the results".

Chinchilla is also four times smaller, so its memory footprint and inference cost are lower. The paper says: "The energy cost of a large language model is amortized through its usage for inference an[d] fine-tuning. The benefits of a more optimally trained smaller model, therefore, extend beyond the immediate benefits of its improved performance." That sentence points at the biggest idea that later work developed.

What the authors said was uncertain

  • Only one large-scale test. "Due to the cost of training large models, we only have two comparable training runs at large scale (Chinchilla and Gopher), and we do not have additional tests at intermediate scales."
  • The power-law assumption bends. They observed some concavity in the optimal size at high budgets, "which suggests that we may still be overestimating the optimal size of large models".
  • Under one epoch only. All analysis runs used fewer than one pass over the data; they flagged repeated data (multiple epochs) as future work.
  • More data means more care. Larger datasets only help "when the data is high-quality", and trillions of tokens bring more toxic, biased and private text, plus more risk of test-set overlap. Chinchilla "does suffer from bias and toxicity" though less affected than Gopher in their tests: on a Winogender coreference test it scored 78.3% against 71.4% for Gopher, and the mean toxicity score of 25,000 unprompted samples was 0.087 against Gopher's 0.081.
  • The data supply becomes the limit. The projected optimum for larger models "is far beyond what is currently used to train large models", which the authors say underscores "the importance of dataset collection". See Pretraining Data.

What later work said

  • A replication attempt found a problem in one of the three methods. Besiroglu, Erdil, Barnett and You (April 2024) tried to replicate Approach 3 by extracting the data from the paper's plots. Their abstract reports that the Approach 3 estimates are inconsistent with the first two approaches, that their fit to the extracted data failed, and that the reported confidence intervals are implausibly narrow (they compute that such intervals would need over 600,000 experiments, while the original work likely ran fewer than 500). When they re-derived the scaling law with their own fit, results agreed with Approaches 1 and 2. I read only the abstract. This is consistent with my script's output above, where the printed constants gave an optimum below what Approaches 1 and 2 suggest, but my calculation does not by itself prove the constants are wrong.
  • Optimal for training is not optimal for use. Sardana, Portes, Doubov and Frankle's paper "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal" (December 2023, ICML 2024) argues that Chinchilla-style laws "neglect to include the cost of inference". If a model will serve a large demand (their illustrative case is around 1 billion requests), a smaller model trained on more data than Chinchilla-optimal costs less overall; they trained 47 models and saw quality still improving at up to 10,000 tokens per parameter. I read only the abstract.

State of play, October 2026

The one dated data point I verified is Llama 3 (Meta, July 2024). Its flagship is a dense Transformer with 405 billion parameters trained on 15.6 trillion tokens, about 38.5 tokens per parameter, using 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs. The paper says its own scaling-law experiments make the 405B size "approximately compute-optimal" for that budget, but also that the smaller models in the family were trained "for much longer than is compute-optimal" because the resulting models "perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget". So current practice differs from "20 tokens per parameter" in the direction Sardana et al. argued. I did not survey reports from 2025 to 2026, and I cannot say what closed-model developers do. Treat the 20-to-1 figure as a historical benchmark for training-only efficiency, not a recipe.

Common misconception

"Chinchilla proved every model should be trained on 20 tokens per parameter." It derived a compute-optimal rule for training loss at a fixed training budget, under the paper's model family, data and assumptions. It says nothing about the cost of serving the model afterwards, and the paper's own caveats note that the true optimum for larger budgets may be smaller than its fits imply. If you will serve a model heavily, the right target is lower total cost, not lowest training loss, and the answer is usually a smaller model trained longer. The related misconception: "bigger models are always better". At fixed compute, too-big models lose.

Analogy: a library with a fixed budget

You have a fixed budget to build a library. You can spend it on the building (model size) or on the books (training tokens). A grand hall with a few shelves of books is impressive and nearly useless; a modest room crammed with an enormous collection can serve readers better. There is a best split, and if you miss it by overspending on the building, you lose in two ways: you paid more and you also pay more to heat it every winter.

Where it breaks down: books can be bought once and read forever, but training tokens are consumed in a single run; parameters keep costing money at serving time, which is why the heating bill (inference) matters so much; and the library analogy hides that book quality matters, a point the paper stresses.

Lessons, and where they connect

  1. Fix the budget, then choose the shape. Write the budget as C = 6ND and pick N and D, not N alone. The calculation is in Parameters, FLOPs & Memory and the planning method in Scaling Laws.
  2. Tune the schedule to the horizon. Much of the disagreement with Kaplan et al. came from not matching the learning-rate decay to the training length. Whenever you compare runs of different length, compare each at its own schedule end.
  3. Cross-check with independent methods. Three approaches agreed on two of three points and visibly disagreed on the third. Report disagreement, and prefer the estimate you can reproduce. See Reading Papers Efficiently.
  4. Include the serving bill. The question changes from "lowest training loss" to "lowest total cost for the quality needed". Tools for that are in The Economics of a Token and The KV Cache & the Cost of Decoding.
  5. Data becomes the scarce input. More tokens need more clean, deduplicated, non-overlapping text, so the work shifts to data pipelines and checking for leakage. Pretraining Data and Contamination, Saturation & Decay.

Systems connection

  • Parallelism, SIMD & GPUs: the FLOPs in C are bought as accelerator-hours; doubling both N and D quadruples C.
  • Designing with ML & LLMs: model size sets serving memory and latency, so the training choice feeds directly into capacity planning.
  • GPUs, Clouds & What Compute Costs: turning a FLOP budget into a bill.

Key takeaways

  • For a fixed compute budget, model size and training tokens should grow together: three methods gave exponents of about 0.5 each, against 0.73 and 0.27 from Kaplan et al.
  • Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) used the same budget as Gopher (280B, 300B tokens) and beat it, for example 67.6% against 60.0% on MMLU, while being four times cheaper to run.
  • Compute-optimal means optimal for training only. Serving cost pushes real models toward more tokens per parameter than about 20; Llama 3 405B used about 38.5, and its smaller siblings far more.
  • Of the three methods, the parametric one is the least reliable according to a later replication attempt; the paper's own numbers show it giving smaller optima than the other two.
  • The authors ran only one large-scale comparison and listed their assumptions; treat the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule of thumb as an approximation within those limits.

C is proportional to the product N × D, so doubling both multiplies it by 2 × 2 = 4. That is why the paper's exponents a and b add to 1: a tenfold budget is split roughly as 3.2× parameters and 3.2× tokens, not 10× in either.

What most plausibly explains why Hoffmann et al.'s exponents differ from Kaplan et al.'s, according to the Chinchilla paper?

  1. Kaplan et al. used a fixed token count and learning-rate schedule for all models, so intermediate losses from long schedules understated how well shorter runs could do, favouring large models
  2. Kaplan et al. used a different programming language
  3. Kaplan et al. trained only on code
  4. The two papers used different definitions of a parameter
The Chinchilla paper's Section 2 gives the schedule explanation and also notes that Kaplan et al.'s runs were mostly under 100 million parameters. The other options are not given as reasons.

Your product will serve one billion requests a month. A colleague says "Chinchilla says we should train 20 tokens per parameter". What is the best reply?

  1. Agreed; that ratio is optimal in every situation
  2. Disagree; Chinchilla shows models should always be as large as possible
  3. Disagree; Chinchilla shows no model should be trained on more than 1.4 trillion tokens
  4. That ratio minimises training loss for a fixed training budget, but heavy serving makes a smaller model trained on more tokens cheaper overall, as later work and Llama 3's smaller models illustrate
The paper optimises for training compute and mentions inference only as an extra benefit of smaller models. Sardana et al. formalise the inference-aware case. There is no 1.4-trillion-token limit in the paper; that is the size of the Chinchilla run.

My script finds a formula-optimal size of about 32B parameters at the Gopher budget, while the real Chinchilla has 70B. What does this show?

  1. The Chinchilla paper contains an arithmetic mistake
  2. The parametric approach predicts smaller optima than the other two, as the paper states, and loss near the optimum is flat, so 70B is close to as good
  3. Chinchilla was trained on too few tokens
  4. The 6ND rule is wrong by a factor of two

The 6ND rule is within 10% of the paper's own FLOP counts for the small models in its appendix table (ratios 0.99 to 1.10), and the discrepancy is in the fit, not the rule.
