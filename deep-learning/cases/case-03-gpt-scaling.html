From GPT-2 to GPT-3

In February 2019 OpenAI described a 1.5-billion-parameter language model that could attempt translation, summarisation and question answering without being trained on any of them, mostly badly. Sixteen months later, a model with 175 billion parameters trained in almost the same way did those tasks far better, and did some (like adding two-digit numbers) that the smaller models essentially could not do at all. This case reads both papers side by side: what changed besides size, what the "in-context learning" result actually shows, and what the authors themselves said they did not understand.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read both primary papers in full: Radford et al., "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners" (OpenAI, 2019; the PDF hosted by OpenAI) and Brown et al., "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (arXiv 2005.14165, version 4, July 2020), including its appendices with per-size results. For the later discussion I fetched and read only the abstracts of five follow-up papers (InstructGPT; Wei et al. on emergent abilities; Schaeffer et al. on whether they are a mirage; Min et al. on demonstrations; Olsson et al. on induction heads), so my statements about them are limited to what those abstracts say. I did not read OpenAI's blog posts or any material about GPT-2's staged release or GPT-3's API launch, so I make no claims about those. All tables of numbers below are from the GPT-3 paper unless marked as mine.

The pattern in one sentence

Keep the recipe fixed and increase scale, and some abilities improve smoothly while others appear to switch on; so measure a whole family of model sizes on the same tasks, because a single large model cannot tell you which kind of ability you are looking at.

Context: the language model as a general task solver

Through 2018, the standard way to build a language-processing system was pre-training and fine-tuning: train a model on lots of unlabelled text, then update its weights on a labelled dataset for one task, such as sentiment or question answering. Brown et al. state the limitation: the architecture is task-agnostic, but "there is still a need for task-specific datasets and task-specific fine-tuning", often thousands to hundreds of thousands of examples for every new task.

GPT-2 proposed a different framing. A language model is trained to predict the next piece of text given what came before (the chain of conditional probabilities in The Language-Modelling Objective). The GPT-2 paper observes that a general system should model p(output | input, task), and that language itself can specify the task: a translation example can be written as a sequence like "translate to french, english text, french text". If the training text contains natural demonstrations of tasks, a good enough predictor of text might learn to do them without any labelled data at all. The paper's abstract claims that language models "begin to learn these tasks without any explicit supervision" when trained on a dataset of millions of web pages it calls WebText.

Zero-shot / one-shot / few-shot
How many worked examples of the task appear in the model's input: none (only an instruction), one, or several (the GPT-3 paper uses 10 to 100 where they fit). The weights never change.
In-context learning
Performing a task from instructions and examples placed in the input text, with no gradient update. The GPT-3 paper uses the term for the "inner loop" of a meta-learning view.
Context window
The maximum number of tokens the model can read at once: 1,024 for GPT-2 and 2,048 for GPT-3.
Petaflop/s-day
A unit of compute: sustaining 1015 floating-point operations per second for one day, about 8.64 × 1019 operations.
Data contamination
Test-set text appearing in the training data, which inflates benchmark scores. See Contamination, Saturation & Decay.

GPT-2: the first evidence, and its limits

GPT-2 was a Transformer decoder in the style described in The Transformer Block, with layer normalisation moved to the input of each sub-block, a vocabulary of 50,257 byte-level byte-pair tokens (the tokeniser in Tokenisation) and a context of 1,024 tokens. The paper trained four sizes, 117 million, 345 million, 762 million and 1,542 million parameters (12 to 48 layers, width 768 to 1,600).

WebText is the interesting design choice. Rather than use a raw web crawl, the authors scraped the outbound links from the social site Reddit that received at least 3 "karma", "a heuristic indicator for whether other users found the link interesting, educational, or just funny". That gave about 45 million links (the version used excludes links created after December 2017); after cleaning, "slightly over 8 million documents for a total of 40 GB of text", with all Wikipedia pages removed so as not to overlap with common test sets. Human curation by proxy was the quality filter.

What did it do, with no fine-tuning and no examples? The paper's results are a mix:

It also reached state-of-the-art on 7 of the 8 language-modelling datasets tested zero-shot, and performance rose steadily across all four sizes: "The capacity of the language model is essential to the success of zero-shot task transfer and increasing it improves performance in a log-linear fashion across tasks." But on most real tasks it was weak, which is why the GPT-3 paper later called the approach "far inferior to fine-tuning". The GPT-2 paper also did a careful overlap check: eight-word sequences from each test set were looked up in WebText with Bloom filters, finding on average 3.2% overlap, against 5.9% between standard training and test splits of the same datasets.

GPT-3: change the scale, keep the recipe

The GPT-3 paper makes its design explicit: "Our basic pre-training approach, including model, data, and training, is similar to the process described in [GPT-2], with relatively straightforward scaling up of the model size, dataset size and diversity, and length of training." The main architectural difference is that layers alternate between dense and locally banded sparse attention, as in the Sparse Transformer. What changed is mostly numbers:

GPT-3's training data was a weighted mixture. The paper filtered Common Crawl (45 TB of compressed text before filtering, 570 GB after, "roughly equivalent to 400 billion byte-pair-encoded tokens") by similarity to high-quality reference corpora, removed near-duplicates, and mixed it with an expanded WebText, two book corpora and Wikipedia. Quality sources were deliberately over-sampled:

The authors say this "essentially accepts a small amount of overfitting in exchange for higher quality training data". All eight GPT-3 models, from 125 million to 175 billion parameters, were trained on the same 300 billion tokens, on V100 GPUs in a cluster provided by Microsoft, using a mixture of model parallelism within each matrix multiply and across layers. How the pieces of data are chosen and cleaned is the subject of Pretraining Data.

The rule matches the paper's table to three figures for every model, so the paper's "3,640 petaflop/s-days" for GPT-3 (3.14 × 1023 FLOPs) is simply 6 × 175 billion × 300 billion. The last column is the one to remember for the next case: GPT-3 175B saw only 1.7 tokens per parameter. The paper deliberately followed Kaplan et al.'s 2020 scaling laws in training very large models on comparatively few tokens, and its Figure 2.2 notes that GPT-3 "3B" and RoBERTa-Large, which is ten times smaller, took similar compute. Whether that was the right split is the question Chinchilla answers.

In-context learning: the surprise

The GPT-3 paper evaluates every task in three conditions, defined in its Section 2. In zero-shot the model gets a natural-language instruction only. In one-shot it also sees one demonstration. In few-shot it sees as many demonstrations as fit in the 2,048-token window, typically 10 to 100. "We emphasize that these 'learning' curves involve no gradient updates or fine-tuning, just increasing numbers of demonstrations given as conditioning." Fine-tuning is the conventional alternative, which the paper did not run.

The headline results are in the paper's Section 3. A selection, with the paper's few-shot numbers set against the best fine-tuned system it cites:

(All cells are copied from the paper's tables, including Appendix H for CoQA's fine-tuned reference; the paper says GPT-3's few-shot TriviaQA is state of the art against fine-tuned models in the same closed-book setting.) Three patterns stand out. First, few-shot beat zero-shot on almost everything, which is the evidence for the title. Second, GPT-3 did well on tasks that look like text continuation (LAMBADA, TriviaQA, translation into English) and poorly on tasks that compare two pieces of text. The authors report that on WiC, a word-sense comparison task, few-shot accuracy was 49.4%, "at random chance"; they also name ANLI, RACE and QuAC as tasks where it struggled. They suggest the one-directional design may be a cause. Third, on SuperGLUE they note that GPT-3 needed fewer than eight examples per task to beat a fine-tuned BERT-Large on the overall score, with 32 examples per task used for the table.

The news-writing experiment was the result most quoted outside research. Participants drawn from the US population were shown short news articles of about 200 words and asked whether a model had written them. Accuracy was 86% for a deliberately poor control model and fell as models grew, to about 52% for GPT-3 175B (95% confidence interval 49% to 54%, chance being 50%). For longer articles of around 500 words the figures were about 88% for the control model and again about 52% for GPT-3. The authors say "human abilities to detect model generated text appear to decrease as model size increases", and note that factual slips, repetition and unusual phrasings were the cues that remained.

Smooth curves and sudden ones

Appendix H of the GPT-3 paper lists every task for all eight sizes. Two rows make the central observation about scale, plotted below. Closed-book question answering on TriviaQA improves smoothly with size. Two-digit addition looks different: the first six models are under 12% accurate, the 13-billion model is at 55.5%, and the 175-billion model is at 100%.

The paper itself describes this as "a significant jump from the second largest model (GPT-3 13B) to the largest model", and the authors checked whether it was memorisation: of 2,000 three-digit addition problems only 17 (0.8%) appeared verbatim in the training data in the forms they searched for, and the wrong answers often looked like computation errors such as not carrying a 1. (A literal string search is a limited test, and the paper says so about contamination checks generally.)

A toy to run: what a prompt can and cannot specify

A real language model is not something to run here, but the logic of the few-shot setup can be shown with a deliberately simple program. The first part below is not a language model: it holds a fixed list of "skills" (stand-ins for what pre-training put into the weights), and demonstrations only rule skills out. It shows two things the paper discusses: why one example can be ambiguous, and why a few examples remove the ambiguity without any training. The second part is the induction pattern [A][B] ... [A] → [B], written as a plain lookup rule.

"""A sketch (NOT a language model) of why a few demonstrations can specify a task without any weight update.
The 'learner' holds fixed skills (a list of functions, standing in for what pre-training put in the weights).
Demonstrations only rule skills out; nothing is trained."""
import random

SKILLS = {
    "identity": lambda w: w,
    "uppercase": lambda w: w.upper(),
    "reverse": lambda w: w[::-1],
    "first letter": lambda w: w[0],
    "last letter": lambda w: w[-1],
    "add 'ly'": lambda w: w + "ly",
    "double": lambda w: w + w,
}
WORDS = ["noon", "level", "stone", "river", "plant", "cloud", "mirror", "tiger", "radar", "bread", "light", "table",
         "grape", "ocean", "sugar", "cabin", "eagle", "flute", "honey", "pearl"]

def predict(demos, query):
    alive = [n for n, f in SKILLS.items() if all(f(x) == y for x, y in demos)]
    return alive, (SKILLS[alive[0]](query) if alive else "?")      # ties broken by list order

random.seed(0)
print("target skill: reverse.  200 random trials per K.")
print(f"{'K demos':>8} {'skills still possible':>22} {'accuracy':>9}")
for k in range(0, 6):
    ok, alive_n = 0, 0
    for _ in range(200):
        xs = random.sample(WORDS, k + 1)
        demos = [(x, SKILLS["reverse"](x)) for x in xs[:k]]
        alive, guess = predict(demos, xs[-1])
        ok += guess == SKILLS["reverse"](xs[-1]); alive_n += len(alive)
    print(f"{k:8d} {alive_n/200:22.2f} {ok/200:9.0%}")

demos = [("noon", "noon")]
print("\none palindrome demo, noon => noon, leaves:", predict(demos, "stone")[0])

# Part 2: the induction pattern [A][B] ... [A] -> [B] (Olsson et al., 2022), as a lookup rather than a trained head.
def induct(tokens):
    cur = tokens[-1]
    for i in range(len(tokens) - 2, -1, -1):        # most recent earlier occurrence of the current token
        if tokens[i] == cur:
            return tokens[i + 1]
    return "?"                                      # no earlier match: this rule has nothing to say

stream = "sea mer sun soleil cat chat sun".split()
print("\ninduction rule on:", " ".join(stream), "->", induct(stream))
print("induction rule on a word not seen before:", " ".join(stream[:-1] + ["moon"]), "->", induct(stream[:-1] + ["moon"]))
target skill: reverse.  200 random trials per K.

       0                   7.00       14%
       1                   1.18       84%
       2                   1.01       99%
       3                   1.00      100%
       4                   1.00      100%
       5                   1.00      100%

one palindrome demo, noon => noon, leaves: ['identity', 'reverse']

induction rule on: sea mer sun soleil cat chat sun -> soleil
induction rule on a word not seen before: sea mer sun soleil cat chat moon -> ?

With no demonstration the toy can only guess among its seven skills (14% accuracy). One demonstration leaves 1.18 candidates on average and 84% accuracy, because some words are palindromes: "noon" reversed is still "noon", which fits identity as well as reverse. Three demonstrations give certainty. This is the logic behind the paper's observation that more shots help. The induction rule completes "sun" correctly, because the pair appeared earlier in the context, but has nothing to say about "moon". A real model trained on text has to supply the skill (translating, reversing, answering) from its weights as well as copying from the context.

What they claimed, what the authors doubted, and what later work said

  • The authors flagged what they did not know. Section 5 says it is unclear whether few-shot learning "actually learns new tasks 'from scratch' at inference time, or if it simply [recognises] and identifies tasks that it has learned during training", placing tasks on a spectrum: made-up tasks like word scrambling look more like learning, translation "clearly must be learned during pretraining". The paper also lists limits: weak performance on some comparison tasks, a pre-training objective that "weights every token equally", poor sample efficiency (far more text than a human sees), and models "both expensive and inconvenient to perform inference on".
  • Contamination was real and admitted. The authors write that "a bug in the filtering caused us to ignore some overlaps, and due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model". Section 4 builds clean subsets of each benchmark. A quarter of benchmarks scored over 50% flagged as potentially contaminated yet performance changed only negligibly; PIQA (29% flagged, 3 points lower on the clean subset) and Winograd were marked with asterisks. The paper's conclusion is cautious: either its method over-estimated contamination or it has little effect. Later practice built on this; see Contamination, Saturation & Decay.
  • What demonstrations do is disputed. Min and colleagues (2022) report that randomly replacing the labels in demonstrations "barely hurts" performance on classification and multiple-choice tasks across 12 models including GPT-3, and argue that demonstrations mainly convey the label space, the input distribution and the format. Olsson and colleagues (2022) present "preliminary, indirect evidence" that induction heads, attention heads that complete [A][B] ... [A] → [B], may be the main mechanism of in-context learning, with causal evidence for small attention-only models and correlational evidence for larger ones. (I read only the abstracts of both papers.)
  • Are the sudden jumps real? Wei and colleagues (2022) define an ability as emergent if "not present in smaller models but is present in larger models", unpredictable from smaller-model trends. Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) argue that apparent emergence may come from "the researcher's choice of metric": discontinuous metrics make smooth progress look like a jump. The addition row above is scored by exact match, which is such a metric. Neither claim is settled by the GPT-3 paper, which reports the jump and does not claim a mechanism.
  • Size alone did not make a helpful assistant. The InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al., 2022) opens by saying that making language models larger "does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent", and reports that human raters preferred outputs from a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT over the 175-billion GPT-3, about 100 times larger. See InstructGPT: RLHF in Practice and Supervised Fine-Tuning.

Common misconception

"In-context learning means the model trains itself on your examples." No weights change. The examples are just text in the input; remove them and the "learning" is gone on the next request. What the paper shows is that the forward pass of a large pre-trained model can use examples in its context to behave differently. Whether that is learning a new task or recognising one already known is, in the authors' words, an open question.

Analogy: a well-read new colleague and a pile of filled-in forms

Hand a widely read new colleague three completed forms and a blank one, and they usually fill in the blank correctly: they have seen enough paperwork to recognise the pattern and the house style. You did not retrain them; you showed them what you want, and they supplied the skill. A less experienced person given the same forms would copy superficially or ask what the fields mean.

Where it breaks down: a colleague remembers the forms tomorrow and the model does not, because the examples live only in the current context window. The colleague also tells you when they are guessing, and the model can fluently fill in a form it has not understood.

Lessons, and where they connect

  1. Train a family, not one model. The paper's most valuable choice is eight sizes on identical data. It is what lets you separate smooth trends from jumps, and what a team planning a training run should copy at small scale: Scaling Laws.
  2. Treat the prompt as part of the system. Few-shot examples cost context and latency on every request, and results moved with phrasing: the authors tried many formulations for WiC without success. Practice in Decoding Strategies and the evaluation habits of the evals course.
  3. Count tokens and compute before you scale. The 6ND check above takes a minute and exposes the tokens-per-parameter choice that the next case shows to be suboptimal. Parameters, FLOPs & Memory.
  4. Take contamination seriously from the start. A filtering bug and a model too costly to retrain is a common shape of failure; leave time and budget to audit the benchmark you will report.
  5. A capable base model is not an assistant. The gap that InstructGPT addressed is why the next course separates base, chat and reasoning models.

Systems connection

  • Parallelism, SIMD & GPUs: why 3.14 × 1023 FLOPs needs a large cluster, and the model parallelism the paper used.
  • Designing with ML & LLMs: a prompt with 32 examples is a cost and latency decision in a production design.
  • the threat-modelling chapter: the paper's section on misuse (spam, phishing, misinformation) is an early threat model for text generators.

Key takeaways

  • GPT-3 was GPT-2's recipe scaled: 175 billion parameters, 300 billion training tokens, 3.14 × 1023 FLOPs (6ND reproduces the paper's table).
  • The new capability was in-context learning: tasks specified by instructions and examples in the prompt, with no gradient updates. Few-shot beat zero-shot on most tasks and sometimes approached fine-tuned systems.
  • Some skills (TriviaQA) improved smoothly with size, while others (two-digit addition) were near zero until 13 billion parameters and then jumped; whether the jump is real or a metric artefact is debated.
  • The authors stated what they did not know: learning versus recognising tasks, weak comparison tasks, and contamination from a filtering bug they could not afford to fix.
  • Tokens per parameter was just 1.7. That choice is the opening for the next case.

Check your understanding

GPT-3 175B was trained on 300 billion tokens. Using C = 6ND, roughly how much compute did that take, and how does the paper's Table D.1 compare?

  1. About 3 × 1021 FLOPs; the paper agrees
  2. About 3 × 1023 FLOPs; the paper's 3.14 × 1023 agrees
  3. About 3 × 1025 FLOPs; the paper's figure is ten times lower
  4. It cannot be estimated without knowing the GPU model
6 × 1.746 × 1011 × 3 × 1011 is about 3.1 × 1023. The rule counts total operations, independent of the hardware, which only affects how long it takes.

A colleague says a few-shot prompt "fine-tunes GPT-3 on the examples for that request". What is the most accurate response?

  1. Correct, but only for the last layer
  2. Correct, which is why few-shot is expensive
  3. Incorrect, because the examples are used to update a small adapter
  4. Incorrect: no weights change; the examples are only input text, so they must be sent again with every request
The paper stresses "no gradient updates or fine-tuning". The cost is context window and per-request tokens, not training.

Few-shot accuracy for two-digit addition across the eight GPT-3 sizes is 2.0, 4.1, 3.5, 4.5, 8.9, 11.9, 55.5 and 100 percent. A sceptic says the "jump" may be an artefact. Which is the most relevant observation?

  1. The task is scored by exact match, a metric that gives no credit for partly correct answers and can make smooth progress look abrupt
  2. The models were trained on different amounts of data
  3. Addition is not a language task
  4. The test used only five examples
Schaeffer et al.'s argument concerns discontinuous metrics. Option (b) is wrong: all eight models saw the same 300 billion tokens. The paper used 2,000 problems per task, not five.

What does the paper's contamination analysis conclude overall?

  1. Contamination invalidates most results, so GPT-3 should be retrained
  2. There was no overlap between the training data and any benchmark
  3. Despite a filtering bug, performance on most benchmarks changed negligibly on clean subsets, while a few (such as PIQA and Winograd) were flagged with asterisks
  4. Only translation was affected
The authors admit the bug and that retraining was infeasible; their clean-subset comparison showed small differences for most tasks, with some flagged. They do not claim zero overlap, and the effects were not limited to translation.

According to the GPT-3 paper's Section 5, what remains unclear about few-shot learning?

  1. Whether few-shot prompts fit in the context window
  2. Whether the model learns new tasks from scratch at inference time or recognises tasks it saw during pre-training
  3. Whether more examples are always better than fewer
  4. Whether GPT-3 can be fine-tuned at all
The paper says the question spans a spectrum from recognising a familiar task to learning de novo, varying by task. The window limit (2,048 tokens) is known, the paper reports that larger K is "usually but not always better", and it says fine-tuning is possible in principle.
