Attention Is All You Need

In 2017 eight researchers at Google published a translation paper whose model, the Transformer, threw out the recurrent layers that every strong translation system used and kept only attention. It scored higher on two standard translation tests while needing a fraction of the training compute. This case reads Vaswani et al.'s paper closely: why recurrence was the bottleneck, what replaced it, which numbers the paper actually reports, and which parts of its reputation the paper itself does not support.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the paper in full from arXiv (1706.03762, version 7, August 2023; the original was submitted on 12 June 2017 and appeared at NIPS 2017) and the arXiv listing page. For the later-adoption claim I fetched and read the Llama 3 paper (arXiv 2407.21783, July 2024), which says its architecture is a "standard, dense Transformer". I did not read the earlier attention paper by Bahdanau et al. (2014), the convolutional translation papers it compares with, or any account of how the work was done beyond the contribution note printed in the paper. Two inconsistencies inside the paper are worth knowing: the abstract and Table 2 give 41.8 BLEU for English-to-French, while the text of Section 6.1 says 41.0; and my own count of the base model's parameters (63.1 million) is a little below the 65 million in Table 3, probably because of vocabulary or bias details the paper does not give. The course-index blurb says the architecture "now runs nearly every frontier model"; I can confirm that only for published models like Llama 3, not for closed ones (see the dated box below).

The pattern in one sentence

When the thing that limits a model is how much of its computation must happen one step after another, redesign it so that each layer does its work for every position at once, and accept a more expensive operation (here, comparing every token with every other) in exchange for being able to use parallel hardware fully.

Context: translation, recurrence and a bottleneck

Machine translation turns a sentence in one language into a sentence in another. In 2017 the dominant approach was the encoder-decoder model: an encoder reads the source words and builds a representation, and a decoder writes the target words one at a time, each time looking at what it has already written. Both halves were usually recurrent neural networks (RNNs, or their gated variants LSTM and GRU), which process a sequence by carrying a hidden state from one position to the next. Sequences & Recurrent Networks builds one and shows where it strains.

The paper's introduction names the strain precisely. A recurrent model computes a hidden state ht from the previous state ht-1 and the input at position t, so its computation is "aligned" with positions in time. "This inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples, which becomes critical at longer sequence lengths, as memory constraints limit batching across examples." In plain terms: to compute step 40 you must first finish step 39, so a GPU with thousands of idle arithmetic units cannot be given step 40 early. The authors note that tricks such as factorised layers and conditional computation had improved efficiency, "the fundamental constraint of sequential computation, however, remains".

Attention had already entered these models. The paper points out that attention lets a model relate positions "without regard to their distance in the input or output sequences", but that in all but a few cases it was used together with a recurrent network. Other groups had tried to remove recurrence with convolutions (the paper names the Extended Neural GPU, ByteNet and ConvS2S), which compute all positions in parallel. Their weakness, the paper says, is that the number of operations needed to relate two distant positions grows with the distance, linearly for ConvS2S and logarithmically for ByteNet.

Sequence transduction
Mapping one sequence to another, such as a French sentence to an English one. The paper's term for translation, parsing and similar tasks.
Self-attention
Attention in which queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence, so every position can read every other. See Attention from First Principles.
Multi-head attention
Several attention operations run side by side on different learned projections, then concatenated.
BLEU
A score from 0 to 100 for translation quality, based on word and phrase overlap with a human reference. Higher is better; differences of one or two points are noticeable, and it is a rough proxy for quality, not a measure of it.
Maximum path length
The number of steps a signal must travel between any two positions. Short paths make long-range dependencies easier to learn.

The idea, and the paper's own accounting

The paper states the proposal in its introduction: a model "eschewing recurrence and instead relying entirely on an attention mechanism to draw global dependencies between input and output", which "allows for significantly more parallelization". Its Section 4 ("Why Self-Attention") justifies the choice against recurrent and convolutional layers on three criteria: total computation per layer, the amount that can be parallelised (the minimum number of sequential operations), and the maximum path length between positions. Table 1 of the paper summarises it, with n the sequence length and d the representation width:

Layer typeComputation per layerSequential operationsMaximum path length
Self-attentionO(n2 · d)O(1)O(1)
RecurrentO(n · d2)O(n)O(n)
Convolutional (kernel k)O(k · n · d2)O(1)O(logk n)
Self-attention, restricted to a neighbourhood rO(r · n · d)O(1)O(n / r)

Read the second column honestly. Self-attention does more arithmetic than recurrence once the sequence is long, because it forms an n × n grid of pairwise scores. The paper says so: self-attention layers are cheaper than recurrent layers "when the sequence length n is smaller than the representation dimensionality d, which is most often the case" for sentence-level translation. The trade is that the extra work is a handful of large matrix multiplications that parallelise perfectly, while the recurrent model's smaller work is a chain that cannot. The paper's last column is the other gain: any two positions are one attention step apart, so a signal does not have to survive n steps of a recurrent state.

The architecture

The Transformer keeps the encoder-decoder shape. The paper's base model uses six identical layers in each stack, a model width dmodel = 512, a feed-forward width of 2,048, and h = 8 attention heads of size dk = dv = 64. Each encoder layer has two sub-layers (multi-head self-attention, then a position-wise feed-forward network). Each decoder layer has three: masked self-attention, attention over the encoder's output, and the feed-forward network. Around every sub-layer sits a residual connection followed by layer normalisation, LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)), and every sub-layer outputs width 512 so that the sum is possible. The Transformer Block assembles and tests exactly this structure.

The attention operation is Equation 1 of the paper, Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / √dk) V. The paper credits the √dk divisor to a specific problem and gives a footnote: if the components of a query and key are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance dk. Large dot products push the softmax into regions with tiny gradients. The authors write that they "suspect" this is why unscaled dot-product attention was beaten by additive attention for large dk. Attention from First Principles derives it; the toy below checks the footnote numerically.

Two further choices are easy to miss. First, because nothing in attention knows token order, the paper adds positional encodings, fixed sine and cosine waves of different wavelengths, to the embeddings. It tried learned position embeddings too and found "nearly identical results" (row E of its Table 3), choosing sinusoids because they might extrapolate to longer sequences. See Positional Information for what replaced both. Second, the paper uses a bespoke learning-rate schedule (linear warm-up for 4,000 steps, then decay proportional to the inverse square root of the step number) with the Adam optimiser (β2 = 0.98), label smoothing of 0.1 and dropout of 0.1 on the base model. These are not incidental: Gradient Descent & Optimisers and Mixed Precision & Training Stability explain why warm-up matters for this architecture.

What the paper reports

The paper trained on the standard WMT 2014 data: about 4.5 million English-German sentence pairs, using a shared byte-pair vocabulary of about 37,000 tokens, and 36 million English-French pairs with a 32,000 word-piece vocabulary. Batches held roughly 25,000 source tokens and 25,000 target tokens. All training ran on one machine with eight NVIDIA P100 GPUs: the base model took about 0.4 seconds per step for 100,000 steps (12 hours), the "big" model 1.0 second per step for 300,000 steps (3.5 days).

Table 2 of the paper gives BLEU and estimated training cost. The authors estimate cost by multiplying training time, number of GPUs and an assumed sustained single-precision speed per GPU (their footnote 5 uses 9.5 TFLOPS for a P100); it is an estimate, not a measured count. Selected rows:

The claims follow from the table. On English-to-German the big model beat every earlier system, including ensembles, by more than 2 BLEU (28.4 against 26.36). The base model, trained in 12 hours, already beat all of them while costing 3.3 × 1018 FLOPs, about 3 times less than the cheapest competitor listed (ConvS2S at 9.6 × 1018). On English-to-French the big model was a new single-model best, though the base model scored lower than every earlier system in the table (38.1). The paper reports that the big model cost less than a quarter of the previous best single model's training compute. Note that the English-French headline is closer than the English-German one: 41.8 against 41.29 for the best ensemble.

Which parts mattered: the ablations

Section 6.2 and Table 3 vary the base model one factor at a time and report BLEU on the development set (newstest2013), where the base model scores 25.8 with 65 million parameters. A few findings are useful beyond translation:

  • Heads. A single attention head (keeping computation constant) scored 24.9, which the paper puts at 0.9 BLEU worse than the best setting; 8 and 16 heads scored 25.8 and 32 heads fell back to 25.4. Quality "drops off with too many heads".
  • Key size. Reducing dk to 16 or 32 hurt (25.1 and 25.4), which the authors take to suggest that judging compatibility "is not easy".
  • Size and regularisation. Bigger helped (dmodel 1,024 gave 26.0; feed-forward width 4,096 gave 26.2), dropout was "very helpful" (0.0 gave 24.6), and label smoothing of 0.0 gave 25.3 against 25.8.
  • Positions. Learned position embeddings scored 25.7, practically the same as sinusoids.

The paper also tried English constituency parsing (predicting the grammatical tree of a sentence) with a four-layer Transformer of width 1,024, with almost no tuning. On the Wall Street Journal test set it scored 91.3 F1 trained on the WSJ's roughly 40,000 sentences alone and 92.7 F1 with a larger semi-supervised corpus, beating the Berkeley Parser (90.4) even in the small-data setting but falling short of a recurrent neural network grammar at 93.3. This is the paper's evidence that the model is not translation-specific, and it is modest evidence: one extra task.

The authors' arithmetic, checked

Two scripts check the paper's own numbers. The first reruns the cost estimate from the footnote and counts parameters from the architecture (my count, with biases, layer-norm parameters, a shared embedding matrix and a 37,000-token vocabulary). It then tabulates Table 1 for d = 512.

"""Check the Transformer paper's own arithmetic: training FLOPs (Sec 6.1 + footnote 5), parameters (Table 3), and Table 1's layer costs."""
P100_TFLOPS = 9.5                                   # footnote 5
def train_flops(days=None, hours=None, gpus=8):
    seconds = (days or 0) * 86400 + (hours or 0) * 3600
    return seconds * gpus * P100_TFLOPS * 1e12
print(f"base: 12 h x 8 GPUs x 9.5 TFLOPS = {train_flops(hours=12):.2e} FLOPs   (Table 2 says 3.3e18)")
print(f"big : 3.5 d x 8 GPUs x 9.5 TFLOPS = {train_flops(days=3.5):.2e} FLOPs   (Table 2 says 2.3e19)")

def params(d_model, d_ff, n_layers, vocab, shared_embeddings=True):
    attn = 4 * d_model * (d_model + 1)                # Wq,Wk,Wv,Wo with biases
    ffn = d_model * d_ff + d_ff + d_ff * d_model + d_model
    ln = 2 * d_model
    enc = attn + ffn + 2 * ln
    dec = 2 * attn + ffn + 3 * ln
    emb = vocab * d_model
    return n_layers * (enc + dec) + emb * (1 if shared_embeddings else 3)
for label, d, ff, v, claim in (("base", 512, 2048, 37000, 65), ("big", 1024, 4096, 37000, 213)):
    print(f"{label}: my count {params(d, ff, 6, v)/1e6:.1f}M parameters   (Table 3 says {claim}M)")

print("\nTable 1 per layer, d = 512 (operation counts up to constants):")
print(f"{'n':>6} {'self-attn n^2*d':>16} {'recurrent n*d^2':>16} {'cheaper':>10}   sequential steps: attn 1, RNN n")
d = 512
for n in (32, 128, 512, 2048, 8192):
    a, r = n * n * d, n * d * d
    print(f"{n:6d} {a:16.2e} {r:16.2e} {('equal' if a == r else 'attention' if a < r else 'recurrent'):>10}")
base: 12 h x 8 GPUs x 9.5 TFLOPS = 3.28e+18 FLOPs   (Table 2 says 3.3e18)
big : 3.5 d x 8 GPUs x 9.5 TFLOPS = 2.30e+19 FLOPs   (Table 2 says 2.3e19)
base: my count 63.1M parameters   (Table 3 says 65M)
big: my count 214.2M parameters   (Table 3 says 213M)

    32         5.24e+05         8.39e+06  attention

The two FLOP estimates match the paper's Table 2 exactly, confirming that the "training cost" is training time × eight GPUs × an assumed 9.5 TFLOPS and nothing more. The parameter counts are within 3% of the table (63.1 million against 65 million; 214.2 million against 213 million), which I attribute to unstated details, not an error in the paper. The last table shows the paper's caveat concretely: with d = 512, attention is cheaper than a recurrent layer up to a sequence length of 512 and costs more beyond it. The cost of attention growing with the square of length is the problem that Efficient Attention and Beyond Attention take up, and the paper itself proposes restricted neighbourhood attention as future work.

The second script reproduces the scaling footnote with random vectors.

"""Check footnote 4 of Vaswani et al. (2017): dot products of unit-variance vectors have variance d_k, so softmax saturates unless scaled."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def softmax(x): e = np.exp(x - x.max(-1, keepdims=True)); return e / e.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
print(f"{'d_k':>5} {'std of q.k':>11} {'max weight, unscaled':>21} {'max weight, scaled':>19} {'softmax grad scale, unscaled':>29}")
for dk in (4, 16, 64, 256, 1024):
    q = rng.normal(size=(2000, dk)); K = rng.normal(size=(2000, 10, dk))      # 2000 queries, 10 keys each
    s = np.einsum("nd,nkd->nk", q, K)
    p_raw, p_sc = softmax(s), softmax(s / np.sqrt(dk))
    grad = (p_raw * (1 - p_raw)).max(-1).mean()                                # largest diagonal entry of softmax Jacobian
    print(f"{dk:5d} {s.std():11.2f} {p_raw.max(-1).mean():21.3f} {p_sc.max(-1).mean():19.3f} {grad:29.4f}")

The standard deviation of the dot product tracks √dk (2.0 at dk = 4, 32.0 at 1,024). Without scaling, the largest attention weight over ten keys is 0.50 at dk = 4 and 0.97 at 1,024, so the softmax is nearly one-hot, and its largest gradient entry falls from 0.21 to 0.023. With the √dk divisor the largest weight sits at about 0.32 for every size. This is a numerical demonstration of the footnote's argument, not a replication of the paper's experiments, which compared additive and scaled dot-product attention in the full model.

What the critics and later work said

  • The title is an overstatement, by the paper's own architecture. The model contains feed-forward layers, residual connections, normalisation and embeddings alongside attention. In the paper's own sizes each feed-forward sub-layer has about 2.1 million weights against about 1.05 million for the attention projections (my count), so most of a layer's weights are not in attention; The Transformer Block counts them. The title is a slogan about recurrence and convolution, not about the whole network.
  • Attention was not new. The paper's reference list includes Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio's 2014 translation model that already attended over encoder states. What the paper changed was using attention for everything, including within each sequence.
  • The quadratic cost became the main limitation. The authors flagged it themselves in Section 4 and the conclusion. Long-context models since have relied on techniques that change how the grid is computed or stored, such as the memory-saving kernels in FlashAttention.
  • The decoder generates one token at a time. The Transformer parallelises training, where the whole target is known and the mask hides the future, but generation is still sequential. The conclusion lists "making generation less sequential" as a goal. See Decoding Strategies and the serving chapters of the next course.
  • Several details were later replaced. According to this course's table in The Transformer Block ("What changed since 2017"), post-norm gave way to pre-norm, ReLU feed-forwards to gated ones and fixed sinusoids to rotary positions. The Llama 3 paper, for instance, describes "a standard, dense Transformer architecture" with grouped-query attention using 8 key-value heads and a RoPE base frequency raised to 500,000.

State of play, October 2026

The one dated fact I verified is from the Llama 3 paper (July 2024): its flagship is "a dense Transformer with 405B parameters", trained on 15.6 trillion tokens, and the paper states that it "does not deviate significantly" from earlier Llama versions in architecture, apart from grouped-query attention, a document mask and a larger RoPE base. That supports "the Transformer is still the base design" for at least one major open-weights family. I could not verify the architecture of closed frontier models, and the blurb's phrase "nearly every" is therefore a claim about the published literature, not something I can confirm. The alternatives (state-space models, linear attention, hybrids, mixtures of experts) are covered in Beyond Attention and Mixture of Experts, and are changing quickly; check recent model cards before generalising.

Common misconception

"The Transformer made models faster to run." Not in general. The paper's speed gain is in training: every position of a training example is processed in parallel. At generation time a decoder still emits one token per step, attention over a long prefix costs more as the prefix grows, and the key-value cache that avoids recomputation uses memory proportional to the context. The 12-hour and 3.5-day figures are training times on eight GPUs, not inference latencies.

Analogy: reading a page with a highlighter

A recurrent model reads like someone passing a note along a line of people, each person whispering what they remember to the next. A Transformer reads like a team given the whole page at once, each member highlighting the words they think are relevant to a particular word, all at the same time, then pooling highlights. The second team finishes sooner because nobody waits for anyone else.

Where it breaks down: the team has to compare every word with every other word, so doubling the page length quadruples the comparisons. And the team has no sense of word order unless someone writes page numbers next to each word, which is the job of positional encodings.

Lessons, and where they connect

  1. Design for the hardware's strength. GPUs reward large, regular matrix multiplications. The paper's central move was to turn a chain into a matrix product. The reasoning is in Parallelism, SIMD & GPUs.
  2. Count the bill, including the part that gets worse. Table 1 is the model of an honest comparison: it lists the cost that grows (n2) next to the costs that shrink. Practise the same accounting with Parameters, FLOPs & Memory.
  3. Report cost with quality. Putting estimated FLOPs beside BLEU made the claim "better and cheaper" checkable, and the footnote stating the assumed GPU speed made the estimate reproducible, as the script above shows.
  4. Test generality, even weakly. The parsing result is small but it prevents the claim from being "a translation trick". Later chapters build on that generality: Building a GPT uses only the decoder half.
  5. Read the hedges. "We suspect" (the scaling factor), "nearly identical" (positions), and the footnoted estimate method all signal what is evidence and what is intuition. See Reading Papers Efficiently.

Systems connection

  • Parallelism, SIMD & GPUs: Amdahl's law is the formal version of "the sequential part limits you"; recurrence is a long serial fraction.
  • Performance Engineering: the paper reports step times (0.4 s and 1.0 s) and batch sizes in tokens, the numbers you would measure to find a bottleneck.
  • GPUs, Clouds & What Compute Costs: turning the paper's FLOP estimates into a bill.

Key takeaways

  • The Transformer replaced recurrence with self-attention so every position in a training example is processed in parallel; the paper reports 28.4 BLEU (English-German) and 41.8 BLEU (English-French) for the big model on 8 P100 GPUs.
  • The base model reached 27.3 BLEU on English-German in 12 hours for an estimated 3.3 × 1018 FLOPs, beating all earlier systems in the paper's table, including ensembles.
  • Self-attention costs O(n2 · d) per layer: cheaper than recurrence only while the sequence is shorter than the width, and the source of the later long-context work.
  • The paper itself reports an unscaled dot-product problem (variance grows with dk), which you can verify in a dozen lines.
  • The speed gain is for training. Generation remains one token at a time.

Check your understanding

Per Table 1 of the paper, for a sequence of n = 2,000 tokens and width d = 512, which statement is correct?

  1. Self-attention does less arithmetic per layer than a recurrent layer
  2. Both need the same number of sequential steps
  3. Self-attention does more arithmetic per layer but needs only O(1) sequential steps
  4. A recurrent layer needs a longer maximum path between positions, but only O(1) sequential steps
Attention is O(n2·d), recurrence O(n·d2), so with n greater than d attention does more arithmetic; but attention needs O(1) sequential steps against O(n). Recurrence has the longer path (O(n)) and the longer chain (O(n)), so (d) is wrong on both counts.

The Transformer base model trained for 12 hours on eight P100 GPUs and the paper estimates 3.3 × 1018 FLOPs. How is that figure obtained?

  1. By counting the operations in each forward and backward pass with a profiler
  2. By multiplying training time, number of GPUs and an assumed sustained speed per GPU (9.5 TFLOPS for a P100)
  3. By multiplying parameters by training tokens by six
  4. By reading the GPU's power draw
The paper's footnote 5 gives the assumed speeds, and 43,200 s × 8 × 9.5 × 1012 reproduces the table. The 6ND rule is a different estimate used in the scaling-law papers; it is not what this paper used.

A colleague says "the Transformer replaced attention-with-RNNs because attention was a new idea in 2017". What is the best correction?

  1. Attention was invented in 2019 and the paper is a preprint
  2. The paper used attention only in the decoder
  3. The paper did not use attention for the encoder
  4. Attention was already used with RNNs; the paper's change was to use attention alone for every layer, within each sequence and between them
The paper says attention was already integral to strong models but "in all but a few cases" used with recurrent networks. Its novelty was dispensing with recurrence and convolution entirely. The model uses attention in the encoder, in the decoder and between them.

Your attention scores come from vectors with dk = 1,024 and unit-variance components. Without scaling, roughly what do you expect from the softmax?

  1. A nearly one-hot distribution with very small gradients, because the dot products have a standard deviation around 32
  2. A nearly uniform distribution, because large dimensions average out
  3. The same distribution as for dk = 4
  4. NaNs on every run
The footnote's argument and the toy output agree: variance grows with dk, so the logits spread out and the softmax saturates (largest weight 0.97 at 1,024 in the toy). It is not uniform, and it does not by itself produce NaNs.
