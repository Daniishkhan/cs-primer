AlexNet and ImageNet, 2012

In the 2012 ImageNet image-classification contest, a convolutional network built by three people at the University of Toronto won with a top-5 error of 15.3%, while the second-placed entry scored 26.2%. The paper, Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton's "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks", did not contain one new idea so much as three old ones that had finally become affordable at the same time: a large labelled dataset, GPUs fast enough to train on it, and a handful of training tricks that kept a 60-million-parameter network from overfitting. This case reads the paper closely, checks its arithmetic, and asks what exactly changed.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read two primary sources in full: the AlexNet paper itself (Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, NIPS 2012, from the NeurIPS proceedings) and the ILSVRC overview paper by Russakovsky and colleagues (arXiv 1409.0575, 2015), which describes how the contest data were built and tabulates every winner from 2010 to 2014. Every number below is from one of those two, or is labelled as my own calculation. I did not read the dropout preprint (Hinton et al., 2012), the earlier GPU-convolution papers it cites, or any retrospective interviews, so I make no claims about how the project was funded, who proposed what, or the often-told story of its development. One discrepancy to flag: the paper's Figure 2 caption lists 253,440 neurons in the first convolutional layer, but 96 maps of 55 × 55 is 290,400; my count in the toy below follows the arithmetic rather than the caption.

The pattern in one sentence

A step change in results usually arrives when data, compute and method each cross a threshold together: the dataset was large enough to justify a big model, the hardware was fast enough to train it in days, and a few well-chosen training techniques made that size trainable without drowning in overfitting.

Context: what the field looked like before

Object recognition in 2011 was an engineering pipeline. A programmer designed a feature extractor (a fixed recipe that turns pixels into a vector of numbers, such as SIFT, which summarises local edge directions), the vectors were pooled and encoded with methods like Fisher vectors or sparse coding, and a linear classifier was trained on top. Neural networks that learned their own features from pixels existed: the AlexNet paper cites LeCun and colleagues' 1990 backpropagation network for handwritten digits, and Cireşan and colleagues' multi-column convolutional networks, which the paper calls similar to its own design. But such networks had mostly been shown on small images and small datasets.

The authors say why in their opening paragraph. Labelled image datasets "were relatively small, on the order of tens of thousands of images", naming NORB, Caltech-101/256 and CIFAR-10/100. On such data, simple tasks can be solved well, and the paper notes that error on MNIST digits was already below 0.3%. But "objects in realistic settings exhibit considerable variability", so a model has to see a great many examples to learn them. That became possible only when ImageNet arrived: the paper describes over 15 million labelled high-resolution images in roughly 22,000 categories, collected from the web and labelled by human workers using Amazon's Mechanical Turk crowdsourcing service.

The contest is a subset. The ILSVRC (ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge) has been run since 2010 and uses about 1,000 images in each of 1,000 categories. The overview paper gives the counts for 2012: 1,281,167 training images, 50,000 validation images and 100,000 test images. The contest's headline metric is top-5 error, which forgives near-misses among fine-grained classes (the overview paper notes that ILSVRC splits the single "dog" category of an older benchmark into 120 breeds): the fraction of test images whose correct label is not among the model's five highest-scoring guesses. Top-1 error counts only the single best guess.

ImageNet / ILSVRC

Share of images where the true class is missing from the five most probable predictions.
ReLU
Rectified linear unit, f(x) = max(0, x). Its slope is 1 for any positive input, so it does not flatten out the way tanh does. See Neurons, Layers & Activations.
Dropout
Randomly setting each hidden unit's output to zero during training so that units cannot depend on specific partners. See Regularising Deep Networks.
Data augmentation
Making extra training examples by applying label-preserving changes (crops, flips, colour shifts) to the ones you have.
Weight sharing
Reusing the same small filter at every image position, which is what makes convolutional layers cheap. See Convolutions & Images.

Ingredient one: data

The paper makes the data argument as a statement about capacity. To learn thousands of objects from millions of images "we need a model with a large learning capacity", yet the task is so complex that even ImageNet cannot specify it fully, so the model should also carry prior knowledge to compensate for data it lacks. Convolutional networks supply that prior: they assume that image statistics are the same everywhere (stationarity) and that nearby pixels matter most (locality). Compared with a dense network of the same layer sizes, the authors write, a convolutional net has far fewer connections and parameters and "so they are easier to train, while their theoretically-best performance is likely to be only slightly worse".

Even with 1.2 million images, size was a problem. The authors note that each training image supplies only about 10 bits of constraint (one choice among 1,000 labels), which "turns out to be insufficient to learn so many parameters without considerable overfitting". Much of the paper's Section 4 is a response to that sentence. They resized every image to 256 × 256 (shorter side rescaled to 256, then a centre crop), subtracted the training-set mean pixel, and fed raw RGB values to the network: no hand-designed features at all.

Ingredient two: compute

The training machine was one computer with two NVIDIA GTX 580 GPUs, each with 3 GB of memory. The paper reports that the network "takes between five and six days to train" and was trained for roughly 90 passes through the 1.2 million images. Its Section 1 states the constraint plainly: "the network's size is limited mainly by the amount of memory available on current GPUs and by the amount of training time that we are willing to tolerate", and the authors expected results to improve "simply by waiting for faster GPUs and bigger datasets".

The 3 GB limit shaped the design. The authors spread the network over both GPUs, putting half the kernels on each, with a further trick: the GPUs exchange information only in some layers. Layer 3 reads all of layer 2's maps (so activations cross between GPUs), but layers 4 and 5 read only the maps on their own GPU. The paper says choosing this connectivity pattern was a matter of cross-validation, and that it let them "precisely tune the amount of communication until it is an acceptable fraction of the amount of computation". It also notes that GPUs of that generation could read each other's memory directly, without going through the host computer. For the engineering underneath this, see Parallelism, SIMD & GPUs.

Ingredient three: method

The paper lists its "novel or unusual features" in order of what the authors thought most important. Each comes with a measured effect, and the numbers are worth keeping together.

The training recipe is plain: stochastic gradient descent with batch size 128, momentum 0.9 and weight decay 0.0005; a learning rate starting at 0.01 that the authors divided by 10 by hand whenever validation error stopped improving, three times in all; weights initialised from a Gaussian with standard deviation 0.01; and biases set to 1 in some layers to give ReLUs positive inputs early on. Every ingredient of this recipe is covered in earlier courses: see Gradient Descent & Optimisers for momentum and learning-rate schedules, and Initialisation, Normalisation & Residuals for why initial weight scale matters.

Notice the CPU/GPU division of labour the authors describe: the augmented images "are generated in Python code on the CPU while the GPU is training on the previous batch of images", so augmentation is "in effect, computationally free". This is the same overlap-the-stages idea you will meet in Performance Engineering: never let the expensive device wait for the cheap one.

What the numbers say

Table 1 of the paper compares results on ILSVRC-2010, the only year whose test labels were public. The best contest entry (an average of six sparse-coding models) had 47.1% top-1 and 28.2% top-5 error; the best published result since then (two classifiers on Fisher vectors) had 45.7% and 25.7%. The CNN scored 37.5% and 17.0%. That is more than 8 points better in top-5 error than anything published.

The 2012 contest is where the result became famous. The paper's Table 2 and the overview paper's Table 5 agree, and the details matter:

  • One CNN: 18.2% top-5 on the validation set. Averaging five similar CNNs: 16.4% (also the test figure). This is the entry the overview paper lists for the team, codenamed SuperVision, as 16.4%.
  • A variant with an extra sixth convolutional layer was first pre-trained on the whole ImageNet Fall 2011 release (15 million images, 22,000 categories) and then fine-tuned on the contest data: 16.6% alone, and 15.3% when two of them were averaged with the five other CNNs. The overview paper notes that this 15.3% entry used extra training data and the 16.4% entry did not.
  • Second place, a Fisher-vector system from the University of Tokyo (team ISI), scored 26.2%.

So the gap was about 10 points whichever AlexNet entry you take. The abstract quotes 15.3% versus 26.2%, which is the larger, extra-data figure; the cleaner comparison is 16.4% versus 26.2%.

Where the parameters and the arithmetic live

The abstract says 60 million parameters and 650,000 neurons. Two small scripts check the claims and show what the paper's choices mean. The first counts parameters, neurons and multiply-adds from the layer sizes in Section 3.5 (all of them are mine: the paper does not tabulate them).

"""Count AlexNet's neurons and parameters from the layer sizes in Krizhevsky et al. (2012), Section 3.5."""
# (name, out_channels, kernel, in_channels_seen_by_each_kernel, output_side)
convs = [
    ("conv1",  96, 11,   3, 55),   # 224x224x3 input, stride 4
    ("conv2", 256,  5,  48, 27),   # sees only the 48 maps on its own GPU
    ("conv3", 384,  3, 256, 13),   # sees all 256 maps (GPUs talk here)
    ("conv4", 384,  3, 192, 13),   # own GPU only
    ("conv5", 256,  3, 192, 13),   # own GPU only
]
fcs = [("fc6", 6 * 6 * 256, 4096), ("fc7", 4096, 4096), ("fc8", 4096, 1000)]

total_params, total_neurons, total_macs = 0, 0, 0
print(f"{'layer':6} {'neurons':>9} {'params':>12} {'multiply-adds':>14}")
for name, out_c, k, in_c, side in convs:
    p = out_c * (k * k * in_c + 1)          # weights + one bias per kernel
    n = out_c * side * side
    m = n * k * k * in_c                    # one multiply-add per weight per output position
    total_params += p; total_neurons += n; total_macs += m
    print(f"{name:6} {n:9,d} {p:12,d} {m:14,d}")
for name, fin, fout in fcs:
    p = fout * (fin + 1)
    m = fin * fout
    total_params += p; total_neurons += fout; total_macs += m
    print(f"{name:6} {fout:9,d} {p:12,d} {m:14,d}")
print(f"{'total':6} {total_neurons:9,d} {total_params:12,d} {total_macs:14,d}")
conv_params = sum(o * (k * k * i + 1) for _, o, k, i, _ in convs)
print(f"conv layers hold {conv_params/total_params:.1%} of the parameters; fc6 alone holds {fcs[0][2]*(fcs[0][1]+1)/total_params:.1%}")
conv_macs = sum(n_ for n_ in [o * s_ * s_ * k * k * i for _, o, k, i, s_ in convs])
print(f"conv layers do {conv_macs/total_macs:.1%} of the multiply-adds")
print("float32 weights:", f"{total_params*4/1e6:.0f} MB", "| with momentum buffer:", f"{total_params*8/1e6:.0f} MB")
print("crops per 256x256 image:", (256 - 224) ** 2 * 2, "(32 x 32 offsets x 2 for reflection)")

# Rough training bill: forward + backward is about 3x the forward multiply-adds, 2 FLOPs per multiply-add.
images, epochs, seconds = 1.2e6, 90, 5.5 * 86400            # "roughly 90 cycles", "five to six days" (Sec. 5)
flops = 3 * 2 * total_macs * images * epochs
print(f"training estimate: {flops:.1e} FLOPs -> {flops/seconds/1e12:.2f} TFLOP/s sustained over 5.5 days (two GPUs together)")

# Rough memory tally for one GPU-pair, float32. The paper gives no breakdown; this ignores pooling/normalisation outputs and library workspace.
batch = 128
weights = total_params * 4
acts = total_neurons * batch * 4

The count reproduces "60 million" (60.97 million with biases) and gives about 659,000 neurons, close to the abstract's 650,000. The distribution is the interesting part. The five convolutional layers hold under 4% of the parameters but do about 92% of the arithmetic, because each small filter is reused at every image position. The first fully connected layer alone holds 62% of the parameters, which is why dropout was applied exactly there, and why it is the layer where overfitting bites hardest. Weights in float32 take only about 244 MB. My rough tally of weights, gradients, momentum and a batch of 128 images' activations (the last line) comes to about 1.4 GB across both cards, so the paper's memory limit must also reflect things I did not count, such as pooling and normalisation outputs and library workspace; the paper gives no breakdown. The training estimate (about 4.7 × 1017 FLOPs, roughly 1 TFLOP/s sustained over 5.5 days across both cards) uses the 3×-forward rule of thumb from Parameters, FLOPs & Memory and ignores pooling, normalisation and data movement, so treat it as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

The second script tests the mechanism behind the ReLU claim. I do not reproduce the paper's six-times-faster number, since that was a particular CIFAR-10 network; instead I measure the thing the explanation rests on, the gate a unit places on the gradient. During backpropagation each unit multiplies the gradient by the derivative of its activation function. For tanh that derivative falls towards zero as the input grows (this is saturation); for ReLU it is exactly 1 for any positive input.

"""The gate each unit puts on the gradient: tanh'(z) shrinks as |z| grows, ReLU'(z) is exactly 1 for z > 0."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
print("derivative at one pre-activation z:")
for z in (0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0):
    print(f"  z={z:3.1f}  tanh'(z)={1 - np.tanh(z) ** 2:.4f}   ReLU'(z)={1.0 if z > 0 else 0.0:.0f}")

print("\n100,000 pre-activations z ~ Normal(0, s^2); the gate is the derivative a unit multiplies into the gradient:")
print(f"  {'s':>4} {'tanh: median gate':>18} {'tanh: gate<0.1':>15} {'tanh: median^8':>15} {'ReLU: median gate':>18} {'ReLU: exactly 0':>16}")
for s in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0):
    z = rng.normal(0, s, 100_000)
    gt = 1 - np.tanh(z) ** 2
    gr = (z > 0).astype(float)
    med_t = float(np.median(gt)); med_r = float(np.median(gr[gr > 0]))
    print(f"  {s:4.1f} {med_t:18.3f} {np.mean(gt < 0.1):15.1%} {med_t ** 8:15.2e} {med_r:18.1f} {np.mean(gr == 0):16.1%}")

When pre-activations have a spread of 2 (not unusual for unnormalised inputs and careless initialisation), a third of tanh units pass less than a tenth of the gradient, and the typical unit passes under a quarter. At a spread of 4, two-thirds are nearly shut. ReLU never shrinks the gradient on the units it keeps. The price is visible in the last column: half of the units are exactly zero for a given input, and a unit that is zero for all inputs stops learning. The "median^8" column multiplies a typical gate across eight layers; it is a crude illustration, not a simulation of a network.

I also tried the direct experiment, racing the two activations on a small dense network and a synthetic classification problem, tuning the learning rate for each. In my scratch run (not shown, about seven minutes of CPU) tanh reached the target loss sooner than ReLU in three of four depth and initialisation settings, and ReLU failed to reach it in the fourth. That is not a contradiction of the paper, which says the effect "varies with network architecture" and measured a convolutional network. It is a reminder that "ReLU trains faster" is a statement about a class of networks, learning rates and initialisations, not a law.

What the critics and later work said

The paper's own text is more cautious than its reputation. Three points stand out.

  • The ablations are partial. Most of the quoted gains come from ILSVRC-2010 runs where each trick was switched off once. They are not a full factorial study, so interactions are unknown, and the two-GPU comparison is, by the authors' own footnote, biased in favour of the control. The headline 2012 result is an ensemble, as the section above shows.
  • The authors name their limits. They expected unsupervised pre-training to help "if we obtain enough computational power", wrote that results had improved as the network grew and trained longer, and added that "we still have many orders of magnitude to go in order to match the infero-temporal pathway of the human visual system". They saw video as the next target.
  • Depth mattered. Removing any single convolutional layer (each holds no more than 1% of the parameters) hurt; losing a middle layer cost about 2 points of top-1. That finding, which the table of parameters makes surprising, is what pushed later networks deeper.

The ILSVRC overview paper documents what happened next. It calls 2012 "a turning point for large-scale object recognition". In 2013 there were 24 participating teams, compared with 21 in the previous three years combined, and "the vast majority of entries in 2013 used deep convolutional neural networks"; the winner, Clarifai, averaged several large CNNs and also used dropout and GPUs. In 2014 GoogLeNet reached 6.7% top-5 error. The overview paper also measured people: after training on 500 images, one expert annotator labelled 1,500 test images with 5.1% top-5 error, against 6.8% for GoogLeNet on the same images (a difference the authors report as statistically significant, p = 0.022). A second annotator, who trained on 100 images and labelled 258, scored 12.0%. The authors conclude that "a significant amount of training time is necessary for a human to achieve competitive performance" on this 1,000-class task. That is a statement about a particular benchmark, not about general visual understanding, and the paper makes that distinction itself.

Common misconception

"AlexNet invented convolutional networks, ReLUs, dropout and GPU training." It did none of these. The paper cites convolutional nets trained by backpropagation from 1990, ReLU units as named by Nair and Hinton (2010), dropout from a 2012 preprint by Hinton and colleagues, and multi-column CNNs by Cireşan and colleagues. What the paper adds is the combination at a scale not previously reported, on a benchmark with a clear scoreboard, plus a highly optimised GPU implementation, which the authors made public. The contribution was a demonstration of scale plus engineering, which is how many step changes happen.

Analogy: a three-key lock

A safe that needs three keys turned together looks, from outside, like it opened because of one dramatic action. Larger datasets, faster hardware and better training recipes had each been improving for years, and each alone gave modest gains. When the third key turned, results jumped by ten points in a single contest year.

Where it breaks down: keys are discrete and the lock opens once. Here the three factors keep trading off against each other (more data justifies a bigger model, which needs more compute, which needs better regularisation), and the same triple has since been pushed again and again, as the later cases in this course show.

Lessons, and where they connect

  1. Match model capacity to data, then fight overfitting directly. A 60-million-parameter model on 1.2 million images only worked because of dropout, augmentation and weight decay. These are the tools of Regularising Deep Networks, and the 2,048-fold crop count is a clean example of augmentation as free data.
  2. Build in the right inductive bias. Convolutions encode locality and stationarity, which is why a network this size could be trained at all. Convolutions & Images builds the operation and tests the bias.
  3. Treat hardware limits as design inputs. The 3 GB memory ceiling produced the two-GPU split, and the CPU-GPU overlap kept the cards busy. The same thinking, applied to a Transformer, is Parameters, FLOPs & Memory.
  4. Make the benchmark honest, then read it carefully. A public contest with fixed splits let a ten-point gap be seen and trusted. Reading it carefully still meant noticing the ensemble and the extra-data entry. Compare how train, validation and test sets protect against fooling yourself.
  5. Expect the pattern to repeat. The next case, Attention Is All You Need, is another case of a method chosen to make better use of parallel hardware; From GPT-2 to GPT-3 and Chinchilla ask how to spend data and compute once you have plenty of both.

Key takeaways

  • AlexNet's top-5 error was 16.4% (5 CNNs) or 15.3% (with extra pre-training data) against 26.2% for second place in ILSVRC-2012. Always say which entry you mean.
  • The three threshold crossings: about 1.2 million labelled images, two 3 GB GPUs for 5 to 6 days, and ReLU, dropout, augmentation and weight decay.
  • Convolutional layers hold under 4% of the parameters and about 92% of the arithmetic (my count); the first dense layer holds 62% of the parameters.
  • ReLU's claimed advantage is gradient flow through saturation, and the size of the speed-up depends on architecture and initialisation.
  • The paper's novelty was combination at scale, not any single technique, and its authors were open about its limits.

Check your understanding

In ILSVRC-2012 the AlexNet team's best entry without extra data scored 16.4% top-5 error, and another entry scored 15.3%. What explains the difference?

  1. The 15.3% entry used test labels during training
  2. The 15.3% entry used networks pre-trained on the full 15-million-image ImageNet release and averaged more models
  3. The 15.3% entry was trained for ten times longer on the same data
  4. The 16.4% entry only used one GPU
The paper's Table 2 and the ILSVRC overview say the 15.3% result averaged seven networks, two of which were pre-trained on the Fall 2011 release of 15 million images. Test labels were not public, so (a) is wrong; both entries used the same two-GPU hardware.

By my count, the five convolutional layers hold under 4% of AlexNet's parameters but about 92% of its multiply-adds. Which design consequence follows?

  1. Dropout should be applied to the convolutional layers
  2. The convolutional layers are where overfitting is most severe
  3. The convolutional layers need the largest memory share on each GPU
  4. Compute speed matters most in the convolutional layers, while regularisation matters most in the dense layers
Weight sharing makes convolutional layers parameter-cheap but arithmetic-heavy, so speed work focuses there. The first dense layer holds 62% of the parameters, which is where the paper applied dropout. Memory for activations is a separate matter and is not dominated by the dense layers.

A reader claims "ReLU makes any network train six times faster". What does the paper actually support?

  1. A six-fold speed-up to 25% training error for one four-layer CNN on CIFAR-10, with the authors noting the size of the effect varies with architecture
  2. A six-fold speed-up for AlexNet itself on ImageNet
  3. A six-fold speed-up for all networks when learning rates are equal
  4. No speed-up at all, only better final accuracy
Figure 1 compares a specific four-layer CNN on CIFAR-10 with separately tuned learning rates. The authors did not run the equivalent test on the full ImageNet network, since saturating units would have made it infeasible, and they say the effect varies with architecture.

Which statement about what AlexNet "invented" is best supported by the paper?

  1. It introduced convolutional networks to computer vision
  2. It was the first system to train any neural network on a GPU
  3. It combined existing ideas (CNNs, ReLU, dropout, GPU training) at a scale not previously run, and released its GPU code
  4. It proved that depth does not matter if the dataset is large enough
The paper cites earlier CNNs, ReLUs and dropout by name, and describes its GPU convolution code as very efficient rather than as the first use of GPUs. It reports the opposite of (d): removing any convolutional layer hurt performance, so depth mattered.
