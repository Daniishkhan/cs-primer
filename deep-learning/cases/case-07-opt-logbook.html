Training OPT-175B: The Logbook

Papers about large models usually describe a clean run. In 2022 a team at Meta AI published the paper "OPT: Open Pre-trained Transformer Language Models" and, alongside it, the day-by-day diary they kept while training the 175-billion-parameter model (the team's final note calls it 148 pages of notes; the PDF in the repository runs to 114): restarts, loss explosions, failing GPUs and, once, a cluster deleted by mistake. This case reads that diary as a record of what it takes to operate a training run, and puts numbers on what the failures cost.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the four progress notes the team posted while training (17 November and 3 and 16 December 2021, and 7 January 2022) from the project's public repository, and the full text of the OPT paper (arXiv 2205.01068). I downloaded the 114-page logbook PDF and extracted its text, then read the passages around the events the notes describe (the Omicron incident, the cloud-provider complaint list, the early divergences, the 6 December entry); I did not read all 24,000 words line by line. The logbook is the team's own contemporaneous record, so it is a primary source for what they saw and decided, but not an independent audit, and several causes in it are labelled as unsolved. All timings and counts below are theirs unless I say "derived" or "toy". I did not see the training curves in the image files, only the text around them.

The pattern in one sentence

A large training run is a long-running production system: its elapsed time is the ideal compute time plus everything lost to failures, so the work of the run is detecting failures quickly, losing little progress when they happen, recovering with spare capacity ready, and writing down every decision so the next run starts wiser.

Context: why publish a diary

The OPT paper's stated aim was to share a suite of decoder-only Transformers from 125 million to 175 billion parameters, because models of this size "are difficult to replicate without significant capital" and, when offered through paid APIs, give no access to weights. The 175B model was trained on 992 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80 GB of memory each, using Fully Sharded Data Parallel with Megatron-LM tensor parallelism (the combination discussed in chapter 3 of the training course), keeping the Adam optimiser state in 32-bit floats and the weights in 16-bit floats with dynamic loss scaling to avoid underflow. The paper reports sustained utilisation of up to 147 TFLOP/s per GPU. The training text was a corpus of about 180 billion tokens, tokenised with the GPT-2 byte-level BPE; the planned run was 300 billion tokens, so the model would see the data more than once.

The authors say the logbook is the point: "by releasing our logbook, we hope to highlight the gap between a theoretical carbon cost estimate that assumes no hardware failures or training instabilities, versus one that aims to include the entire LLM development lifecycle." They also say that such "ad-hoc design decisions" are "generally omitted from previous publications, likely due to the inability to fully ablate changes made mid-flight". That is the reason to read it: it is unusually candid evidence about the part of the job that papers leave out.

Checkpoint
A saved copy of the model weights and optimiser state, from which a stopped run can resume. Progress since the last checkpoint is lost on a failure.
Restart
Stopping the job and resuming from a checkpoint, either by a person (manual) or by tooling (automatic).
Buffer nodes
Spare machines held ready so a failed host can be swapped in at once. The team's word for hot spares.
ECC error
An error reported by a GPU's error-correcting memory. Correctable errors are fixed silently; uncorrectable ones usually mean the card must be replaced.
Dynamic loss scale
A multiplier applied to the loss in 16-bit training so small gradients do not underflow, raised and lowered automatically (see chapter 17).
Experiment lineage (11.xx, 12.xx)
The team's names for sequences of restarts that continue one configuration. 12.43 means the 43rd restart in lineage 12.
On-call
A rotation of people who respond when the job stops, at any hour.

The timeline

The 175B run was not one run. It was two lineages and about a hundred restarts, over roughly two and a half months. Dates are from the progress notes and logbook headings.

Act one: instability (5 to 11 November)

The team launched the first full-scale run (experiment 11.0) on 5 November 2021 with a batch of about 2 million tokens, FP32 Adam, 8-way tensor parallelism, a modified architecture (Normformer, which adds extra normalisation layers) and learned positional embeddings initialised with sinusoids. The 17 November note describes the pattern: "From experiment 11.4 onward, we saw grad norm explosions / loss explosions / nans after a couple hundred updates after each restart, along with extremely unstable loss scales that would drop to the point of massively underflowing." The interventions escalated: lower the learning rate, clip gradients harder (from 2.5 to 1.5 to 1.0), raise weight decay to 0.1, lower Adam's beta2 to 0.95, and by 11.10, swap GeLU for ReLU and use a more stable attention computation. The logbook has a brainstorm of "interventions that require full restart", marked "unanimously unpopular". Its analysis of run 11.5 records a gradient-norm spike and then values of minus infinity at the input to layer 92's attention normalisation, with the loss scale already very low: "Unsolved mystery: which layer really the problem?" Separately, an ablation found that the learned positional embeddings were not training at all: the sinusoidal initialisation "effectively froze" them.

On 11 November the team executed "plan B", which they had written down on 18 October before starting any of these runs: abandon the configuration inherited from their earlier codebase and adopt, as far as possible, the Megatron and GPT-3 settings, because those had reportedly trained 175B-scale models. The reasons given are practical: training needs about 33 days on 1,024 80 GB A100s, and "time was running out before EOY". Lineage 12 started that day with standard initialisation, no Normformer, no embedding scaling. By the 17 November note, restarts "were all related to hardware issues".

The same note records two other things. The dataset had been rebuilt, and early small runs showed "unreasonably low" perplexity, which they traced to JSON encoding artefacts (extra backslashes added in deduplication), whitespace problems and corpus-specific formatting issues. They also had no time to train a new BPE on the final data, so they fell back to the GPT-2 BPE. This is a data-pipeline story (chapter 13) inside a training story.

Act two: hardware (17 November to mid-December)

The 3 December note opens: "It's been really rough for the team since the November 17th update. Since then, we've had 40+ restarts in the 175B experiment for a variety of hardware, infrastructure, or experimental stability issues." The causes, as the team lists them: the vast majority were hardware failures, made worse by "the lack of ability to provision a sufficient number of 'buffer' nodes to replace a bad node with once it goes down with ECC errors". Replacing a node through the cloud interface "can take hours for a single machine", and "more often than not we would end up getting the same bad machine again". The logbook's 18 November list of complaints for the cloud provider puts it plainly: they could not train continuously for more than one to two days on a cluster of 128 nodes; uncorrectable ECC errors occurred every one to two days; and InfiniBand problems degraded throughput by about 20%. The team's asks included burn-in and ECC checks before nodes are allocated, and a way to retire problem nodes so they are not handed back.

They built tooling as they went: scripts to replace nodes, GPU burn-in tests to find memory errors, automated InfiniBand tests, and monitoring of the training log. Restoring a checkpoint was itself a failure point: a single model checkpoint was 1.6 TB in 992 files of about 1.7 GB each, and downloads from blob storage "would start hanging nondeterministically". The note is frank about the cost of launching without this in place: "given the time crunch of shipping a trained 175B model by end of H2 2021, we had no choice but to launch and see how far we would get without this additional tooling."

Numerical problems returned. Over the following week gradient overflow errors drove the loss scale to its minimum threshold, "which also causes training to grind to a halt". Restarts from earlier checkpoints got "a bit further (~100 or more steps)" and hit the same wall. The team tried SGD-like settings for Adam (beta1 to 0, epsilon to 1), only to discover that reinitialising Adam from a checkpoint also reloaded the saved betas, so the change had not taken effect; they then wrote an FP16-friendly vanilla SGD, found their learning rate was too low for it to make progress, and on the morning of 3 December decided to stay with Adam and lower the learning rate. It "seems to have a surprising effect on reducing gradient and activation norms". The paper's summary agrees: SGD "plateaued quickly" and they "reverted back to AdamW". The note on 16 December reports that when a gradient-norm drift appeared again on run 12.43 a 10% cut in learning rate stabilised it, and on 12.45.2, after "a few large grad norm spikes", they cut it to two-thirds of the GPT-3 175B value.

Act three: spares, a deleted cluster, and the finish (16 December to 6 January)

By 16 December the escalation had worked: "the team has since received additional compute for a larger 'buffer pool'". The cloud provider added 18 spare hosts; five hosts had gone down together on 6 December when the buffer was four. The three longest uninterrupted stretches of the run came in those two weeks, at 1.5, 2.8 and 2 days. The note also records an unexplained change: after an accidental upgrade of Megatron from version 2.4 to 2.6, the trajectory of the activation norm "changed significantly", which the team traced to a one-line change that stopped a particular PyTorch JIT setting being executed. They were "still unclear why this one-liner affected training dynamics". The paper later lists "switching to a newer version of Megatron" as a change that "reduced pressure on activation norms and improved throughput".

On 21 December, while topping up the buffer pool over the holidays, the provider's support team "accidentally deleted our entire cluster". The logbook's "Omicron SEV" entry shows the aftermath: after reallocation, pairs of nodes reported the same IP address, some nodes were unreachable while the scheduler still saw them as alive, health checks flagged high correctable ECC counts and corrupted GPU firmware information, and one node's measured network bandwidth was below the threshold of 180. Sixteen machines failed the team's checks. By the end of that day they had the minimum number of machines to resume, and by 23 December the buffer pool was restored. Between Christmas and New Year they recovered automatically from eight hardware failures, and set a goal for January of 14 days at full scale without human intervention, at which point they could "officially dissolve the role of the on-call".

Training completed at 12:46 pm Pacific time on 6 January 2022, 300 billion tokens, "~4.30E+23 FLOPs of compute, or roughly ~33 days of continuous training on 1024 80GB A100s (assuming no hardware issues, no numerical instabilities, etc.)". The final note puts this beside GPT-3's 3.14E+23 FLOPs, linking a 2021 carbon-emissions study (Patterson et al.) for the figure of 14.8 days on 10,000 V100 GPUs; I did not read that study. The note says their final validation loss against GPT-3's was "close, but not quite the same" and suggests that training on only 180 billion distinct tokens "may have led us to overfit on the training set and plateau earlier on validation". It is a hypothesis, stated as one.

What the numbers add up to

Two figures in the sources invite a calculation. Ideal compute time was about 33 days. Lineage 12 started on 11 November and finished on 6 January, which is 56 days. The paper says hardware failures caused "at least 35 manual restarts" and cycling of more than 100 hosts over two months, and estimates "70+ automatic restarts". (The final note counts "~90 restarts" in the 12.xx lineage; the counts are not the same quantity, and I use about 105 as the paper's order of magnitude.) Failures every few hours cost time in two ways: the work done since the last checkpoint is lost (the logbook says in one entry that they checkpointed every 250 steps), and the cluster is idle while the problem is found and fixed.

The script below is a small simulation I wrote. It takes three inputs from the sources (33 ideal days, the 250-step checkpoint interval, about 105 restarts, from the paper's 35 manual and 70-plus automatic) and treats failures as random events at a constant rate. Each failure loses whatever was done since the last checkpoint and then costs a fixed recovery time. It is calibrated, in the sense that I chose the recovery time to see which value reproduces the 56 days, so it cannot confirm the story, only show what the story implies.

"""Toy: how failures stretch a long training run. A simulation I wrote; the inputs marked (source) come
from the OPT-175B paper and the Meta logbook notes, the rest are assumptions to vary."""
import random, statistics

IDEAL_DAYS = 33.0                    # (source) ~33 days on 1024 A100s with no failures, final update note
UPDATES = 143_052                    # (source) mu143052 in the logbook's run names; 300B tokens / ~2.1M per update
CKPT_EVERY = 250                     # (source) logbook: "we checkpoint every 250 steps"
FAILS_PER_DAY = 105 / IDEAL_DAYS     # (derived) 35 manual + 70 automatic restarts, spread over the ~33 days of useful running
upd_hours = IDEAL_DAYS * 24 / UPDATES
ckpt_hours = CKPT_EVERY * upd_hours

def one_run(recover_h, rng, ckpt_h=ckpt_hours, rate=FAILS_PER_DAY):
    done, wall, restarts = 0.0, 0.0, 0                 # hours of useful progress at the last checkpoint / wall clock
    total = IDEAL_DAYS * 24
    while done < total:
        gap = rng.expovariate(rate / 24)      # hours until the next failure
        if done + gap >= total: wall += total - done; break
        saved = int(gap / ckpt_h) * ckpt_h             # progress that survives = whole checkpoint intervals
        done += saved; wall += gap + recover_h; restarts += 1
    return wall / 24, restarts

print(f"one update = {upd_hours*3600:.1f} s, checkpoint interval = {ckpt_hours*60:.0f} min")
print("recovery per restart | mean days to finish | 5th-95th pct | restarts | share of time lost")
for rec in (0.25, 1, 2, 4, 5, 8):
    runs = sorted(one_run(rec, random.Random(s)) for s in range(500))
    days = [r[0] for r in runs]
    print(f"{rec:12.2f} h        {statistics.mean(days):9.1f}        {days[25]:5.1f}-{days[475]:5.1f}      {statistics.mean(r[1] for r in runs):6.0f}      {1-IDEAL_DAYS/statistics.mean(days):6.0%}")
print("\nDoubling checkpoint interval at 4 h recovery:",
      round(statistics.mean(one_run(4, random.Random(s), ckpt_h=2*ckpt_hours)[0] for s in range(500)), 1), "days")
print("Observed (source): 11 Nov launch of run 12.00 to 6 Jan finish =", 56, "days")

nodes = 992 // 8                                       # assumption: 8 GPUs per node
print(f"\nCluster of {nodes} nodes failing {FAILS_PER_DAY:.2f} times per running day: cluster MTBF {24/FAILS_PER_DAY:.1f} h, per-node MTBF {nodes/FAILS_PER_DAY:.0f} days")
print("More nodes at the same per-node reliability (the work stays 33 days; I ignore that more nodes would also run faster), 4 h recovery, 83 min checkpoints:")
for mult in (1, 2, 4, 8):
    days = statistics.mean(one_run(4, random.Random(s), rate=FAILS_PER_DAY * mult)[0] for s in range(200))
    print(f"  {mult}x nodes: failure every {24/(FAILS_PER_DAY*mult):4.1f} h -> {days:7.0f} days to do 33 days of work")

Reading the output. With about 3.2 failures per running day, an update takes 19.9 seconds (33 days spread over 143,052 updates, a figure I read from the logbook's run names, where it appears as mu143052; 300 billion tokens over that many updates is about 2.1 million per update, consistent with the 2-million-token batch) and a checkpoint interval is 83 minutes, so on average a failure throws away about 40 minutes of work. If recovery took 15 minutes the run would have finished in about 37 days. To end up at 56 days, the model needs an effective recovery of about 4 hours per restart, which is the same order as the team's own remark that replacing a node through the cloud interface "can take hours" and often returned the same bad machine. This effective figure also absorbs everything else that stopped progress, such as time spent diagnosing instability and rewinding after learning-rate cuts, so it is an upper bound on hardware recovery alone. Doubling the checkpoint interval at 4 hours of recovery adds about 5.6 days (61.1 against 55.5), while cutting recovery to 1 hour saves about 14.5: checkpoint frequency is a real lever, but a smaller one than recovery time. The last block is a warning about scale. Cutting the cluster's mean time between failures from 7.5 hours to 0.9 hours, as eight times as many nodes at the same per-node reliability would, makes the same 33 days of work take 393 days in this model; the run spends almost all of its time recovering. That is the serial-composition argument of reliability engineering: if any one of N components failing stops the whole job, the job's failure rate is N times a component's. The numbers 124 nodes and 39 days per node are my derived values (992 GPUs at an assumed eight per node, and the failure rate above) and include non-hardware restarts, so read the per-node figure as a loose lower bound on how reliable a node was.

Three families of failure

Hardware and infrastructure

GPUs lost mid-job, uncorrectable ECC errors every one to two days on 128 nodes, InfiniBand and NCCL faults, hangs after adding a node, partial checkpoint uploads, slow blob storage, a deleted cluster. Answer: health checks, burn-in, spares, automation, an on-call rotation with runbooks.

Numerical stability

Gradient-norm explosions, NaNs, the dynamic loss scale collapsing, activation norms drifting. Answer: lower learning rate and restart from a healthy checkpoint, clip harder, and in the end, copy a recipe known to work. The paper links divergences to the loss scale falling towards 0 and the final-layer activation norm spiking.

Process and data

A data pipeline with artefacts, no time to retrain the tokeniser, learned positions that never trained, an optimiser whose settings silently reloaded from a checkpoint, an accidental dependency upgrade that changed dynamics. Answer: more logging of gradient and parameter statistics, and careful change control.

What critics and later work said

  • The logbook is candid but partial. It is a self-report. Several entries end in open questions (which layer was at fault in 11.5; why the Megatron one-liner mattered), and the team says explicitly that results from small-scale runs "do not necessarily hold when scaled-up" and that they had too little time and compute for the ablations that would explain choices. A diary supports "what they tried"; it does not establish cause.
  • The timeline was shaped by a deadline. The notes repeatedly cite the end of 2021. That pushed a launch before the tooling existed and a dataset of 180 billion tokens instead of a larger curated one; the final note says they "did not have time to curate a larger dataset". This was not a benchmarking exercise: the model was trained to completion on what they had.
  • Counts are not strictly comparable. The paper's "35 manual and 70+ automatic" restarts and the final note's "~90 restarts" in lineage 12 count different things over different date ranges, and the note's "148 pages" does not match the 114-page PDF I downloaded. I treat them as one order of magnitude.
  • Carbon accounting. The paper reports an estimated 75 tons of CO2 equivalent for OPT-175B, against 500 tons estimated for GPT-3 and 380 for Gopher, then cautions that "these estimates are not universally reported, and the accounting methodologies for these calculations are also not standardized". It also notes that its own logbook exposes a gap between estimates that assume no failures and the whole development lifecycle. The comparison is therefore indicative only.
  • Other runs report smoother paths. Chapter 17 compares the spikes reported for PaLM and for OPT with the Llama 3 and DeepSeek-V3 reports of very stable runs. The contrast shows that stability is a property of the recipe and the infrastructure, not of scale alone; OPT's second lineage deliberately adopted a recipe from an already successful run.

Common misconception

"The cost of a training run is parameters times tokens times six, divided by what the GPUs can do." That formula (chapter 16) gives the ideal compute time, which for OPT-175B was about 33 days. The elapsed time was 56 days from the start of the successful lineage, after an earlier lineage that did not survive, and the team also spent compute on small-scale experiments and evaluation. The 16 December note says it directly: "allocating just enough compute budget to train a large-scale model won't be enough to guarantee a better model." Budget for failures, spares, experiments, and people on call.

Analogy: a mountaineering expedition's journal

A summit attempt is planned in terms of distance and altitude, but the schedule is set by weather windows, porters who fall ill, and equipment that fails at the worst moment. The expedition leader keeps a camp journal so the team can see what was tried at each camp, sets turn-around times in advance, and keeps rested climbers in reserve at base camp. Reaching the top is the goal; finishing the trip safely is the job. The OPT team's checkpoints are the camps, the buffer nodes are the rested climbers, and the "plan B" fixed on 18 October is the turn-around rule.

Where it breaks down: climbers choose whether to continue and may pause for weather. A training run is idle and burning money every hour it is down, and its "weather" includes errors that appear only at a scale where nobody has tested the equipment.

Systems connection

Nearly every item above is a reliability topic in its own right. The failure arithmetic is availability maths; the on-call rotation and runbooks belong to alerting and on-call and incident response; the logbook itself, and the habit of writing down what was tried, is what postmortems formalise; and the spare pool and the deleted cluster connect to blast radius. The ML-specific tooling is covered in checkpoints, failures and long runs in the training course.

Lessons

  • Decide your fallback before you start. The "plan B" was written on 18 October and executed on 11 November without argument. Pre-commitment saved days.
  • Measure the failure rate, then size the spares. The team's own summary is that about two machines went down per day. A buffer of four hosts was beaten by a five-host outage on 6 December; 18 more fixed it.
  • Automate recovery in the order it hurts. Health checks, node replacement, checkpoint restore and restart were all manual at first; the goal became 14 days with no human, and eight failures were handled automatically in the holiday week.
  • Choose restart points by the health of the state, not only its age. The paper says they picked checkpoints at which the dynamic loss scale was still "healthy" (at least 1.0) and activation norms trended down.
  • Log what you need for the next question. They added "a lot more logging of gradients / param statistics" after the first lineage; the stability runbook in chapter 17 lists the same signals.
  • Treat dependencies as part of the experiment. A minor Megatron upgrade changed the activation-norm trajectory; pin versions and diff behaviour, as in configuration and infrastructure as code.
  • Write it down. Public or not, the log is what lets a later team see which of 100 interventions mattered.

(2) Set FAILS_PER_DAY to one-third of its value, as if the buffer pool and burn-in testing had removed two thirds of the failures, and compare. (3) Using chapter 16, estimate the ideal days to train a 7B model on 1.4 trillion tokens on 256 GPUs at 40% utilisation of a 312 TFLOP/s peak; then ask what failure rate you would need to assume to add 25%. (4) Write down which entry in your own current project's log you could not reconstruct if the author left tomorrow.

Key takeaways

  • OPT-175B took about 56 days from the start of the successful lineage against about 33 days of ideal compute, with at least 35 manual and about 70 automatic hardware restarts, and an abandoned first lineage before it.
  • Three problem families interleave: hardware, numerical instability, and process or data.
  • The most useful fixes were operational: spares, health checks, automation, a pre-agreed fallback recipe, and lower learning rates when norms drifted.
  • Recovery time dominates elapsed time; at larger scale, failures arrive faster, so recovery must be automatic.
  • A public logbook is evidence of what was tried, not proof of why it worked; read it with that in mind.

Check your understanding

The final note puts the ideal training time at about 33 days; the successful lineage actually took 56 days from 11 November to 6 January. What best explains the gap?

  1. The model was larger than planned
  2. The GPUs ran well below their rated throughput the whole time
  3. The team trained for more than 300 billion tokens
  4. Time lost to restarts: work since the last checkpoint, and hours of detecting, replacing and restoring after each failure
The 33 days is explicitly "assuming no hardware issues, no numerical instabilities". Each restart costs lost progress plus recovery. Throughput of 147 TFLOP/s per GPU was while running, and the token budget stayed at 300 billion.

In the toy model, which single change reduces elapsed time the most?

  1. Cutting average recovery per restart from 4 hours to 1 hour
  2. Doubling the checkpoint interval
  3. Making the update step 10% faster without touching failures
  4. Changing the ideal job to 30 days of work
Recovery from 4 to 1 hour takes 55.5 days to 41.0. Doubling the checkpoint interval at 4 hours raised the finish to 61 days. A faster step helps, but failures still cost the same hours each. Reducing the work by 3 days saves about 3 days at most.

On 6 December five hosts went down together when the team held only four buffer nodes. What was the lesson drawn?

  1. Tensor parallelism should be abandoned
  2. Checkpoints should be taken every step
  3. The spare pool must be sized to the observed failure rate and the chance of correlated failures; the provider added 18 more hosts
  4. Use fewer GPUs so that fewer can fail
The 16 December note says the larger buffer pool "greatly reduced our experiment recovery time". Fewer GPUs would make the run slower, and checkpointing every step is far too costly for 1.6 TB checkpoints.

The team found that restarting Adam from a checkpoint did not apply their new beta1 and epsilon. What does this illustrate?

  1. Adam cannot be resumed from checkpoints
  2. Optimiser state saved in a checkpoint can override settings you change on restart, so a restart can silently differ from what you intended
  3. SGD needs a larger learning rate than Adam
  4. Loss scaling is incompatible with checkpoints
The 3 December note says "reinitializing the Adam optimizer from a checkpoint also reloaded the previously saved betas". It shows the need to verify that a change took effect. The SGD learning-rate problem was a separate issue they found afterwards.
