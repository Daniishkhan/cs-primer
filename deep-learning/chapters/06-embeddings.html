Embeddings: Words as Vectors

A neural network can only do arithmetic on numbers, and words are not numbers. This chapter follows the idea that solved that: give every word a short list of learned numbers so that words used in similar ways end up close together. We build it twice (by counting, and by word2vec's prediction game), measure what the geometry gets right and wrong on 17 million words of Wikipedia text, and finish with the cases where it misleads.

Why this matters

Every language model in this course starts with the same step: turn a token into a vector. The embedding table that does it is a sizeable fraction of a small model's parameters, it is the part you inspect first when a model behaves oddly, and the same idea runs through search (A5 chapter 7), recommendation and categorical features in tabular models. The word-level version in this chapter is the smallest setting where you can see all of it: how meaning can be turned into geometry, what training signal does it, and why "similar" in a vector space does not always mean what you hoped. After this chapter you will be able to train word vectors from scratch, evaluate them honestly, and explain why a Transformer's first layer is "just" a table.

From words to numbers

Suppose a model must read the word "cat". Its input has to be a vector of numbers, and the simplest scheme is to number the vocabulary (the fixed list of words the model knows, size V) and give each word a vector with V entries, all zero except a 1 in the word's own position. That is a one-hot vector. It is exact, it needs no training, and it has two serious flaws.

First, size: with a 50,000-word vocabulary every word is a vector of 50,000 numbers, nearly all zeros. Second, and worse, no similarity: any two different one-hot vectors have a dot product of zero (A0 chapter 5), so the representation says that "cat" is as different from "dog" as from "carburettor". A model fed these vectors cannot generalise from what it learned about cats to dogs; every word starts as a stranger. The remedy is an embedding: a dense vector of a few hundred numbers, learned from data, in which words used in similar ways have similar vectors.

import numpy as np
vocab = ["cat", "dog", "car", "truck"]
idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
onehot = np.eye(len(vocab))                         # row i is the one-hot vector of word i

print("one-hot cat:", onehot[idx["cat"]], " dog:", onehot[idx["dog"]])
print("dot(cat, dog) =", onehot[idx["cat"]] @ onehot[idx["dog"]], "  dot(cat, truck) =", onehot[idx["cat"]] @ onehot[idx["truck"]])

An embedding is a lookup table

An embedding table is a matrix E with one row per vocabulary entry and d columns, where d (called the embedding dimension, often written C or d_model later in this course) is a design choice from tens to thousands. Multiplying a one-hot vector by E picks out one row, which the last two lines of the script above confirmed: the product of a one-hot vector for "dog" with E is row "dog" of E. So the first layer of a network that reads words is a linear layer whose input is a one-hot vector, and in practice nobody does the multiplication. They index into the matrix (E[token_id]), which copies d numbers instead of doing V × d multiply-adds. That is all torch.nn.Embedding is.

The table has V × d parameters, which at realistic sizes is a large number:

Vocabulary sizes and widths in the first two rows come from the GPT-2 and BERT papers (Radford et al., 2019; Devlin et al., 2018); the third row is an illustrative assumption. In small models the embedding table is a fifth to a third of all parameters; in very large models it shrinks as a share, but its memory is still a gigabyte or so per copy in 16-bit numbers. Many language models save parameters by weight tying: the output layer that turns the final hidden vector into scores over the vocabulary reuses the same matrix, transposed (Press and Wolf, 2016, "Using the Output Embedding to Improve Language Models").

Everything so far is bookkeeping. The interesting question is where the numbers in E come from, which is a learning problem with no labels attached. Nobody has a dataset saying "the right vector for cat is ...". We need a source of supervision that costs nothing, and language provides one.

You shall know a word by the company it keeps

The linguist J. R. Firth wrote in 1957: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." This is the distributional hypothesis (a related form is due to Zellig Harris, 1954): words that occur in similar contexts have similar meanings. "Tiger" and "lion" appear near "jungle", "roar", "predator" and "cage"; "tiger" and "carburettor" do not. So instead of defining meaning, we measure context, and let the vectors fall out.

The counting approach

Choose a context window, say 5 words on either side. Over a large corpus, count how often each pair of words appears within the window of each other. The result is a co-occurrence matrix C with V rows and V columns, where C[w, c] is how many times word c appeared near word w. Row "tiger" is a (very sparse) description of the contexts of "tiger". Raw counts are dominated by words like "the", which appear near everything. The fix is pointwise mutual information (PMI), borrowed from information theory (A0 chapter 14):

PMI(w, c) = log [ P(w, c) / ( P(w) P(c) ) ]

It compares how often w and c occur together with how often they would if they were unrelated. Positive PMI means they co-occur more than chance; we clip negative values to zero (PPMI), because most negative values come from pairs that were simply never seen. Finally we compress each long, sparse row to d = 100 numbers with a truncated singular value decomposition (A0 chapter 7), which keeps the strongest directions of variation: words with similar rows end up with similar short vectors. Levy, Goldberg and Dagan ("Improving Distributional Similarity with Lessons Learned from Word Embeddings", 2015) describe this recipe and its details, including raising the context counts to the power 0.75 as done below.

The corpus is text8: the first 100 million characters of cleaned English Wikipedia text, prepared by Matt Mahoney (the file dates from 2006) and widely used for small word-vector experiments. It has 17,005,207 words. We keep the 8,000 most frequent words, count within a window of 5, and factor the PPMI matrix with a randomised SVD in NumPy.

import numpy as np, sys, os, time
t0 = time.time()
V, WIN, K = 8000, 5, 100
words = open(sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "data_text8/text8").read().split()
vocab, counts = np.unique(words, return_counts=True)
top = np.argsort(-counts, kind="stable")[:V]
itos = [vocab[i] for i in top]; stoi = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(itos)}
ids = np.fromiter((stoi.get(w, -1) for w in words), dtype=np.int32, count=len(words))

C = np.zeros((V, V), dtype=np.float32)
for d in range(1, WIN + 1):                         # count pairs of words d positions apart
    a, b = ids[:-d], ids[d:]; ok = (a >= 0) & (b >= 0)
    C += np.bincount(a[ok].astype(np.int64) * V + b[ok], minlength=V * V).reshape(V, V).astype(np.float32)
C += C.T                                            # symmetric window

total = C.sum(); row = C.sum(1, keepdims=True); col = C.sum(0, keepdims=True) ** 0.75
col = col / col.sum() * total                       # smoothed context counts
with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
    ppmi = np.maximum(np.log(C * total / (row * col)), 0).astype(np.float32)
ppmi[~np.isfinite(ppmi)] = 0

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                      # randomised SVD: project, orthogonalise, decompose
Q = ppmi @ rng.standard_normal((V, K + 20)).astype(np.float32)
for _ in range(3): Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(ppmi @ (ppmi.T @ Q))
U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(Q.T @ ppmi, full_matrices=False)
E = (Q @ U)[:, :K] * np.sqrt(S[:K])                # (V, K): one row per word
E /= np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)
np.save("data_text8/E.npy", E); open("data_text8/vocab.txt", "w").write("\n".join(itos))
print("vocab", V, " tokens", len(words), " vector shape", E.shape, f" ({time.time()-t0:.0f}s)")
vocab 8000  tokens 17005207  vector shape (8000, 100)  (14s)

That took about fifteen seconds on a laptop. The result is a matrix E of shape (8000, 100) whose rows we normalise to length 1, so that the dot product of two rows is their cosine similarity (the cosine of the angle between them; 1 means the same direction, 0 unrelated). To judge it, ask for each word's nearest neighbours:

import numpy as np

    sims = M @ M[stoi[w]]
    return [f"{itos[i]} {sims[i]:.2f}" for i in np.argsort(-sims)[1:k + 1]]

Look at what came out of counting alone. The neighbours of "france" are countries; of "monday", the other days; of "tiger", other big animals. The two lists to read most carefully are the last two. "Python" is next to "monty", "sketch" and "cleese": in Wikipedia text from this period, the comedy troupe dominated the word's contexts, and there is exactly one vector for the word, so the programming language is invisible. And "doctor" is next to "series", "daleks" and "episode", because the television show Doctor Who is mentioned often enough to outweigh the profession. One vector per word cannot represent several senses at once; it blends them in proportion to their frequency in the corpus. That limitation is the reason contextual models took over (see the end of the chapter).

The last two lines of the output make two further points. The space has no privileged axes: multiplying every vector by a random rotation leaves every neighbour list unchanged, because rotations preserve dot products. Only relative positions carry information. And the arrows from a country to its capital are far more parallel than chance (average cosine 0.58, against about 0 for random word pairs), a property we return to below.

Common misconception

"Dimension 17 of the embedding means something, perhaps 'royalty' or 'animal'." Individual coordinates do not carry meaning. The rotation test above shows why: you can mix the coordinates with any orthogonal matrix and every similarity is identical, so there is no principled sense in which coordinate 17 is special. What carries information is direction and distance. A "gender direction" or a "capital-of direction" can exist, but it is a combination of many coordinates, not one.

word2vec: learning the vectors by prediction

Counting needs a V × V matrix, which is fine for 8,000 words and impossible for 1,000,000. In 2013 Mikolov and colleagues ("Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space" and "Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality") showed that you can learn the vectors directly with a tiny neural network trained on a prediction task, scanning the corpus once per pass without ever building the matrix. The first paper reports that it takes "less than a day" to learn high-quality vectors from a 1.6 billion word data set. The family is called word2vec.

The skip-gram variant plays this game: given a word in the middle of a sentence, predict a word within the window around it. Slide a window along the text and each position produces several (word, context) training pairs.

There are two vectors per word, an input vector v (used when the word is the centre) and an output vector u (used when it is a context word). The model scores a pair by the dot product v · u: the more aligned they are, the more likely the pair. Turned into a probability by a softmax over the whole vocabulary this would cost V dot products per pair, which is far too slow for a large vocabulary. Negative sampling replaces the V-way question with a handful of yes-or-no questions: is this pair real, or is it a random pairing? For each real pair (w, c) we also draw k random "noise" words n1 ... nk and minimise

Plug in numbers. Two-dimensional vectors, one real context word and k = 2 noise words. Each line is one term of the loss.

The idea

  1. v_w, u_c and two noise vectors u_n: made-up 2-d vectors
  2. real pair: cost ← −log σ(v·u_c)
  3. each noise word: cost ← −log σ(−v·u_n)
  4. loss ← real cost + sum of the noise costs
"""Word2vec negative-sampling loss for one real pair and k = 2 noise words."""
import math


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))


def dot(p, q):
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(p, q))


# ── 1. Two-dimensional vectors (made up) ─────────────────────────────────
v_w = [1.0, 0.5]                  # input vector of the centre word
u_c = [0.8, 0.6]                  # output vector of the true context word
noise = [[-0.5, 0.4], [0.9, -0.2]]   # output vectors of k = 2 noise words

# ── 2. The real pair: want sigma(v . u_c) near 1 ─────────────────────────
score = dot(v_w, u_c)
real_term = -math.log(sigmoid(score))
print(f"real pair: v.u = {score:+.2f}  sigma = {sigmoid(score):.3f}  "
      f"cost = {real_term:.3f}")

# ── 3. Each noise word: want sigma(-v . u_n) near 1, i.e. v . u_n low ────
noise_total = 0.0
for n, u_n in enumerate(noise, start=1):
    score = dot(v_w, u_n)
    term = -math.log(sigmoid(-score))
    noise_total += term
    print(f"noise {n}:   v.u = {score:+.2f}  sigma(-v.u) = "
          f"{sigmoid(-score):.3f}  cost = {term:.3f}")

# ── 4. The loss: the real-pair cost plus every noise cost ────────────────
print(f"loss = {real_term:.3f} + {noise_total:.3f} = "
      f"{real_term + noise_total:.3f}")
real pair: v.u = +1.10  sigma = 0.750  cost = 0.287
noise 1:   v.u = -0.30  sigma(-v.u) = 0.574  cost = 0.554
noise 2:   v.u = +0.80  sigma(-v.u) = 0.310  cost = 1.171
loss = 0.287 + 1.725 = 2.013

where σ is the logistic sigmoid (chapter 01). The first term wants the real pair's score high. The others want each noise pair's score low. Mikolov et al. draw noise words from the unigram distribution raised to the power 3/4, which they found worked "significantly" better than either the plain unigram or the uniform distribution, and they suggest k between 5 and 20 for small datasets and 2 to 5 for large ones. They also discard frequent words such as "the" at random while reading (subsampling, with a threshold around 10-5), which speeds training and improves vectors for rarer words.

The following worked example uses two-dimensional vectors so that every number can be checked. Step through one update.

Step through

One skip-gram update, with real numbers

  1. The setup. The corpus contains "the cat eats ...", so (cat, eats) is a true pair. Training also draws random noise words, here rice and the. Each word has an input vector (for cat) or an output vector (for the context words); all four are drawn here as arrows in a plane.
  2. Score every pair. The dot product of cat's vector with each context vector, squashed by the sigmoid, is the model's probability that the pair is real. Right now it is nearly 0.5 for everything: the model cannot tell the true context from noise yet. The loss is the sum of -log(0.517) for the true pair and -log(1 - 0.445) and -log(1 - 0.555) for the noise.
  3. Read off the gradients. The true pair's score should rise, so the gradient pulls cat towards eats (and eats towards cat). The noise pairs' scores should fall, so cat is pushed away from rice and from the. A noise word that currently scores high is pushed harder.
  4. Take the step. Cat and eats rotate towards each other, the noise vectors move away from cat, and the loss falls from 2.057 to 1.821 on this one example. Repeat over millions of pairs and words that share contexts end up with similar vectors.

The numbers come from this script, so you can check each line of the stepper:

import numpy as np
sig = lambda x: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))
v = np.array([0.5, -0.2])                              # centre word "cat": input vector
u_pos = np.array([0.3, 0.4])                           # context word "eats": output vector
u_neg = np.array([[-0.4, 0.1], [0.2, -0.6]])           # two sampled negatives, "rice" and "the"
lr = 0.5

def loss(v, u_pos, u_neg):
    return -np.log(sig(v @ u_pos)) - np.log(sig(-(u_neg @ v))).sum()

print("scores  positive:", round(float(v @ u_pos), 3), "  negatives:", np.round(u_neg @ v, 3))
print("sigma   positive:", round(float(sig(v @ u_pos)), 3), "  negatives:", np.round(sig(u_neg @ v), 3))
print("loss before:", round(float(loss(v, u_pos, u_neg)), 4))

g_pos = sig(v @ u_pos) - 1                             # dL/d(score) for the true pair (negative: push score up)
g_neg = sig(u_neg @ v)                                 # dL/d(score) for noise pairs (positive: push score down)
grad_v = g_pos * u_pos + g_neg @ u_neg
print("gradient on v:", np.round(grad_v, 4))
v2 = v - lr * grad_v
u_pos2 = u_pos - lr * g_pos * v
u_neg2 = u_neg - lr * g_neg[:, None] * v
print("new v:", np.round(v2, 4), " new u_eats:", np.round(u_pos2, 4))
print("new u_rice:", np.round(u_neg2[0], 4), " new u_the:", np.round(u_neg2[1], 4))
print("scores  positive:", round(float(v2 @ u_pos2), 3), "  negatives:", np.round(u_neg2 @ v2, 3))
print("loss after :", round(float(loss(v2, u_pos2, u_neg2)), 4))

The update rule is not special. It is the gradient of the loss above, as chapter 02 taught: for the true pair the gradient of the score is σ(s) − 1, which is negative so the score is pushed up; for noise pairs it is σ(s), which is positive so the score is pushed down, and harder the more the model wrongly believes the pair. Over millions of pairs, two words that keep appearing next to the same contexts receive the same pulls and pushes, so their vectors drift together. That is the entire mechanism by which "similar company" becomes "similar vector".

The whole algorithm in 40 lines

Here it is at small scale on a toy language we control: two kinds of noun, six animals and six foods, in templated sentences ("the cat eats bread", "we cook rice with soup"). Animals share contexts with each other, foods with each other. The code builds all the (word, context) pairs, samples noise from the 3/4-power distribution, and does minibatch SGD by hand with the gradients above (this version uses vectors of 8 numbers).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
animals = ["cat", "dog", "horse", "cow", "fox", "rabbit"]
foods = ["bread", "cheese", "apple", "rice", "soup", "cake"]
sents = []
for _ in range(3000):                               # a toy language with two kinds of nouns
    a, a2, f, f2 = rng.choice(animals), rng.choice(animals), rng.choice(foods), rng.choice(foods)
    sents.append(rng.choice([f"the {a} eats {f}", f"the {a} chases the {a2}", f"the {a} sleeps",
                             f"we cook {f} with {f2}", f"we buy {f} and {f2}", f"the {a} runs"]).split())
vocab = sorted({w for s in sents for w in s}); idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}; V, D, WIN, NEG = len(vocab), 8, 2, 5

pairs = [(idx[s[i]], idx[s[j]]) for s in sents for i in range(len(s))
         for j in range(max(0, i - WIN), min(len(s), i + WIN + 1)) if j != i]
pairs = np.array(pairs); freq = np.bincount(pairs[:, 0], minlength=V) ** 0.75; noise = freq / freq.sum()
print(f"{len(sents)} sentences, vocabulary {V}, {len(pairs)} (word, context) pairs")

Win = (rng.standard_normal((V, D)) * 0.1)           # "input" vectors v (what we keep)
Wout = np.zeros((V, D))                             # "output" vectors u for contexts
sig = lambda x: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))
lr = 0.05
print(f"chance level (all scores zero): {(1 + NEG) * np.log(2):.3f}")
for epoch in range(1, 11):
    rng.shuffle(pairs); total = 0.0
    for b in range(0, len(pairs), 128):
        w, c = pairs[b:b + 128, 0], pairs[b:b + 128, 1]; n = rng.choice(V, (len(w), NEG), p=noise)
        v, uc, un = Win[w], Wout[c], Wout[n]        # (B,D), (B,D), (B,NEG,D)
        sp, sn = sig((v * uc).sum(1)), sig(np.einsum("bd,bkd->bk", v, un))
        total += -(np.log(sp + 1e-9).sum() + np.log(1 - sn + 1e-9).sum())
        gv = (sp - 1)[:, None] * uc + np.einsum("bk,bkd->bd", sn, un)
        np.add.at(Wout, c, -lr * (sp - 1)[:, None] * v)
        np.add.at(Wout, n, -lr * sn[:, :, None] * v[:, None, :])
        np.add.at(Win, w, -lr * gv)
    if epoch in (1, 2, 5, 10): print(f"epoch {epoch:2d}  mean loss per pair {total / len(pairs):.3f}")

E = Win / np.linalg.norm(Win, axis=1, keepdims=True)
cos = lambda a, b: float(E[idx[a]] @ E[idx[b]])
within = np.mean([cos(a, b) for a in animals for b in animals if a < b])
between = np.mean([cos(a, f) for a in animals for f in foods])
print(f"mean cosine, animal-animal {within:.2f}   animal-food {between:.2f}")
for w in ["cat", "bread"]:

Check the story the output tells. The loss starts at 4.159 (that is 6 × ln 2: with all scores at zero, every sigmoid is 0.5, and one real plus five noise terms each cost ln 2) and drops quickly, then flattens near 2.2. It cannot reach zero because the sentences are random: after "the cat eats" the next word really is one of six foods, so no model can be sure. The learned geometry is the point: animals have a mean cosine of 0.97 with each other and 0.32 with foods; "cat" has every other animal as its nearest neighbours and "bread" has the other foods. The model was never told what an animal is. The only information it had was which words appear in which contexts.

Two roads to the same place

Levy and Goldberg ("Neural Word Embedding as Implicit Matrix Factorization", NeurIPS 2014) showed that skip-gram with negative sampling is, at its optimum, factorising a word-context matrix of PMI values shifted by log k. So the counting method above and the prediction method are close cousins: one factors the matrix explicitly with SVD, the other approaches the same factorisation by gradient descent without ever writing the matrix down. In their later comparison, much of word2vec's advantage turned out to come from design choices such as the smoothing and subsampling, which can be transferred to count-based methods. GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014) sits in between: it fits vectors to the logarithm of the co-occurrence counts directly.

What the geometry gets right, and what it does not

Analogies

The result that made word vectors famous is that relations appear as consistent offsets between vectors. Mikolov, Yih and Zweig ("Linguistic Regularities in Continuous Space Word Representations", 2013) showed that vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) lands near vec(queen). Then measure it properly rather than cherry-picking. For three relations, take every pair of examples from a list (for example france:paris and germany:berlin) and ask the model to complete the analogy using the other pair's first word, counting a hit only when the correct word is ranked first among all 8,000. import numpy as np
E = np.load("data_text8/E.npy"); itos = open("data_text8/vocab.txt").read().split("\n")
stoi = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(itos)}

def ranking(a, b, c, exclude=True):                     # a is to b as c is to ?  ->  words ranked by cosine with b - a + c
    v = E[stoi[b]] - E[stoi[a]] + E[stoi[c]]
    return [itos[i] for i in np.argsort(-(E @ v)) if not exclude or itos[i] not in (a, b, c)]

With a larger corpus (the original vectors used billions of words) and tuned settings accuracy is much higher; with 17 million words, treat the numbers above as a realistic picture of a small model.

Bias

Embeddings learn from text, and text reflects its writers. Caliskan, Bryson and Narayanan ("Semantics derived automatically from language corpora contain human-like biases", Science, 2017) showed that embeddings reproduce the association patterns found in human psychology tests, and Bolukbasi et al. ("Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker? Debiasing Word Embeddings", 2016) showed gender stereotypes in vectors trained on news text. You can see the effect with a single measurement: build a "male minus female" direction by averaging the differences of pairs such as he/she and king/queen, and project occupation words on it.

import numpy as np
E = np.load("data_text8/E.npy"); itos = open("data_text8/vocab.txt").read().split("\n")
stoi = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(itos)}

         ("son", "daughter"), ("husband", "wife"), ("king", "queen"), ("his", "her")]

print("occupation    projection on the male-minus-female direction")

That is the polysemy problem again, now affecting a bias measurement. The practical lesson is that when embeddings feed a downstream system (a résumé screener, a search ranker, a recommender), associations in the training text pass through to the behaviour, and debiasing the vectors tends to hide the symptom more than remove it. Test the system's outputs, not only its inputs; design chapter 20 and the evaluation course return to this.

Other limits worth knowing

  • Frequency. Rare words get noisy vectors and frequent words distort distances. Real pipelines filter by minimum count (word2vec's default drops words seen fewer than a handful of times).
  • Similarity is not synonymy. Antonyms such as "hot" and "cold" occur in the same contexts and therefore sit close together. A vector space measures substitutability in text, not agreement in meaning.
  • Out-of-vocabulary words. A word never seen has no row. fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2016, "Enriching Word Vectors with Subword Information") builds a word's vector from its character n-grams, so even a misspelling gets a sensible one. Chapter 13 takes this further, giving up whole words for subword tokens.
  • One vector per word. The "python" and "doctor" results above.

Analogy: a map made from phone-call logs

Imagine drawing a map of a country knowing nothing except which towns phone each other and how often. Towns that call each other a lot get placed near each other, and with enough calls the map starts to look like geography: regions, then roads. Word vectors are that map, drawn from who "calls" whom in text. No one told the algorithm where Paris is; its position follows from its neighbours.

Where it breaks down: a town is in one place. A word like "bank" or "python" has several senses, and the map has to put it somewhere in the middle of all of them. Phone logs also describe what people do, not what is right: if callers favour certain towns for the wrong reasons, so does the map.

Beyond words

Embeddings inside a Transformer

Part 3 of this course builds a GPT. Its first layer is exactly the table of this chapter, indexed by token instead of word (chapter 13 explains tokens). The difference from word2vec is the training signal: nobody pre-trains the table with a separate game. The table is initialised randomly and trained by backpropagation as part of the language-modelling objective (chapter 14), so the vectors become whatever helps predict the next token. They still end up with neighbours-are-related structure, for the same distributional reason. What changes is that a Transformer then updates the vector at every layer using the surrounding words (chapter 09), which fixes the one-vector-per-word problem. ELMo (Peters et al., 2018, "Deep contextualized word representations") and BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) were the first widely adopted models whose vector for "bank" depends on the sentence it is in. The word-level tables of this chapter are therefore called static embeddings, and the vectors that come out of a deep model are contextual embeddings.

Embedding anything with an identity

The lookup-table idea works for any discrete thing that can be seen in a context: products bought in the same basket, songs played in the same session, users who click the same items, nodes in a graph, and categorical columns in a table of data. Guo and Berkhahn ("Entity Embeddings of Categorical Variables", 2016) used learned embeddings for store and day-of-week columns in a sales-forecasting competition, which A1 chapter 19 and A1 chapter 17 treat in the tabular setting. The sentence and document embeddings used for search are produced by a full Transformer rather than a lookup, but they are compared the same way, by cosine similarity, and retrieved with the nearest-neighbour indexes of A5 chapter 11.

Systems connection

An embedding lookup is a gather: read a few rows from a very large table by index. It does almost no arithmetic per byte moved, so it is limited by memory bandwidth, not compute (the memory hierarchy). Recommendation systems can have embedding tables of billions of rows that no single machine holds, so the table is sharded across machines and a lookup becomes a network call, with all the latency and failure questions of any network call. Searching vectors by similarity, rather than looking up by id, is the job of the vector indexes of search systems: the exact search in this chapter compares a query with all 8,000 rows, which does not scale to a billion, so practical systems accept approximate answers for speed.

State of play, October 2026

Static word vectors are rarely the final component of a modern system. The embedding table inside a language model is learned jointly with everything else, and applications that need text vectors, such as search, usually call a dedicated embedding model that outputs one vector per passage. Which of these models tops a public leaderboard changes frequently, so choose with an evaluation on your own data (A5 chapter 6) rather than by ranking. What does not change is the geometric reading from this chapter: nearest neighbours by cosine, directions as relations, and the caveats about bias and sense blending. Word2vec itself remains a useful baseline for small domain corpora, because it trains in minutes on a laptop and needs no GPU.

Key takeaways

  • One-hot vectors are exact but give no notion of similarity. An embedding is a learned dense vector per word, stored as a V × d table that is indexed, not multiplied.
  • The distributional hypothesis supplies free supervision: words in similar contexts get similar vectors. You can get it by counting (PPMI plus SVD) or by prediction (word2vec's skip-gram with negative sampling), and the two are closely related.
  • Individual coordinates mean nothing; directions and distances do. Rotating the whole space changes no similarity.
  • Analogy arithmetic works for some relations and fails for others, and it is easy to overstate: always exclude the query words and report accuracy over many questions.
  • Embeddings absorb the associations, biases and sense frequencies of their training text. One vector per word blends senses; contextual models fix that.
  • In a Transformer the embedding table is learned end to end with the model. The same idea extends to products, users and categorical features.

Check your understanding

A vocabulary has 10,000 words and each word is given a one-hot vector. What is the dot product between the vectors for two different words, and what does it imply?

  1. It is 1, so every pair counts as identical
  2. It is 0, so the representation encodes no similarity between any pair of words
  3. It depends on how often the two words occur together
  4. It is the cosine of the angle between meanings, which is small for unrelated words
Different one-hot vectors have their single 1 in different positions, so the dot product is 0 for every pair: "cat" is as far from "dog" as from "carburettor". Co-occurrence counts and meaning do not enter the one-hot representation at all, which is why dense learned vectors were introduced.

An embedding table has 50,000 rows and 768 columns. A batch contains the token with id 1,204. What does the first layer do for that token?

  1. Multiplies a 50,000-long one-hot vector by a 50,000 by 768 matrix, which costs about 38 million multiply-adds
  2. Computes the cosine similarity of the token with every other row
  3. Averages all 50,000 rows weighted by frequency
  4. Reads row 1,204 of the table, 768 numbers, and passes them on
The one-hot product would give exactly row 1,204, so frameworks index the row directly. The cosine and the frequency average are things you might compute to inspect the table, not what the layer does. During training only the rows that appeared in the batch receive gradients.

In skip-gram with negative sampling, why are noise words drawn from the unigram distribution raised to the power 3/4 rather than simply uniformly?

  1. Uniform sampling picks rare words far too often relative to real text, while the plain unigram picks very frequent words too often; 3/4 is a compromise that worked best in the authors' tests
  2. Because the 3/4 power makes the softmax cheaper to compute
  3. Because rare words should never be used as noise
  4. Because it guarantees that the true context word is never drawn as noise
The exponent flattens the frequency distribution part of the way: frequent words still appear as noise more than rare ones, but less dominantly than in the raw unigram. Mikolov et al. report that it outperformed both alternatives. It has nothing to do with the cost of a softmax (negative sampling avoids the softmax entirely), and rare words are used as noise in practice.

Word vectors trained on a news corpus place "doctor" near "nurse", and a team uses them as features in a CV screener. What is the most important concern?

  1. The vectors are too small to carry any information
  2. Cosine similarity cannot be computed on vectors with 100 dimensions
  3. Associations in the text, including stereotypes, flow into the screener's behaviour, so its outputs need testing for them
  4. The vectors will always be identical for every word after training
Embeddings absorb the associations of their training text, and downstream models inherit them. The first, second and fourth options are false: 100 dimensions are plenty for similarity, and training makes vectors differ. Measure the screener's decisions across groups rather than trusting that the inputs are neutral.

You compute king minus man plus woman and take the single nearest vector, without excluding any words. What will often happen, and why?

  1. The answer is always queen, which is why the test is so popular
  2. The nearest vector is frequently "king" or another input word itself, because the offset b - a + c is still closer to b than to anything else
  3. The result is zero, since king and man cancel
  4. The computation fails because the vectors have different lengths
In the experiment the "inputs allowed" accuracy was far below the "inputs excluded" one (3% against 43% for comparatives), because the query point stays close to b. Published analogy tests therefore exclude the three input words. The vectors do not cancel to zero, and all word vectors have the same length.

Two sentences contain "bank": one about a river, one about a loan. With static word2vec vectors, how do the two uses compare?

  1. They get different vectors, since the contexts differ
  2. They get vectors near "river" and "loan" in separate dimensions
  3. They are averaged only if the sentences are in the same document
  4. They get the same single vector, a blend of senses weighted by corpus frequency
A static embedding table has one row per word type, so every occurrence of "bank" maps to the same vector. Contextual models solve this by computing a new vector for each occurrence from its sentence. The chapter's "python" and "doctor" neighbours showed the blending: the dominant sense in the corpus wins.
