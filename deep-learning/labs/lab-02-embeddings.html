Lab 2: Word Embeddings

Chapter 6 argued that a word's meaning can be read off the company it keeps, and showed vectors learned by predicting which words appear near which. In this lab you write that learning algorithm yourself: skip-gram with negative sampling, the method behind word2vec, in plain Python. You turn raw text into (centre, context) pairs, draw noise words from the 3/4-power distribution, compute the loss and its gradient for one pair (and reproduce the worked example of chapter 6 digit for digit), run stochastic gradient descent over tens of thousands of pairs, and then ask the finished vectors for nearest neighbours and analogies. The corpus is a small made-up language whose structure you know, so you can tell exactly what the model learned and what it did not.

Why this lab matters

The first thing every language model does with a token is look up a row of a table (chapter 6), and the numbers in that table are learned by gradient descent like any other weights. Skip-gram with negative sampling is the smallest complete example of learning such a table without labels: the text supplies both question and answer, and the supervision is free. Everything you build here returns later at larger scale. The "score a pair with a dot product, push real pairs up and noise pairs down" idea is the contrastive loss that trains the embedders behind search and retrieval (A4 chapter 23). The geometry you probe at the end, cosine similarity and offsets between vectors, is the vocabulary of every vector database.

The second reason is calibration. Word-vector analogies ("king minus man plus woman") are the most quoted result in the area and one of the most over-sold. Chapter 6 measured them on real text and found that results are patchy; here you can see in a controlled setting why, and the checker makes you handle the classic trap that inflated early reports: letting the question words themselves be the answer.

A toy language, a few seconds, no downloads

Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. Real word vectors need millions of words (chapter 6 used 17 million words of Wikipedia text). Pure Python cannot do that in the time budget, so make_corpus() in the starter file generates 3,000 short sentences in a toy language with 60 words: six animals (each with a favourite adjective, in singular and plural), six foods, and four pairs of people (king and queen, man and woman, boy and girl, prince and princess) who share their items and differ in the pronoun. Because you wrote the rules, you know what a good embedding must discover. Everything is seeded, so python3 check.py solution gives identical numbers every run and takes about three seconds. Milestone 4 trains a small model; milestone 5 uses saved vectors from the reference solution (vectors.json) so that it does not depend on your training loop being finished.

What you'll build

The milestones:

  1. Tokens, vocabulary and pairs: tokenise text into sentences of words, build_vocab with counts, and make_pairs inside a window.
  2. Negative sampling: noise_distribution (counts to the power 3/4) and sample_negatives with a cumulative table and binary search, never returning the true context.

Milestone 1: tokens, vocabulary and pairs

The distributional hypothesis of chapter 6 ("you shall know a word by the company it keeps", Firth, 1957) needs data in a specific shape: for each word occurrence, the words near it. The pipeline has three steps, and none of them involves a neural network.

Tokenise. Split text into sentences at ., ! and ?, and each sentence into lowercase words. A word here is a run of letters and apostrophes, so "don't" stays whole. This is deliberately crude: real tokenisers are the subject of chapter 13, and word-level splitting is exactly what that chapter replaces. Windows do not cross sentence boundaries, which is why the sentences matter.

Build the vocabulary. Count every word, keep those seen at least min_count times, and sort by count (most frequent first, ties alphabetical, so the order is the same on every machine). A word's id is its position in that list; everything downstream works with ids, because ids index rows of a table. counts[i] is kept for the noise distribution in milestone 2.

Make pairs. The skip-gram game: given a word, predict a word near it. For each position in a sentence, the word there is the centre word, and every word within window positions on either side is a context word. Each (centre, context) combination is one training pair. Words missing from the vocabulary (below min_count) are dropped before the window is applied, so the window closes over the gap, as word2vec does. With window 2, a word in the middle of a long sentence yields four pairs, one near the edges yields fewer. For a sentence of L words the number of pairs is the sum over positions of the number of neighbours in range; for L = 5 and window 2 that is 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14.

from starter import tokenise, build_vocab, make_pairs

text = "The purring cat eats bread. The barking dog chases the purring cat!"
sentences = tokenise(text)
vocab, counts = build_vocab(sentences)
index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
print(sentences)

print(len(pairs), "pairs from the first sentence, for example:")

def tokenise(text):
    """Split text into sentences (on . ! ?) and each sentence into lowercase words."""
    sentences = []
    for chunk in re.split(r"[.!?]+", text):
        words = re.findall(r"[a-z']+", chunk.lower())
        if words:
            sentences.append(words)
    return sentences

def build_vocab(sentences, min_count=1):
    """Return (vocab, counts): words seen at least min_count times, most frequent first.

    Ties are broken alphabetically, so the order is deterministic. counts[i] is the count of vocab[i].
    """
    freq = {}
    for s in sentences:
        for w in s:
            freq[w] = freq.get(w, 0) + 1
    vocab = sorted((w for w in freq if freq[w] >= min_count), key=lambda w: (-freq[w], w))
    return vocab, [freq[w] for w in vocab]

def make_pairs(sentences, index, window):
    """All (centre_id, context_id) pairs with the context within `window` words of the centre.

    Words not in `index` are dropped first, so the window closes over the gap (as word2vec does).
    A word is never paired with itself at the same position; windows do not cross sentences.
    """
    pairs = []
    for s in sentences:
        ids = [index[w] for w in s if w in index]
        for i, centre in enumerate(ids):
            for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(ids), i + window + 1)):
                if j != i:
                    pairs.append((centre, ids[j]))
    return pairs

Milestone 2: negative sampling

Now the idea that makes word2vec cheap. The natural way to train "predict the context word" is a softmax over the whole vocabulary: score the centre word against every one of the V words, normalise, and take the log of the true word's probability. That costs V dot products per pair; for a vocabulary of 50,000 words and tens of millions of pairs, it is far too slow, and the normaliser touches every output vector every time. Negative sampling (Mikolov et al., 2013, "Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality") replaces the V-way question with a handful of yes-or-no questions. For each real pair, draw k random "noise" words and ask the model to say "real" for the true pair and "not real" for each noise pair. With k = 5 that is 6 dot products instead of 50,000, about 8,000 times fewer for that vocabulary.

Which words should be the noise? Not a uniform draw: a uniform sampler would spend most of its negatives on rare words the model barely knows, which are easy to reject. Not the plain word frequency either: that gives "the" a fifth of all negatives (in our corpus 20.9%) and starves the content words. Mikolov et al. found that drawing from the noise distribution proportional to count3/4 worked best: it shifts probability from the most frequent words to the rarer ones without ignoring frequency. The table below shows it on the toy corpus.

from starter import make_corpus, build_vocab, noise_distribution

vocab, counts = build_vocab(make_corpus())
unigram = noise_distribution(counts, power=1.0)
smooth = noise_distribution(counts)                 # count ** 0.75
print(f"{'word':8s} {'count':>6s} {'unigram':>8s} {'^0.75':>8s} {'ratio':>6s}")
for w in ("the", "he", "cat", "bread", "queen", "tutor"):
    i = vocab.index(w)
    print(f"{w:8s} {counts[i]:6d} {unigram[i]:8.4f} {smooth[i]:8.4f} {smooth[i] / unigram[i]:6.2f}")

"The" drops from 20.9% to 12.3% of the draws (a ratio of 0.59), while "cat", a rare word here, rises by 55%. The exponent 0.75 is an empirical choice, not a theorem. Setting it to 1 gives the plain unigram distribution and setting it to 0 gives a uniform one, both of which the checker lets you try.

Sampling from an arbitrary distribution needs one trick. Build the cumulative table of running totals (the given cumulative does this), draw a uniform number u between 0 and the last total, and find which interval it falls in with binary search (bisect.bisect_right). Each word owns an interval as long as its probability, so it is chosen with exactly that probability, in O(log V) time per draw. There is one more rule: the noise must never include the true context word of the current pair, or the model would be told that the same pair is both real and fake. If a draw lands on that word, draw again. This re-normalises the remaining probabilities, which the checker tests by measuring frequencies over 20,000 draws. It also insists you use only the rng object you are given, not the global random module, so that a training run is reproducible from its seed.

import random
from starter import make_corpus, build_vocab, noise_distribution, cumulative, sample_negatives

vocab, counts = build_vocab(make_corpus())
probs = noise_distribution(counts)
cum = cumulative(probs)
the = vocab.index("the")
rng = random.Random(0)
draws = [n for _ in range(4000) for n in sample_negatives(cum, 5, the, rng)]   # 20,000 negatives for the context 'the'
print("times 'the' was drawn:", draws.count(the))
for w in ("he", "bread", "tutor"):
    i = vocab.index(w)
    expected = probs[i] / (1 - probs[the])            # re-normalised once 'the' is excluded
    print(f"{w:6s} drawn {draws.count(i) / len(draws):.4f}   expected {expected:.4f}")

he     drawn 0.0440   expected 0.0441
bread  drawn 0.0217   expected 0.0224

Excluding "the" changes the others' frequencies by the factor 1 / (1 − 0.1234); the draws match the expected re-normalised values to within sampling error (4,000 draws of 5 each).

def noise_distribution(counts, power=0.75):
    """Probabilities proportional to count ** power (Mikolov et al. use 3/4)."""
    w = [c ** power for c in counts]
    total = sum(w)
    return [x / total for x in w]

def sample_negatives(cum, k, avoid, rng):
    """Draw k word ids from the distribution whose running totals are `cum`, never returning `avoid`.

    Draw u = rng.random() * cum[-1] and take the first id whose running total exceeds u (bisect);
    if it equals `avoid`, draw again.
    """
    out = []
    while len(out) < k:
        n = bisect.bisect_right(cum, rng.random() * cum[-1])
        if n != avoid:
            out.append(n)
    return out

Milestone 3: the loss and its gradient for one pair

Each word has two vectors (chapter 6): an input vector v used when it is the centre word (this is the one you keep), and an output vector u used when it is a context word. A pair is scored by the dot product s = v · u, and the sigmoid σ(s) = 1 / (1 + e−s) turns it into the model's probability that the pair is real. For a centre vector v, a true context upos and noise vectors u1 to uk, the loss is

L = − ln σ(v · upos) − ∑i=1..k ln σ(− v · ui)

The first term is small when the true pair scores high. Each other term is small when the noise pair scores low (using σ(−s) = 1 − σ(s)). This is the binary cross-entropy of A0 chapter 14 applied to k + 1 yes-or-no questions with known answers. As a check on your intuition: with every vector zero, every score is 0, every σ is 0.5 and each of the k + 1 terms costs ln 2, so the loss is (k + 1) ln 2, which is 4.159 for k = 5. Training must take the loss well below that.

The gradient follows from the chain rule of chapter 2, and it is simple because of the same trick as softmax cross-entropy. For a score s with target 1, ∂L/∂s = σ(s) − 1, which is negative: increasing the score lowers the loss. For a score with target 0, ∂L/∂s = σ(s), which is positive and large when the model wrongly believes the pair is real. Since s = v · u, the gradient on u is (∂L/∂s) · v, and the gradient on v collects (∂L/∂s) · u over the true pair and every noise word.

Here is the worked example of chapter 6, run through your functions. The 2-dimensional vectors are small enough to check by hand: v · upos = 0.5 × 0.3 + (−0.2) × 0.4 = 0.07, so σ = 0.517, and the two noise scores are −0.22 and 0.22. One gradient step with learning rate 0.5 lowers the loss from 2.0571 to 1.8209, the numbers printed in the chapter's step-through.

One practical point: math.exp overflows for arguments above about 709, so a naive 1 / (1 + math.exp(-x)) raises an OverflowError for x below −709. Real training occasionally produces scores that large, so sigmoid must be safe for any input. For x ≥ 0 compute 1 / (1 + e−x); for x < 0 compute ex / (1 + ex), which only ever exponentiates a negative number. And sgns_grads must compute all gradients from the vectors as they are now, without changing any of them; the training loop updates afterwards.

def sigmoid(x):
    """1 / (1 + e^-x), safe for any x (no OverflowError for large negative x)."""
    if x >= 0:
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-x))
    e = math.exp(x)
    return e / (1.0 + e)

def sgns_loss(v, u_pos, u_negs):
    """-ln sigmoid(v . u_pos) - sum over negatives of ln sigmoid(-v . u_n)."""
    loss = -math.log(sigmoid(dot(v, u_pos)))
    for u in u_negs:
        loss -= math.log(sigmoid(-dot(v, u)))
    return loss

def sgns_grads(v, u_pos, u_negs):
    """Gradients of sgns_loss: returns (grad_v, grad_u_pos, grad_u_negs).

    With s = v . u: d loss / d s is sigmoid(s) - 1 for the true pair and sigmoid(s) for a noise pair.
    """
    g_pos = sigmoid(dot(v, u_pos)) - 1.0
    g_negs = [sigmoid(dot(v, u)) for u in u_negs]
    grad_v = [g_pos * a for a in u_pos]
    for g, u in zip(g_negs, u_negs):
        grad_v = [gv + g * a for gv, a in zip(grad_v, u)]
    grad_u_pos = [g_pos * a for a in v]
    grad_u_negs = [[g * a for a in v] for g in g_negs]
    return grad_v, grad_u_pos, grad_u_negs

Milestone 4: the training loop

With a loss and a gradient for one pair, training is a loop (A0 chapter 10). Keep two tables, W_in and W_out, each with one row of d numbers per word. Initialise W_in with small random numbers (uniform in ±0.5/d, as in word2vec) and W_out with zeros. That is safe because the gradient on v is built from the u vectors and the gradient on u from v: v is random so W_out starts receiving gradients on the first step, and then W_in follows. The loss at the start is exactly (k + 1) ln 2 = 4.159, the chance level you computed above.

Each epoch, shuffle the list of pairs with your seeded rng, then for each pair (w, c): draw k negatives for c (milestone 2), fetch the rows v = W_in[w], upos = W_out[c] and the noise rows, compute the loss and gradients (milestone 3), and subtract lr times each gradient from its row. This is stochastic gradient descent with a batch of one pair: noisy, but each step touches only seven rows of 16 numbers (the centre word, the true context and five noise words), and there are 47,620 pairs per epoch. Two details matter. First, compute all the gradients before changing any vector, because the gradient on v uses the old u vectors and the gradients on the u use the old v. Second, a noise word may appear twice in one draw; updating its row twice is correct and harmless.

Only the rows of the words in the current pair change. This is why embedding tables scale: a step touches k + 2 rows of a table that may have 100,000, and the update is sparse (chapter 6 makes the same point about the backward pass of a lookup).

Run it for six epochs on the full corpus. The loss that is returned is the mean per pair over the epoch, so the first epoch's average already includes much of the initial improvement. The scores at the end tell you what the model believes: P(real pair) for the centre word "cat" against a few contexts.

from starter import (make_corpus, build_vocab, make_pairs, train, sigmoid, dot)

sentences = make_corpus()
vocab, counts = build_vocab(sentences)
index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
pairs = make_pairs(sentences, index, window=2)
W_in, W_out, losses = train(pairs, len(vocab), counts, dim=16, negatives=5, lr=0.05, epochs=6, seed=0)

print(f"{len(pairs):,} pairs, vocabulary {len(vocab)}, chance level {6 * 0.6931:.3f}")
print("mean loss per pair, by epoch:", " ".join(f"{x:.3f}" for x in losses))
v = W_in[index["cat"]]
for context in ("purring", "eats", "chases", "tutor", "queen"):
    score = dot(v, W_out[index[context]])
    print(f"P(real pair)  cat -> {context:8s} {sigmoid(score):.3f}")

Common misconception

"The loss going down means the embedding is good." The loss measures how well the model separates real pairs from noise, not whether the geometry is useful. It reaches its floor after two or three epochs here, while the structure of the vectors is still sharpening, and in other settings a lower loss can come with worse neighbours. That is why this lab checks the thing you actually want, neighbours and analogies, directly. The check in milestone 4 does both: it requires the loss to fall below 2.0 and animals to be far closer to each other than to foods.

def train(pairs, vocab_size, counts, dim=16, negatives=5, lr=0.05, epochs=6, seed=0):
    """Train SGNS by stochastic gradient descent, one pair at a time.

    Returns (W_in, W_out, losses): W_in and W_out are (vocab_size, dim); losses[e] is the mean
    loss per pair in epoch e. Initialisation: W_in uniform in [-0.5/dim, 0.5/dim], W_out zeros.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    W_in = [[rng.uniform(-0.5 / dim, 0.5 / dim) for _ in range(dim)] for _ in range(vocab_size)]
    W_out = [[0.0] * dim for _ in range(vocab_size)]
    cum = cumulative(noise_distribution(counts))
    order = list(pairs)
    losses = []
    for _ in range(epochs):
        rng.shuffle(order)
        total = 0.0
        for w, c in order:
            negs = sample_negatives(cum, negatives, c, rng)
            v, u_pos, u_negs = W_in[w], W_out[c], [W_out[n] for n in negs]
            total += sgns_loss(v, u_pos, u_negs)
            gv, gu_pos, gu_negs = sgns_grads(v, u_pos, u_negs)
            for i in range(dim):                      # update after ALL gradients are computed
                v[i] -= lr * gv[i]
                u_pos[i] -= lr * gu_pos[i]
            for n, gu in zip(negs, gu_negs):
                un = W_out[n]
                for i in range(dim):
                    un[i] -= lr * gu[i]
        losses.append(total / len(order))
    return W_in, W_out, losses

Milestone 5: neighbours and analogies

The vectors are trained; now ask them questions. The tool is cosine similarity (A0 chapter 5): the cosine of the angle between two vectors, dot(a, b) / (|a| |b|), which is 1 for the same direction, 0 for perpendicular, and −1 for opposite. It ignores length, which is the right choice because a vector's length mostly reflects how often the word occurred. Normalising every row to length 1 first (the given normalise_rows) turns every cosine into a plain dot product.

Nearest neighbours. nearest(E, vocab, word, k) ranks all other words by cosine to word and returns the top k with their similarities. In the toy language the check expects animals near animals and foods near foods: no label said which is which; the shared contexts did.

Analogies. The result that made word vectors famous (Mikolov, Yih and Zweig, 2013, "Linguistic Regularities in Continuous Space Word Representations") is that relations appear as consistent offsets: vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) lands near vec(queen). To answer "a is to b as c is to ?", compute the target b − a + c and return the words with highest cosine to it. There is a trap, and chapter 6 discusses it: the target is often closest to b itself (if c is similar to a, then b − a + c is nearly b), or to c. A scoring that lets the three question words be the answer reports success for the wrong reason. The standard remedy is to exclude a, b and c from the candidates, and your analogy takes an exclude flag so you can see both.

def cosine(a, b):
    """Cosine similarity of two vectors: dot(a, b) / (|a| |b|)."""
    return dot(a, b) / (math.sqrt(dot(a, a)) * math.sqrt(dot(b, b)))

def nearest(E, vocab, word, k=5):
    """The k words most similar to `word` by cosine, as [(word, similarity), ...], best first.

    The word itself is excluded. Ties break towards the earlier vocabulary entry.
    """
    i = vocab.index(word)
    sims = [(cosine(E[i], E[j]), j) for j in range(len(vocab)) if j != i]
    sims.sort(key=lambda t: (-t[0], t[1]))
    return [(vocab[j], s) for s, j in sims[:k]]

def analogy(E, vocab, a, b, c, k=1, exclude=True):
    """'a is to b as c is to ?': the k words closest (cosine) to vec(b) - vec(a) + vec(c).

    With exclude=True the three query words are not allowed as answers.
    """
    ia, ib, ic = vocab.index(a), vocab.index(b), vocab.index(c)
    target = [y - x + z for x, y, z in zip(E[ia], E[ib], E[ic])]
    banned = {ia, ib, ic} if exclude else set()
    sims = [(cosine(target, E[j]), j) for j in range(len(vocab)) if j not in banned]
    sims.sort(key=lambda t: (-t[0], t[1]))
    return [vocab[j] for _, j in sims[:k]]

def analogy_accuracy(E, vocab, questions, exclude=True):
    """Fraction of (a, b, c, d) questions whose top-1 answer to 'a:b :: c:?' is d."""
    hits = sum(analogy(E, vocab, a, b, c, 1, exclude)[0] == d for a, b, c, d in questions)
    return hits / len(questions)

  • Neighbours work. "cat" is closest to other animals (cosine 0.77 to 0.83), "bread" to other foods (0.95 to 0.98), "king" to "prince", "boy" and "man", and then to the item word "school". The pronoun "she" is closest to "he": the two pronouns appear in the same sentence frames, which is exactly the distributional hypothesis, and it also shows its blind spot, since "he" and "she" are opposites in meaning and neighbours in vector space.
  • Plural is a clean direction, gender is not. The animal-to-plural analogies are right every time (100% of 30). The person pairs reach only 25% top-1, yet the checker also confirms that all the top answers are female words (woman, queen, girl or princess): the vectors know the direction "male to female", but not which pair's female word is the right one. Why, we did not test; a plausible reason is that each animal has its own adjective, so the six animals sit far apart in the space, while the four person pairs share items and pronouns and sit close together, so the right word is hard to separate from its neighbours. You can test that hypothesis in the "Going further" list. Chapter 6 found the same fragility on real text, where man:king :: woman:? ranked "queen" 13th.
  • Hyper-parameters matter, and unevenly. In the final table, five negatives give perfect plural analogies at every window and one negative gives 63 to 87%. Window size has no simple trend: the average animal-food cosine (lower means cleaner separation) is 0.16 at window 2 but 0.33 at window 1 and 0.24 at window 4 with five negatives, and we did not investigate why. The loss itself is not comparable across rows with different k, because it has k + 1 terms.

Going further

  • Subsampling frequent words. Mikolov et al. discard each occurrence of a very frequent word with a probability that grows with its frequency (their threshold is about 10−5), which speeds training and helps rare words. Add it to make_pairs with a seeded rng and see whether gender analogies improve.
  • Test the hypothesis about gender. Give each of the eight people two private context words (like the animals' adjectives) and rerun. If top-1 on the gender analogies rises, the identity of each word was the missing ingredient.
  • Sum the two tables. Use W_in + W_out as the final vectors, as GloVe does with its two tables. Does it help neighbours? Analogies?
  • Learning-rate decay. word2vec decays the rate linearly to near zero over training. Add it and compare the final loss and the clustering gap.
  • CBOW. Predict the centre word from the average of its context vectors instead. It is a different pair of functions and a different shape of gradient.
  • Counting versus predicting. Build the co-occurrence matrix, weight it by positive pointwise mutual information and compress it with a truncated SVD, as in chapter 6, and compare neighbours with SGNS. Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed the two optimise closely related objectives.
  • A real corpus. Download text8 (chapter 6 gives the source), keep the 2,000 most frequent words, and train for a few minutes in pure Python on a 200,000-word slice. Then look for "python" and "doctor" the way chapter 6 does.
  • Probe for bias. Chapter 6 shows how occupations line up with a gender direction in real vectors. Add occupations to the toy language with a skew in the pronoun they appear with, and measure the same projection.

Systems connection

The whole of W_in is the embedding table of a language model, and a lookup is a row gather: for a vocabulary of 128,000 and a width of 4,096, the table alone is half a billion numbers (chapter 6). Updates are sparse, touching only the rows in the current batch, which is one reason large embedding tables can be sharded across devices and updated sparsely. Once you have vectors, finding the nearest ones among millions is a systems problem of its own: exact search is a matrix-vector product, and approximate indexes trade recall for speed. The negative-sampling idea itself reappears whenever a model must pick one item from a huge set: the contrastive losses that train retrieval embedders replace "all documents" with a few sampled negatives for the same reason you replaced "all words".

What you learned

  • Skip-gram turns raw text into free supervision: every word within a window of another gives a (centre, context) pair, with no labels needed.
  • Negative sampling replaces a V-way softmax with k + 1 yes-or-no questions; with k = 5 that is 6 dot products per pair instead of V. The noise comes from count3/4, never including the true context.
  • The gradient of the loss with respect to a score is σ(s) − 1 for the real pair and σ(s) for a noise pair; gradients on the vectors are those numbers times the other vector. The loss starts at (k + 1) ln 2.
  • Each word has two vectors; only the rows of the words in the current pair change in a step, so the update is sparse.
  • Cosine similarity compares directions; analogies add and subtract vectors, and must exclude the question words from the answer or they will usually win.
  • A relation that is clean in the data (singular to plural here) gives clean offsets; a muddier one (gender across four pairs) gives the right direction but the wrong word. Treat analogy scores as a diagnostic, not a promise.

Check your understanding

A sentence has 5 words and the window is 2 (two words each side). How many (centre, context) pairs does it produce?

  1. 10
  2. 12
  3. 14
  4. 20
Count neighbours per position: the two end words have 2 each, the two next to them have 3, and the middle word has 4, so 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14. Twenty would be 5 words times 4 neighbours, which ignores that windows are cut off at the sentence edges.

What does raising the word counts to the power 0.75 do to the noise distribution, compared with using the raw counts?

  1. Draws very frequent words less often and rare words more often than their share of the text
  2. Draws every word equally often
  3. Draws rare words less often, because they carry less information
  4. Makes no difference to the probabilities, only to the speed of sampling
An exponent below 1 flattens the distribution: "the" falls from 20.9% to 12.3% of draws in our corpus and "cat" rises. Uniform sampling corresponds to an exponent of 0, not 0.75. The effect is on the probabilities, not on speed (the cumulative-table trick sets the speed).

During training, a noise pair currently has score s = 3 and another has s = −3. Which one receives the larger push on its score, and what is the gradient of the loss with respect to that score?

  1. The s = −3 pair: the gradient is −0.95, because it is far from the target
  2. The s = 3 pair: the gradient is σ(3) ≈ 0.95, against σ(−3) ≈ 0.05 for the other
  3. Both equally: the gradient is 1 for any noise pair
  4. Neither: noise pairs have no gradient, only the true pair does
For a noise (target 0) pair, dL/ds = σ(s). A pair the model wrongly scores high (s = 3, so σ = 0.95) gets a large positive gradient and is pushed down hard; one already scored low (s = −3, σ = 0.05) barely moves. The gradient of a noise pair is never negative, so the first option has the wrong sign as well.

With all vectors initialised so that every score is exactly 0 and k = 5 negatives, what is the loss on the first pair?

  1. 0, because the model has not made any wrong predictions yet
  2. ln 2 = 0.693, the loss of one coin flip
  3. 5 ln 2 = 3.466, one term per negative
  4. 6 ln 2 = 4.159, one term for the real pair and one for each negative
With s = 0 every sigmoid is 0.5 and every term costs −ln 0.5 = ln 2. There are k + 1 = 6 terms: the real pair and five noise pairs. The third option forgets the real pair's term. This is the value the first epoch of the training loop starts from, and the benchmark against which the final loss is compared.

Why must an analogy solver exclude the three question words from its candidate answers?

  1. Because their vectors are lost after the subtraction
  2. Because b − a + c is often closest to b or c themselves, so without exclusion the "answer" is frequently just one of the inputs
  3. Because a word cannot be its own neighbour in cosine similarity
  4. Because excluding them makes the search faster without changing the answer
If c is close to a, then b − a + c is close to b, and b is the nearest vector. In this lab, 92% of the unrestricted answers on the gender questions are one of the three inputs, and accuracy falls from 25% to 0%. The exclusion changes the answer, so it is not merely an optimisation; early analogy reports that allowed the inputs overstated their results.

On the gender analogies, top-1 accuracy is only 25% but every top answer is a female word. What is the best reading of that result?

  1. The vectors contain no information about gender
  2. The test is broken, since a wrong answer cannot be a female word
  3. The male-to-female direction is encoded, but the vectors do not separate the four female words well enough to pick the right one
  4. Four pairs are too few for any analogy to be possible, so the 25% is by chance
A relation direction can be present while the identity of the target is blurred: the model lands in the right region (all female words) but among near neighbours picks the wrong one. A result with no gender information would put some answers on male words. The point is the pattern of errors, not the single number. Whether the cause is shared contexts is a hypothesis to test, not something this run shows.
