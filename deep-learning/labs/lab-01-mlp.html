Lab 1: A Neural Network from Scratch

Chapters 1 and 2 gave you the pieces of a neural network on paper and in NumPy: a weighted sum, a bend, a softmax, and four lines of matrix calculus that turn a loss into a gradient for every weight. In this lab you build all of it again with nothing but Python lists, so that every multiplication is a loop you can see. You write the matrix operations, the activation and the loss, the forward pass and the backward pass, and then a training loop that teaches a small network to separate two interleaved half-moons and to solve XOR. A gradient check you meet in milestone 4 will tell you, to nine decimal places, whether your calculus is right.

Why this lab matters

Every model in this course, up to the GPT of chapter 12, is this network with more layers and different shapes. The feed-forward sublayer inside a Transformer block is literally the two-layer network you build here, and the way a framework such as PyTorch trains it (chapter 5) is the loop you write in milestone 5. You will rarely write a backward pass by hand again, which is exactly why it is worth writing one now: a loss that will not move, a NaN at step ten thousand, a gradient that is silently a factor of the batch size too large, are all statements about the backward pass, and you can only debug what you can derive. The same reasoning is behind Karpathy's micrograd and his Zero to Hero lectures, whose spirit this lab follows: build the small version until nothing in it is mysterious, then trust the big version.

The second reason is the habit, not the code. Hand-written gradients are checked against finite differences before they are believed (A0 chapter 9, chapter 2). You write that check yourself here and see what a wrong gradient looks like, including the most common wrong one: forgetting that a mean loss divides the first gradient by the batch size.

Pure Python, seeded, a few seconds

Python 3.9 or newer and nothing else: no NumPy, no downloads, no GPU. Chapters 1 and 2 used NumPy for speed and brevity; here a matrix is a list of rows, so matmul is three nested loops in disguise. That is slow (the 300-epoch training run in milestone 5 does about 11.5 million multiply-adds in well under a second, where NumPy would be far faster), and the slowness is the point of the exercise: you can step through every number. Everything is seeded, so python3 check.py solution prints identical numbers every time on every machine (up to the last digit of a float) and finishes in about two seconds. Random property tests print their seed when they fail, and you can replay one with python3 check.py solution 4 11, which means milestone 4, seed 11.

What you'll build

Five milestones, each building on the last:

  1. Matrix operations: matmul, transpose, add_bias (a broadcast), col_sums (its undo), hadamard and scale, on lists of rows.
  2. Activations and loss: ReLU and its slope, a numerically safe softmax, the mean cross-entropy, and the one-line gradient on the logits.
  3. Parameters and the forward pass: He-scaled random weights from a seed, and a forward pass that remembers what the backward pass will need.
  4. Backpropagation by hand: the four-line recipe of chapter 2 for both layers, verified against finite differences.
  5. Training: gradient descent, predictions and accuracy, a full loop, and two toy problems (two moons and XOR) that a linear model cannot solve.

Milestone 1: matrix operations

A neural network is a short list of matrix operations, and in chapter 2 you saw that all the difficulty lives in the shapes. Here a matrix is a list of rows, each row a list of floats, so a batch X of 32 examples with 2 features is a list of 32 lists of 2 numbers, and its shape is (32, 2). The rule that organises everything is the one from chapter 2: the product of an (m, n) matrix and an (n, p) matrix is (m, p), and the two inner sizes must agree. In code that rule is a ValueError, because a silent broadcast of a wrongly shaped matrix is the kind of bug that costs an afternoon.

You need six operations. matmul(A, B) computes each entry as a row of A dotted with a column of B; the neat way to get columns of B in Python is zip(*B). transpose(A) swaps rows and columns, again with zip(*A). add_bias(A, b) adds the vector b to every row of A: this is the broadcast in z = aW + b, one vector copied down a whole batch. col_sums(A) sums down the rows, and it is the backward pass of that broadcast: a value copied to every row in the forward pass is summed over the rows in the backward pass (chapter 2 states this as "db = sum of δ over the batch"). hadamard(A, B) is the elementwise product ⊙ that appears when a gradient passes through an activation. scale(A, s) multiplies every element by a number.

Analogy: a spreadsheet of unit prices

Think of X as an order book: each row is a customer, each column a product, each cell a quantity. W holds the price of each product in each of several currencies, one column per currency. matmul(X, W) is the invoice: for each customer and currency, quantity times price summed over products. add_bias then adds the same handling fee to every customer's invoice, and col_sums is the accountant's question in reverse: "what was the total fee across customers?"

Where it breaks down: an invoice is the end of the story, but in a network it is the start of the next layer, and the numbers in W are not fixed prices but quantities that training will change.

Common misconception

"[[0.0] * cols] * rows makes an empty matrix." It makes rows references to the same row, so writing to one cell changes a whole column. The given zeros helper uses a comprehension for this reason, and the same trap lurks in any function of yours that builds output by copying a row. Build a new list for each row, and never modify the arguments you were given: the checker passes in matrices it then compares against a copy.

Here is the shape discipline on a concrete case, which you can run once your milestone passes (it runs against your starter.py; the output below is from the reference solution). X holds two examples with three features, W maps three features to two outputs, and b is a bias per output:

from starter import matmul, transpose, add_bias, col_sums

     [4.0, 5.0, 6.0]]            # (2, 3): two examples, three features
W = [[1.0, 0.0],
     [0.0, 1.0],
     [1.0, -1.0]]                # (3, 2)
b = [10.0, 20.0]                 # (2,)

The second row (4, 5, 6) gives (10, −1) and then (20, 19). col_sums adds the two rows: 14 + 20 = 34 and 19 + 19 = 38. The point of printing is the shapes: (2, 3) times (3, 2) is (2, 2), and the transpose of X is (3, 2).

Solution spoiler 
def matmul(A, B):
    """(m, n) @ (n, p) -> (m, p). Raise ValueError if the inner sizes differ."""
    if shape(A)[1] != shape(B)[0]:
        raise ValueError(f"cannot multiply {shape(A)} by {shape(B)}")
    cols = list(zip(*B))                       # columns of B, so each entry is a row . column dot
    return [[sum(a * b for a, b in zip(row, col)) for col in cols] for row in A]

def transpose(A):
    """(m, n) -> (n, m)."""
    return [list(col) for col in zip(*A)]

def add_bias(A, b):
    """Add the vector b (length n) to every row of A (m, n): the broadcast of z = aW + b."""
    if len(b) != shape(A)[1]:
        raise ValueError(f"bias of length {len(b)} does not fit {shape(A)}")
    return [[x + bj for x, bj in zip(row, b)] for row in A]

def col_sums(A):
    """Sum down the rows: (m, n) -> a list of n numbers. The backward pass of add_bias."""
    return [sum(col) for col in zip(*A)]

def hadamard(A, B):
    """Elementwise product of two matrices of the same shape."""
    if shape(A) != shape(B):
        raise ValueError(f"shapes {shape(A)} and {shape(B)} differ")
    return [[a * b for a, b in zip(ra, rb)] for ra, rb in zip(A, B)]

def scale(A, s):
    """Multiply every element by the number s."""
    return [[x * s for x in row] for row in A]

Milestone 2: activations, softmax and the loss

The network will bend its signal with ReLU (chapter 1): relu(z) = max(0, z). Its backward pass needs the slope, which is 1 where the unit was active (z > 0) and 0 where it was clipped. You return it as a matrix of 1.0 and 0.0 so that the backward pass can multiply by it with hadamard. At exactly z = 0 the slope is undefined; the convention here (and in most frameworks) is 0, and a test pins it down.

The last layer produces logits, raw scores with one column per class. Softmax turns each row into probabilities: pk = ezk / ∑j ezj. Written naively this overflows: the exponential of 1000 is far beyond the largest float, so math.exp(1000) raises an OverflowError in Python (and NumPy would return inf and then nan). The fix from A0 chapter 11 costs one line: subtract the row's largest logit before exponentiating. That changes nothing mathematically (the same constant divides numerator and denominator) and keeps the largest exponent at e0 = 1.

The loss is the mean cross-entropy (A0 chapter 13): for each example, minus the natural log of the probability the model gave to the true class, averaged over the batch. And the starting point of backpropagation is the neat result derived in chapter 2: the gradient of that loss with respect to the logits is (P − one-hot(y)) / B, the predicted probabilities minus the truth, divided by the batch size because the loss is a mean. The / B is the line most often forgotten. Every other gradient in the network is built on this one, so a missing factor here scales all of them.

The worked example is the one from the chapter 2 quiz: logits (2, 1, 0.1), true class 0.

from starter import softmax_rows, cross_entropy, softmax_ce_grad

P = softmax_rows([[2.0, 1.0, 0.1]])
print("probabilities", [round(p, 4) for p in P[0]])
print("loss, class 0", round(cross_entropy(P, [0]), 4))
print("gradient     ", [round(g, 4) for g in softmax_ce_grad(P, [0])[0]])
print("huge logits  ", softmax_rows([[1000.0, 1001.0]]))
probabilities [0.659, 0.2424, 0.0986]
loss, class 0 0.417
gradient      [-0.341, 0.2424, 0.0986]
huge logits   [[0.2689414213699951, 0.7310585786300049]]

The model puts probability 0.659 on the right class, so the loss is −ln 0.659 = 0.417, and the gradient says: raise logit 0 (−0.341, the sign that lowers the loss when subtracted), lower the other two in proportion to how much probability they hold. The three gradient entries sum to zero, which is a handy property to test: probabilities sum to one, the one-hot vector sums to one, and their difference sums to zero. The last line shows the max-subtraction working: with logits (1000, 1001) the probabilities are (0.2689, 0.7311), the same as for (0, 1).

Solution spoiler 
def relu(Z):
    """max(0, z) elementwise."""
    return apply(lambda z: z if z > 0 else 0.0, Z)

def relu_grad(Z):
    """The slope f'(z) as a matrix of 1.0 and 0.0: 1 where z > 0, else 0."""
    return apply(lambda z: 1.0 if z > 0 else 0.0, Z)

def softmax_rows(Z):
    """Softmax of each row. Subtract the row maximum first so exp never overflows."""
    out = []
    for row in Z:
        m = max(row)
        e = [math.exp(z - m) for z in row]
        s = sum(e)
        out.append([x / s for x in e])
    return out

def cross_entropy(P, y):
    """Mean of -ln P[i][y[i]] over the batch."""
    return -sum(math.log(P[i][label]) for i, label in enumerate(y)) / len(y)

def softmax_ce_grad(P, y):
    """Gradient of the mean cross-entropy with respect to the logits: (P - one_hot(y)) / B."""
    B = len(y)
    G = [row[:] for row in P]
    for i, label in enumerate(y):
        G[i][label] -= 1.0
    return scale(G, 1.0 / B)

Milestone 3: parameters and the forward pass

A two-layer network has four parameter tensors: W1 of shape (Cin, H), b1 of length H, W2 of shape (H, K) and b2 of length K. You will store them in a dictionary with those names, so that backward can return a dictionary of gradients with the names dW1, db1, dW2, db2 and the update step can walk both in the same order. The biases start at zero. The weights must not start at zero, or at any single shared value, for a reason you will see in the experiment at the end of this lab: if every hidden unit starts identical, they receive identical gradients and stay identical forever.

The scale of the random weights matters too (chapter 3). Each hidden unit sums Cin products, and if the weights are too large or too small the signal grows or shrinks layer by layer. He initialisation (He et al., 2015, "Delving Deep into Rectifiers") draws the weights of a ReLU layer from a normal distribution with standard deviation √(2 / fan-in), the factor of 2 compensating for ReLU zeroing half of its inputs on average. The output layer gets √(1 / H). With random.Random(seed).gauss(0, sigma) the same seed always gives the same weights, which is what makes the whole lab reproducible. For a (40, 60, 3) network the check measures the spread of your weights and expects √(2/40) = 0.224 for W1 and √(1/60) = 0.129 for W2, within sampling error.

forward(params, X) then follows the figure: z1 = X W1 + b1, a1 = relu(z1), logits = a1 W2 + b2. It returns the logits and a cache, a dictionary holding X, z1, a1 and logits. The cache is the entire reason training uses more memory than prediction (chapter 2): the backward pass needs X for dW1, z1 for the ReLU slope, a1 for dW2, and the logits to recompute the probabilities. A prediction-only forward pass could throw each of them away as soon as the next layer had used it.

Check your forward pass against the network from chapter 2, whose numbers you already know: x = (1, 2), W1 = [[0.5, −1], [0.25, 0.25]], W2 = (0.6, −0.4)T. The hidden pre-activations were (1.0, −0.5), the ReLU kept the first and clipped the second, and the output was 0.6.

Solution spoiler 
def init_params(sizes, seed=0):
    """He-style initial weights for a (C_in, H, K) network, as in chapter 02.

    W1 ~ Normal(0, sqrt(2 / C_in)), W2 ~ Normal(0, sqrt(1 / H)), both biases zero.
    """
    c_in, h, k = sizes
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    s1, s2 = math.sqrt(2.0 / c_in), math.sqrt(1.0 / h)
    return {
        "W1": [[rng.gauss(0, s1) for _ in range(h)] for _ in range(c_in)],
        "b1": [0.0] * h,
        "W2": [[rng.gauss(0, s2) for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(h)],
        "b2": [0.0] * k,
    }

def forward(params, X):
    """Return (logits, cache). cache keeps what the backward pass needs: X, z1, a1 and the logits."""
    z1 = add_bias(matmul(X, params["W1"]), params["b1"])      # (B, H)
    a1 = relu(z1)                                             # (B, H)
    logits = add_bias(matmul(a1, params["W2"]), params["b2"])  # (B, K)
    return logits, {"X": X, "z1": z1, "a1": a1, "logits": logits}

Milestone 4: backpropagation by hand

This is the heart of the lab, and it is shorter than it looks, because chapter 2 derived it for you. Walk the figure from the right. Start with δ2 = (P − one-hot(y)) / B, shape (B, K), from milestone 2. A linear layer's backward pass needs three products, all reusing the cached forward values:

  • Weights: dW2 = a1T δ2, shape (H, B) times (B, K) is (H, K), the shape of W2.
  • Biases: db2 = column sums of δ2, shape (K), the broadcast undone.
  • The input to the layer: da1 = δ2 W2T, shape (B, K) times (K, H) is (B, H).

Then through the ReLU: δ1 = da1 ⊙ f′(z1), the elementwise product with the slope matrix (zero wherever a unit was clipped). Then the same three products for the first layer: dW1 = XT δ1, shape (Cin, H), and db1 = column sums of δ1. Nothing is passed further back, because the data X has no parameters. Here is every tensor in one table; if a formula ever feels uncertain, the shapes usually force the answer.

The checker first runs a hand case you can verify on paper: chapter 2's network with two output classes (W2 = [[0.6, 0.2], [−0.4, 0.1]]), the single example x = (1, 2), true class 0. The logits are (0.6, 0.2), so P = (0.5987, 0.4013) and δ2 = (−0.4013, 0.4013). Since a1 = (1, 0), only the first row of dW2 is non-zero. Then da1 = (−0.1605, 0.2007); the second hidden unit was clipped, so δ1 = (−0.1605, 0), and dW1 is xTδ1 = [[−0.1605, 0], [−0.3210, 0]]. A unit that was off for an example learns nothing from it, and the second input, twice as large as the first, gets twice the gradient.

The gradient check

Hand values catch formula slips, but only a gradient check proves the whole backward pass. The idea is a finite difference: nudge one weight by +ε and by −ε, recompute the loss both times, and the slope is (L(w + ε) − L(w − ε)) / 2ε. If your analytic gradient is right, it agrees with the slope to many digits. The rules from chapter 2 apply: tiny network, float64 (Python floats already are), ε around 10−6, compare with the relative error |a − b| / (|a| + |b|), and check every parameter tensor, not only the last one. The listing below is a reusable checker for your own backward pass; the output is from the reference solution.

from starter import init_params, forward, backward, loss_of, make_moons

X, y = make_moons(6, seed=3)
params = init_params((2, 5, 2), seed=1)
_, cache = forward(params, X)
grads = backward(params, cache, y)

def numeric_grad(name, eps=1e-6):
    """Centred finite difference for every number in params[name]."""
    P = params[name]
    out = []
    for i in range(len(P)):
        if isinstance(P[i], list):
            row = []
            for j in range(len(P[i])):
                old = P[i][j]
                P[i][j] = old + eps; up = loss_of(params, X, y)
                P[i][j] = old - eps; down = loss_of(params, X, y)
                P[i][j] = old
                row.append((up - down) / (2 * eps))
            out.append(row)
        else:
            old = P[i]
            P[i] = old + eps; up = loss_of(params, X, y)
            P[i] = old - eps; down = loss_of(params, X, y)
            P[i] = old
            out.append((up - down) / (2 * eps))
    return out

def flat(v):
    return [x for r in v for x in r] if isinstance(v[0], list) else list(v)

for name in ("W1", "b1", "W2", "b2"):
    num, ana = flat(numeric_grad(name)), flat(grads["d" + name])
    rel = max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(num, ana)) / (max(map(abs, num)) + max(map(abs, ana)))
    print(f"d{name}: {len(ana):2d} numbers   max relative error {rel:.2e}")
dW1: 10 numbers   max relative error 3.27e-10
db1:  5 numbers   max relative error 2.16e-10
dW2: 10 numbers   max relative error 2.04e-10
db2:  2 numbers   max relative error 3.59e-10

Errors near 10−9 to 10−10 are as good as finite differences get. Below roughly 10−6 the gradient is right; between 10−4 and 10−2 there is a real bug; near 1 it is a gross one such as a missing transpose or a wrong sign. The code is also a good argument for why nobody trains by finite differences: each number needs two full forward passes, so a network with a million weights would need two million passes for one gradient, where backpropagation needs about two.

def backward(params, cache, y):
    """Gradients of the mean cross-entropy loss: a dict with dW1, db1, dW2, db2."""
    P = softmax_rows(cache["logits"])                           # (B, K) probabilities
    dz2 = softmax_ce_grad(P, y)                                 # (B, K): the first delta
    dW2 = matmul(transpose(cache["a1"]), dz2)                   # (H, K)
    db2 = col_sums(dz2)                                         # (K,)
    da1 = matmul(dz2, transpose(params["W2"]))                  # (B, H)
    dz1 = hadamard(da1, relu_grad(cache["z1"]))                 # (B, H)
    dW1 = matmul(transpose(cache["X"]), dz1)                    # (C_in, H)
    db1 = col_sums(dz1)                                         # (H,)
    return {"dW1": dW1, "db1": db1, "dW2": dW2, "db2": db2}

Milestone 5: training

Backpropagation only computes gradients; it does not learn anything (a point chapter 2 makes in its misconception box). Learning is the update rule that uses them. The simplest is gradient descent (A0 chapter 10): move every parameter a small step against its gradient, p ← p − lr · dp, where the learning rate lr sets the step size. A gradient says which way is uphill, so subtracting it goes downhill on the loss. Here you run it on the whole training set every step, called full-batch gradient descent, and one full pass is an epoch. Mini-batches (a random subset per step) are what real training uses; with 200 points, full batch is simpler and exactly reproducible.

You write four small functions. sgd_step(params, grads, lr) updates the parameters in place and returns nothing. predict returns the class with the largest logit for each row (softmax is monotonic, so you do not need it). accuracy is the fraction correct. And train loops: forward, record the loss, backward, update, returning the list of losses, entry i being the loss before the i-th update.

The data come from two generators in the starter file. make_moons draws two interleaved half-circles with Gaussian noise: no straight line separates them, so a linear classifier cannot be perfect (with the bend removed, the network of this lab reaches 91.5% on its training points and 89.5% on 200 new ones), and a network with a bend can do better. make_xor puts points near the four corners (±1, ±1) and labels a point 1 when its two coordinates have different signs: the classic problem that no single neuron can solve, because the two classes sit on opposite diagonals. Training accuracy only tells you the network has memorised its training points, so the check also generates 200 new moons with a different seed and measures accuracy there (A1 chapter 1).

The learning rate is the one knob to feel for yourself. These runs use the solution on the moons: same network, same initial weights, four step sizes.

Random He-scaled weights do not give uniform probabilities; they give confident, mostly wrong ones, which the first few epochs correct.

Solution spoiler 
def sgd_step(params, grads, lr):
    """In place: every parameter moves against its gradient, p <- p - lr * dp."""
    for name in ("W1", "W2"):
        W, dW = params[name], grads["d" + name]
        for i, row in enumerate(W):
            for j in range(len(row)):
                row[j] -= lr * dW[i][j]
    for name in ("b1", "b2"):
        b, db = params[name], grads["d" + name]
        for j in range(len(b)):
            b[j] -= lr * db[j]

def predict(params, X):
    """Predicted class (argmax of the logits) for each row of X."""
    logits, _ = forward(params, X)
    return [max(range(len(row)), key=lambda k: row[k]) for row in logits]

def accuracy(params, X, y):
    """Fraction of rows classified correctly."""
    return sum(p == t for p, t in zip(predict(params, X), y)) / len(y)

def train(params, X, y, lr=0.5, epochs=300):
    """Full-batch gradient descent. Returns the loss before each step (length epochs)."""
    losses = []
    for _ in range(epochs):
        logits, cache = forward(params, X)
        losses.append(cross_entropy(softmax_rows(logits), y))
        grads = backward(params, cache, y)
        sgd_step(params, grads, lr)
    return losses

    for k in ("W1", "W2"):
        p[k] = [[0.0] * len(r) for r in p[k]]

    for k in ("W1", "W2"):
        p[k] = [[0.3] * len(r) for r in p[k]]

    p["W2"] = [[0.0] * len(r) for r in p["W2"]]

Zero only in W2 is fine: the hidden layer still differentiates because it is W1 that carries the randomness. This is why a network needs random starting weights, not merely small ones.

Systems connection

Your matmul is the operation GPUs exist to do. Each output entry is independent of the others, so thousands of them can be computed at once, and the hardware's advantage is in doing exactly this multiply-add pattern at enormous width (Zero to Systems chapter 21). The cache you built in milestone 3 is the training-memory story in miniature: activations are kept until the backward pass has used them, and for a Transformer that cache, not the weights, often decides what fits on a GPU (chapter 16). And when a training run is slow, the method of chapter 22 applies unchanged: time the forward, the backward and the update separately before guessing.

What you learned

  • A neural network is a short chain of matrix operations; the whole difficulty is shapes, and a gradient always has the shape of the thing it differentiates.
  • The backward pass of z = aW + b is dW = aTδ, db = column sums of δ and da = δWT; through a ReLU, multiply by the 0/1 slope. A bias broadcast forward becomes a sum backward.
  • For softmax with cross-entropy, the gradient on the logits is (P − one-hot) / B; forgetting the / B makes every gradient B times too large without raising any error.
  • A finite-difference check with relative error below about 10−6 is how you trust a hand-written gradient; nothing else in this lab is as valuable to carry into future work.
  • Random, correctly scaled initial weights are required: identical hidden units never differentiate, and a network without a non-linearity cannot solve XOR.
  • Training accuracy only shows memorisation; measure on points the network has not seen, and remember that a network's behaviour away from the data is unconstrained.

Check your understanding

A batch X has shape (32, 2) and the hidden layer has 16 units. What are the shape of the first-layer weight gradient dW1 and the formula that produces it?

  1. (32, 16), from X δ1
  2. (2, 16), from XT δ1
  3. (16, 2), from δ1T XT summed over the batch
  4. (2, 32), from XT averaged over the hidden units
dW1 must have the shape of W1, which is (2, 16), and the batch dimension must be summed away: XT is (2, 32) and δ1 is (32, 16), so their product is (2, 16). The first option has a batch-sized dimension left over and is not even a valid product of those shapes; the third has the transposed shape and could not be subtracted from W1.

The bias b1 (length 16) is added to every row of a (32, 16) matrix in the forward pass. In the backward pass, db1 is obtained by:

  1. Taking the first row of δ1, since every row used the same bias
  2. Averaging δ1 over the hidden units to get one number
  3. Summing δ1 down the 32 rows to get 16 numbers
  4. Multiplying δ1 by XT
The bias touched every example with coefficient 1, so its gradient is the sum of the error signals over the batch rows: 16 numbers, one per unit. The first option ignores 31 of the 32 examples. Multiplying by XT is the weight gradient, which also weights each term by the input; the bias has no input.

A gradient check reports relative errors of 3e-10 for dW1, db1 and db2, but 4e-3 for dW2. What is the most reasonable conclusion?

  1. There is a bug in how dW2 is computed or in the cached values it uses; the others are fine
  2. Everything is fine: 4e-3 is within floating-point noise
  3. The loss is missing its division by the batch size, since that affects all gradients
  4. The finite-difference step ε is too small, and you should rerun with ε = 1e-2
Errors in float64 of 1e-4 to 1e-2 mean a real bug, and since the other three tensors agree to nine digits, the problem is local to dW2 (a missing transpose of the wrong operand, or using z1 where a1 was needed). A missing 1/B would scale every gradient and show up everywhere, as a ratio of exactly B. Changing ε to 1e-2 would make the check less accurate, not more.

You initialise every weight and bias in both layers to exactly zero and train with ReLU hidden units. What happens?

  1. It trains normally; biases and weights find their own values
  2. Nothing moves: the loss stays at ln 2 and accuracy at 50%
  3. It trains, but takes many more epochs
  4. The loss becomes nan on the first step
With all zeros, z1 = 0 for every example, the ReLU slope at 0 is 0, so no error reaches W1 or b1; and since a1 = 0, dW2 = a1Tδ2 is zero too (only b2 moves, and it cannot separate the classes). The model predicts 50/50 forever. There is no division by zero anywhere, so no nan.

Both layers are initialised with all weights equal to 0.3 (biases zero), and training uses ReLU and gradient descent. After training, the 16 hidden units:

  1. Have learned 16 different features, because gradient descent adds noise
  2. Are all zero, because their gradients cancel
  3. Differ only in their biases
  4. Are still exact copies of each other, so the layer acts like a single unit
Identical units receive identical gradients (the symmetry argument), so every update keeps them identical. The experiment above reaches 91.5%, the accuracy of the single-unit run. Random starting weights are what break the symmetry; there is no noise in full-batch gradient descent to do it for you. The gradients are not zero (the loss falls), so they do not cancel.

Which change to the training loop would make a finite-difference gradient check useless (a false pass or a false fail), even if backward is correct?

  1. Using a batch of 5 examples instead of 200
  2. Computing the check in float64 rather than float32
  3. Randomly dropping 20% of the hidden units differently in each of the two forward passes of the finite difference
  4. Perturbing a weight by 1e-6 instead of 1e-5
A finite difference compares the loss at two nearby points of the same function. If randomness (dropout, shuffling) differs between the two evaluations, the difference measures the noise instead of the slope. Small batches, double precision and a smaller ε are all recommended practice in chapter 2.
