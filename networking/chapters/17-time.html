Time, Clocks & Ordering

Every computer has a clock, and every one of them is slightly wrong in its own way. In this chapter you'll see why "which thing happened first?" is surprisingly hard to answer across a network, and then learn the beautiful trick that distributed systems use instead: stop asking what time it was, and track what could have influenced what.

Why this matters

Almost every question a distributed system must answer is secretly a question about order. Which write wins? Did this reply come after that question? Was the lock released before the next person took it? In a single program, the CPU runs one instruction at a time and "first" is obvious. Across machines connected by unreliable networks (chapter 15) with no shared clock, it is not. Get this wrong and you lose data silently, with no error message anywhere. The ideas here (Lamport clocks, vector clocks) are the foundation for replication in chapter 18.

Why we care about order

Imagine a chat app built the obvious way. Every message gets stamped with the time on the sender's phone, and the app shows messages sorted by that stamp. Alice sends "Lunch?", and a second later Bob replies "Sure!". Everything should be fine.

Except Alice's phone clock happens to be 3 seconds fast. Her message is stamped 12:00:03, while Bob's reply, from a perfectly accurate phone, is stamped 12:00:02. Sorted by timestamp, the chat shows the reply above the question.

This is a harmless version of the problem. Here are the less harmless ones:

  • Which write wins? Two users edit the same record. A common rule is last-write-wins (LWW): keep the version with the latest timestamp and discard the other. If timestamps come from unsynchronised clocks, "latest" is partly luck.
  • Debugging. Logs from ten servers are merged and sorted by timestamp to reconstruct what happened. If the clocks disagree, the merged log can show effects before their causes and send you hunting for a bug that isn't there.
  • Expiry. "This lock expires at 12:00:10." Whose 12:00:10? If the lock server's clock and the lock holder's clock disagree, two processes can both believe they hold the lock.

So we need to be careful about two different questions that sound alike:

"What time is it?"
Useful for humans, billing and certificate expiry. Physical clocks answer this, approximately.
"What happened first?"
What correctness depends on. Physical clocks answer this only as well as they are synchronised. Logical clocks answer it exactly, for the cases that matter.

Physical clocks: how they work and how they drift

Inside your computer is a tiny quartz crystal that vibrates at a nearly fixed frequency (commonly 32,768 times per second for the always-on clock chip). A counter ticks once per vibration, and the operating system turns "ticks so far" into a date and time. Cheap, tiny and low-power, but not perfect: a crystal's frequency depends slightly on temperature, age and manufacturing.

The error is measured in parts per million (ppm). A clock that is 1 ppm fast gains one microsecond for every second that passes. That sounds like nothing, but it adds up. The arithmetic: there are 86,400 seconds in a day, so a 1 ppm error gains 86,400 µs, or about 0.086 s, per day.

Analogy: a hundred cheap wristwatches

Imagine everyone in a town owns a wristwatch that gains or loses a few seconds a day, each by a different amount. At noon they all agree. By the weekend nobody does, and no watch can tell which of the others is right. To keep the town roughly in step, someone has to read the church clock and tell everyone how far off they are. That is what NTP does for computers.

Where it breaks down: the person carrying the message from the church takes an unknown time to walk there and back, and the road might be uphill in one direction. That uncertainty in the "message delay" is exactly what limits how accurate clock synchronisation can be.

NTP: asking a better clock what time it is

The Network Time Protocol (NTP) lets a computer ask a time server (which itself synchronises with more accurate clocks, such as GPS receivers or atomic clocks) for the current time and then adjust its own. It runs over UDP (chapter 7) on port 123.

The hard part is that the reply is already out of date when it arrives. If the server says "it is 10:00:00.000" and the reply took 60 ms to cross the network, what time is it now? NTP's trick is to record four timestamps in one request/reply exchange:

Go deeper: the NTP formulas optional

With t0 (client send), t1 (server receive), t2 (server send) and t3 (client receive):

round-trip delay  δ = (t3 − t0) − (t2 − t1)
clock offset      θ = ((t1 − t0) + (t2 − t3)) / 2

The offset θ is how far the server's clock is ahead of the client's. With our numbers: δ = (10.062 − 10.000) − (10.532 − 10.530) = 0.062 − 0.002 = 0.060 s, and θ = ((10.530 − 10.000) + (10.532 − 10.062)) / 2 = (0.530 + 0.470) / 2 = +0.500 s. The client adds 0.5 s to its clock.

Why does averaging work? t1 − t0 is the true outbound delay plus the offset; t2 − t3 is the offset minus the return delay. Add them and, if the two delays are equal, the delays cancel and leave twice the offset. If the path is asymmetric (say the outbound leg took 50 ms and the return leg 10 ms) the estimate is wrong by half the difference between the legs (here 20 ms), and the client can't know. The worst-case error is therefore bounded by half the round-trip delay, which is why NTP prefers servers with short, stable round trips and takes many samples.

Small offsets are usually corrected by slewing: speeding up or slowing down the clock very slightly until it catches up, so time never jumps. Large offsets (roughly more than a tenth of a second for classic implementations) are often corrected by stepping: setting the clock directly, which is where backwards jumps come from.

How accurate is it, really?

Over the public Internet, NTP typically keeps clocks within a few milliseconds to a few tens of milliseconds of true time. On a well-run local network with a nearby server it can do better than a millisecond; specialised protocols (such as PTP, with hardware timestamping) reach microseconds. But "within a few ms" is an enormous amount of time to a computer: in 1 ms a modern server can run millions of instructions and handle many requests. Two events 3 ms apart on different machines cannot be ordered by their NTP-synchronised timestamps. And NTP can fail: the server might be unreachable, the network asymmetric, or a virtual machine paused and resumed, leaving its clock minutes behind until the next correction.

Clocks can jump backwards

When NTP steps a clock that was running fast, the reading goes backwards: you might read 12:00:05.2, and a moment later 12:00:04.9. Other causes: an administrator setting the time, a VM restored from a snapshot, and leap seconds. Earth's rotation is slightly irregular, so every so often the world's time authorities insert an extra second into UTC (23:59:60) to keep clocks aligned with the sun. There have been 27 so far, the last at the end of 2016, and in 2022 the international community agreed to stop adding them in the coming decade or so. Some systems handle them by repeating a second, others by smearing the extra second gradually over many hours. Either way, software that assumed "time only moves forward" has crashed on leap-second days.

Common misconception: "I'll just synchronise the clocks perfectly"

You can't. Synchronising means exchanging messages, and messages take unpredictable time (chapter 15). Two clocks can be made very close but never provably identical, and the difference between "very close" and "identical" is exactly where silent data loss lives. The right question isn't "how do I get perfect clocks?" but "how do I make my system correct even when clocks are wrong?"

Two kinds of clock on every computer

Operating systems give programs two different clocks, and mixing them up is a classic bug:

Wall-clock time

"What's the date and time?" Counts from a fixed starting point (usually 1 January 1970 UTC). Corrected by NTP, so it can jump forwards or backwards. Use it for timestamps shown to humans and for dates. Examples: Python time.time(), JavaScript Date.now().

Monotonic time

"How much time has passed since some arbitrary moment (like boot)?" It never goes backwards and isn't set by anyone, so it's meaningless as a date. Use it to measure durations, such as timeouts and latency. Examples: Python time.monotonic(), JavaScript performance.now().

Consider a timeout written as "if now() - start > 5 seconds, give up". If now() is the wall clock and NTP steps it back 10 seconds mid-request, the elapsed time becomes negative and the timeout never fires. If it steps forward, the request times out instantly. With the monotonic clock neither happens. This is the clock to use for the timeouts of chapter 16. (Note that a monotonic clock is still local to one machine: comparing monotonic readings from two machines is meaningless.)

Go deeper: Google's TrueTime and honest uncertainty optional

Google's globally distributed database Spanner takes a different attitude: if you can't know the exact time, say how unsure you are. Its clock API, TrueTime, doesn't return a single instant. It returns an interval, "the true time is somewhere between earliest and latest". Google keeps that interval narrow (on the order of a few milliseconds) by putting GPS receivers and atomic clocks in its data centres and having machines sync against them frequently, while assuming the worst possible drift in between.

Knowing the uncertainty lets the database order transactions safely. Before committing a transaction with timestamp T, it simply waits until the interval says that T is definitely in the past (a "commit wait"). Any transaction that starts later is then guaranteed to get a later timestamp, even if it runs on the other side of the planet. The price is a few milliseconds of waiting on each write, and the clock hardware. Without such infrastructure, the safe approach is the one in the rest of this chapter: don't rely on wall clocks for correctness.

The danger: last-write-wins with skewed clocks

Now we can see how wrong clocks silently destroy data. Suppose a database replicates a value to several copies, and when copies disagree, it keeps the write with the highest timestamp (LWW). Machine X has an accurate clock. Machine Y's clock is 2 seconds behind.

The user who wrote x = 2 got a success response, and their data is gone. Nothing crashed; nothing was logged as wrong. This failure is real in systems that resolve conflicts by wall-clock timestamp, and it is why engineers who understand it treat LWW as a deliberate, lossy choice (acceptable for, say, a "last seen online" field, dangerous for a bank balance) rather than a default.

Logical time: order without clocks

In 1978 Leslie Lamport wrote a paper (Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System) with a liberating insight: we don't need to know when things happened; we need to know what could have caused what. Physical time is only a means to that end, and a poor one. Cause and effect can be tracked exactly using nothing but the messages the machines already exchange.

The happens-before relation

Define events as things that happen on a process: doing a calculation, sending a message, receiving a message. Say event a happens before event b, written a → b, if a could have influenced b. Precisely, a → b holds if any of these is true:

  1. Same process: a and b are on the same machine and a occurred earlier. (A single machine knows its own order.)
  2. Message: a is the sending of a message and b is the receiving of that same message. (You can't receive something before it's sent.)
  3. Transitivity: there is some c with a → c and c → b. (Influence passes along chains.)

Two events where neither a → b nor b → a are called concurrent, written a ∥ b. This does not mean "at the same instant"; it means no chain of messages connects them, so neither could have known about the other. It is a statement about information, not about the clock.

Analogy: letters between cities

Three friends live in three cities and communicate only by letter. If Priya writes a letter and Quinn reads it, then replies, Quinn's reply is definitely after Priya's writing, whatever either one's watch says. But if Priya writes a letter in the morning and Ravi, who has never heard from either of them, mows his lawn that afternoon, there is no sense in which one "caused" the other. We may argue which was "first" by the sun, but to the three friends it simply doesn't matter, and no one can ever tell.

Where it breaks down: in real systems the "letters" can be lost, duplicated or delayed indefinitely, and some "mowing the lawn" events (such as a database being modified by an external process) influence others through channels the system doesn't see. Happens-before only tracks influence that travels through messages we observe.

Lamport clocks: a counter that respects causality

Can we give every event a number, such that if a → b then a's number is smaller? Yes, with a very small piece of machinery. Each process keeps one integer counter, its Lamport clock (named after its inventor), starting at 0. The rules:

  1. Local event: increment your counter by 1. The event's timestamp is the new value.
  2. Send: increment your counter, then attach the new value to the message as its timestamp.
  3. Receive: set your counter to max(your counter, the message's timestamp) + 1. The receive event gets that value.

The third rule is the heart of it. If your counter is at 3 and a message stamped 17 arrives, you jump ahead to 18. The receive is now stamped later than the send, as it must be, and every later event of yours is stamped even higher.

What Lamport clocks guarantee, and what they don't

Writing L(e) for the timestamp of event e, the rules give a precise guarantee:

The Lamport guarantee

If a → b then L(a) < L(b).

(Why? Along a single process the counter only goes up; across a message the receive rule forces the receive above the send; and a chain of such steps keeps growing.)

The converse is false. If L(a) < L(b), you cannot conclude that a → b. The events might be concurrent, and the numbers just happen to come out that way. Imagine Process 1 does nothing for a while and Process 2 does five local events, then process 1 does one. P2's fifth event has L = 5 and P1's first has L = 1. They are concurrent, yet "1 < 5". A Lamport timestamp is a correct necessary condition for causality (if the timestamps are in the wrong order, it can't be causal) but not a sufficient one.

Common misconception: "smaller Lamport timestamp means it happened first"

It only means that it could have happened first. Lamport timestamps can't tell "a happened before b" from "a and b are concurrent". If you need to know whether two updates were concurrent (for example to detect a conflict), a single counter is not enough. That's what vector clocks, below, are for.

Turning it into a total order

Even though Lamport timestamps can't detect concurrency, they are still very useful, because they give every event a number, and numbers can be sorted. Two events can have the same number (on different processes), so we break ties with the process id: order by the pair (L, process id). The result is a total order over all events: every pair of events is ranked, and all machines compute the same ranking. It agrees with cause and effect (if a → b, a sorts first), but where events are concurrent it picks an arbitrary yet consistent order. That is often exactly what's needed: Lamport used it to build a distributed mutual-exclusion algorithm, and many systems use it to give all replicas the same order for a stream of updates. A chat app with Lamport timestamps can never show a reply above the question it answers.

Vector clocks: detecting concurrency

To recognise concurrent events we need each event to carry more information than one number. A vector clock gives every process its own counter, and each event carries the whole array: with three processes A, B, C, a timestamp looks like [2, 0, 1], meaning "I know about 2 events on A, 0 on B, 1 on C". Think of it as a summary of everything the process knows has happened anywhere.

The rules extend Lamport's. Process i keeps a vector V of N counters (N = number of processes):

  1. Local event: increment your own slot, V[i] += 1.
  2. Send: increment your own slot, then attach a copy of the whole vector to the message.
  3. Receive: merge: set each slot to the element-wise maximum of your vector and the message's, then increment your own slot.

The beauty is in comparing two timestamps V(a) and V(b):

a happened before b

Every slot of V(a) is ≤ the matching slot of V(b) (and the vectors differ). Everything a knew about, b knew about too.

b happened before a

The same test the other way round.

Concurrent

Neither vector is ≤ the other: a is bigger in some slot and b is bigger in another. Each knows something the other doesn't.

A worked example

Three processes. Follow the vectors and, for comparison, the Lamport values:

#EventHow the vector is computedVectorLamport
1A: local eventA's slot +1[1,0,0]1
2A sends to BA's slot +1; message carries this[2,0,0]2
3B receivesmax([0,0,0],[2,0,0]) = [2,0,0], then B's slot +1[2,1,0]max(0,2)+1 = 3
4C: local eventC's slot +1[0,0,1]1

Now compare some pairs:

  • #2 vs #6: [2,0,0] ≤ [2,2,2] in every slot, so #2 happened before #6. Correct: A's message influenced B, whose message influenced C.
  • #4 vs #6: [0,0,1] ≤ [2,2,2], so #4 happened before #6 (same process, C).
  • #4 vs #2: [0,0,1] vs [2,0,0]: the first is bigger in C's slot (1 > 0), the second bigger in A's slot (2 > 0). Concurrent. C's local event and A's send have nothing to do with each other.

Look at the Lamport column for that last pair: #4 has L = 1 and #2 has L = 2. A Lamport clock says "1 < 2", which looks like #4 came first. The vector clock tells the truth: they're concurrent. That is the entire difference between the two schemes in one example.

Where vector clocks are used, and what they cost

Vector clocks matter wherever several copies of data can be updated independently and conflicts have to be noticed, not hidden. Dynamo-style stores (the design behind the Dynamo paper and databases such as Riak) attach a version vector to each stored object. Say two users add items to the same shopping cart through two different servers while they can't talk to each other:

  • User 1's server stores the cart with version {X:1}; user 2's server stores a different cart with {Y:1}.
  • When the servers later compare, neither version is ≤ the other, so the vectors are concurrent. The database does not pick a winner; it keeps both "siblings" and shows them to the next reader.
  • The application merges them (for a cart: take the union of items) and writes the result back with a vector that dominates both, such as {X:1, Y:1} bumped once more. Now one version is clearly newer and the older ones can be discarded.

Compare that with last-write-wins, which would silently drop one cart. The cost is complexity: the application must handle conflicts. And the vector itself grows: it holds one counter per process that has ever written. Lamport clocks are one integer regardless of system size; vector clocks are O(N). With thousands of clients or nodes that gets heavy, so real systems prune old entries or track only the replicas (not the clients), accepting some imprecision.

Go deeper: hybrid logical clocks optional

A hybrid logical clock (HLC) combines both worlds in a single compact timestamp: the physical wall-clock time plus a small logical counter that breaks ties and absorbs clock jumps. It preserves the Lamport guarantee (if a → b then HLC(a) < HLC(b)) while staying close to real time, so humans can read it and "give me everything since 12:00" still works. Several distributed databases use this approach. Like Lamport clocks it can't detect concurrency, but it is cheap, constant size and robust to clock skew.

(Your numbers will differ, and some newer macOS or Linux versions may not ship sntp; the idea is what matters.) The +/- in the first line is the uncertainty, tying back to NTP's round-trip error and to TrueTime's intervals: even the measurement of the error has an error.

Another safe check needs no special tools. Every web server sends its own idea of the time in the HTTP Date header (chapter 10). Compare it with your machine:

$ curl -sI https://example.com | grep -i '^date'; date -u
date: Mon, 12 Oct 2026 10:15:21 GMT
Mon Oct 12 10:15:22 UTC 2026                     # your own clock, same format

They differ by about a second here, but HTTP dates are rounded down to whole seconds and include network delay, so this is only a rough check. Try it on two machines, or on a phone and a laptop, and compare. If you have a spare VM that has been suspended for a while, compare it too: its clock is often wildly off.

Finally, see the two kinds of clock in code. This Python program measures a 2-second sleep with both:

import time

wall0, mono0 = time.time(), time.monotonic()
time.sleep(2)
wall1, mono1 = time.time(), time.monotonic()

print(f"wall clock elapsed: {wall1 - wall0:.3f}s")
print(f"monotonic elapsed:  {mono1 - mono0:.3f}s")
$ python3 clocks.py
wall clock elapsed: 2.002s
monotonic elapsed:  2.002s

Normally they agree. But if something steps the system clock during those 2 seconds (NTP, or you changing the time in settings, which needs admin rights so it's entirely optional to try), the wall-clock result would be wildly wrong, even negative, while the monotonic result stays at about 2.002. That is why timeouts and benchmarks use monotonic().

Distributed systems connection

Chapter 16 taught that you can't tell a slow machine from a dead one. This chapter adds a second unsettling fact: you can't trust two machines to agree on what time it is. Together they push distributed systems toward designs that do not depend on synchronised clocks for correctness: logical clocks for ordering, monotonic clocks for timeouts, and wall clocks only where an error of a few milliseconds is harmless.

The next question follows naturally. Once you keep several copies of your data on different machines so the system survives failures, those copies will receive updates in different orders at different times. How do you keep them in sync, and what exactly can a reader expect to see? That is chapter 18: Replication & Consistency, where version numbers, vector clocks and last-write-wins reappear as practical tools, and where we'll learn what a system can promise during a network partition.

Key takeaways

  • Computer clocks drift (tens of ppm means seconds per week) and are corrected by NTP, which is accurate to roughly milliseconds, never perfectly, and can make time jump backwards.
  • Use monotonic clocks to measure durations and timeouts; use wall clocks only for human-facing dates and loose ordering.
  • Last-write-wins with wall-clock timestamps can silently discard the newer write when a clock is behind.
  • Happens-before (→) tracks possible causality: same-process order, send before receive, and transitivity. Events not connected by it are concurrent.
  • Lamport clocks: if a → b then L(a) < L(b), but not the other way round. With process-id tie-breaks they give a consistent total order.
  • Vector clocks (one counter per process, merged by element-wise max) exactly distinguish before, after and concurrent, at O(N) cost per timestamp.
  • Design so that correctness doesn't depend on clocks agreeing; when you do need real time, make the uncertainty explicit (TrueTime).

Check your understanding

A server's clock gains 20 µs every second (20 ppm) and is never corrected. Roughly how far off is it after one week?

  1. About 12 milliseconds
  2. About 1.2 seconds
  3. About 12 seconds
  4. About 2 minutes
20 µs × 86,400 s per day ≈ 1.7 s per day, and 7 days ≈ 12 s. Drift sounds negligible in ppm but accumulates, which is why clocks need constant correction.

You're writing code that gives up on a network request after 5 seconds. Which clock should measure the 5 seconds?

  1. The monotonic clock, because it never jumps backwards or gets set by NTP
  2. The wall clock, because it is synchronised with the real world by NTP
  3. Either one, they are equally reliable for durations
  4. A Lamport clock, because it respects causality
The wall clock can be stepped by NTP or an admin, making an elapsed-time calculation too large, too small, or negative. The monotonic clock is built for measuring durations. A Lamport clock counts events, not seconds.

Two replicas use last-write-wins by wall-clock timestamp. Machine Y's clock is 2 seconds behind. Y writes x=2 one second after X wrote x=1. What happens?

  1. Both values are kept and the user is asked to choose
  2. x=2 wins because it really happened later
  3. The system detects the clock problem and reports an error
  4. x=1 wins and the later write is silently lost
Y's write is stamped earlier than X's despite happening later, so LWW discards it, and no one is told. Keeping both values would need a mechanism like vector clocks that detects the conflict.

Event a has Lamport timestamp 3 and event b has Lamport timestamp 7. What can you conclude?

  1. a happened before b
  2. Either a happened before b, or they are concurrent; b did not happen before a
  3. b happened before a
  4. a and b are concurrent
The guarantee is only "a → b implies L(a) < L(b)". Its contrapositive says that if L(a) is not less than L(b), a cannot have happened before b. Here L(b) > L(a), so b → a is ruled out, but a → b and a ∥ b both remain possible.

Vector clocks [2,1,0] and [1,2,0] are attached to two versions of a record. What is their relationship?

  1. The first happened before the second
  2. The second happened before the first
  3. They are concurrent: each has an update the other has not seen
  4. They are identical copies
The first is larger in slot 0 (2 > 1) and the second is larger in slot 1 (2 > 1). Neither is ≤ the other in all slots, so neither happened before the other: a conflict that needs merging.

What is the main cost of vector clocks compared with Lamport clocks?

  1. Each timestamp grows with the number of processes (one counter per process)
  2. They need a GPS receiver in every machine
  3. They can't be ordered at all
  4. They require all machines' wall clocks to agree
A vector clock holds one counter per process, so its size is proportional to the number of processes, while a Lamport clock is a single integer. In exchange you get exact detection of concurrency. Neither kind of logical clock needs synchronised wall clocks at all.
