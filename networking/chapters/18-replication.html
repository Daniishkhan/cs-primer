Replication & Consistency

Keep one copy of your data and a single dead machine takes you down. Keep several, and you've traded that problem for a harder one: how do the copies stay in sync when messages are slow, lost or reordered, and what is a reader allowed to see in the meantime? This chapter is a tour of the answers, and of the famous trade-off at their centre.

Why this matters

Nearly every serious system you use (your bank, your chat app, your cloud storage) stores each piece of data on several machines. Whenever you've seen a post vanish after a refresh, a "like" count flicker, or a shopping cart that briefly forgot an item, you've met the consequences of replication. Understanding why these glitches happen, and which of them a system has chosen to allow, is the difference between using distributed data and being surprised by it.

Why keep copies?

Replication means keeping a copy of the same data on several machines (called replicas). People do it for four reasons:

Durability

A disk dies, a data centre floods. If the data lives on only one machine, it's gone. With copies in several places, losing one loses nothing.

Availability

Machines crash and get rebooted (chapter 15). If one replica is down, another can keep serving. If each machine is down 1% of the time and failures were independent, all three of three copies would be down only about one time in a million (0.01 × 0.01 × 0.01). In real life, failures are not perfectly independent (same rack, same power), so copies should be spread out.

Read scaling

One machine can only answer so many questions per second. Ten replicas can answer roughly ten times as many reads, if reads can go to any copy.

Latency

Light in fiber travels about 200,000 km/s, so London to Sydney (about 17,000 km) costs at least 85 ms one way, 170 ms round trip, before any processing (chapter 13). A copy in Sydney answers Sydney users in a few milliseconds.

The catch is the whole subject of this chapter. If the data never changed, replication would be trivial: copy it and you're done. But data changes, and the network between replicas is unreliable: messages are delayed, lost, duplicated, and replicas crash and come back (chapter 16). So at any moment, different replicas may hold different values for the same item. Every design in this chapter is a different answer to: how do we apply updates to all copies, in a sensible order, despite failures, and what do readers see while copies disagree?

Single-leader replication

The simplest scheme, and the most common, is single-leader replication (also called primary/backup or leader/follower). One replica is chosen as the leader. The rules:

  1. All writes go to the leader. It applies each change to its own data and appends it to an ordered replication log (a list of changes: "set x = 5", "add 1 to y", …).
  2. Followers copy the log. Each follower receives the leader's log and applies the changes in the same order. Since every replica applies the same changes in the same order, they end up in the same state.
  3. Reads can go anywhere: to the leader (always current) or to a follower (spreading the load).

Notice how neatly this sidesteps the problem of the previous chapter. The leader serialises writes into one sequence, so no clocks or vector clocks are needed to decide order. The price is that the leader is a bottleneck and a single point of failure for writes (more on that shortly).

Analogy: the minute-taker

A committee has one official minute-taker. Everyone who wants something recorded tells the minute-taker, who writes it in the master minutes in order. Each member then makes their own photocopy of the minutes, updating it whenever new pages are circulated. Anyone can read their own copy, but a member who hasn't received the latest pages is reading yesterday's meeting.

Where it breaks down: a human minute-taker doesn't vanish without warning, and if one does, the committee can see it. In a distributed system, replicas can't tell whether the leader is dead or just slow (chapter 16), which is what makes replacing the leader dangerous.

Synchronous or asynchronous?

When should the leader tell the client "your write succeeded"? The choice is a trade-off:

  • Asynchronous replication: the leader writes locally, replies "OK" immediately, and sends the change to followers in the background. Fast, and the leader keeps working even if followers are slow or down. But if the leader crashes before the change reaches any follower, an acknowledged write is lost.
  • Synchronous replication: the leader waits until a follower confirms it stored the change before replying "OK". Now the write exists on at least two machines before the client is told it succeeded, so losing one machine can't lose it. But every write takes at least one extra round trip, and if that follower is slow or unreachable the write stalls (or must time out, chapter 16).

Making all followers synchronous is rarely done: with ten followers, one slow one slows every write, and any single failure blocks writes entirely. Many systems therefore run fully asynchronous replication, accepting that a leader crash may lose the last few writes, in exchange for speed.

Replication lag and its strange symptoms

With asynchronous replication, a follower is always a little behind the leader. The delay is called replication lag. It's usually milliseconds, but can stretch to seconds or minutes when the network is congested or a follower is overloaded. While lag exists, reading from a follower gives you slightly old data. Two specific anomalies matter in practice:

Read-your-writes violation

You post a comment. The write goes to the leader, which replies "saved". You refresh the page, and that read is served by a follower that hasn't yet received your comment. The page shows no comment. You've just watched your own write vanish.

Monotonic reads violation

You read from follower 1, which is up to date, and see a comment. You refresh, and the load balancer sends you to follower 2, which is further behind. The comment disappears. Time appears to run backwards. The guarantee that you never see data older than what you've already seen is called monotonic reads, and plain asynchronous replication doesn't provide it.

Common fixes are cheap compared with fully synchronous replication: route a user's reads to the leader for a short time after they write, or remember the log position of the user's last write and only read from a follower that has caught up to it ("read-your-writes"); and send each user to the same replica every time ("sticky" routing) for monotonic reads. These are examples of session guarantees, which we'll place on a ladder shortly.

When the leader dies: failover

If the leader crashes, someone must take over, in a process called failover: detect that the leader is gone, choose a new one (typically the follower with the most complete log), and tell clients and other followers to use it. Each step has traps:

  • Detection is a guess. Followers conclude "the leader is dead" when it stops responding for some timeout. But as chapter 16 explained, silence can mean slow, not dead. A too-short timeout causes needless failovers during a busy moment, which makes everything worse.
  • Lost writes. With asynchronous replication the new leader may not have the latest acknowledged writes. Those are discarded, even though clients were told they succeeded.
  • Split brain. The old leader may only have been cut off or paused, and then come back still believing it is the leader. Now two machines both accept writes, the data diverges, and merging it is painful. Systems defend against this by "fencing" the old leader (refusing its requests via a changing epoch or term number), but doing so reliably requires a group of machines to agree on who the leader is.

That last requirement, agreement among unreliable machines, is exactly the consensus problem. We'll solve it properly in chapter 20, which is how modern systems automate leader election safely.

Multi-leader and leaderless replication

A single leader means every write must reach one place, which may be on the far side of the planet or unreachable at the moment. Two alternatives drop that restriction.

Multi-leader

Several replicas accept writes, typically one leader per data centre. Each leader replicates to the others asynchronously. Local writes are fast and survive a data-centre outage or an inter-data-centre link failure. Offline-capable apps (a calendar on your phone) work the same way: your phone is a leader that syncs later.

Leaderless

No leader at all. A client sends each write to several replicas directly, and each read asks several replicas and picks the newest answer. Replicas that missed an update are fixed later in the background. This is the design of Dynamo-style databases (Amazon's Dynamo paper, Cassandra, Riak), and quorums (next section) are its core idea.

Both give up something single-leader gave for free: a single order of writes. Two users can now update the same item at the same moment on different replicas. Neither update is "first", and the replicas discover the clash only when they exchange updates. That is a write conflict, and it must be resolved somehow.

Resolving conflicts

  • Last write wins (LWW). Keep the update with the highest timestamp, drop the other. Simple, always converges, and, as chapter 17 showed, silently loses data, particularly when timestamps come from skewed wall clocks. Acceptable only when losing an update is fine.
  • Keep both, ask the application. Use version vectors (chapter 17) to recognise that the updates were concurrent, store both as "siblings", and let application code merge them on the next read (for a shopping cart: union of the items). Correct, but pushes work to the application.
  • Merge automatically with CRDTs. Conflict-free replicated data types are data structures designed so that updates made independently on different replicas can always be merged, in any order, into the same result. A counter where each replica tracks its own increments and the total is their sum, or a set that only grows, are the simple examples; collaborative text editors use more elaborate ones. Applications get automatic convergence, but only for operations that fit such structures.

Quorums: reading and writing a majority

Leaderless systems rely on a clever overlap argument. Say you have N replicas of each item. Choose two numbers:

  • W, the write quorum: a write is considered successful once W replicas have acknowledged it.
  • R, the read quorum: a read asks R replicas and uses the answers.

The key rule: if R + W > N, every read set overlaps every write set in at least one replica. That overlapping replica saw the latest successful write, so the reader will find it.

Why it must be true: a successful write is on at least W replicas. A read looks at R replicas. If those two groups shared no replica, together they'd contain W + R different replicas, but only N exist. Since W + R > N, they can't all be different; they must share at least one. (It's the pigeonhole principle: 5 pigeons can't each get their own hole if there are only 4 holes.)

But how does the reader know which of the R answers is the freshest? Every stored value carries a version: a number that increases with every update. The reader takes the answer with the highest version. Where do versions come from? A single leader can just count. Without one, systems use timestamps (with the clock problems of chapter 17) or version vectors, which can also say "these two are concurrent".

Two things to remember. First, a smaller W or R means fewer replicas to wait for, so lower latency: the request completes when the W-th fastest replica answers, not the slowest, which helps with tail latency (chapter 13). Second, the operation fails if fewer than W (or R) replicas are reachable, so larger quorums tolerate fewer failures. Replication is a dial between "fast and loose" and "safe and strict", not a free lunch.

    Go deeper: where quorums fall short optional

    R + W > N sounds like a guarantee of "always read the latest value", but several real-world effects weaken it:

    • Sloppy quorums and hinted handoff. To stay available when some of the "home" replicas are unreachable, a system may accept the write on other nodes temporarily and hand it back later. The W acknowledgements then don't have to be from the replicas a reader will ask, so the overlap argument no longer holds until the hand-off completes.
    • Failed writes aren't undone. A write that reached fewer than W replicas is reported as failed, but those replicas still hold it. A later read may or may not return it.
    • Concurrent writes. Two clients writing at once need conflict resolution (LWW, siblings or CRDTs, above). If the winner is chosen by clock timestamps, chapter 17's problems return.
    • Reads racing writes. While a write is still spreading, reader A's quorum may include a replica that has the new value, and reader B's (later) quorum may include only replicas that don't yet. A sees the new value, then B sees the old one, which is not linearizable (next section) even though R + W > N.
    • Restored replicas. If a node with fresh data is replaced by one restored from an old backup, the number of replicas holding the latest value silently drops below W.

    So quorums give you probably fresh, not provably the latest. For the strongest guarantees, systems use consensus (chapter 20) instead.

    The consistency ladder

    When copies of data can differ, the useful question becomes: what is a reader allowed to see? A consistency model is a promise about that. There is a ladder of them, from strong promises that make programming easy but cost coordination, down to weak promises that are cheap and available but put more burden on you.

    Let's make each rung concrete.

    Linearizability: one copy, in real time

    Linearizability (often just called "strong consistency") is the gold standard. The system behaves as if there were only one copy of the data, and every operation happened atomically at some instant between when it was sent and when it was answered. The crucial part is that it respects real time: if operation A finished before operation B started, B must see A's effects.

    Example: a live football score. Alice refreshes and sees 3–2. She immediately phones Bob, who then loads the page. With linearizability Bob is guaranteed to see 3–2 or later, never the old 2–2. Without it, Bob's request might hit a replica that hasn't caught up, and Alice would be (rightly) puzzled. Things that truly need linearizability include locks ("only one holder at a time"), unique-username checks and leader election: they all depend on everyone agreeing about the single latest state.

    Sequential consistency: same order for everyone

    A slightly weaker promise: all clients see operations in one common order, consistent with each client's own program order, but that order doesn't have to line up with the wall clock. Everyone agrees on what happened and in which sequence, but a replica is allowed to be "behind", showing the world as it was a moment ago, as long as it never shows things out of order.

    Causal consistency: causes before effects

    Causal consistency only guarantees order for events that are causally related, using the happens-before relation from chapter 17. If you post a question and someone replies to it, nobody anywhere will ever see the reply without the question. But two unrelated posts may appear in different orders to different people, and nobody can complain, because neither influenced the other. This is a sweet spot: it keeps the guarantees people intuitively expect, and it can be provided without every operation waiting on a global coordinator, so it is among the strongest models a system can offer while remaining available during a network partition (next section).

    Session guarantees: it makes sense to me

    Cheaper still are promises made to a single user's session:

    • Read-your-writes: after you write something, your later reads reflect it. (The comment example above.)
    • Monotonic reads: once you've seen a value, you never later see an older one.
    • Monotonic writes / writes-follow-reads: your writes are applied in the order you made them, and a write that responds to something you read is ordered after it.

    They're often implemented with small tricks, such as sticky routing or tracking log positions, and they remove the most jarring glitches without any global coordination.

    Eventual consistency: it'll sort itself out

    The weakest useful promise: if no new updates are made, eventually all replicas converge to the same value. It says nothing about when, or what you might see before then. The Domain Name System (chapter 9) is a familiar example: change a record and, for up to the TTL, different resolvers around the world keep serving the old answer, then the new one. That is eventual consistency, and it's perfectly fine for DNS. It would be a disaster for a bank balance.

    Common misconception: "eventual consistency means broken" (or "strong consistency means always correct")

    These are tools, not grades. Eventual consistency is a sensible choice for like counts, view counters, caches, and many "last seen" fields where being briefly out of date is harmless and being fast and available matters. Strong consistency is the right choice for money transfers or locks, but it costs latency (a round trip to a majority, perhaps across continents) and sometimes availability. The art is matching the model to what each piece of data actually needs.

    The CAP theorem, stated carefully

    You'll often hear "CAP: pick two of consistency, availability and partition tolerance". That slogan is misleading. Here is what the theorem actually says.

    Definitions, as used in the theorem:

    • Consistency (C) means linearizability, the top rung of the ladder. (It is not the "C" of database ACID transactions.)
    • Availability (A) means every request sent to a non-failed node receives a (non-error) response.
    • Partition (P): the network drops or delays messages between some nodes indefinitely, splitting them into groups that can't talk to each other.

    The CAP theorem

    If a network partition happens, a replicated system cannot provide both linearizability and availability. It must choose.

    Here is the argument. Two replicas each hold x = 5, and a partition cuts the link between them. A client on one side writes x = 9 to replica 2. A second client on the other side asks replica 1 for x. Replica 1 cannot know about the write, because it cannot hear from replica 2. It has only two choices: answer anyway (x = 5, a stale value, so not linearizable), or refuse to answer until the partition heals (so not available). There is no third option.

    Common misconception: "pick any two of three"

    You cannot choose to drop P. Partitions are something the network does to you (chapter 15's "the network is reliable" fallacy), not an option you can decline. A "CA" system is really a single-node system, or one that simply breaks when a partition occurs. And when there's no partition, CAP says nothing: a system can be both consistent and available. CAP is a statement about what you give up during a partition, not a permanent label for a database.

    PACELC: the rest of the time

    Partitions are rare; normal operation is the common case, and CAP is silent about it. Daniel Abadi's PACELC refinement fills the gap: if there is a Partition, choose between Availability and Consistency; Else (normal operation), choose between Latency and Consistency. The second half matters every day: to give strong consistency, replicas must talk to each other before answering (a round trip, perhaps across continents), which costs time even when nothing is broken.

    Try it yourself

    Five experiments with the quorum explorer

    Use the widget above. Each experiment is a small thought-experiment you can check with your own eyes. Predict the outcome first, then click.

    1. The fast-and-loose setup. Choose preset 3 / 1 / 1. Set "A write reaches" to lowest-numbered. Press Write: only R1 gets it. Now click R1 to take it down, and press Read a few times. What do you get, and why? (Expected: a stale read. R2 and R3 never received the write, and R1, the only replica that had it, is unreachable. R + W = 2 ≤ 3, and with the one replica that held the data down, you can't even detect the problem.)
    2. The majority setup. Choose 3 / 2 / 2, set writes to lowest-numbered and reads to highest-numbered. Write, then read repeatedly. The write goes to R1 and R2, the read asks R2 and R3: they overlap at R2, so every read is fresh. Try other combinations of the selects, including random: you will never see a stale read.
    3. Taking nodes down. Still at 3 / 2 / 2, click one replica to take it down: reads and writes still work (2 up ≥ 2). Take down a second: both now fail, because fewer than 2 replicas are up. The status panel predicted this: "Failures tolerated: 1 down".
    4. The boundary. Choose 5 / 2 / 2 (R + W = 4 ≤ 5), writes lowest, reads highest, and read after a write: stale. Now raise R to 4 (R + W = 6 > 5) and read again: fresh, because 4 replicas must include one of the 2 written. Which single number change made the difference?
    5. Write-all, read-one. Choose 5 / 5 / 1. Reads are always fresh (a write reached every replica) and super fast. Now take one replica down and try to write. Why does it fail? What did we trade for those fast reads?

    After any stale read, press Sync up replicas and read again. This mimics the background repair that real systems use: replicas that are up exchange what they know and converge, which is the "eventual" in eventual consistency.

    Distributed systems connection

    Replication gave us availability and speed, but it raised new questions. Where does the data live when it no longer fits on one machine, or when a single replica group can't handle all the traffic? Next, chapter 19 splits data across machines (partitioning, or sharding, not to be confused with network partitions) using consistent hashing, so that adding a machine moves only a small slice of the data. Real systems combine both: split the data into shards, and replicate each shard.

    And everything in this chapter that said "this requires agreement" (who is the leader? which write is first? what is the latest value?) points at one of the deepest problems in the field. How can a group of unreliable machines, connected by an unreliable network, reliably agree on anything at all? That's chapter 20: Consensus & Raft.

    Key takeaways

    • We replicate for durability, availability, read scaling and latency; the price is keeping copies in sync over an unreliable network.
    • Single-leader replication gives one write order; async is fast but can lose acknowledged writes, sync is safer but slower and less available. Lag causes anomalies like lost read-your-writes and non-monotonic reads.
    • Failover is dangerous: failure detection is a guess, and split brain or lost writes are real risks. That needs consensus (chapter 20).
    • Multi-leader and leaderless designs allow concurrent writes, so they need conflict resolution: LWW (lossy, clock-dependent), siblings with vector clocks, or CRDTs.
    • Quorums: with N replicas, if R + W > N every read overlaps the latest write (pigeonhole), and versions pick the newest. Bigger quorums tolerate fewer failures and cost more latency. Caveats (sloppy quorums, concurrency) mean it is not full linearizability.
    • The consistency ladder: linearizable → sequential → causal → session guarantees → eventual. Stronger means fewer surprises and more coordination cost.
    • CAP: during a partition, choose between linearizability and availability. PACELC: otherwise, choose between latency and consistency. Neither is a permanent "pick 2" label.

    Check your understanding

    You post a comment and immediately refresh, but it isn't there. A minute later it appears. Which is the most likely explanation?

    1. The comment was lost and then recovered from a backup
    2. Your read went to a follower that hadn't yet received the write (replication lag)
    3. The leader rejected your write, then retried it
    4. Your computer's clock was wrong
    With asynchronous single-leader replication, a follower can briefly be behind the leader. Your write succeeded on the leader but your read hit a lagging follower: a read-your-writes violation. Routing recent writers' reads to the leader is a typical fix.

    A system has N = 5 replicas. Which choice of (W, R) guarantees that every read overlaps the most recent successful write?

    1. W = 2, R = 2
    2. W = 1, R = 4
    3. W = 3, R = 1
    4. W = 3, R = 3
    You need R + W > N = 5. W=2,R=2 gives 4; W=1,R=4 gives 5, which is not greater than 5 (the read could avoid the one replica written); W=3,R=1 gives 4. Only W=3,R=3 gives 6 > 5. (W=1, R=5 or W=5, R=1 would also work.)

    With asynchronous replication, what is the main risk when the leader crashes and a follower is promoted?

    1. Writes the old leader had acknowledged to clients may be missing on the new leader and be lost
    2. Every follower immediately deletes its data
    3. Clients can no longer read anything
    4. All timestamps are reset to zero
    Async replication acknowledges before followers have the change. If the leader dies in that window, the new leader never saw the write. Synchronous (or semi-synchronous) replication reduces that window at the cost of latency and availability.

    Alice sees a score of 3–2 on a linearizable service and immediately tells Bob, who then loads the page. What is Bob guaranteed?

    1. Nothing: his request might be served from an older copy
    2. He sees exactly 3–2, never a newer score
    3. He sees 3–2 or a later score, never an earlier one
    4. He sees whatever the closest replica has, but it will be correct within a minute
    Linearizability respects real time: Bob's read begins after Alice's finished, so he must see at least what she saw. That is what separates it from weaker models such as eventual consistency, where Bob could see the old score.

    Which statement about the CAP theorem is correct?

    1. A distributed system can freely choose any two of C, A and P, and must give up the third forever
    2. During a network partition, a system cannot be both linearizable and available; it must choose
    3. CAP proves that eventual consistency is always better than strong consistency
    4. CAP only applies to databases that use SQL
    Partitions can't be wished away, so P isn't optional. The theorem says that when a partition occurs you must choose between consistency (linearizability) and availability. When there is no partition, a system can offer both. It applies to any replicated system, and says nothing about which choice is "better".

    Two users edit the same record at the same time on different replicas of a leaderless store that resolves conflicts by last-write-wins using wall-clock timestamps. What is the danger?

    1. The replicas will never converge
    2. Both updates are always kept, doubling storage
    3. One update is silently discarded, and with skewed clocks it may be the genuinely later one
    4. The system returns an error to both users
    LWW always converges, but at the price of throwing away one of the concurrent updates without telling anyone. If clocks are skewed (chapter 17), the update dropped may be the one that actually happened later. Version vectors or CRDTs avoid silent loss.
