TLS & Security Basics

Your packets cross a dozen networks run by strangers. How can you type a password or a credit-card number and trust that nobody along the way can read or change it? The answer is a small miracle of mathematics called TLS, the thing behind the padlock in your browser, and you'll see how it works without needing any of the maths.

Why this matters

Everything in the chapters so far is sent "in the clear": readable by every device that carries it. Today nearly all web traffic, and most traffic between servers in data centers, is wrapped in TLS. Understanding it explains the padlock, certificate errors, and why "https" matters. And when you design distributed systems you will have to think about encryption between services, expiring certificates, and the extra round trips security costs.

The problem: the network is not on your side

Remember how a packet travels: from your laptop through your Wi-Fi, your router, your ISP, and perhaps ten more routers owned by other companies (chapter 5). The headers you saw in chapter 3 are just bytes in the frame, and every device that touches the frame can read them. If the payload is plain HTTP, that is readable too: your password, your messages, the page you asked for.

Three different bad things can happen to a plaintext connection:

Eavesdropping

Someone on the path (a snooper on the café Wi-Fi, a compromised router) reads your data.

Tampering

Someone changes data in flight: inserting ads into a page you fetch, or altering the amount of a bank transfer.

Impersonation

Someone pretends to be the server (say, a fake bank site, or a rogue Wi-Fi hotspot that redirects you), and you hand over your secrets.

To defend against all three, a secure connection needs three properties:

Confidentiality
Only the two endpoints can read the data. Others see scrambled bytes. (Defeats eavesdropping.)
Integrity
Any change to the data in transit is detected. (Defeats tampering.)
Authentication
You know who you're talking to: that the server really is example.com. (Defeats impersonation.)

TLS (Transport Layer Security) provides all three. It sits between TCP and the application: HTTP is simply spoken inside a TLS tunnel, and that combination is HTTPS. (TLS's older name was SSL; people still say "SSL certificate", but the SSL versions are long obsolete and insecure.) Because TLS works on bytes, the same trick protects email, chat, database connections and the calls between your own microservices.

Building blocks, from the ground up

Symmetric encryption: fast, but needs a shared secret

Encryption scrambles data so that it can only be unscrambled by someone holding the right key. With symmetric encryption (for example AES or ChaCha20) the same key locks and unlocks. It is extremely fast, so fast that modern CPUs have dedicated instructions for it, and it is what actually protects the bulk of your traffic.

The catch is obvious: both sides need the same secret key, before they can talk privately. How do you agree on a key when the only channel you have is one that everybody can listen to? If you just send the key over the open network, the eavesdropper copies it. This is the key exchange problem, and for centuries the answer was "meet in person beforehand". That can't work for a browser meeting a website for the first time.

Public-key cryptography: a lock anyone can close, but only you can open

Analogy: the padlock you hand out

You make thousands of identical open padlocks and leave them on a public table, but you keep the one and only key to all of them. Anyone who wants to send you a secret puts it in a box, snaps one of your padlocks shut on it, and mails the box. Everyone can close a padlock; nobody but you can open one. The padlock is your public key; the key you never reveal is your private key.

Where it breaks down: real public-key operations are mathematical, not physical, and they are slow compared to symmetric encryption, so they're used to set things up rather than to carry whole web pages. Also, the analogy hides the hardest question: how do you know the padlock on the table is really yours and not one an impostor put there? Hold that thought; it's what certificates are for.

That solves the "meet beforehand" problem: anyone can use your public key to send you something that only you can read. But there's an even neater trick that lets two strangers invent a shared secret key out of thin air, in the open.

Key agreement: mixing paint in public

In TLS 1.3, the two sides don't encrypt a secret to each other; they use a key exchange (called Diffie-Hellman, after its inventors) where each side combines its own private number with the other side's public number, and both arrive at the same secret. The standard way to picture it is mixing paint:

Analogy: paint mixing

Alice and Bob agree out loud on a public colour (yellow). Each secretly picks a private colour (Alice red, Bob blue), mixes it with the yellow, and sends the resulting mixture across the room. Everyone sees orange and green go by. Then Alice adds her private red to Bob's green, and Bob adds his private blue to Alice's orange. Both end up with exactly the same final mix, which no onlooker can make, because that needs one of the private colours.

Where it breaks down: real paint is a poor model of the security. The maths is stronger: with big enough numbers, working backwards is believed to be astronomically hard for any attacker, whereas with paint a good chemist might separate the pigments. And, as we'll see, this trick alone does not tell you who you've mixed with.

The result is a fresh secret key known only to the two endpoints. TLS then uses that key with fast symmetric encryption for everything that follows. A bonus: the keys are made up per connection and thrown away afterwards (forward secrecy), so if a server's long-term secrets leak next year, an attacker who recorded today's traffic still can't decrypt it.

Hashes and signatures

Two more tools are needed.

A hash function (like SHA-256) turns any data into a short, fixed-size fingerprint. The same input always gives the same fingerprint; change one bit of the input and the fingerprint changes completely; and you can't work backwards from a fingerprint to the input. Fingerprints let you check whether data was altered.

A digital signature is the padlock idea run in reverse. The owner uses the private key to produce a "stamp" over some data, and anyone can use the matching public key to check that the stamp is genuine and that the data hasn't changed since. Only the private-key holder could have made it, but everybody can verify it. A signature is how a server proves "I am the holder of this key" and how an authority vouches for someone else, as we're about to see.

Integrity during the connection comes for free: modern TLS encryption modes attach an authentication tag to every record, so if even one bit is flipped in transit, the receiver rejects the record instead of using it.

Certificates: who does this public key belong to?

Here is the gap in the paint story. A clever attacker sitting between you and the server (a man-in-the-middle) can do a separate paint exchange with each side, and silently relay messages between the two. You'd have a perfectly private conversation... with the attacker. Key agreement gives secrecy, but not identity. We need a way to know that the public key we received really belongs to www.example.com.

The answer is a certificate: a small signed document that says, roughly, "the public key K belongs to the domain www.example.com; valid from this date to that date; signed by the Certificate Authority". The signer is a Certificate Authority (CA), an organisation that checks that the applicant really controls the domain before issuing (the process is usually automated: prove you can publish a specific file or DNS record on the domain). Free CAs such as Let's Encrypt made certificates cheap and ubiquitous.

But why would your browser believe the CA's signature? Because of a chain of trust:

Your operating system and browser come with a built-in list of trusted root certificates (the trust store). When a server presents its certificate (plus any intermediates), the browser verifies each signature up the chain until it reaches a root on its list. It also checks that the certificate's name matches the host it intended to contact, that today's date is within the validity period, and that the server can prove it holds the matching private key (by signing part of the handshake with it). Any failure produces the scary full-page warning you've probably seen.

Common misconception: "the padlock means the site is safe"

The padlock tells you two things: your connection to that domain is encrypted, and you are really talking to whoever controls that domain name. It says nothing about whether they're honest. A phishing site at examp1e-login.com can get a perfectly valid certificate for that name and show a perfectly valid padlock. Check the domain, not the padlock. It also doesn't protect data once the server has it: a server with a padlock can still be hacked, or sell your data.

Go deeper: what if a CA misbehaves or a key leaks? optional

The CA system is a web of trust with some weak spots: any trusted CA can technically issue a certificate for any domain. Two defences matter most. Certificate Transparency requires CAs to publish every certificate they issue to public, append-only logs that anyone can watch, so a fraudulent certificate for your domain can't be issued secretly for long. And revocation lets a certificate be declared invalid before it expires, though revocation checking is notoriously hard to make reliable, which is one reason certificate lifetimes are being shortened (Let's Encrypt certificates last 90 days; public certificates are heading toward even shorter lives).

The TLS 1.3 handshake

Now we can put the pieces together. The handshake is the opening conversation in which client and server agree on how to encrypt, agree on a key, and the server proves its identity. Here is TLS 1.3 (2018), the current version, simplified. It begins after the TCP handshake has finished (chapter 8), and it needs just one round trip:

Step through

A TLS 1.3 handshake

  1. ClientHello. The browser says: here are the encryption methods I support, and here is my half of a key exchange (my key share: the "mixed paint"). It also names the website it wants (the SNI, server name indication, so one server can host many domains with different certificates), and which application protocol it hopes to speak (HTTP/2 or HTTP/1.1). Sending the key share in the very first message is what makes TLS 1.3 fast: the browser guesses a method the server will accept instead of asking first.
  2. ServerHello. The server picks one of the offered methods and sends back its own key share. Now both sides can combine their private value with the other's public share and compute the same secret, exactly like the paint. From this moment everything is encrypted: the rest of the handshake included. An eavesdropper sees only these two hellos.
  3. Server proves who it is. Encrypted under the new keys, the server sends its certificate chain and a signature over the handshake so far, made with the private key that matches the certificate. Only the real owner of that certificate could produce this signature, which is what defeats the man-in-the-middle. A Finished message (a hash covering the whole conversation) confirms nothing was tampered with.
  4. Client checks, and finishes. The browser walks the certificate chain up to a trusted root, checks that the name matches and the dates are valid, and verifies the signature. If all is well it sends its own Finished. If any check fails it aborts with a warning. The client can send its first encrypted application data right away, together with this message. The handshake has cost one round trip (TLS 1.2 needed two).
  5. Encrypted application data. The HTTP request and response now flow inside TLS "records", each encrypted and authenticated with fast symmetric cryptography. To anyone watching, it is random-looking bytes. The expensive public-key work happened once, in the first millisecond.
Go deeper: 0-RTT resumption, and why it comes with a warning optional

If you've connected to a server before, the two sides can save a secret from the last session (a pre-shared key or "session ticket"). On the next visit TLS 1.3 can resume using it, which skips the certificate dance. It even allows 0-RTT ("early data"): the client sends encrypted application data, such as an HTTP request, in the very first flight, along with its ClientHello, with no waiting at all.

The catch is replay. Early data isn't protected against someone recording that first flight and sending it to the server again: the server may see the same "transfer 100 euros" request twice. So servers should accept 0-RTT only for requests that are safe to repeat, such as an idempotent GET (chapter 10), and many refuse it for anything else. It's a clean illustration of a recurring theme: speed bought at the price of a weaker guarantee, which you must handle with idempotency (chapter 16).

What HTTPS hides, and what it doesn't

TLS is excellent but it does not make you invisible. It is worth knowing the boundaries:

Hidden from people on the path

The URL path and query (/account/statement?month=may), all headers and cookies, the request and response bodies, and the page contents. Tampering is detected.

Still visible

The IP addresses of both ends (routers need them, chapter 5). The port. When you connect and roughly how much data moves each way. Usually the domain name too: it appears in your DNS lookup (unless you use DoH/DoT, chapter 9) and in the SNI field of the ClientHello.

The SNI is sent before encryption starts, as plain text, because the server needs it to pick the right certificate. A newer extension called Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) encrypts that part too, so on-path observers could no longer see which of the sites hosted at an address you asked for. It needs support from both the website's hosting and your browser, and is still being rolled out, so don't count on it everywhere yet. Even when the domain is hidden, the size and timing patterns of traffic can leak surprising amounts about what you're doing (for example, which video is streaming), and of course the website itself, and any company it shares data with, still knows who you are.

Try it yourself

Inspect a real TLS handshake

These are read-only. They connect to example.com on port 443 and print what happened. Your output will differ (versions, certificate issuer, dates). On macOS the built-in curl and openssl are different builds (LibreSSL), so some lines look different from the samples; the ideas are the same.

$ curl -v https://example.com 2>&1 | grep -i -E "ssl|tls|certificate|issuer|subject"

* TLSv1.3 (IN), TLS handshake, Server hello (2):
* TLSv1.3 (IN), TLS handshake, Encrypted Extensions (8):
* TLSv1.3 (IN), TLS handshake, Certificate (11):
* TLSv1.3 (IN), TLS handshake, CERT verify (15):
* TLSv1.3 (IN), TLS handshake, Finished (20):
* TLSv1.3 (OUT), TLS handshake, Finished (20):
* SSL connection using TLSv1.3 / TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384
* Server certificate:
*  subject: C=US; ST=California; O=Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers; CN=www.example.org
*  issuer: C=US; O=DigiCert Inc; CN=DigiCert Global G2 TLS RSA SHA256 2020 CA1
*  SSL certificate verify ok.

Compare the list of messages with the stepper: Client hello, Server hello, Certificate, a signature check (CERT verify), then Finished from each side. Note the subject (who the certificate is for) and the issuer (the CA that signed it). The subject www.example.org looks odd for example.com: a certificate can list several names, and this one covers both. Now look at the whole chain:

$ openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com </dev/null
CONNECTED(00000003)
Certificate chain
 0 s:CN = www.example.org
   i:C = US, O = DigiCert Inc, CN = DigiCert Global G2 TLS RSA SHA256 2020 CA1
 1 s:C = US, O = DigiCert Inc, CN = DigiCert Global G2 TLS RSA SHA256 2020 CA1
   i:C = US, O = DigiCert Inc, CN = DigiCert Global Root G2
... (the server's certificate in PEM text, then handshake details) ...
New, TLSv1.3, Cipher is TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384
Verify return code: 0 (ok)

Read it as a chain, from the top: certificate 0 is the website's (s: = subject, i: = issuer), signed by the intermediate CA, which is certificate 1; certificate 1 in turn was issued by a root (DigiCert Global Root G2), which isn't sent by the server because your machine already has it in its trust store. Verify return code: 0 (ok) means the whole chain checked out. The -servername option sets the SNI. To see just the validity dates (the thing that expires!):

$ openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -dates
subject=CN = www.example.org
issuer=C = US, O = DigiCert Inc, CN = DigiCert Global G2 TLS RSA SHA256 2020 CA1
notBefore=Jan 15 00:00:00 2026 GMT
notAfter=Feb 15 23:59:59 2027 GMT

In a browser: click the padlock (or the "connection" icon) at the left of the address bar, then look for "Connection is secure" and "Certificate" to see the same subject, issuer, chain and expiry dates in a friendly window. Try it on a site you use, and compare the issuers of a few different sites.

Distributed systems connection

Inside a data center the "network" is still not something you can fully trust, and the same tools are used between services:

  • Mutual TLS (mTLS). In ordinary HTTPS only the server shows a certificate. In mTLS the client has one too, so each service can prove its identity to the other: "I really am the payments service." Service meshes issue and rotate these certificates automatically so that every internal call is both encrypted and authenticated.
  • Certificate expiry is a classic outage. Certificates deliberately stop working on a date. When someone forgets to renew one (or an automated renewal silently breaks), every client refuses to connect, instantly and everywhere at once. Many famous outages were just this. The cure is automation, monitoring of expiry dates, and shorter lifetimes that force the renewal process to be practised constantly.
  • Handshakes cost round trips. A new TLS connection adds at least one round trip on top of TCP's (two in TLS 1.2). That is why services keep connections open and reuse them (connection pooling), why HTTP/3 merges the handshakes, and why resumption exists. Chapter 13 will turn "round trips" into a budget.
  • Security has failure modes too. An expired certificate, a clock that is wrong enough to make a certificate look "not yet valid" (chapter 17 shows how clocks drift!), or a missing intermediate certificate all look like network failures to the caller.

Key takeaways

  • Plaintext traffic can be read, changed and faked by anything along the path. Secure connections need confidentiality, integrity and authentication.
  • Symmetric encryption is fast but needs a shared key; public-key tricks (key agreement, signatures) let strangers establish one safely.
  • A key exchange alone doesn't say who you're talking to. Certificates, signed by CAs that your device already trusts (the chain of trust), bind a domain name to a public key.
  • The TLS 1.3 handshake is one round trip: hellos with key shares, then the server's encrypted certificate and signature, then Finished, then encrypted data.
  • The padlock means "encrypted, and this really is that domain", not "this site is trustworthy".
  • HTTPS hides content, paths and headers, but IP addresses, timing, sizes and usually the domain name (via DNS and SNI) remain visible; ECH aims to close the SNI gap.
  • At scale: mTLS between services, expiring certificates cause outages, and handshake round trips are a real cost.

Check your understanding

Why does TLS use public-key techniques only to set up a connection, then switch to symmetric encryption for the data?

  1. Symmetric encryption is more secure than public-key encryption
  2. Public-key encryption can only handle 8 bytes
  3. Symmetric encryption is much faster, but needs a shared key, which public-key techniques let the two sides agree on safely
  4. Browsers don't support public-key encryption for large files
The two kinds of cryptography complement each other: public-key methods solve the "how do strangers agree on a secret?" problem but are slow; symmetric ciphers are very fast but need that secret first. TLS uses each for what it is good at.

A man-in-the-middle does a separate key exchange with you and with the server. Which part of TLS stops this from working?

  1. The server's certificate and signature: the attacker can't produce a valid signature for the real site's name
  2. The speed of symmetric encryption
  3. The TCP three-way handshake
  4. The HTTP Host header
Key exchange alone gives a private channel with somebody. Authentication comes from the certificate chain (trusted by your device) plus a signature made with the private key matching that certificate, which the attacker doesn't have.

A site shows a valid padlock. What can you conclude?

  1. The site is run by an honest company
  2. The site cannot be hacked
  3. Your data is safe even after the server stores it
  4. Your connection to the site's domain is encrypted and you're talking to whoever holds a certificate for that domain
A padlock is about the connection and the domain's identity, not about honesty or the server's own security. Phishing sites routinely have valid certificates for their (deceptive) domain names.

Which of these can an eavesdropper on your Wi-Fi usually still see when you load an HTTPS page?

  1. Your password and the page contents
  2. The IP address you connected to, and typically the domain name via DNS or the SNI
  3. The cookies sent with each request
  4. Which exact page of the site you opened (the path)
Contents, paths, headers and cookies are encrypted. IP addresses must stay visible for routing, and the domain usually leaks through the DNS lookup or SNI (until ECH and encrypted DNS are in use), along with timing and rough sizes.

A team's internal service suddenly fails for all callers at 00:00 UTC on a Sunday, with TLS errors, although nothing was deployed. What is a very likely cause?

  1. The routers ran out of IP addresses
  2. DNS TTLs expired
  3. A TLS certificate expired and wasn't renewed
  4. HTTP/3 was disabled
Certificates have an end date, and clients enforce it strictly and at the same moment everywhere, producing sudden, total failures. It is one of the most common causes of "nothing changed but everything broke".
