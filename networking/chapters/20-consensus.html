Consensus & Raft

Five computers, any of which may crash at any moment, connected by a network that may delay or drop any message. Can they still agree on a single fact, say who the leader is, and never change their minds? Amazingly, yes. This chapter shows how, using the algorithm designed to be understood: Raft.

Why this matters

Almost every serious distributed system has a small core that must be exactly right: who is the leader of this database, which server holds this lock, what is the cluster's configuration, in which order did these writes happen? That core is built on consensus. When you use Kubernetes, you are relying on a consensus-based store (etcd). When a database fails over without losing your data, consensus is usually what decided who takes over. Understanding it is the difference between using these systems and trusting them for the right reasons.

The problem: getting several machines to agree

In the previous chapters we met the ingredients: machines fail independently (chapter 15), you can't tell slow from dead (chapter 16), clocks can't be trusted to order events (chapter 17), and replicas can disagree (chapter 18). Consensus is the problem that puts the pieces together:

The consensus problem

A group of machines each propose a value. They must decide on exactly one of those values, such that: all machines that decide pick the same value; the value was actually proposed by someone; and the decision is final. All of this must hold even if some machines crash and some messages are delayed or lost.

That sounds abstract, so here are the four jobs in real systems that boil down to it:

Choosing one leader

"Which server is the primary for this database?" Everyone must agree, or you get split brain (two leaders accepting conflicting writes, chapter 18).

Ordering operations

"Which write came first?" If every replica applies the same commands in the same order, they end up in the same state. This is the replicated log.

Locks and leases

"Who owns this lock right now?" Two clients both believing they hold it defeats the point of a lock.

Configuration and membership

"Which machines are in the cluster? Which node owns partition 7?" (The partition map from chapter 19 has to live somewhere everyone trusts.)

Several well-known systems exist largely to provide this service to others: etcd, ZooKeeper and Consul each run a small cluster (usually 3 or 5 servers) that stores a modest amount of critical data with consensus, and bigger systems consult them for decisions that must not be wrong. Many databases also embed their own consensus protocol to elect leaders and replicate their logs.

Why it's harder than it sounds

Your first attempts at solving this probably fail in instructive ways. Here are four plausible-sounding schemes and how each breaks.

Naive ideaWhy it fails
"The server with the lowest ID is the leader."Cut the network into {S1, S2} and {S3, S4, S5}. Each side can't hear the other, so each side concludes the other is dead. S1 leads one side, S3 the other: two leaders. And if S1 crashes and reboots every minute, leadership flaps constantly.
"Everyone proposes with a timestamp; the earliest wins."Clocks disagree and drift (chapter 17). A server whose clock runs slow will "win" every time, and different servers can receive the proposals in different orders.
"Ask everyone and wait until all have answered."If one server has crashed, you wait forever. Waiting for everyone makes the system less available than a single machine. (Two-phase commit has exactly this weakness; see chapter 21.)
"Each server votes for the first candidate it hears, once."Better, but with no notion of rounds, a vote split 2-2-1 is stuck forever. And if a voter crashes after voting, nobody knows whether its vote still counts.

Each failure points at a requirement. We need a rule for how many servers must take part (not all, not just a few), a notion of rounds so we can retry after a stalemate or a crash, and a way to make sure that a decision, once made, cannot be undone by a later round. Majorities, terms and logs are the answers.

The impossibility result (honestly)

In 1985, Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved something sobering, now called the FLP result: in a purely asynchronous system, one with no upper bound on how long a message can take and no usable clocks, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee that consensus will always be reached if even one process might crash. The reason is the same slow-or-dead dilemma from chapter 16: a process can never be sure whether to wait for a silent peer or to carry on without it, and an adversarial schedule of delays can keep the algorithm hesitating forever.

This does not mean consensus is impossible in practice. It means a perfect guarantee of finishing is impossible in the worst case. Real algorithms make a deal with the devil: they use timeouts (and randomness) to make progress whenever the network behaves reasonably, and they are built so that they stay correct even when it doesn't. Which brings us to the two kinds of promise:

Safety
"Nothing bad ever happens." Two servers never decide different values; a decided value is never lost. Consensus algorithms keep safety no matter what: crashes, delays, reordering, partitions.
Liveness
"Something good eventually happens." A leader is eventually elected; commands eventually commit. This is only promised when enough of the system is working and the network is behaving well enough for long enough.

A good mental model: during a bad network storm, a consensus system may stop making progress (lose liveness), but it will never tell two clients two different answers (lose safety). It prefers to go quiet over being wrong.

Majorities: the key trick

The central idea is deceptively simple: never require everyone; require a majority. A majority (or quorum) of N servers is more than half: ⌊N/2⌋ + 1. Why does that help?

  • The group can still act when a minority is down, so crashes don't block it.
  • Any two majorities overlap in at least one server. If 3 of 5 servers agreed to something yesterday, and a different 3 of 5 are asked a question today, at least one of today's three was also in yesterday's group and remembers. (This is the same overlap property that made read and write quorums work in chapter 18.)

Here is how many failures a group can survive:

Servers (N)Majority neededFailures toleratedNote
110No fault tolerance at all
220Worse than 1: two things to break, and both are required
321The common small cluster
431Same as 3: the fourth server buys nothing
532The common production cluster
642Same as 5
743Rare; every write needs 4 acknowledgements

The pattern: to tolerate f crashed servers you need 2f + 1 in total, because you need f + 1 survivors to still form a majority. Three servers survive one failure, five survive two.

Common misconception: "more servers is always safer"

An even number of servers doesn't help. With 4 servers a majority is 3, so you still tolerate only one crash, same as with 3, yet you have more machines to fail and one more acknowledgement to wait for. Worse, 4 servers can split 2 + 2 in a partition, leaving neither half with a majority, so the whole cluster stalls. Use odd sizes. And larger isn't faster: every extra server makes each write wait for a bigger group.

The big idea: a replicated log

Agreeing on one value is nice, but real systems need to agree on a never-ending stream of them: every write, every config change, forever. The trick, called state machine replication, is:

  1. Model the service as a deterministic state machine: given the same starting state and the same sequence of commands, it always ends in the same state. (A key-value store is one: commands are set x=5, delete y.)
  2. Give every server a copy of the state machine and a log: an ordered list of commands.
  3. Use consensus to make all the logs identical: same commands, same order.
  4. Each server applies its log, in order, to its own state machine.

Then, with no further coordination, every copy ends up in the same state. The hard part has been reduced to "agree on the next entry in the log", repeated.

Analogy: identical recipe books, one agreed page order

Five chefs in five kitchens each follow the same recipe: "add flour, then eggs, then sugar". If they follow the steps in the same order, five identical cakes come out, with no need to compare cakes. The only thing the chefs must agree on is the sequence of steps in the book. If one chef got "sugar, then eggs", the cakes would differ.

Where it breaks down: chefs read a finished book. Servers write the book live, one line at a time, while some chefs fall over and others come back, and everyone must agree on each new line before moving on.

Raft, step by step

Many consensus algorithms exist. The most famous old one, Paxos, is notoriously hard to understand and harder to turn into a working system. In 2014, Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout published Raft with an explicit goal in the title of their paper: "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm". Raft solves the same problem with the same safety guarantees, but breaks it into three pieces you can reason about separately: leader election, log replication and safety. Its core design decision: at any time there is one leader, and everything flows through it.

Roles

Every server is, at any moment, in one of three roles:

  • Follower: passive. It answers requests from leaders and candidates and does nothing on its own, except notice when the leader has gone quiet.
  • Candidate: a follower that has decided there is no leader and is campaigning to become one.
  • Leader: handles all client requests, decides the order of the log, and keeps the followers' logs matching its own. There is at most one leader per term.

Terms: Raft's clock

Raft cuts time into numbered periods called terms: term 1, term 2, term 3, and so on. Each term begins with an election. If a candidate wins, it is the leader for the rest of that term; if the vote splits and nobody wins, the term ends with no leader and a new term (with a bigger number) begins.

Terms are a logical clock (chapter 17): not wall-clock time, just a counter that only goes up and that every message carries. They make out-of-date information easy to spot. The rules are blunt:

  • Every server remembers the highest term it has seen.
  • If a server receives any message with a higher term, it adopts that term at once (and, if it was a leader or candidate, steps down to follower).
  • If it receives a message with a lower term, it rejects it, replying with its own larger term so the sender can catch up.

It's the same idea as the Time-To-Live and hop counters in IP packets: a small number in the header that lets any machine decide, without asking anyone, whether this message is still relevant.

Leader election

Here is the whole election procedure:

  1. Followers expect heartbeats. Each follower has an election timeout, a countdown that is randomly chosen (the Raft paper suggests 150–300 ms). Every valid message from the current leader resets it.
  2. On timeout, start an election. The follower increments its term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself, and sends RequestVote to all the others. The message carries the candidate's term and a summary of its log (the term and index of its last entry).
  3. Each server votes at most once per term, first come first served, and only if the candidate's log is at least as up to date as its own. "More up to date" means: compare the terms of the last log entries (higher term wins); if equal, the longer log wins.
  4. A majority of votes makes you leader. You immediately start sending heartbeats so everyone knows.
  5. No majority? If a rival wins first (you see its heartbeat) you become a follower. If nobody wins (a split vote), your timeout expires again, you start a new term, and you try again.

The randomized timeouts are the clever bit. If all servers used the same timeout they would all become candidates at the same instant and split the vote forever. With random timeouts, one server usually wakes first, becomes a candidate, and collects the votes before the others even stir. Collisions can still happen, but each retry draws new random numbers, so repeated stalemates become rapidly unlikely.

Common misconception: "the highest term wins" means "the newest leader's data is best"

Terms tell you whose authority is current, not whose log is complete. That is why a vote also checks log freshness. A server that was cut off for a while might have a high term from failed election attempts but a short, stale log; it must not become leader just because its number is big.

Log replication

Once there is a leader, normal operation is pleasantly boring:

  1. A client sends a command to the leader (clients that contact a follower are redirected to the leader).
  2. The leader appends the command to its own log, tagged with the current term and the next index.
  3. The leader sends AppendEntries to all followers, carrying the new entry (and the leader's commit index).
  4. Each follower checks the entry fits after what it already has, appends it, and replies.
  5. As soon as the entry is stored on a majority of servers (including the leader), it is committed. The leader applies it to its state machine and answers the client.
  6. Followers learn the new commit index from later messages and apply the entry to their state machines.

Committed is the crucial word. It is Raft's promise: a committed entry will never be lost or changed, even if the leader crashes right now. An entry that is merely stored on the leader, or on fewer than a majority, may still disappear, which is why the leader only tells the client "done" after the commit.

Why an old leader can't do damage

Suppose a leader S2 is cut off by a network problem, the others elect S4 (term 2), and then S2's network heals. S2 still thinks it's the leader of term 1 and may try to push entries to followers. They reject its messages, because the term is lower, and the rejection carries the newer term, so S2 steps down.

But what if S2 tries to commit something while it's still isolated? It needs a majority to store the entry, and it can only reach a minority. So it cannot commit anything. Put the two facts together and you see the safety argument in miniature: to commit, you need a majority; to be elected, you need a majority; any two majorities overlap, and voters refuse candidates whose log is behind theirs; so a new leader always already has everything the previous leader committed.

Go deeper: the log matching property and how leaders repair logs optional

Raft maintains an invariant called log matching: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and the same term, then (a) they store the same command, and (b) all the entries before it are identical too. It is enforced cheaply with a consistency check inside every AppendEntries: the message includes the index and term of the entry immediately before the new ones (prevLogIndex, prevLogTerm). A follower accepts only if it has an entry that matches that pair; otherwise it replies "no". By induction, one successful check proves the entire prefix matches.

After leader changes, followers' logs can diverge: a follower might have extra uncommitted entries from an old leader, or be missing some. The new leader handles this by finding, for each follower, the latest point where their logs agree (it starts at the end of its own log and steps back after each "no"), then overwriting everything after that point with its own entries. Only uncommitted entries can ever be overwritten; committed ones are on a majority and the voting rule makes sure that the winning leader has them.

A subtle rule: a leader never counts replicas to commit an entry from an earlier term directly. It commits entries from its own term, and that commits all earlier entries along with them. This is why the widget's new leader appends a no-op entry as soon as it is elected. (The Raft paper's "Figure 8" shows the scenario this rule prevents.)

Go deeper: reads, membership changes and snapshots optional

Reads. If a client reads from a leader without checking, it might read from a leader that has been deposed but doesn't know it yet, and see stale data. Real systems either run reads through the log, or have the leader confirm with a majority that it is still leader before answering, or use time-based leases (which bring back the clock assumptions of chapter 17).

Membership changes. Replacing servers in a live cluster is delicate: if half the cluster switches to the "new" membership while the other half is on the "old", two separate majorities could exist. Raft handles it with a careful procedure (the paper describes joint consensus; many implementations change one server at a time).

Snapshots. Logs would grow forever, so servers periodically save the state machine's current state and throw away the log entries before that point.

The other families: Paxos and Byzantine consensus

Paxos. Devised by Leslie Lamport (the author of chapter 17's clocks), Paxos was the first widely proven solution and has the same safety goals and the same majority-based overlap argument as Raft. It is famously hard to grasp, and the basic version only agrees on one value, leaving engineers to work out for themselves how to build a full replicated log on top (usually called Multi-Paxos). Raft was deliberately designed as a more teachable alternative, and today it is the algorithm most new systems pick.

Byzantine fault tolerance. Raft and Paxos assume servers fail by stopping (crash faults): they may be slow or dead, but they never lie. If servers can be hacked, buggy or malicious and send contradictory messages to different peers, you need Byzantine consensus, which costs more: the classic PBFT algorithm needs 3f + 1 servers to tolerate f traitors. Blockchains such as Bitcoin tackle an even harsher setting, where anyone can join and nobody trusts anybody, using tools like proof-of-work to agree on one history of transactions. Inside a company's own data center, crash-fault algorithms like Raft are the norm.

The price of agreement

Consensus is wonderful, but it is not free, and knowing its costs tells you when not to use it.

  • Latency. Every committed write needs at least one round trip from the leader to a majority of followers (chapter 13). Within one data center that's a millisecond or so; if the servers are on different continents, each write pays for intercontinental round trips.
  • Throughput. All writes flow through one leader, so adding servers does not add write capacity; it slightly lowers it, because a larger majority must acknowledge each write.
  • Availability. No majority, no progress. A cluster split 2 + 3 keeps working on the 3 side only; the 2 side refuses writes. That is chapter 18's CAP choice made concrete: Raft picks consistency over availability when a partition occurs.

That's why the architecture of large systems typically has a small consensus-based control plane (3 or 5 servers storing configuration, leader identities, locks, partition maps) and a large data plane (hundreds of machines using cheaper replication, partitioning from chapter 19, or eventual consistency). Use the expensive tool only for the decisions that must be right.

3. Thought experiments

  1. A five-server cluster is cut by a network fault into groups of 3 and 2. Which group can elect a leader? Can the other group's old leader (if it was there) commit a write? What does a client in the minority group experience?
  2. A leader receives a command, writes it to its own log, sends it to the followers, and crashes before any follower replies. Was the entry committed? Might it survive? Under what conditions is it lost, and is that acceptable? (Hint: the client never received an "OK".)
  3. Why would an election be more fragile if the heartbeat interval were longer than the election timeout?

Distributed systems connection

Consensus is the foundation of coordination: electing leaders, ordering writes, handing out locks, storing the cluster's configuration. It is also why those operations are comparatively slow and rare: each one costs a majority round trip. Good designs confine it to a small, critical core and surround it with cheaper techniques.

In the final chapter we'll zoom out to the patterns built on these foundations (queues, logs, sagas) and notice that one of them, the append-only partitioned log, is really just chapter 19's partitioning plus this chapter's replicated log in disguise. Next: Patterns & Where to Go Next →

Key takeaways

  • Consensus means a group agrees on one value (or one order of commands) despite crashes and delays. It underlies leader election, locks, configuration and replicated logs.
  • FLP says guaranteed termination is impossible in a fully asynchronous system with a crash; real protocols use timeouts for liveness but never compromise safety.
  • Use majorities: 2f + 1 servers survive f failures, and any two majorities overlap. Use odd cluster sizes; even sizes add cost without adding tolerance.
  • State machine replication: identical logs applied in the same order to deterministic machines give identical state.
  • Raft: followers, candidates and a single leader per term; randomized election timeouts avoid split votes; one vote per term, only for candidates with an up-to-date log.
  • An entry is committed once stored on a majority, and committed entries survive any leader change. Stale leaders are defanged by higher terms.
  • Consensus costs a majority round trip per write, so use it for the small control core, not for bulk data.

Check your understanding

What is the smallest consensus cluster that keeps working after any 2 of its servers crash?

  1. 3
  2. 5
  3. 4
  4. 6
Tolerating f failures needs 2f + 1 servers: with f = 2 that's 5, leaving 3 survivors, which is still a majority of 5. Three servers would be left with 1 of 3 (no majority), and four servers need 3 alive, so can lose only 1. Six would also work, but 5 is the minimum.

Why is a cluster of 4 servers no better at fault tolerance than a cluster of 3?

  1. Four servers cannot communicate
  2. Raft requires exactly three servers
  3. A majority of 4 is 2, the same as for 3
  4. A majority of 4 is 3, so it still survives only one crash, while adding another machine that can fail
The majority of N is ⌊N/2⌋ + 1: 2 for N = 3 and 3 for N = 4. Both clusters tolerate exactly one failure. The fourth machine adds cost and an extra acknowledgement per write, and a 2 + 2 partition leaves no side with a majority.

In Raft, why do followers use randomized election timeouts?

  1. So that usually only one server becomes a candidate first, avoiding repeated split votes
  2. So that attackers can't guess when elections happen
  3. To make the leader's heartbeat arrive faster
  4. To give lower-numbered servers priority
If every server had the same timeout, they would all become candidates simultaneously and split the vote again and again. Random timeouts make it likely that one server wakes up, wins the votes and sends heartbeats before the others even time out.

A candidate asks for your vote in term 5. You haven't voted in term 5, but the candidate's log is shorter and older than yours. What do you do?

  1. Vote yes: you haven't voted this term
  2. Vote yes, but only if its term is higher than yours
  3. Refuse: you only vote for candidates whose log is at least as up to date as your own
  4. Ignore the message; candidates can't ask for votes in the same term
The up-to-date check is what stops a server missing committed entries from becoming leader and overwriting them. A committed entry lives on a majority; any winner needs votes from a majority; those groups overlap, and the overlapping server will refuse a candidate that lacks the entry.

A leader is frozen by a long pause (or cut off from the network) and later wakes up, still believing it is the leader of term 3. The cluster is now in term 4. What happens when it sends a heartbeat?

  1. Followers obey it, since leaders are always right
  2. Followers reject it because its term is older; the rejection tells it about term 4, and it steps down to follower
  3. The cluster has two leaders until an administrator intervenes
  4. All servers go back to term 3
Terms only ever go up. A message from a lower term is rejected and the reply carries the higher term; seeing a higher term makes any server adopt it and become a follower. Stale leaders are defused automatically.

When is a log entry considered committed in Raft?

  1. When it has been stored on a majority of the servers' logs (and the leader has recognised that)
  2. As soon as the leader appends it to its own log
  3. When every server has stored it
  4. When the client sends it twice
Storing on the leader alone isn't enough (it might crash). Requiring every server would block on one crash. A majority is the sweet spot: it tolerates failures and guarantees that every future leader, who needs a majority of votes, has the entry.
