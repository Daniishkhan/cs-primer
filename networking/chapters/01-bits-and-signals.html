Bits, Bytes & Signals

Everything on a network, from a cat photo to a bank transfer, is a long string of 1s and 0s turned into electricity, light or radio waves. In this chapter you'll learn how that works, and then meet the single most important distinction in all of networking: how much you can send per second (bandwidth) is not the same as how long it takes to arrive (latency).

Why this matters

Almost every performance mystery you will ever meet ("why is this page slow when my internet is fast?", "why does the data center in another country feel sluggish?") comes down to confusing bandwidth with latency. Later chapters on TCP, HTTP, CDNs and distributed systems keep returning to this chapter's numbers. It's also where the guide's first physical limit shows up: the speed of light, which no engineer can negotiate with.

Why computers use only 1s and 0s

Your laptop deals with photos, music, text and videos. Underneath, all of it is stored and sent as numbers, and the numbers are written using just two symbols: 0 and 1. Why not ten symbols, like we do by hand?

The answer is about reliability, not about computers being simple. A wire carries some physical quantity, for example a voltage. Suppose we wanted to send ten different digits by using ten voltage levels, 0 V, 0.5 V, 1 V and so on up to 4.5 V. A little electrical noise, say a flicker of 0.3 V, would turn a 3 into a 4 and nobody could tell. Now suppose we only use two levels, "low" (near 0 V) and "high" (near 3 V). The receiver just asks one easy question: is it above or below 1.5 V? Noise has to be enormous before it gets the answer wrong.

Analogy: shouting across a noisy playground

If you only need to signal "yes" or "no", you can wave one arm or two arms, and your friend will get it from far away, in the rain, even half-blind. If you had to signal a number from 0 to 9 using ten different arm angles, your friend would constantly misjudge them. Two clearly different states are the easiest thing to tell apart.

Where it breaks down: modern radio and cable systems actually do use many signal levels to squeeze in more data, but they still build everything on top of bits, and they use careful error correction to cope with the mistakes.

A single 0 or 1 is called a bit (short for binary digit). It's the smallest possible piece of information: one yes/no answer. Eight bits grouped together are a byte. The byte is the basic unit that computers store and move around.

Counting in binary

You already know a number system with place values: decimal. In 345, the 5 is worth 5 × 1, the 4 is worth 4 × 10, and the 3 is worth 3 × 100. Each place is worth 10 times the one to its right because we have ten digits.

Binary works identically, except each place is worth 2 times the one to its right, because we only have two digits. The places in a byte, from right to left, are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128:

To read a binary number, add up the place values wherever there is a 1. For example, 01001000 has 1s in the 64 place and the 8 place, so it equals 64 + 8 = 72. The number 11111111 has every place switched on: 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 255. So one byte can represent 256 different values (0 through 255), because there are 28 = 256 ways to arrange eight bits.

Going the other way, from decimal to binary, you ask for each place value, largest first, "does it fit in what's left?" To write 200: 128 fits (72 left), 64 fits (8 left), 32 doesn't, 16 doesn't, 8 fits (0 left), the rest are 0. That gives 11001000. You will use exactly this skill in chapter 4 to understand IP addresses.

How text becomes numbers

Computers have no built-in idea of letters, so people agreed on a table that assigns a number to each character. The classic table is ASCII (the American Standard Code for Information Interchange, from the 1960s). It uses numbers 0 to 127. Capital A is 65, B is 66, lowercase a is 97, the digit character 0 is 48, and a space is 32. So the word "Hi" is the two numbers 72 and 105, which as bytes are 01001000 01101001.

ASCII only covers English letters, digits and punctuation. The world has thousands of other characters: é, ñ, ж, 世, € and emoji. The modern solution is Unicode, which gives every character a number (a "code point"), and UTF-8, a way of writing those numbers as bytes. UTF-8 is the clever part: the first 128 characters use exactly one byte and are identical to ASCII, while rarer characters use two, three or four bytes.

Remember the key idea: bits have no built-in meaning. The same byte 01001000 is the number 72, the letter "H", a shade of grey in an image, or part of a program, depending on what the receiver was told to expect. A big part of networking (and the "protocols" from chapter 0) is the sender and receiver agreeing on how to interpret the bits.

Hexadecimal: a shorthand for humans

Writing 32 bits as 11000000101010000000000100010100 is miserable and error-prone. Engineers use hexadecimal ("hex") instead: base 16, with digits 0–9 and then A–F for ten to fifteen. The magic fact is that one hex digit equals exactly four bits, so a byte is always two hex digits.

You'll see hex everywhere in networking: MAC addresses (a4:83:e7:1c:52:2f, chapter 2), IPv6 addresses (chapter 4) and packet dumps in tools like Wireshark.

Kilo, mega, giga, and the bits-versus-bytes trap

The prefixes are the familiar metric ones: kilo = thousand, mega = million, giga = billion, tera = trillion. A kilobyte (KB) is 1,000 bytes, a megabyte (MB) is 1,000,000 bytes, a gigabyte (GB) is 1,000,000,000 bytes.

A small wrinkle: 1,000 or 1,024?

Operating systems have historically sometimes used "KB" to mean 1,024 bytes (210), which is a convenient power of two. To remove the ambiguity there are separate names: KiB, MiB, GiB mean powers of 1,024. The difference is about 2.4% at kilo, 4.9% at mega and 7.4% at giga. In networking, speeds are always in plain decimal units (1 Mbps = 1,000,000 bits per second), so you can ignore the 1,024 business here.

Now the trap that catches nearly everybody. Storage and file sizes are measured in bytes (capital B). Network speeds are measured in bits per second (lowercase b). There are 8 bits in a byte, so the numbers differ by a factor of eight.

Common misconception: "my 100 megabit connection should download 100 MB per second"

Your internet plan says 100 Mbps (megabits per second). A file download shows MB/s (megabytes per second). To convert, divide by 8: 100 Mbps ≈ 12.5 MB/s. A 500 MB file therefore takes at least 500 × 8 = 4,000 megabits ÷ 100 Mbps = 40 seconds, not 5. Real downloads are a little slower still, because every packet carries headers (chapter 3) that aren't part of your file.

How bits physically travel

We now have a stream of bits. How do we get them from one machine to another? We need a physical thing that can take two clearly different states over time, and a receiver that can notice which one it is. There are three main choices, and every network you use is built from them.

Copper: voltage

The oldest idea: send electricity down a metal wire and change its voltage. High voltage means 1, low voltage means 0, and the receiver samples the wire on a steady beat. (Real Ethernet uses cleverer encodings with several voltage levels and signal transitions to stay in sync, but the principle is the same.) The cable in your wall is usually twisted pair: pairs of wires twisted together so interference hits both equally and cancels out. Standard Ethernet over twisted pair works up to about 100 metres per cable run, then the signal is too weak.

Fiber optics: pulses of light

An optical fiber is a hair-thin strand of glass. A laser or LED at one end flashes light on and off very fast; a detector at the far end sees the flashes. The light doesn't escape through the sides because of total internal reflection: the core of the strand is made of glass with a higher refractive index than the layer around it (the cladding), so light hitting that boundary at a shallow angle is reflected back in, like a mirror. It zig-zags along the strand almost without loss.

Fiber loses very little signal per kilometre and isn't bothered by electrical interference, so it carries more data much farther than copper. Undersea cables are fiber: the Internet's connections between continents are glass strands lying on the ocean floor, with amplifiers placed along them every few dozen kilometres or so.

Radio: waves through the air

Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and mobile networks use radio waves. The sender takes a steady wave at some frequency (Wi-Fi uses bands around 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz) and slightly alters it: its strength, its frequency or its timing. The pattern of changes encodes the bits, and the receiver decodes them. No cable is needed, which is wonderful, but there's a catch: radio is a shared medium. Everyone in range hears everyone else, so devices must take turns (chapter 2), and walls, distance and other devices interfere.

Signals get weaker, and errors happen

Whatever the medium, signals get weaker with distance (called attenuation) and pick up noise from outside: a nearby motor, a microwave oven, cosmic radiation, heat in the electronics. This is where our binary choice pays off: the receiver only asks "above or below the threshold?"

To fight attenuation, long links use repeaters or amplifiers that boost or regenerate the signal every so often. To fight the occasional bit that still gets flipped, networks add redundancy. The simplest example: a parity bit. Append one extra bit so the total number of 1s is always even. If one bit flips in transit, the count becomes odd and the receiver knows something went wrong. Real networks use stronger versions called checksums and CRCs (cyclic redundancy checks): the sender computes a short number from all the data and sends it along; the receiver recomputes it and compares. A mismatch means "corrupted, throw it away".

You'll meet these again: Ethernet frames end with a 4-byte check value (chapter 2), IP and TCP headers contain checksums (chapters 4 and 8). Note that a checksum only detects errors; recovering from them (usually by asking the sender to send it again) is a higher layer's job, and a recurring theme in this guide.

The big idea: bandwidth is not latency

We now have enough to tackle the most important concept in this chapter. People say "fast internet" as if speed were a single number. It isn't. There are two separate quantities that both feel like "speed":

Bandwidth
How much data a link can carry per unit of time. Measured in bits per second (Mbps, Gbps). Think of it as the width of the road.
Latency
How long it takes for one bit (or one tiny message) to get from A to B. Measured in time (ms). Think of it as the length of the road.

Analogy: a water pipe

Picture a pipe from a water tank to your garden. A wide pipe lets lots of water through each second (high bandwidth). A long pipe means the first drop takes a while to arrive after you open the tap (high latency). You can have a very wide, very long pipe: nothing seems to happen for a long time, then a torrent. Or a narrow, short pipe: instant, but a trickle. Replacing the pipe with a wider one makes the torrent bigger; it does not make the first drop arrive any sooner.

Where it breaks down: water in a pipe is pushed by pressure, whereas data signals travel at a fixed physical speed regardless of how much you send. Also a real network path is many pipes joined in a row, and the narrowest one limits the flow.

Which one matters depends on what you're doing. Downloading a 4 GB game is a bandwidth problem: you need a huge amount through the pipe. Loading a page that makes 50 small requests, or clicking in a cloud app, is mostly a latency problem: lots of tiny messages, each waiting for a round trip. We'll quantify this properly in a moment.

Where the time goes: four kinds of delay

When a message crosses a network, the time it takes is the sum of four distinct delays. Each has a different cause, and only some of them can be reduced by buying faster equipment.

Propagation delay: the speed of light is the limit

Signals travel at finite speed. In a vacuum, light does about 300,000 km per second. In glass fiber it's slower, roughly 200,000 km/s (about two thirds). Copper is similar. That gives us a number worth memorising:

light in fiber ≈ 200 km per millisecond ≈ 5 microseconds per kilometre

A millisecond (ms) is a thousandth of a second; a microsecond (µs) is a thousandth of a millisecond. Take London to New York, about 5,600 km in a straight line. The minimum time for a signal to cross is 5,600 ÷ 200,000 = 0.028 s = 28 ms, one way. A reply takes another 28 ms, so the absolute minimum for a request and its answer is about 56 ms. In practice it's more: cables don't follow great-circle routes, and routers add their own delays, so real London–New York pings are noticeably higher than 56 ms. But no technology, however expensive, can ever make it lower than the physics allows.

Transmission delay: squeezing bits onto the wire

A sender can't put a whole message on the wire at once; it emits bits one after another at the link's bandwidth. So the time to push a message onto the link is:

transmission delay = message size (bits) ÷ bandwidth (bits per second)

A 1,500-byte packet is 12,000 bits. On a 1 Gbps link that takes 12,000 ÷ 1,000,000,000 = 12 µs. On a slow 1 Mbps link it takes 12 ms. This is the delay that more bandwidth shrinks.

Analogy: a convoy leaving a toll booth

Ten cars must pass a toll booth that releases one car every 6 seconds, then drive 100 km to the next town. The time for the whole convoy to get through the booth (60 seconds) is transmission delay: it depends on convoy length and booth speed. The time to drive the 100 km is propagation delay: it depends on distance and road speed, and is the same whether the convoy is 1 car or 1,000. The last car arrives after both.

Where it breaks down: in a real wire, the first bit is already travelling while later bits are still being pushed on, so a long message can be partly "on the road" in several places at once.

Queuing and processing delay

Routers handle many conversations at once. If packets arrive faster than the outgoing link can send them, the extras wait in a queue, just like shoppers at a checkout. Queuing delay is the unpredictable one: zero when the network is quiet, tens or hundreds of milliseconds when it's congested. This variation is what makes ping times jitter. Processing delay, the time for the router to examine the header and decide where the packet goes, is usually only microseconds on modern hardware.

So the end-to-end time for one packet is:

total = processing + queuing + transmission + propagation (summed over every hop)

Worked examples: which delay dominates?

Let's compare two links for sending the same data.

  • Link A: "fast but far". 1 Gbps, London to New York (5,600 km → 28 ms propagation).
  • Link B: "slow but near". 10 Mbps, 100 km away (0.5 ms propagation).

Case 1: a 1 KB message (1,000 bytes = 8,000 bits).

  • Link A: transmission = 8,000 ÷ 109 = 8 µs; propagation = 28 ms. Total ≈ 28.008 ms.
  • Link B: transmission = 8,000 ÷ 107 = 0.8 ms; propagation = 0.5 ms. Total = 1.3 ms.

The "slow" link wins by a factor of more than 20. For small messages, distance is everything. On Link A, the 100-fold bandwidth advantage saves only 8 µs out of 28 ms.

Case 2: a 1 GB file (109 bytes = 8 × 109 bits).

  • Link A: transmission = 8 × 109 ÷ 109 = 8 s; propagation = 0.028 s. Total ≈ 8.03 s.
  • Link B: transmission = 8 × 109 ÷ 107 = 800 s (over 13 minutes); propagation 0.0005 s. Total ≈ 800 s.

Now the "fast but far" link wins by a factor of 100. For huge transfers, bandwidth is everything, and the extra 28 ms is a rounding error.

Common misconception: "faster internet" means everything is faster

When your provider upgrades you from 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps, you've made the road wider. Big downloads and video streams improve a lot. But a request to a server 5,600 km away still pays at least 28 ms each way, whatever plan you buy, so loading ordinary web pages, which are dominated by many small round trips, barely changes. When something is "laggy" in a game or a video call, the culprit is usually latency (or jitter), not bandwidth.

Round-trip time

Most useful network exchanges are conversations, not one-way broadcasts: you ask, the other side answers. The time from sending a request to receiving the reply is the round-trip time (RTT). It's the number ping reports, and it's the natural unit for measuring network cost: "that takes two round trips" is a statement about latency that doesn't depend on how much bandwidth you have.

Why does RTT matter so much? Because many protocols are chatty: they need several round trips before real data flows. As a preview of later chapters, opening a secure web connection can take around three round trips (TCP handshake, then TLS, then the actual request). At 56 ms RTT that's a 168 ms delay before the first byte of the page even starts, and no bandwidth could shorten it. Engineers fight this by moving servers closer to users (CDNs, chapter 14) and by designing protocols that need fewer round trips (chapters 8, 10 and 11).

Throughput versus bandwidth

A final distinction. Bandwidth is the capacity of a link: the most it could carry. Throughput is what you actually achieve, end to end, right now. Throughput is always at most the bandwidth of the slowest link on the path (the bottleneck), and usually lower, because of:

  • sharing: other people's traffic uses the same links,
  • overhead: headers and acknowledgements use capacity but aren't your data,
  • waiting: a sender that must wait for a reply before sending more can leave the pipe empty (this is where latency sneaks back in; we'll see it in the TCP chapter),
  • packet loss and retransmissions.

This is why a speed test on your 500 Mbps plan might show 300 Mbps on Wi-Fi: Wi-Fi's own bandwidth, signal quality and interference became the bottleneck.

Go deeper: the bandwidth-delay product optional

Combine the two quantities and you get a very useful number: how much data is "in flight" on a link at one moment. Think of the pipe's volume: width × length.

bandwidth-delay product = bandwidth × round-trip time

For our 1 Gbps London–New York link with a 56 ms RTT: 109 bits/s × 0.056 s = 56 million bits = 7 MB. That's how much a sender must have "on the wire", not yet acknowledged, to keep the pipe completely full. If a protocol allows only, say, 64 KB of unacknowledged data at a time, it can use less than 1% of that link, no matter how fat the pipe is. Fixing this is a central job of TCP (chapter 8), and why long fat links are famously hard to fill.

Go deeper: can anything beat fiber's 200,000 km/s? optional

Light in a vacuum travels about 50% faster than light in glass. Radio waves through air travel at nearly vacuum speed. That's why some trading firms built chains of microwave towers between financial centres (shaving milliseconds off a straight-line route) and why low-Earth-orbit satellite networks can beat fiber over very long distances, even though the signal must go up and come down again. These are expensive niche solutions, and they have far less bandwidth than fiber, but they show the trade-off in action: physics sets latency, money mostly buys bandwidth.

Some servers don't answer ping at all (it's often filtered); if you get timeouts, try a different host.

Now check your bandwidth. A speed test website such as fast.com or speedtest.net reports download and upload in Mbps (bits!) and also a latency figure. On recent macOS you can run networkQuality in the terminal for a similar test. Try dividing the download number by 8 to see what MB/s your next big download should reach. Run it on Wi-Fi and then, if you can, with a cable, and see which of the two numbers (bandwidth or latency) changes.

Distributed systems connection

Latency is a physical limit you cannot engineer away. A signal from California to Singapore takes tens of milliseconds even in a perfect world, and everything in distributed systems has to live with that. Because bandwidth keeps growing cheaply while the speed of light stays fixed, the number of round trips becomes the main cost unit of distributed design: a database commit that must be confirmed by a replica on another continent costs at least one intercontinental RTT; a service that makes 50 sequential calls to other services pays 50 times the RTT, even if each call carries one byte.

That's why designers batch requests, keep related data close together, cache aggressively, and replicate data near users. It's also why "keep the servers in the same data center" is a performance decision as well as an operational one (chapter 2 shows that even same-rack versus cross-datacenter is a noticeable difference). We'll return to these numbers in chapter 13, and the cost of coordinating over long distances will be the heart of the replication and consensus chapters.

Key takeaways

  • Computers use bits because two clearly different states survive noise. Eight bits make a byte (256 values); place values in binary double each step; hex writes four bits per digit.
  • Bits have no built-in meaning. ASCII and UTF-8 are agreements for turning text into numbers and bytes.
  • Mbps are megabits per second and MB/s are megabytes per second: divide by 8 to convert.
  • Bits travel as voltage in copper, light pulses in fiber, or radio waves. Signals weaken and pick up noise, so networks use checksums to detect corruption.
  • Bandwidth is how much per second (road width); latency is how long one message takes (road length). They are independent.
  • Total delay = processing + queuing + transmission (size ÷ bandwidth) + propagation (distance ÷ speed, about 5 µs/km in fiber). Small messages are dominated by propagation; huge transfers by transmission.
  • RTT has a physical floor (London–New York ≥ ~56 ms), and "faster internet" mostly raises bandwidth, not latency.

Check your understanding

Your plan is "200 Mbps". Roughly how fast can a download go, in megabytes per second?

  1. 200 MB/s
  2. 1,600 MB/s
  3. 25 MB/s
  4. 2 MB/s
Mbps counts bits and MB/s counts bytes, and a byte is 8 bits. So 200 ÷ 8 = 25 MB/s at best. 1,600 would be what you'd get by multiplying by 8, the wrong direction.

What is the decimal value of the byte 00101010?

  1. 42
  2. 52
  3. 84
  4. 21
The 1s are in the 32, 8 and 2 places: 32 + 8 + 2 = 42. (In hex that byte is 0x2A.) 84 would be this number shifted left by one place; 21 is shifted right.

You send a tiny 1 KB request to a server 8,000 km away. You upgrade your link from 100 Mbps to 10 Gbps. What happens to the delivery time?

  1. It becomes 100 times shorter
  2. It becomes about 10 times shorter
  3. It gets longer, because the signal is faster and gets distorted
  4. It barely changes, because propagation (about 40 ms) dominates transmission (microseconds)
Propagation is 8,000 km ÷ 200,000 km/s = 40 ms, which no upgrade changes. Transmission of 8,000 bits is only 80 µs at 100 Mbps and 0.8 µs at 10 Gbps. Saving 79 µs out of 40 ms is negligible. This is the classic latency-bound case.

Why do digital systems represent information with just two voltage levels instead of, say, ten?

  1. Because computers cannot handle numbers bigger than 1
  2. Because two levels are easy to tell apart reliably, even when the signal is weakened and noisy
  3. Because binary numbers are shorter than decimal ones
  4. Because ten levels would be too fast for copper wire
The receiver only has to decide "above or below the threshold", so a lot of noise is needed to cause a mistake. Binary numbers are actually longer than decimal ones; the win is reliability, not compactness.

Which delay is determined by message size ÷ link bandwidth?

  1. Propagation delay
  2. Queuing delay
  3. Transmission delay
  4. Processing delay
Transmission delay is the time to push all of a message's bits onto the link. Propagation depends on distance, queuing on how busy the router is, and processing on the router's hardware.

A client in Sydney talks to a server in London, roughly 17,000 km apart. Using 200,000 km/s in fiber, what is the lowest possible round-trip time, even with infinite bandwidth and a straight cable?

  1. About 85 ms
  2. About 170 ms
  3. About 340 ms
  4. About 17 ms
One way: 17,000 ÷ 200,000 = 0.085 s = 85 ms. A round trip doubles it to 170 ms. Real-world RTTs are higher, because cables aren't straight and routers add delay, but they can never be lower.
