Routing

Your packet has a destination address on the other side of the planet, and no single machine knows the way. In this chapter you'll see how thousands of routers, each knowing only its immediate neighbourhood, still manage to hand a packet along, hop by hop, until it arrives.

Why this matters

Routing is the reason the Internet is one network instead of millions of islands. It also explains things you will meet constantly as an engineer: why traceroute shows a list of strange hostnames, why latency suddenly jumps at an ocean crossing, why packets from the same connection can take different paths, and why a single typo in one company's configuration can make a popular website vanish for half the world. And for distributed systems, it's the root cause of "the network sometimes just stops working between two machines that are both perfectly healthy".

What is a router?

In chapter 2 you met the switch: it connects devices inside one local network and forwards frames by MAC address. But a switch only knows its own neighbourhood. To get from your home to a server in another country you need something that connects different networks to each other.

That something is a router: a computer with several network interfaces (several "plugs", each attached to a different network) whose job is to receive packets on one interface and forward them out of another. Your home Wi-Fi box is a router: one interface faces your home network, another faces your Internet provider. The big machines in Internet exchange buildings are routers with dozens or hundreds of interfaces.

Analogy: the postal sorting office

A sorting office doesn't deliver your letter to the door and doesn't know the whole journey. A van drops sacks of mail at the loading bay. Clerks glance at the address on each parcel, decide "that's for the northern region, put it on the northern truck", and move on. The next sorting office does the same, one step closer. Nobody ever plans the whole trip; everyone just makes a good next decision.

Where it breaks down: a router makes that decision in a few millionths of a second, for millions of packets per second. And unlike a clerk, it never opens the parcel, only reads the address on the outside.

The default gateway: "not on my LAN? Ask the router"

Start from the sender's point of view. Your laptop wants to send a packet to 203.0.113.10. The first question it asks is cheap to answer: is that address on my own network? Using the subnet mask from chapter 4, your laptop (192.168.1.20/24) can tell that the local network is 192.168.1.0 through 192.168.1.255. The destination is outside that range.

  • Inside the LAN: send the frame directly to that device's MAC address (found with ARP, chapter 6). No router involved.
  • Outside the LAN: you can't reach it directly, and you don't have to figure out the way. Hand the packet to one trusted neighbour that knows more. That neighbour is the default gateway, usually 192.168.1.1, your home router.

That's the whole rule: "if it's not on my LAN, send it to the router." Every ordinary host, from a phone to a web server, is configured with exactly one such gateway address (DHCP hands it out, chapter 6). Notice what the laptop does not know: how many routers there are between here and the destination, or where any of them are. It only knows its first hop.

Common misconception: the IP packet is addressed to the router

When your laptop sends to the gateway, the Ethernet frame is addressed to the router's MAC (it's the next hop on this link), but the IP packet inside is still addressed to the final destination, 203.0.113.10. This is exactly the "IP stays the same, MAC changes at every hop" idea from chapter 3. The router isn't the destination; it's just the next stop.

The routing table

How does a router decide where to send a packet? It consults its routing table (also called the forwarding table): a list of rows that say, in effect, "packets for addresses in this range go that way". Each row has:

  • a destination prefix, a block of addresses written in CIDR notation like 198.51.100.0/24 (chapter 4);
  • a next hop: the address of the neighbouring router to hand the packet to (or "directly connected" if the destination network is attached to this very router);
  • an interface to send it out of (which "plug" the next hop is reachable through).

Here is a toy routing table for a router we'll call R2:

Destination prefixNext hopMeaning
192.0.2.0/24R4That network is reached through neighbour R4
198.51.100.0/24R4A bigger block, also through R4
198.51.100.128/25R5A smaller block inside the one above, but through R5
203.0.113.0/24R4Through R4 again
0.0.0.0/0R1Default route: everything else

Two things stand out. First, the rows are about blocks of addresses, not individual machines. A router can't possibly list all 4 billion IPv4 addresses, which is why addresses are handed out in contiguous blocks (chapter 4): one row can cover millions of machines. Second, blocks can overlap. The third row sits inside the second. Which row wins?

Longest-prefix match

The rule is simple: among all rows that match the destination, use the one with the longest prefix (the largest number after the slash, i.e. the most specific). The idea is that a more specific route carries more precise knowledge. "Everything in this street goes left" is overridden by "but house number 42 goes right".

Let's work through destinations against the R2 table above, step by step. A prefix matches when the first n bits of the destination equal the first n bits of the prefix.

DestinationRows that matchWinnerWhy
198.51.100.7198.51.100.0/24, 0.0.0.0/0/24 → R4Its last byte, 7, is below 128, so the /25 (128–255) doesn't match. /24 beats /0.
198.51.100.200/25, /24, /0/25 → R5200 is ≥ 128, so the /25 matches too. Three matches; 25 > 24 > 0.
203.0.113.10203.0.113.0/24, /0/24 → R4The first 24 bits (203.0.113) are equal.
8.8.8.80.0.0.0/0 only/0 → R1Nothing specific matches, so the default route catches it.

The row 0.0.0.0/0 is the default route. Its prefix length is zero, so zero bits have to match, which means it matches every address in existence. Because it is always the shortest possible prefix, it never beats a more specific row; it only catches what nothing else wants. "Default route" is just the default gateway idea again, now seen from inside the table: if I know nothing more specific, send it to the router that probably does.

Go deeper: how fast is a lookup, and what's actually in the table? optional

Core Internet routers carry a global table with roughly a million IPv4 prefixes, and they must look up every packet's destination in it at line speed. They don't scan the list row by row; they use specialised data structures (tries over the address bits) and dedicated hardware (such as TCAM memory) that can answer "longest prefix match" in a handful of nanoseconds. Keeping the global table small is a real engineering concern, which is one reason address blocks are allocated hierarchically: an ISP can announce one big prefix instead of thousands of little ones (this is called aggregation).

Strictly speaking, routers keep two things: a routing table (what routing protocols have learned) and a forwarding table (the optimised version used for per-packet lookups). Beginners can treat them as one.

Hop-by-hop forwarding

Now put the pieces together. The packet leaves the laptop addressed to the final destination. At every router, the same three things happen:

  1. The router receives a frame, strips the link-layer header, and reads the destination IP address.
  2. It looks the address up in its table (longest-prefix match) and picks a next hop.
  3. It wraps the packet in a new frame addressed to that next hop and sends it on.

Each router decides only the next hop. It does not know, and does not need to know, the whole route. This is a remarkable design: the sender doesn't plan a path, and no router is in charge of the journey. The path emerges from many independent local decisions, as long as their tables are consistent with each other. (If they aren't, you can get routing loops, which is what the TTL field, below, protects against.)

TTL: a packet's countdown

What if the tables disagree and two routers each think the other is the way forward? The packet would bounce between them forever, and thousands of such packets would clog the links. To make this harmless, every IP packet carries a TTL (time to live) field, a number between 0 and 255 in the IP header (called "hop limit" in IPv6). The rules are:

  1. The sender sets it to some starting value (64 is common on Linux and macOS, 128 on Windows).
  2. Every router decrements it by one before forwarding.
  3. If it reaches 0, the router discards the packet and sends a small ICMP "time exceeded" message back to the original sender (ICMP is the network's error-reporting protocol; chapter 6).

Despite its name, TTL has nothing to do with seconds. It really counts hops. It guarantees every packet eventually dies, even in a routing loop.

Traceroute: turning a safety feature into a map

The "time exceeded" message names the router that sent it, so it reveals that router's address. Engineers noticed they could exploit this: if I send probes with TTL 1, then 2, then 3, and so on, each probe dies one hop further along, and the router where it dies introduces itself. Collect the replies and you have a list of the routers on the path. That's traceroute.

Note that the request and the replies might not even use the same route (more on that shortly). Traceroute shows you the path the probes took, one clever hack at a time.

How do routers learn their routes?

Someone has to fill in those tables. There are two ways.

Static routes

A person (or a configuration tool) types the rows in by hand: "to reach 192.0.2.0/24, send to 10.0.0.2". That's fine for your home router (which has a single static default route to the ISP) or for a tiny office. It doesn't scale, and it can't react when a link breaks. Nobody is awake at 3 a.m. to edit a thousand tables.

Dynamic routing: routers talk to each other

In dynamic routing, routers run routing protocols: they tell their neighbours what they can reach, learn from the neighbours' announcements, and recompute their tables automatically when something changes. If a link dies, the news spreads and every table adjusts, usually within seconds or less. That's the "cut a link" button in the widget, done by software.

The Internet is run by many organisations, and what is sensible inside one organisation is different from what is needed between them. So routing is split in two levels.

Inside one organisation: interior protocols

Everyone cooperates and wants the best technical path. Protocols such as OSPF work like this: each router describes its own links ("I'm connected to B at cost 1 and C at cost 5") and floods that to all other routers. Everyone ends up with the same map, then each router independently computes the shortest paths on it (Dijkstra's algorithm) and fills its table.

Between organisations: BGP

Independent companies don't share their internal maps and don't just want "shortest". They have contracts, prices and politics. BGP lets each network announce which prefixes it can reach and lets each network choose which announcements to believe, based on policy.

Autonomous Systems and BGP

The Internet is a network of networks (chapter 0). Technically, each independently administered network, such as an ISP, a university, a cloud provider or a big company, is an Autonomous System (AS), and is identified by an AS number (ASN). Tens of thousands of ASes exist. Inside an AS, the operator can use whatever interior protocol it likes. Between ASes, everyone speaks BGP, the Border Gateway Protocol.

The idea of BGP in plain words: each AS announces to its neighbours the prefixes it can deliver traffic to. A content network says "I can reach 203.0.113.0/24." Its neighbour passes that on to its own neighbours, adding itself to the front of a list (the AS path): "203.0.113.0/24 is reachable via me, then AS 64499." Announcements ripple outward, and every AS ends up knowing one or more paths for each prefix, each described as a list of ASes. (For this reason BGP is called a path-vector protocol.)

When an AS has several routes to the same prefix, it applies policy. Typical considerations: prefer routes learned from customers (they pay me) over peers (free exchange) over providers (I pay them); avoid a link that is nearly full; honour contract terms; and only after all that, prefer the shorter AS path. So the route BGP picks is not necessarily the shortest, fastest or cheapest for you; it's the one that makes sense for the networks it crosses. BGP sessions between neighbouring routers run over a plain TCP connection (port 179), which we'll meet in chapters 7 and 8.

Analogy: airlines and alliances

Each country's postal service (an AS) agrees with neighbouring services on which mail it will carry to which destinations, and on price. The cheapest route on a map may not be the one used, because the contracts say otherwise. BGP is the standard way these services announce "I can get mail to this region" to each other.

Where it breaks down: BGP is largely based on trust. In the simplest version, nothing stops an AS from announcing a prefix it doesn't actually own, and its neighbours will often believe it.

BGP is the glue, and it's fragile

BGP is sometimes called "the glue of the Internet", because it's what stitches tens of thousands of independent networks into one reachable whole. It is also one of the most delicate parts. Since announcements are largely trusted, a mistake spreads. There have been a number of well-known incidents in which an operator's misconfiguration caused a network to announce prefixes it shouldn't have (a "route leak" or "hijack") or to stop announcing its own. The effect: other networks sent traffic for popular services to the wrong place, where it was silently dropped, or the affected services became unreachable from large parts of the world for anything from minutes to hours. These events are usually not attacks, just a human typing the wrong thing in a very powerful place. They also show how routing really works: the "map" of the Internet is just what the routers have been told, by people who can make mistakes.

Go deeper: preventing BGP hijacks optional

The community has been adding safeguards over the years. Operators filter what their customers are allowed to announce. A system called RPKI lets the owner of a prefix publish a signed statement saying which AS number is allowed to originate it, so receiving routers can reject announcements from anyone else. Adoption is growing but is not universal, so the Internet's routing is still more trust-based than most people expect.

Another BGP detail is that it is deliberately slow and cautious: it uses timers and damping so that a flapping link doesn't cause a storm of updates across the whole world. After a failure, global BGP convergence can take from seconds to a few minutes, much slower than OSPF inside a network.

Paths can change, and can differ by direction

Because every router makes its own decision from its own table, and tables change when links fail, congestion is rerouted, or policies are updated, two consecutive packets of the same conversation can take different paths. Usually they don't (routers prefer stability, and many deliberately keep one flow on one path so that its packets aren't reordered), but nothing guarantees it.

Also, the path from you to a server and the path back are computed by different routers using different tables and different policies. They are often different. This is called asymmetric routing, and it's completely normal on the Internet. Consequences: a traceroute shows only the forward path; a round-trip time is the sum of two possibly different one-way trips; and a "stateful" device in the middle (a firewall) that only sees one direction can get confused.

Common misconception: "the Internet has a map and finds the best path"

No router has the map of the whole Internet. Each router has a table of next hops derived from what its neighbours told it. The path is the result of many local decisions, shaped by policy, and nobody guarantees it's the shortest, the fastest, or even stable from one minute to the next.

Try it yourself

Trace the path to a website

On macOS and Linux run traceroute example.com. On Windows the command is tracert example.com. It takes a few seconds to a minute. (On some Linux systems you may need to install it first.)

$ traceroute example.com
traceroute to example.com (93.184.216.34), 64 hops max, 52 byte packets

 2  10.20.0.1 (10.20.0.1)  8.9 ms  9.4 ms  8.6 ms              # first router inside your ISP
 3  * * *                                                       # a router that didn't answer
 4  core1.isp.example.net (198.51.100.9)  11.8 ms  12.3 ms  11.5 ms
 5  peer.exchange.example.net (203.0.113.77)  13.0 ms  12.6 ms  14.1 ms
 6  edge1.nyc.example.net (203.0.113.130)  17.5 ms  18.2 ms  17.1 ms
 7  edge2.lon.example.net (198.51.100.201)  83.9 ms  84.6 ms  83.3 ms   # the jump: across the Atlantic
 8  93.184.216.34 (93.184.216.34)  85.0 ms  84.2 ms  84.8 ms

Your output will look different: different hostnames, addresses, hop counts and times (the hostnames above are made up). Here's how to read it:

  • Each numbered line is one hop: one router whose TTL-expired reply was received. The number is the TTL of the probe that reached it.
  • The three times are three probes sent to that hop; you see their round-trip times in milliseconds. They vary because the network is shared, and each probe is a separate packet.
  • Hop 1 is usually your default gateway, the first router. Early hops often have private addresses (like 10.x.x.x or 192.168.x.x) from your home network and the ISP's inside network (chapter 4).
  • * * * means "no reply for any of the three probes". It does not mean the path is broken, since the later hops answered. Many routers are configured not to send ICMP replies, rate-limit them, or sit behind a firewall that drops the probes. Packets still pass through just fine.
  • The latency jump from 17 ms to 84 ms between hops 6 and 7 is a long-distance link. Light in fiber covers roughly 200,000 km/s, and New York to London is around 5,600 km in a straight line, so even a perfect, straight cable needs about 28 ms one way, around 56 ms round trip, and real cables are longer and wind around. No upgrade can beat that; it's physics (chapter 13).
  • Time at a hop is the time to reach and return from that router; it can even be lower than the previous hop's, because router CPUs treat the replies as low-priority work. Don't over-interpret single hops.

Distributed systems connection

Routes change. Links fail, cables get cut by ships' anchors, routers crash and reboot, and a configuration change in one network re-routes traffic for another. For your software this means several things you cannot control: a packet can be delayed because its path suddenly got longer; two messages sent back-to-back can take different paths and arrive out of order; during the seconds a route is being recomputed, packets can be lost or looped until TTL kills them; and in the worst case the network is partitioned, meaning two groups of machines are each healthy but cannot reach one another. From inside the system, "the other machine crashed" and "I can't route to it" look exactly the same. That ambiguity is at the heart of chapters 15 and 16, and the CAP theorem in chapter 18 is a statement about what you can still promise during a partition.

Key takeaways

  • A router has several interfaces and forwards packets between networks by looking at the destination IP address only.
  • A host's default gateway is the rule "not on my LAN, send it to the router". The matching table row is the default route, 0.0.0.0/0.
  • A routing table maps prefixes to next hops. When several rows match, the longest prefix wins.
  • Forwarding is hop by hop: each router chooses only the next hop, and nobody knows the full path.
  • TTL is decremented at each hop; at 0 the packet is dropped and an ICMP "time exceeded" is sent back. Traceroute uses this to list the routers on a path.
  • Routes are learned dynamically: interior protocols (like OSPF) share link information and compute shortest paths; BGP connects Autonomous Systems using policy, and is built on trust.
  • Paths can change mid-conversation and can differ in each direction, which is a source of delay, reordering, loss and partitions.

Check your understanding

Your laptop is 192.168.1.20/24 and wants to send to 192.168.1.77 and to 93.184.216.34. What happens?

  1. Both packets are sent to the default gateway
  2. Both are sent directly to the destination's MAC address
  3. The first is delivered directly on the LAN; the second goes to the default gateway
  4. The first goes to the gateway; the second is sent directly
The first address is inside 192.168.1.0/24, so the laptop reaches it directly (after finding its MAC with ARP). The second is outside, so the laptop hands it to the default gateway and lets routers take it from there.

A router has rows 10.0.0.0/8 → A, 10.1.0.0/16 → B and 0.0.0.0/0 → C. Where does a packet for 10.1.5.9 go?

  1. B
  2. A
  3. C
  4. The router picks randomly among A and B
All three rows match. The longest prefix (/16) wins, so the packet goes to B. The /8 is less specific, and the default route (/0) is always the last resort. Longest-prefix matching is deterministic; there's no randomness.

What does a router do when a packet's TTL reaches 0?

  1. It forwards it anyway but marks it as late
  2. It waits until a route appears
  3. It sends the packet back to the sender unchanged
  4. It drops the packet and sends an ICMP "time exceeded" message to the sender
TTL stops packets circulating forever in a loop. The router discards the packet and tells the sender via ICMP, and that message is precisely what traceroute collects to learn each router's address.

Why does BGP use policy instead of simply picking the shortest path?

  1. Shortest-path algorithms don't work on large networks
  2. The networks involved are independent organisations with commercial contracts and preferences, not one team optimising one map
  3. BGP packets cannot carry hop counts
  4. Routers in different countries can't measure distance
An Autonomous System chooses routes to match its business: who pays whom, what capacity it has, what it agreed to carry. Shorter AS path is only one factor, usually considered after those. That's what makes BGP about politics as much as technology.

In a traceroute, one line shows * * * but later lines respond normally. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. The packet was lost, so the path is broken
  2. That router is the destination
  3. That router forwarded your probes but didn't send (or had filtered) the time-exceeded reply
  4. Your TTL ran out
Since later hops answered, probes obviously passed through that router. Many routers rate-limit or block ICMP replies, so they stay silent. A * * * only becomes alarming if every subsequent line is also stars.

Why is it normal for a reply to take a different route home than your request took?

  1. Replies are always sent through a backup network
  2. Each direction is routed independently by different routers with different tables and policies (asymmetric routing)
  3. Traceroute is lying about the path
  4. IP forbids using the same path twice
There is no end-to-end "circuit". Each router decides only its own next hop for each packet, and the return packets enter the network at a different place with different policies, so the two directions often diverge.
