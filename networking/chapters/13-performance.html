Latency, Throughput & Performance

Why does a fast Internet connection still feel slow? Because speed is not one number. In this chapter you'll learn to tell latency from throughput, to feel how many orders of magnitude separate a memory read from an ocean crossing, and to see why the slowest 1% of requests often decide how fast a whole system feels.

Why this matters

Every performance conversation in industry (“the p99 is spiking”, “we're latency bound”, “add a cache”) uses the ideas in this chapter. They are also the first reason distributed systems are hard: once a request has to cross the network, you are paying a price measured in millions of CPU-instruction times, and when a request fans out to hundreds of machines, rare slowness becomes the normal case.

Two different questions: how long, and how much

In chapter 1 we separated two ideas that everyday language mashes together as “speed”. Let's restate them with a picture of a pipe, then go further.

Latency
How long one thing takes to get from A to B (or there and back). Measured in time: milliseconds. Think of it as the length of the pipe.
Bandwidth
The maximum rate at which a link can carry data. Measured in bits per second (Mbps, Gbps). Think of it as the width of the pipe.
Throughput
The rate you actually achieve on a real transfer. It is always at most the bandwidth of the slowest link on the path, and often much less.
Round-trip time (RTT)
Latency there and back: what ping measures. Most protocols spend their time waiting for replies, so RTT is the number that matters most.

A wide pipe doesn't make it shorter. If you want to send one tiny question (“is the server up?”) across an ocean, it makes no difference whether the link is 10 Mbps or 10 Gbps: the answer takes about 150 ms either way. Upgrading your home Internet from 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps will make a big file download faster, but will do almost nothing for how quickly a web page starts to appear.

Where latency comes from

The delay of a single hop (and of the whole path, summed) has four ingredients:

ComponentWhat it isRough size
PropagationTime for the signal to physically travel. Light in fiber covers about 200,000 km/s, i.e. 5 ms per 1,000 km one way.London to New York (about 5,600 km) is at least ~28 ms one way, ~56 ms round trip, before any equipment is involved
TransmissionTime to push all the bits onto the wire: size ÷ bandwidth.1,500 bytes (12,000 bits) at 100 Mbps = 0.12 ms
QueueingWaiting in line inside routers and servers because others got there first.Zero to hundreds of ms; the most variable part
ProcessingRouters reading headers, servers running code, databases looking things up.Microseconds for a router, milliseconds or more for a server

Only transmission time depends on bandwidth. Propagation is set by physics and the length of the cable. You can buy more bandwidth. You cannot buy a faster speed of light. The practical consequence: to reduce latency to far-away users you must move the data closer to them, which is exactly what CDNs do (chapter 14).

Common misconception: “faster Internet” means lower latency

ISP adverts quote bandwidth (“1 Gigabit!”). That number says nothing about latency. A satellite link can have enormous bandwidth and still take 600 ms round trip for a geostationary orbit, because the signal must climb about 36,000 km and back down, twice. Always ask which of the two your problem is bound by.

The bandwidth-delay product: how much is “in the pipe”?

Here is a question that sounds odd until you answer it: how much data is in flight on a link at any one moment? Consider a 100 Mbps link with a 100 ms round trip. A sender that blasts bits for one RTT will have sent 100 Mbit/s × 0.1 s = 10 megabits (1.25 MB) before the very first acknowledgement can possibly come back. That quantity, bandwidth × RTT, is the bandwidth-delay product (BDP). It is the volume of the pipe.

Why do we care? Because TCP (chapter 8) will only keep a limited amount of unacknowledged data in flight at once; this limit is the window. If the window is smaller than the pipe's volume, the sender must stop and wait for acknowledgements while the pipe sits partly empty. The maximum throughput of a TCP connection is therefore roughly:

throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT

LinkBandwidthRTTBDP (bytes in flight to fill it)
Wi-Fi to a nearby server100 Mbps20 ms100×10⁶ × 0.02 ÷ 8 = 250 KB
Inside a data center10 Gbps0.5 ms10×10⁹ × 0.0005 ÷ 8 = 625 KB
Across a continent1 Gbps40 ms1×10⁹ × 0.04 ÷ 8 = 5 MB
Across an ocean1 Gbps100 ms1×10⁹ × 0.1 ÷ 8 = 12.5 MB

The original TCP header had a 16-bit window field, so the window could be at most 65,535 bytes (about 64 KB). Plug that into the formula for the ocean-crossing path: 65,535 bytes × 8 ÷ 0.1 s ≈ 5.2 Mbps, on a link that could carry 1,000 Mbps. A modern fix, window scaling, lets windows grow up to about 1 GB, and every modern operating system uses it. Links with a large BDP (high bandwidth and high delay) are called long fat networks, and they are the reason TCP needs large windows and careful congestion control.

Go deeper: the BDP is Little's Law in disguise optional

“Amount in flight = rate × delay” is a general law of any system where things enter, spend time inside, and leave. We'll meet it again below as Little's Law. For a network pipe the “things” are bits, the rate is bandwidth, and the time inside is the delay. For a web server the things are requests, the rate is requests per second, and the time inside is the response time.

Why round trips dominate web performance

Now apply this to something you do every day. In chapter 10 we followed what happens when you type a URL. Count the times the browser has to wait for an answer before the first byte of the page can arrive, assuming nothing is cached and the server is far away (100 ms RTT):

  1. DNS lookup (chapter 9): ask the resolver “what's the IP for this name?” At least one round trip to the resolver, more if it has to walk the DNS hierarchy itself.
  2. TCP handshake (chapter 8): SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. Your data can't start until the SYN-ACK returns: one round trip.
  3. TLS handshake (chapter 11): agree on keys. One round trip with TLS 1.3, two with TLS 1.2.
  4. HTTP request and response: send the GET, wait for the first byte of the reply: one more round trip.

Four round trips at 100 ms is 400 ms of pure waiting, during which no useful page data has moved and your 100 Mbps link is idle. And then the HTML links to CSS, scripts and images, and some of those link to more files (a stylesheet that loads a font), so the dependency chain adds further round trips. This is why web performance advice is mostly about avoiding round trips:

  • Reuse connections (HTTP keep-alive, HTTP/2 multiplexing) so you pay the TCP and TLS cost once, not per file.
  • Resume sessions: TLS session resumption and HTTP/3's QUIC (which merges the transport and crypto handshakes into one round trip, and can send data immediately on a repeat visit) cut the setup cost.
  • Be closer: a CDN edge 10 ms away turns a 400 ms setup into 40 ms.
  • Cache: the fastest round trip is the one you never make. DNS answers, TLS sessions and HTTP responses are all cached.
  • Hint ahead: preconnect and dns-prefetch hints let the browser start handshakes early, overlapping waits.

Analogy: ordering by letter vs. by phone

Suppose a shop is overseas and mail takes 10 days each way. Asking “do you have the red shirt?” (10 days), then “ok, here's my address” (10 days), then “ok, here's my payment” (10 days) means a month of waiting, even though each letter is one line long. You'd be much better off writing a single letter that includes everything at once. That's what protocol designers do when they merge handshakes and send data early. Buying a wider mailbox would not help at all.

Where it breaks down: real networks can also deliver part of a message quickly and the rest later, and a protocol can run several conversations in parallel on one connection. Letters can't.

In human time, a trip to memory is a minute and a half, and a spinning-disk seek is nearly four months. A round trip to the other side of an ocean is years. This is the central fact of distributed computing: a network call is not “like a function call, only a bit slower”. It is six to eight orders of magnitude slower, and a program that makes thousands of them in a row is spending nearly all its life waiting.

Averages lie: percentiles and tail latency

Suppose you measure a service and report “average response time: 150 ms”. That sounds like a good summary. It isn't. Consider ten requests: nine took 50 ms and one took 1,050 ms. The mean (add up and divide) is (9×50 + 1050) ÷ 10 = 150 ms. But nine out of ten users saw 50 ms and one user waited over a second. The mean describes nobody.

The fix is to look at the distribution. Sort all the response times, and read off values at particular positions:

Median (p50)
Half of requests are faster than this. The “typical” experience.
p95
95% of requests are faster; 1 in 20 is slower.
p99
99% are faster; 1 in 100 is slower.
p99.9
999 out of 1,000 are faster; 1 in 1,000 is slower.

The slow end of the distribution is called the tail, and the high percentiles measure tail latency. Here is what a realistic response-time histogram looks like. It isn't a symmetric bell curve: most requests are quick, but a long tail stretches far to the right.

Common misconception: you can average percentiles

If server A reports p99 = 100 ms and server B reports p99 = 300 ms, the fleet's p99 is not 200 ms. Percentiles cannot be averaged or added; you need the underlying distributions (or histograms you can merge). Dashboards that average per-minute p99s are quietly wrong.

Why the tail matters more at scale: fan-out amplification

“Only 1% of requests are slow” sounds fine. It stops sounding fine the moment one user action needs many backend calls, which in modern systems is nearly always. Loading a page may involve a profile service, a recommendations service, an ads service, a search index, and so on. The page is done only when the slowest of them returns.

Suppose each backend call independently has a 1% chance of being slow (it's beyond its p99). The chance that a single call is fast is 0.99. The chance that all N calls are fast is 0.99N. So the chance that at least one is slow, and hence the whole page is slow, is:

P(page is slow) = 1 − 0.99N

Backend calls per page (N)0.99N (all fast)Pages that hit a slow call
10.9901%
100.904about 9.6%
500.605about 39.5%
1000.366about 63.4%
2000.134about 86.6%
5000.0066about 99.3%

With 100 calls, roughly 63% of pages hit at least one 1%-case. The p99 of the backends has become the median of the page. (This assumes the calls are independent, which is a simplification; correlated slowness behaves differently, often worse.) Even at p99.9 per call, 100 calls give 1 − 0.999100 ≈ 9.5% slow pages. This is the main message of a famous 2013 paper from Google engineers Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso, The Tail at Scale: at scale, you must engineer the tail, not the average.

Where do slow requests come from?

Mostly mundane things: a request queued behind a big one, a garbage-collection pause, a cache miss that falls through to a slow database, a packet loss that triggers a TCP retransmission timeout (hundreds of milliseconds), a background job hogging the disk, or a noisy neighbour on a shared machine. None of them is a bug in the usual sense; they are the price of sharing resources.

Go deeper: hedged requests optional

One cheap trick for cutting the tail: send the request to one replica, and if no answer arrives within some threshold (say the p95 latency), send a second copy to another replica. Use whichever answer comes first and cancel the other. Only ~5% of requests ever trigger the duplicate, so the extra load is small, but the slowest cases are almost always rescued because it is rare for both replicas to be slow at once. Hedging only works safely for operations that are harmless to run twice (idempotent ones, which we study in chapter 16).

Queueing: why the last 10% of capacity is so expensive

Why do tails exist at all? Often it is queueing. A server can only do so much work per second. When requests arrive faster than it can finish them, they wait.

Analogy: the supermarket checkout

A cashier takes about one minute per customer. If a customer arrives every 5 minutes, the cashier is idle most of the time and nobody waits. If one arrives every 2 minutes, the cashier is busy half the time; sometimes two customers overlap, so there's the occasional short wait. If one arrives every 1.1 minutes, the cashier is busy almost all the time. Now any random cluster of arrivals creates a line that takes a long time to drain, because there are almost no idle moments in which to catch up. At exactly one every minute (or faster) the line grows forever.

Where it breaks down: a real server can often work on several requests at once, and real queues can be capped (extra customers are turned away), but the shape of the problem is the same.

Define utilisation ρ as the fraction of time the server is busy (arrival rate ÷ service rate). For the simplest textbook model (random arrivals, random service times, one server, called M/M/1), the average time a request spends in the system, as a multiple of the bare service time S, is:

time in system = S ÷ (1 − ρ)

Look at the numbers: at 50% utilisation a request takes twice the bare service time; at 90% it takes ten times as long; at 99% a hundred times. The system looks healthy at 70% and falls over at 95%, and a small increase in traffic is all that separates the two. This is why operators keep headroom (many aim to run well below saturation) and why autoscaling triggers long before a server is “full”.

Little's Law

One of the neatest results in the field: in a stable system, the average number of items inside = arrival rate × average time spent inside. Written L = λ × W. It holds regardless of arrival patterns or the scheduling order.

Example. A service handles λ = 200 requests per second, and each takes W = 50 ms (0.05 s) on average. Then L = 200 × 0.05 = 10 requests in progress at any moment. Now a database slowdown pushes W to 500 ms while clients keep sending 200 per second: L = 200 × 0.5 = 100 requests in progress. Each holds a thread, a connection and some memory, so the server may run out of them. That is how slowness turns into outage.

Go deeper: why queues make slowness contagious optional

When service A calls service B and B slows down, A's requests sit waiting for B. By Little's Law A now has more requests in flight, so A's own resources fill up and A slows down, and then so does everyone who calls A. Delays travel upstream along the call graph. We'll see tools against this (timeouts, retry budgets, circuit breakers, load shedding) in chapter 16. A common last resort is load shedding: deliberately rejecting some requests quickly so the rest stay fast, rather than letting everyone queue forever.

Caching: the main weapon against latency

If latency can't be shrunk below the speed of light, the best move is to avoid paying it. A cache keeps a copy of data in a faster, closer place so most requests never go to the slow, far one. The hierarchy mirrors the latency table: a CPU cache beats memory, memory beats SSD, SSD beats a network call to a database, and a nearby network call beats a distant one.

How much does a cache help? What matters is the hit ratio: the fraction of requests found in the cache. The average time is:

average = hit × tcache + (1 − hit) × tmiss

Hit ratioCalculation (cache 1 ms, origin 100 ms)Average
0% (no cache)100 ms every time100 ms
50%0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 10050.5 ms
90%0.9 × 1 + 0.1 × 10010.9 ms
99%0.99 × 1 + 0.01 × 1001.99 ms

Going from 90% to 99% hits cuts the average by more than 5×, because the rare misses dominate the average. Notice this is also a tail-latency story: the misses are the tail. And it comes with a catch: a cache can serve stale data when the original changes, which is why “there are only two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things”. We saw one version of this tradeoff with DNS TTLs (chapter 9) and HTTP caching headers (chapter 10). Caching close to the user, on servers around the world, is the idea behind CDNs, the subject of the next chapter, Proxies, Load Balancers & CDNs.

Try it yourself

Break one request into its round trips with curl -w

curl can print its own timing breakdown. The -w option takes a format string, -o /dev/null throws away the page body, and -s hides the progress bar. This is read-only and safe. (On Windows, use curl.exe in PowerShell and NUL instead of /dev/null.)

$ curl -o /dev/null -s -w "dns:        %{time_namelookup}\nconnect:    %{time_connect}\ntls:        %{time_appconnect}\nfirst byte: %{time_starttransfer}\ntotal:      %{time_total}\n" https://www.example.com
dns:        0.012

Run it a second time against http:// (no TLS) and the tls line shows 0.000 (there is no TLS phase). This is the waterfall from the earlier diagram, measured on your own machine.

min is the closest thing to the “pure” propagation-plus-processing delay: nothing queued. avg is higher, because queueing adds time. max exposes a tail event. stddev (standard deviation) summarises how much the times wobble; on Linux the label is mdev, and on Windows you get min/max/average only. A small stddev means a steady path, a large one means jitter. Notice again how one outlier of 29.7 ms moved the average away from the typical 14 ms.

Distributed systems connection

Three ideas from this chapter become design rules later:

  • Latency budgets. If a user-facing request must finish in 200 ms and one cross-region call costs 80 ms, you can afford at most two such calls in sequence. Architects literally write down a time budget and split it across services.
  • Cross-region calls are expensive. Keeping a database's replicas in different continents means that waiting for them to agree (chapter 18, chapter 20) costs at least a transcontinental RTT on every write. Many consistency trade-offs are really latency trade-offs.
  • Tail latency at scale. The more machines a request touches, the more the 99th percentile becomes the median. Timeouts, hedging and retries (chapter 16) exist largely to tame the tail.

Key takeaways

  • Latency (time) and bandwidth (capacity) are different; only transmission time depends on bandwidth, and nothing beats the speed of light.
  • The bandwidth-delay product (bandwidth × RTT) is how much data must be in flight to fill a link. It's why TCP windows must be large on long fat links: throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT.
  • A cold page load spends ~4 round trips (DNS, TCP, TLS, HTTP) before the first byte, so performance work is mostly about avoiding or overlapping round trips.
  • Know the orders of magnitude: memory ~100 ns, SSD ~100 µs, data-center hop ~0.5 ms, disk seek ~10 ms, intercontinental RTT ~100 ms. A network call is millions of times slower than a memory read.
  • Averages lie. Use percentiles (p50, p95, p99, p99.9). With fan-out, a 1% per-call tail makes ~63% of 100-call pages slow (1 − 0.99100).
  • Waiting time explodes as utilisation nears 100% (S ÷ (1 − ρ)), so keep headroom. Little's Law (L = λW) tells you how much is in flight.
  • Caching is the main latency weapon; hit ratio decides how much it helps, and CDNs are caches close to users.

Check your understanding

You upgrade from a 100 Mbps to a 1 Gbps connection. Which of these improves the most?

  1. The ping time to a server on another continent
  2. The time for a small API call to a distant server
  3. The time to download a 2 GB file from a nearby server that isn't the bottleneck
  4. The time a DNS lookup takes
Bandwidth only helps when the time is dominated by pushing lots of bits through the pipe. Ping, small API calls and DNS lookups are dominated by round-trip latency, which is set by distance and physics, not by link width.

A link has 1 Gbps bandwidth and a 100 ms RTT. Roughly how much data must be in flight to keep it fully busy?

  1. 12.5 MB
  2. 100 KB
  3. 1.25 GB
  4. 64 KB
BDP = bandwidth × RTT = 10⁹ bits/s × 0.1 s = 10⁸ bits = 12.5 MB. A sender limited to a 64 KB window would reach only about 5 Mbps on this path, which is why window scaling exists.

Your service reports a mean latency of 80 ms and a p99 of 900 ms. What is the best interpretation?

  1. Everyone gets about 80 ms
  2. 1% of users get exactly 900 ms and nobody gets more
  3. The mean is wrong and should be recalculated
  4. Most requests are quick, but about 1 in 100 takes 900 ms or longer, and the mean hides that
p99 = 900 ms means 99% of requests were faster than 900 ms and 1% were at least that slow (possibly much slower). The mean is correct but is not a summary of anyone's actual experience.

A page makes 100 independent backend calls, each with a 1% chance of being slow. About what fraction of pages will include at least one slow call?

  1. 1%
  2. 63%
  3. 100%
  4. 10%
The chance all 100 are fast is 0.99100 ≈ 0.366, so the chance at least one is slow is 1 − 0.366 ≈ 63%. Rare per-call slowness becomes common per page.

A server's utilisation rises from 50% to 90%. By the simple M/M/1 model, how does the average time in the system change?

  1. It rises by 80%
  2. It roughly doubles
  3. It rises about five-fold (from 2×S to 10×S)
  4. It stays almost the same
Time in system is S ÷ (1 − ρ): 2×S at 50% and 10×S at 90%. Queueing delay grows non-linearly near saturation, which is why capacity planning leaves headroom.

A cache costs 1 ms to read and the origin costs 100 ms. Raising the hit ratio from 90% to 99% changes the average latency from about…

  1. 10.9 ms to 2 ms
  2. 90 ms to 99 ms
  3. 1 ms to 100 ms
  4. 50 ms to 25 ms
average = hit × 1 + (1 − hit) × 100: 0.9 + 10 = 10.9 ms at 90%, and 0.99 + 1 = 1.99 ms at 99%. The rare misses dominate the average.
