What is a network?

A network is two or more devices that can send each other messages. That's it. Two tin cans joined by a string are a network. So are two computers joined by a cable, and so are the billions of devices joined together into the Internet.

For the messages to be useful, both sides have to agree on rules: who speaks first, how a message starts and ends, what to do if part of it gets lost. A set of rules like this is called a protocol.

Analogy: a phone call

When you call someone, you follow a protocol without thinking about it. The phone rings; they say "Hello?"; you say who you are; you take turns talking; if the line crackles, you say "Sorry, can you repeat that?"; you both say "Bye" before hanging up. Computers do exactly the same kinds of things, except the rules are written down precisely, because computers can't improvise.

The Internet is not one network. It is a network of networks: your home network, your school's network, your phone company's network, Google's network and tens of thousands of others, all agreeing to pass each other's messages along. Nobody owns all of it, and there is no central computer in charge.

Messages travel as packets

Before the Internet, the phone system worked by circuit switching: when you made a call, a continuous wire path was reserved just for you from end to end, even during the silences. That wastes a lot of capacity.

The Internet uses packet switching instead. Your message is chopped into small pieces called packets. Each packet carries the destination address on it, like a postcard, and travels on its own. Packets from millions of people share the same cables, taking turns. At the other end, the pieces are put back together.

Analogy: mailing a book as postcards

Imagine you had to mail a whole book, but the post office only accepts postcards. You'd write a few lines on each card, number them, and address every card to your friend. The cards might travel in different trucks and arrive in a jumbled order; one might even get lost. Your friend sorts them by number and asks you to resend any missing ones.

Where it breaks down: real packets move at nearly the speed of light, and the "asking to resend" is done automatically by software (that's TCP, chapter 8).

The journey of one message

Let's follow a single chat message from your phone to a friend in another country. Every step below is something you'll understand properly by the end of this guide; the chapter numbers tell you where. Use the buttons (or your arrow keys) to step through.

Step through

Sending "hi" to a friend overseas

  1. You press send. The app turns "hi" into numbers (bytes, ch. 1). It needs the chat server's address, so it asks DNS, the Internet's phonebook, to translate a name like chat.example.com into a number (ch. 9).
  2. Wrapping. Your phone wraps the message in several "envelopes", one per layer of the network, each adding information like which program should receive it and which computer it's for (ch. 3). Big messages are split into packets.
  3. Radio waves. The phone sends the packet as radio waves to your home Wi-Fi router (ch. 2).
  4. Out of the house. The router passes the packet over a cable to your Internet Service Provider (ISP). On the way out, the router rewrites the "from" address, because your phone's home address isn't valid on the wider Internet (NAT, ch. 4).
  5. Across the world. The packet hops through maybe 10–20 routers. Each one looks at the destination address and decides which neighbour to pass it to, like a sorting office (ch. 5). Crossing an ocean takes tens of milliseconds, limited by the speed of light (ch. 13).
  6. Arriving at the data center. A load balancer picks one of hundreds of identical chat servers (ch. 14). TCP has made sure no packet was lost or jumbled (ch. 8), and TLS has kept the contents secret from everyone along the way (ch. 11).
  7. Saving it safely. The server stores your message on several machines, so a single broken disk can't lose it (ch. 18). Those machines must agree on the order of messages in the chat (ch. 17, ch. 20). This is distributed systems territory.
  8. Delivery. The server pushes the message to your friend's phone, making the same kind of journey in reverse. The whole trip usually takes less than a fifth of a second.

Notice how many separate problems were solved along the way: turning text into signals, finding addresses, picking routes, recovering lost packets, keeping secrets, spreading work over many machines and keeping copies in agreement. That list is basically the table of contents of this guide.

Where we're heading: distributed systems

A distributed system is a group of computers that work together over a network and appear to their users as a single system. Google Search, WhatsApp, your bank and every online game are distributed systems. They exist because one computer isn't big enough, fast enough or reliable enough on its own.

The hard part isn't the computers; it's the network between them. Messages get delayed, lost, duplicated or arrive in the wrong order. Machines crash halfway through a task. Clocks disagree. Most of the cleverness in distributed systems is about coping with an imperfect network, which is exactly why we learn networking first.

A famous joke that is also true

"A distributed system is one in which the failure of a computer you didn't even know existed can render your own computer unusable."
— Leslie Lamport, computer scientist (you'll meet his work in chapter 17)

Your first experiment

Try it yourself: ping

Open a terminal. On a Mac, press ⌘ + Space, type Terminal and press Enter. On Windows, open the Start menu and search for Command Prompt. Then type:

$ ping -c 4 google.com     # on Windows: ping google.com
PING google.com (142.250.187.206): 56 data bytes
64 bytes from 142.250.187.206: icmp_seq=0 ttl=117 time=14.2 ms
64 bytes from 142.250.187.206: icmp_seq=1 ttl=117 time=13.8 ms
64 bytes from 142.250.187.206: icmp_seq=2 ttl=117 time=15.1 ms
64 bytes from 142.250.187.206: icmp_seq=3 ttl=117 time=14.0 ms

ping sends a tiny "are you there?" packet and times how long the reply takes. You just measured a round trip: from your computer to one of Google's machines and back, in about 14 thousandths of a second. Your numbers will differ. Notice also:

  • google.com was turned into a number like 142.250.187.206. That's an IP address, found using DNS.
  • The times vary a little each time. The network is shared, so packets sometimes wait in line.

Try ping on a website hosted far away (for example a university on another continent) and compare. We'll explain the difference in chapter 1.

Key takeaways

  • A network is devices exchanging messages; a protocol is the agreed set of rules for doing so.
  • The Internet is a network of independently run networks, with no central control.
  • Data travels in packets that share the cables with everyone else's and may arrive out of order or not at all.
  • Each protocol solves one specific problem. Learn the problem first and the protocol makes sense.
  • Distributed systems are about many computers acting as one, and their main enemy is the imperfect network.

Check your understanding

What is a protocol?

  1. A cable that connects two computers
  2. A very fast computer that controls the Internet
  3. An agreed set of rules for how to exchange messages
  4. A type of password
A protocol is a set of rules both sides follow, like the etiquette of a phone call. Cables carry the signals; protocols decide what the signals mean and what to do next.

Who runs the Internet?

  1. One international company with a central server
  2. Nobody in particular: tens of thousands of independent networks cooperate
  3. The government of the country you are in
  4. Google
The Internet is a network of networks. Each piece is run by someone different, and they cooperate by following shared protocols. There is no central computer to switch off.

Why does the Internet split messages into packets instead of reserving a wire per conversation?

  1. So many conversations can share the same cables efficiently
  2. Because packets cannot be lost
  3. So messages always arrive in order
  4. Because computers can only count to 1,000
Packet switching lets everyone take turns on shared links, so no capacity sits idle reserved for a silent caller. The price is that packets can be lost, delayed or reordered, which later chapters deal with.

In the ping output, what does time=14.2 ms measure?

  1. How long Google took to load its homepage
  2. How long a packet takes to go one way
  3. Your Internet speed in megabits
  4. How long a small packet took to reach the other computer and for the reply to come back
It is the round-trip time (RTT): there and back. Round-trip time is one of the most important numbers in networking and comes up again and again.
