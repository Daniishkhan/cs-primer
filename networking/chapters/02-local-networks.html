Local Networks & Ethernet

Before a message can cross the planet, it has to get out of the room. This chapter is about the smallest kind of network: the devices in your home, office or server rack. You'll learn how many devices share one medium without talking over each other, what a MAC address is, and how a humble switch quietly learns who is plugged into which socket.

Why this matters

Every journey across the Internet begins and ends with a hop across a local network, and every hop in between is also one. This is the "link layer" from chapter 3: the orange envelope with the courier's label. Understanding it explains why your Wi-Fi is slower than a cable, what a "MAC address" in your settings is, and why networks inside data centers can be so fast.

What is a local network?

A local area network (LAN) is a group of devices close together (one home, one office floor, one rack of servers) that can reach each other directly, without going through a router. Your laptop, phone, printer and TV at home are all on one LAN. You may think of "the Wi-Fi" as the network, but strictly the LAN is the whole neighbourhood of devices that share the same link-layer connectivity, whether they're attached by Wi-Fi or by cable.

Two devices on the same LAN reach each other using the link layer: the layer-2 job from chapter 3 of "deliver this to the next device on the same local network". The workhorse technology for that is Ethernet, invented at Xerox in the 1970s and still the standard today (in vastly faster form). Wi-Fi is its wireless cousin and uses similar frames.

The shared-medium problem

Early Ethernet was gloriously simple: a single long coaxial cable ran past every computer, and all of them were tapped into it. That's a shared medium. Anything one computer sent was heard by all the others. This raises an immediate problem.

Analogy: a room full of people talking

Imagine twenty people in one room with no moderator. If one person talks, everyone hears them, and the right person listens. But if two people start at the same moment, their words overlap and nobody understands either. Polite people listen first, wait for silence, then speak. If two start at the same instant anyway, they both notice the clash, stop, wait a random short time (so they don't clash again), and retry.

Where it breaks down: in a room you can hear yourself being talked over after a second; on a wire, signals travel for a finite time (chapter 1), so two senders can start without knowing about each other and only discover the clash a moment later.

When two devices transmit at once, their signals overlap and become garbage. That's a collision. Early Ethernet handled this with a scheme called CSMA/CD (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection). In plain words:

  1. Carrier sense: before sending, listen. If the cable is busy, wait.
  2. Multiple access: if it's quiet, go ahead and send. Everyone shares the same cable.
  3. Collision detection: keep listening while you send. If what you hear isn't what you sent, someone else started too. Stop, send a brief jam signal so everybody knows, and wait a random time before retrying (with longer random waits after each repeated clash, called exponential backoff).

This worked, but the more devices on the cable, the more collisions, and the less useful bandwidth you got. That pushed engineers to build networks that avoid sharing in the first place.

Hubs and switches

The first improvement was the hub: a box with several sockets that simply repeats whatever arrives on one socket out of all the others. It lets you plug computers into a central box rather than a long shared cable, but electrically it's still one shared medium. Everything is heard by everyone, and collisions are still possible.

The real fix was the switch. A switch looks the same from outside, but it's smart: it reads the destination of each incoming frame and sends it out of only the one socket (port) where that destination lives. Every port gets its own private connection, so two pairs of computers can talk at the same time without disturbing each other.

Modern switches also let each connection send and receive at the same time (full duplex: separate wires or channels for each direction). With a dedicated link per device and full duplex, collisions simply can't happen on a wired Ethernet network, so CSMA/CD is now of mostly historical interest even though the name "Ethernet" lives on. Hubs have almost disappeared.

But how does the switch know "which socket is that destination on"? Before we answer, we need to know what a destination is. That's the MAC address.

MAC addresses: names for network cards

Every network interface (the Ethernet port or Wi-Fi chip in a device) has a MAC address (Media Access Control address). It's a 48-bit number (6 bytes), written as six pairs of hex digits (chapter 1), usually separated by colons or hyphens:

a4:83:e7:1c:52:2f

That gives 248, about 281 trillion, possible addresses. The addresses are meant to be globally unique per network card, so that no two devices on any LAN ever clash. To make that work, the first half and second half have different owners:

a4:83:e7OUI: manufacturer prefix (3 bytes, 24 bits)
1c:52:2fdevice-specific part (3 bytes, 24 bits)

The first three bytes are an OUI (Organizationally Unique Identifier), a prefix the standards body IEEE sells to each manufacturer. The manufacturer then numbers each card it makes in the last three bytes. That's why you can sometimes look up an unknown device on your network by the OUI and see the company that made its network chip.

Analogy: a serial number on a courier's parcel

A MAC address is like the serial number stamped on a device at the factory, or the label a courier sticks on a parcel for one particular leg of its journey (the analogy from chapter 3). It identifies which device on this local network. It doesn't say where in the world the device is, just as a serial number doesn't tell you what city your toaster is in. That's why MAC addresses can't be used to cross the Internet. Chapter 4's IP addresses do that job.

Where it breaks down: a serial number is permanent, but MAC addresses can be changed by software, and as we'll see, many phones now change theirs on purpose.

Two special things to know:

  • The address ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff (all 1s) is the broadcast address, meaning "everyone on this LAN". A frame sent there is delivered to every device.
  • A MAC address is only meaningful on its own LAN. When a packet crosses a router, the old MAC addresses are discarded and new ones written for the next LAN (chapter 3's "MAC addresses change at every hop").

Common misconception: "MAC addresses are permanent and can't be faked"

The address burned into a card is just the factory default. Software can announce any MAC address it likes, and modern phones and laptops deliberately use randomised, per-network MAC addresses when joining Wi-Fi, so shops and hotspots can't track you by one fixed identifier. Randomised addresses have a special bit set (the "locally administered" bit, which shows up as 2, 6, A or E as the second hex digit of the first byte). Don't treat a MAC address as a secure identity or an access-control password.

The Ethernet frame

On a LAN, data travels in units called frames (chapter 3). An Ethernet frame is the link-layer envelope: a small header, the payload from the layer above (usually an IP packet), and a check value at the end.

Destination MAC6 bytes
Source MAC6 bytes
Type2 bytes
Payload (usually an IP packet)46 to 1,500 bytes
FCS4 bytes
Destination MAC
Who this frame is for on this LAN. Placed first so a switch can read it right away and start forwarding.
Source MAC
Who sent it. This is what a switch uses to learn where devices are.
Type
What kind of thing is inside, so the receiver hands the payload to the right software. 0x0800 means an IPv4 packet, 0x86DD IPv6, 0x0806 ARP (chapter 6).
Payload
The data from the layer above, between 46 and 1,500 bytes. The 1,500 upper limit is the MTU you met in chapter 3. If the data is shorter than 46 bytes, padding is added.
FCS
Frame Check Sequence: a 4-byte CRC (chapter 1) computed over the frame. The receiver recomputes it, and if it doesn't match, the frame was corrupted and is silently dropped.

Header and trailer together are 14 + 4 = 18 bytes, so a full frame is 1,518 bytes and the smallest is 64 bytes. (In real life a frame is also preceded on the wire by an 8-byte preamble of alternating 1s and 0s that lets the receiver's clock synchronise. It isn't counted as part of the frame, so we'll leave it out.)

Go deeper: why is the minimum payload 46 bytes? optional

It comes from the collision-detection days. A sender has to still be transmitting when news of a collision from the far end of the cable comes back, otherwise it can't know its frame was damaged. At 10 Mbps on the longest permitted cable, that round trip took roughly 51 microseconds, which is about 64 bytes of data. So frames shorter than 64 bytes (header + payload + check value) were padded. The rule survived into the switched era as a compatibility legacy. The extra 4 bytes in "VLAN-tagged" frames (802.1Q) are used by enterprise networks to carve one physical LAN into several logical ones; you can ignore them for now.

How a switch learns

Now back to the question: how does a switch know which port leads to which MAC address? Nobody programs it. It figures it out by watching traffic, using a simple trick. The switch keeps a MAC address table (also called a forwarding table or CAM table): a list of "this MAC address was last seen on that port". And it follows these rules for each incoming frame:

  1. Learn: look at the source MAC. Record "source MAC → arrival port" in the table (replace any old entry).
  2. Forward: look at the destination MAC. If it's in the table, send the frame out only of that port. (If that's the same port it arrived on, drop it.)
  3. Flood: if the destination is unknown, or it's the broadcast address, send the frame out of every port except the one it came in on.

Entries are forgotten after a few minutes of silence, so if you move a cable or a laptop, the table heals itself. Step through an example with four computers. To keep the picture readable, we'll use two-letter stand-ins like AA for the six-byte addresses.

Step through

A switch learns who is where

  1. A sends to B. A builds a frame with source AA and destination BB and sends it to the switch on port 1. The switch's table is empty: it knows nobody yet.
  2. Learn, then flood. Rule 1: the source is AA, and it arrived on port 1, so the switch records AA → port 1. Rule 3: it has no entry for BB, so it floods the frame out of ports 2, 3 and 4.
  3. Everybody hears it; only B keeps it. The frame reaches B, C and D. Each compares the destination BB with its own MAC address. B matches and accepts it; C and D ignore it. Flooding is wasteful but only happens until the switch has learned.
  4. B replies. B builds a frame from BB to AA and sends it to the switch on port 2.
  5. Learn, then forward directly. The switch records BB → port 2. It already knows AA → port 1, so this time it sends the frame only out of port 1. C and D see nothing.
  6. The table now works. When A sends to B again, the switch finds BB → port 2 and forwards the frame there and nowhere else. After a few exchanges, the switch has learned the whole LAN and almost never needs to flood.

Two consequences are worth remembering. First, a switch needs no configuration to do its job, which is why you can plug an unmanaged switch into a home network and it simply works. Second, broadcasts and unknown destinations reach everyone: all devices that a frame can reach by flooding make up one broadcast domain. A small home LAN is one broadcast domain; a big company might split its network into many, so one device's shouting doesn't reach everyone.

Go deeper: what if switches are connected in a loop? optional

If you connect switches in a ring, a flooded frame can chase itself around the loop forever (Ethernet frames have no "time to live", unlike IP packets in chapter 4). Within seconds the network drowns in a broadcast storm. Switches avoid this with the Spanning Tree Protocol, which switches talk to each other to discover loops and temporarily disable the redundant links, keeping them as backups. Large data center networks often avoid loops differently, by using routing between layers of switches instead.

Teaser: two questions this chapter can't answer

"I know the IP address of the printer. How do I find its MAC address so I can build the frame?" That's the job of ARP, a tiny helper protocol in chapter 6, which works by broadcasting "who has this IP address?" to the whole LAN. "What if the computer I want is not on my LAN?" Then the frame is addressed to your router, which forwards it onward, the subject of chapter 5.

Wi-Fi in brief

Wi-Fi (the IEEE 802.11 family) is Ethernet's wireless cousin. A device doesn't plug into a switch; it connects over radio to an access point (AP), which in a home is usually built into the same box as the router. The AP bridges between the wireless side and the wired network, and it still delivers frames using MAC addresses.

The difference is that the medium is the air, which is shared by everything in range on the same channel, including the neighbour's network and the microwave oven. A radio can't easily listen while it transmits on the same frequency, so Wi-Fi can't use collision detection. It uses collision avoidance instead, CSMA/CA: listen first; if the channel is idle, wait a small random time (the backoff) and then send; and since you can't tell if the frame collided, the receiver sends a short acknowledgement for every frame. No acknowledgement means "assume it was lost, wait, and retry".

That explains why Wi-Fi is, on the whole, slower and less reliable than a cable:

  • All devices on a channel take turns on the air, and the total bandwidth is shared. One slow device (far away, weak signal) uses more airtime per bit and drags everyone down.
  • Radio waves are weakened by walls and distance and disturbed by interference, so the actual speed varies from moment to moment. Expect more lost frames and more retries than on a cable, which shows up as higher and more variable latency.
  • Every frame needs an acknowledgement and a random wait, which adds overhead a switched cable doesn't have.

A practical rule: use a cable for anything stationary where you care about reliability (desktop, game console, server), and Wi-Fi for convenience.

Try it yourself

See your neighbours' MAC addresses

Your computer keeps a small list of the LAN neighbours it has recently talked to, with their IP and MAC addresses. This list comes from ARP (chapter 6). Run arp -a (works on macOS, Windows and Linux; on Linux you can also use ip neigh). It only reads information; nothing is changed.

$ arp -a
? (192.168.1.1) at 3c:52:82:0b:9d:41 on en0 ifscope [ethernet]     # your router
? (192.168.1.34) at f0:18:98:77:aa:02 on en0 ifscope [ethernet]
? (192.168.1.255) at ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff on en0 ifscope [ethernet]  # broadcast
? (224.0.0.251) at 1:0:5e:0:0:fb on en0 ifscope permanent [ethernet]

Your output will differ. Find the router (usually the .1 address): that's the MAC address your computer puts in the frame whenever it wants to leave the LAN. The ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff line is the broadcast address. To see your own MAC address and learn which hardware port is which:

$ networksetup -listallhardwareports          # macOS
Hardware Port: Wi-Fi
Device: en0
Ethernet Address: a4:83:e7:1c:52:2f

Hardware Port: Thunderbolt Bridge
Device: bridge0
Ethernet Address: (not shown)

> getmac /v                                      # Windows

On Linux, ip link prints each interface with its link/ether address. Look at the second hex digit of the first byte of your Wi-Fi address: if it is 2, 6, A or E, your device is probably using a randomised "private" address for this network, as described above. You can usually turn that behaviour on or off in the Wi-Fi settings for each network.

Distributed systems connection

In a data center, hundreds of servers sit in racks, and each rack has a top-of-rack switch that connects its servers with fast links (commonly 10, 25 or 100 Gbps). Traffic between two servers in the same rack crosses one switch; traffic to another rack crosses several layers of switches; traffic to another data center crosses routers and long-distance fiber. Each step up adds latency and often reduces the bandwidth available per server.

Roughly speaking, a round trip within a rack takes tens of microseconds, across a data center a few hundred microseconds, and between distant data centers tens of milliseconds, a difference of three or more orders of magnitude, and a direct consequence of chapter 1's speed-of-light limit. That's why distributed systems care where machines are: a replica in the same rack is cheap to ask, one on another continent is not. Designers deliberately spread copies across racks and data centers for failure protection, and pay for it in latency.

Key takeaways

  • A LAN is a group of devices that reach each other directly at the link layer. Ethernet is the standard wired technology; Wi-Fi is the wireless one.
  • A shared medium causes collisions. Early Ethernet used CSMA/CD (listen, send, detect, back off); Wi-Fi uses CSMA/CA (avoid and acknowledge).
  • A hub repeats everything to everyone; a switch forwards each frame only to the right port, after learning MAC-to-port mappings from source addresses and flooding when the destination is unknown or broadcast.
  • A MAC address is 48 bits (six hex pairs). The first half identifies the manufacturer (OUI). It names a network card on a LAN, not a place in the world, and can be changed or randomised.
  • An Ethernet frame is: destination MAC, source MAC, type, payload (46–1,500 bytes), FCS check value.
  • Wi-Fi is slower and less reliable than cable because the air is shared, signals fade and every frame needs an acknowledgement.
  • Finding a MAC from an IP is ARP (chapter 6); leaving the LAN needs a router (chapter 5).

Check your understanding

A switch receives a frame whose destination MAC address is not in its table. What does it do?

  1. Drops the frame because it can't deliver it
  2. Sends it out of every port except the one it arrived on
  3. Sends it back to the sender with an error
  4. Asks the router for the right port
Unknown destinations are flooded to all other ports. The right device will accept the frame, others ignore it, and when the reply comes back the switch learns the missing entry from its source address.

How does a switch learn which port a device is on?

  1. An administrator types the table in by hand
  2. It reads the destination MAC address of frames it receives
  3. Devices register themselves with the router
  4. It records the source MAC address of each frame against the port the frame arrived on
The source address in an incoming frame tells the switch "this device is reachable through the port I just heard it on". The destination address is what the switch uses to look up where to forward, not to learn.

Which statement about MAC addresses is correct?

  1. They are 48 bits and identify a network interface on a local network
  2. They are 32 bits and identify a computer anywhere on the Internet
  3. They never change, so they safely identify a person's device
  4. They are assigned by your Internet provider
MAC addresses are 6 bytes and only meaningful on one LAN; routers rewrite them at each hop. They can be changed in software, and phones commonly randomise them. The 32-bit address that works across the Internet is the IPv4 address (chapter 4).

Why does Wi-Fi use collision avoidance with acknowledgements, rather than collision detection like old shared Ethernet?

  1. Because Wi-Fi frames are too large to check
  2. Because Wi-Fi is always full duplex
  3. Because a radio generally can't listen for a clash while it is transmitting, so it must confirm success afterwards
  4. Because wireless signals never collide
Detecting collisions requires hearing the medium while sending, which radios can't do well on one frequency. So Wi-Fi listens first, waits a random backoff, and relies on a returned acknowledgement to know a frame arrived.

What is the maximum size of the payload in a standard Ethernet frame?

  1. 46 bytes
  2. 1,500 bytes
  3. 1,518 bytes
  4. 65,535 bytes
The payload limit (MTU) is 1,500 bytes. 46 is the minimum; 1,518 is the maximum size of the whole frame including the 14-byte header and 4-byte FCS; 65,535 is the largest an IPv4 packet can theoretically be, which is why bigger data must be split up.
