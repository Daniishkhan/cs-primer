Patterns & Where to Go Next

You now have the foundations, from electrical signals to consensus. This last chapter is a guided tour of the patterns engineers build on top of them, a map of everything you've learned, and a plan for what to do next.

Why this matters

Real systems are not built by inventing protocols from scratch. They are assembled from a small vocabulary of well-worn patterns, each one a response to a problem you now understand: unreliable networks, slow or dead machines, disagreeing copies. Knowing the names and the trade-offs lets you read an architecture diagram, join a design discussion, and know which book to open next.

Patterns for building on top of the network

None of what follows is new physics. Each pattern is a clever arrangement of tools from earlier chapters: timeouts and retries (16), replication (18), partitioning (19), consensus (20). We'll take them in roughly the order you'd meet them.

Message queues

A message queue is a durable waiting line between a producer (who has work) and a consumer (who does it). The producer drops a message in the queue and moves on; a worker takes it out when ready. This decouples them in time (the worker can be down for a minute without the producer noticing) and in speed (a burst of 10,000 orders is buffered and worked off steadily). Add more workers and the work spreads automatically.

The catch is the delivery guarantee from chapter 16. If a worker takes a message and crashes before finishing, the queue must give it to someone else, so most queues promise at-least-once delivery: nothing is lost, but a message may arrive twice. The standard cure is an idempotent consumer: handling the same message twice must have the same effect as handling it once (for example by recording each message's ID and skipping repeats).

Publish/subscribe

In publish/subscribe a message goes to a named topic and every subscriber gets its own copy. The publisher doesn't know who's listening. A "new order" event can feed billing, shipping, analytics and email independently, and adding a fifth listener requires no change to the publisher. A queue shares work among consumers; pub/sub broadcasts facts to all of them.

The log: one abstraction to rule them

Systems such as Apache Kafka are built around a very simple structure: an append-only log. Producers add records to the end; records are never edited, only appended; every record has a number (its offset). Consumers read from any offset they like and each remembers its own position, so many different readers can consume the same data at different speeds, and a new reader can replay history from the beginning.

If this looks familiar, it should. The log is chapter 20's replicated log with a different job. A big log is split into partitions (chapter 19, usually by hashing a key so related records stay together and in order), and each partition is replicated (chapter 18) to survive failures. A great deal of modern data infrastructure is, at heart, "agree on a log and let everyone derive their own view from it".

Event-driven architecture

In an event-driven design, services announce facts that happened ("OrderPlaced", "PaymentReceived") instead of calling each other directly, and other services react. It keeps services loosely coupled and makes it easy to add new behaviour. The price is that the flow of a business process is scattered across many reactions, and everything is eventually consistent (chapter 18): after "OrderPlaced", the email service might lag the billing service by a few seconds. Some teams go further and store the events themselves as the source of truth (event sourcing): current state is whatever you get by replaying the log.

Sagas: workflows without distributed transactions

Suppose booking a trip means reserving a seat (service A), charging a card (service B) and issuing a ticket (service C), each with its own database. How do you make all three succeed or none? The textbook answer is two-phase commit (2PC): a coordinator asks everyone "can you commit?" (prepare), and if all vote yes it says "commit". It works, but if the coordinator crashes after participants voted yes, they are stuck holding locks, unable to commit or abort without it. It blocks, a bad fit for services that fail independently. (Consensus-based commit protocols exist, but are heavier.)

A saga takes a different approach: a chain of ordinary local transactions, where each step has a compensating action that semantically undoes it. If a later step fails, run the compensations for earlier steps in reverse. You never hold locks across services, but you accept that other people can briefly see the half-finished state, and that compensations must themselves be idempotent and retried until they succeed.

Circuit breakers and bulkheads

When a downstream service is struggling, calling it harder (retries!) makes things worse. A circuit breaker watches the failure rate of calls to a dependency. When it crosses a threshold the breaker "opens": calls fail immediately without touching the sick service, giving it room to recover. After a pause it lets a few trial calls through ("half-open"); if they succeed, it closes again. Like the electrical device, it trades some failures now for avoiding a meltdown. Bulkheads (named after the sealed compartments of a ship's hull) limit the damage differently: give each dependency its own separate pool of threads or connections, so one slow dependency can't use up everything and sink the whole service.

Rate limiting and backpressure

Every system has a capacity; the question is what happens beyond it. Rate limiting caps how many requests a client can make (a common method is the token bucket: tokens refill at a steady rate and each request spends one), answering excess requests quickly with HTTP 429 "Too Many Requests" (chapter 10). Backpressure is the broader principle that a slow consumer must be able to tell its producers to slow down, instead of letting an unbounded queue grow until memory runs out. You've met it already: TCP's flow control (chapter 8) is backpressure between two machines.

Observability: seeing inside a system you can't stand inside

With dozens of services, "why is this page slow?" can't be answered by logging into one machine. Teams rely on three kinds of signals:

Logs

Timestamped records of discrete events. Great for "what exactly happened to this request?" Costly in bulk.

Metrics

Numbers over time: request rate, error rate, latency percentiles (chapter 13), queue depth. Cheap, good for dashboards and alerts.

Traces

The path of one request across many services, with the time spent in each. Made possible by passing a unique trace ID along with every call.

Traces matter because latency adds up across hops (chapter 13), and the slow hop is rarely the one you suspect. And since clocks on different machines disagree (chapter 17), good tracing relies on parent/child relationships between calls, not just on comparing timestamps.

Analogy: learning a city by walking it

You started at the front door with a single packet, and walked street by street until you could see the whole city: its roads, its postal system, its traffic rules and finally the council meetings where the city decides things. Now, whenever someone mentions an unfamiliar neighbourhood, you know roughly which direction it lies in.

Where it breaks down: a city map stays still. This field keeps rebuilding itself, so the best map is the one you keep redrawing as you learn.

Projects that make it stick

Reading gets you perhaps half way. The other half comes from building something that breaks. Here is a ladder of projects, roughly in order of difficulty:

1. A chat server with sockets

One server, many clients, in a language you like (Python or Go are friendly). Use TCP sockets directly (chapters 7 and 12). Add usernames, a simple message format, and handle clients disconnecting mid-message. You'll meet framing and partial reads quickly.

2. Watch it in Wireshark

Capture traffic while your chat server runs, and find the TCP handshake, your messages and the connection teardown. Then capture a DNS lookup and a TLS handshake. (wireshark.org)

3. A key-value store with replication

Write a tiny store with get and put. Add a second and third copy with a leader that forwards writes. Then kill a process and see what your reads return. Add timeouts, retries and an idempotency key (chapter 16).

4. Add partitioning

Split your store across several nodes with a consistent-hash ring (chapter 19). Measure how many keys move when you add a node. Add virtual nodes and compare.

5. Implement Raft

Follow the paper's summary figure, one piece at a time: first leader election, then log replication, then persistence. It's hard, and it's exactly where chapter 20 turns from understanding into mastery.

6. MIT 6.5840 (formerly 6.824)

MIT's distributed systems course has publicly available lectures, readings and programming labs (MapReduce, Raft, a fault-tolerant key-value service, sharding). Doing the labs is the single best structured path from this guide into real distributed systems engineering.

You started this guide not knowing what a packet was. Distributed systems connection

Everything in this chapter is the answer to one sentence from chapter 15: "the network is not reliable, and machines fail independently." Queues absorb it, logs record it, sagas cope with it, circuit breakers contain it and observability lets you see it. The deep tools underneath (retries, clocks, replication, partitioning, consensus) are all you need to understand any new system you meet.

Key takeaways

  • Queues decouple and buffer (at-least-once plus idempotent consumers); pub/sub broadcasts to many; a log is an ordered, replayable, partitioned and replicated history.
  • Sagas use compensating actions in place of distributed transactions, because two-phase commit can block when its coordinator dies.
  • Circuit breakers, bulkheads, rate limits and backpressure stop one overloaded part from taking down the whole system.
  • Logs, metrics and traces let you see inside a system; tracing follows one request across services.
  • The same ideas recur at every level: acknowledgements and retries, TTLs and versions, numbering for order, and overlapping majorities.
  • The best next step is to build: a socket chat server, a replicated key-value store, then Raft.

Check your understanding

A queue delivers a message to a worker, which crashes before acknowledging it, so the queue delivers it to another worker. What property do the workers need so that this is safe?

  1. Synchronized wall-clock time
  2. A higher Raft term than the queue
  3. Idempotent handling, so processing the same message twice has the same effect as once
  4. A larger MTU
At-least-once delivery means duplicates are possible, so the consumer must tolerate them (chapter 16). Clocks, terms and MTU are unrelated to the problem.

Why do sagas use compensating actions instead of two-phase commit?

  1. 2PC can block with locks held if the coordinator fails, whereas sagas never hold locks across services
  2. 2PC is not able to commit more than one step
  3. Compensating actions are always instant and atomic
  4. Sagas guarantee nobody ever sees intermediate states
Sagas trade isolation for availability: intermediate states can be visible, and compensations must be retried, but there's no blocking global lock. 2PC does commit multiple steps; its weakness is blocking.

Which pair of ideas is the closest parallel?

  1. DNS TTL and TCP's three-way handshake
  2. MAC addresses and Raft terms
  3. Ports and consistent hashing
  4. TCP retransmission after a timeout and an application retrying a failed request
Both are "no acknowledgement within a timeout, so try again", and both raise the same duplicate problem, which is why idempotency matters. The other pairings compare unrelated mechanisms.

A circuit breaker is "open" for a failing dependency. What does it do?

  1. Retries each call ten times as fast as possible
  2. Fails calls immediately without contacting the dependency, giving it time to recover
  3. Copies every request to a second data center
  4. Encrypts all traffic to the dependency
An open breaker fails fast. After a pause it allows trial calls (half-open) and closes again if they succeed. Hammering a struggling service with retries is exactly what it prevents.

A Kafka-style log topic is split into partitions, each replicated across machines. Which earlier chapters' ideas does this combine?

  1. Subnetting and ARP
  2. Ethernet frames and TLS
  3. Partitioning (chapter 19) and replicated logs / replication (chapters 18 and 20)
  4. HTTP caching and DNS
Splitting the log by key is partitioning; keeping several copies of each partition's log in agreement is replication, ultimately a replicated-log problem like Raft's.

A user's request passes through six services and is slow. What helps most to find the slow hop?

  1. A single log file on the first service
  2. A distributed trace that follows one request's trace ID through all six services
  3. Increasing the TCP window on every machine
  4. Adding a seventh service
Tracing shows where one request spent its time across services. Logs on one service see only that service, and the other options don't address the question.
