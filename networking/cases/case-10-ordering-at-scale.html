Ordering at Scale: Spanner & the Log

Chapter 17 ended with a warning: don't trust wall clocks to say what happened first. This case study looks at two serious engineering answers to "how do we give a whole planet's worth of machines one agreed order of events?" One answer builds a better clock and admits how wrong it might be. The other drops clocks and uses a numbered list that everyone reads in the same order.

The pattern in one sentence

Global order comes from somewhere: either from clocks trustworthy enough to compare across machines (Google's Spanner), or from a sequencer, a log that decides the order by the position in which it appends things (the idea behind Kafka and replicated state machines).

We use chapters 17 (clocks, happens-before), 18 (linearizability), 20 (Paxos groups, the replicated log) and 21 (the log as an abstraction). Two primary sources anchor the story: the Spanner paper (Corbett et al., OSDI 2012) and Jay Kreps' essay on the log (LinkedIn Engineering, 2013).

Why order is the hard part

Suppose you transfer money, then your bank statement is generated. If the statement is built from a view of the data that includes only part of the transfer, or that sees the transfer on one shard but not the other, the numbers don't add up. To keep things sensible, a database wants external consistency (the Spanner paper's phrase, which it says is equivalent to linearizability, chapter 18): if transaction T1 commits before T2 starts, then T1 must be ordered before T2, as seen by everyone.

On a single machine, that's easy: one clock, one CPU. Across data centers there are two broad ways to get there. Keep the idea of "timestamp", but make timestamps trustworthy. Or give up on physical time, and let an ordered log supply the numbers.

Part 1: Spanner and the honest clock

The cast

Spanner
Google's globally distributed database. The paper describes it as "multi-version, globally-distributed, and synchronously-replicated", and as the first system to offer externally consistent distributed transactions at global scale.
Paxos groups
Data is split into shards. Each shard is replicated by a Paxos group (chapter 20): a leader plus other replicas that agree on a log of writes. The paper says writes "must initiate the Paxos protocol at the leader".
Two-phase commit across groups
A transaction touching several shards runs two-phase commit between the groups, and each group's part is itself made durable through Paxos. The paper argues that "running two-phase commit over Paxos mitigates the availability problems" that make people wary of it.
TrueTime
A clock API that admits uncertainty. Its heart is one call, TT.now(), which returns an interval [earliest, latest] that is guaranteed to contain the true, absolute time at which it was called.

Time as an interval

Chapter 17 showed that every computer's clock is a little wrong. Spanner's move is to quantify the wrongness. Instead of "it is 12:00:00.000", TrueTime says "it is somewhere between 11:59:59.996 and 12:00:00.004". The paper also offers TT.after(t) ("true if t has definitely passed") and TT.before(t).

To keep the interval narrow, Google uses two kinds of time reference, because they fail differently. Each data center has time master machines: most have GPS receivers with dedicated antennas, and the rest ("Armageddon masters") carry atomic clocks. The paper explains that GPS can fail through antenna problems, radio interference or outages, while atomic clocks "can fail in ways uncorrelated to GPS", though they drift over long periods. Every machine runs a daemon that polls several masters, rejects liars, and between polls advertises a slowly growing uncertainty based on worst-case drift.

The numbers the paper gives: the uncertainty bound it calls ε (half the interval's width) is typically a sawtooth, "varying from about 1 to 7 ms" over each poll interval, so about 4 ms "most of the time". The daemon polls every 30 seconds, and the applied worst-case drift rate is 200 microseconds per second. In the introduction the authors say the implementation keeps uncertainty "generally less than 10ms".

Commit wait: pay the uncertainty back

Here is the clever part. Spanner gives each write transaction a commit timestamp s, and two rules guarantee that timestamps respect real-time order:

  1. Start: pick s no smaller than TT.now().latest, taken when the commit request arrives at the coordinator.
  2. Commit wait: do not let anyone see the data until TT.after(s) is true, meaning s is definitely in the past.
Step through

One commit with commit wait

  1. Ask the clock. The coordinator calls TT.now() and gets an interval that is guaranteed to contain the real time of the call. The width of the interval is 2ε.
  2. Pick the timestamp. It sets s to the interval's latest end. Whatever the true time was, s is at least as late.
  3. Commit wait. It holds the result back until TT.after(s) is true. The paper notes that because s was chosen from latest, "the expected wait is at least 2ε", and that this wait is "typically overlapped with Paxos communication".
  4. Visible, and ordered. Only now do clients see the commit. Any transaction T2 that starts after that moment gets an interval whose latest end is later than s, so T2's timestamp is larger. If T1 finishes before T2 starts in real time, T1's timestamp is smaller.

Read that again, because the trick is subtle. Spanner never needs to know the exact time. It only needs to know a bound on how wrong it can be, and to wait for that long. Uncertainty is converted into latency. The paper's wording is that if the uncertainty is large, "Spanner slows down to wait out that uncertainty". Better clocks mean shorter waits; the correctness doesn't change.

What does it cost in practice? In the paper's one-replica latency experiments, commit wait was about 5 ms and Paxos latency about 9 ms. Those figures come from Google's test setup in 2012, so they say how the cost is shaped, not what you would pay elsewhere.

Analogy: the notary with a foggy watch

A notary's watch might be up to 4 minutes off in either direction. To prove that document 2 was signed after document 1, she signs document 1 with the latest time her watch could be showing, then waits until even the most pessimistic reading of her watch passes that mark before accepting document 2. Nobody can then claim 2 came first.

Where it breaks down: a notary just waits. Spanner must also keep its error bound honest. If a machine's clock drifts further than TrueTime promised, the guarantee quietly fails. That's why the paper describes evicting machines whose clocks look broken, and why the time masters cross-check each other.

What else the timestamps buy

Because timestamps are globally meaningful, Spanner can do a read of the whole database at a chosen timestamp, a consistent snapshot, without locks, even while transactions are running. The paper credits the same machinery for consistent backups, consistent MapReduce runs and atomic schema updates "at global scale". The price is that the whole design leans on a hardware-and-software time service that few organisations run.

Part 2: the log as a sequencer

Now the opposite instinct. Jay Kreps, writing about his experience building data infrastructure at LinkedIn, describes the log as "perhaps the simplest possible storage abstraction": an "append-only, totally-ordered sequence of records ordered by time". Records are added at the end; each gets a unique, sequential entry number.

The key observation is about those numbers. Kreps points out that the entry number works as a kind of timestamp, and that it "is decoupled from any particular physical clock", a property he calls essential for distributed systems. The log doesn't need to know what time it is. It only needs one place that decides who is next. That place is a sequencer, and in chapter 20's terms it is the leader of a replicated log.

From a log to a replicated state machine

Kreps states what he calls the State Machine Replication Principle: "If two identical, deterministic processes begin in the same state and get the same inputs in the same order, they will produce the same output and end in the same state." That is chapter 20's replicated-log idea: you don't replicate state, you agree on the order of the inputs and let every replica replay them. Notice what "deterministic" excludes: any dependence on the wall clock. A process that calls gettimeofday mid-computation, he notes, isn't deterministic in this sense.

The same log then serves many readers. Each subscribing system "reads from this log as quickly as it can, applies each new record to its own store, and advances its position in the log". A search index, a cache and a data warehouse can each derive their own view from one ordered history, and the log number gives all of them a common "point in time" to compare against.

Scaling the log: partitions

A single sequencer has a ceiling: one leader can only append so fast. To scale, Kafka (the system Kreps and colleagues built) cuts a log into partitions, using chapter 19's ideas. Kreps writes that "each partition is a totally ordered log, but there is no global ordering between partitions". Writers choose the partition, most often by key such as a user id. The Apache Kafka introduction states the guarantee the same way: events with the same key go to the same partition, and "any consumer of a given topic-partition will always read that partition's events in exactly the same order" as they were written.

Each consumer tracks its own position. Kafka's design documentation says the position of a consumer in each partition "is just a single integer" (the offset of the next message to read), and that "a consumer can deliberately rewind back to an old offset and re-consume data". That makes the log replayable: a new service, or a service that had a bug, can read history again from the start.

Kreps is straightforward about the limit. The lack of a global order across partitions "is a limitation, but we have not found it to be a major one", because the interactions come from many independent processes, so a total order over all of them isn't usually meaningful. What is guaranteed is that each partition is order-preserving, and that appends to one partition from a single sender are delivered in the order sent.

Common misconception: "Kafka keeps everything in order"

It keeps each partition in order. If two events must be processed in order relative to one another, they need to land in the same partition, which usually means giving them the same key (for instance one customer id). Events for different keys, in different partitions, have no defined relative order. If you need a single order over everything, you are back to one partition, with one sequencer and one throughput limit.

Comparing the two

These aren't rivals so much as answers to different questions. The table below states what the sources say, plus a few general engineering observations (marked "general").

Clock-based (Spanner)Sequencer / log (Kafka-style)
Where order comes fromTimestamps from bounded-uncertainty clocksPosition in an append-only log
What you must trustThe clock error bound. The guarantee depends on it (the paper)One leader per log to pick the order, replicated for failures (chapter 20)
Cost of orderingCommit wait, about the clock uncertainty, per write transaction; GPS and atomic clock hardwareAppends are cheap; the cost is that a global order means a single sequencer (general)
Scope of orderGlobal, across shards, for transactions (external consistency)Total within a partition; none between partitions
What it is good forGeneral transactions over data that is sharded worldwideEvent streams, feeding many derived views, replication (general)
If something goes wrongLarge clock uncertainty slows writes; broken clocks could break ordering, so machines with bad clocks are evictedLeader failure triggers election; a partition can be briefly unavailable (general)

Choosing, in general terms. If you need transactions that span many shards, with every client seeing a single history that respects real time, the clock approach gets you that without a global bottleneck, if you can operate the clock infrastructure. If your problem is a stream of facts that several systems should consume in a consistent order, the log is simpler, cheaper and replayable, and per-key order is often all you need. If your problem demands one total order over unrelated events, either approach will be limited somewhere: the clock approach by waiting, the log by one sequencer.

Go deeper: they're built from each other optional

The two ideas are less opposed than they look. Inside Spanner, every shard is a Paxos group, and Paxos is how a replicated log is agreed (chapter 20): the paper says each replica runs a Paxos state machine, and a transaction's two-phase-commit steps are logged through Paxos. So Spanner uses logs to order writes within a shard and clocks to order transactions across shards. Kreps knew of the system: his essay's reading list mentions Spanner as a database that "tries to use physical time and models the uncertainty of clock drift directly by treating the timestamp as a range".

Try it yourself

A twenty-line partitioned log

This toy program (Python 3, no network) appends keyed events to a two-partition log, then lets two consumers each read from their own offsets. It is not Kafka (Kafka's real partitioner uses a different hash), but it shows the shape: order per key, offsets as positions, and replay.

$ cat log.py
import zlib

class Log:
    """A tiny partitioned, append-only log."""
    def __init__(self, partitions=2):
        self.parts = [[] for _ in range(partitions)]

    def append(self, key, value):
        p = zlib.crc32(key.encode()) % len(self.parts)   # same key -> same partition
        self.parts[p].append((key, value))
        return p, len(self.parts[p]) - 1                 # (partition, offset)

log = Log()
for key, value in [("alice", "add book"), ("bob", "add pen"), ("alice", "add mug"),
                   ("bob", "remove pen"), ("alice", "checkout")]:
    print(key, value, "->", log.append(key, value))

# Two consumers, each remembering its own offset per partition.
offsets = {"billing": [0, 0], "search": [0, 0]}
for name, pos in offsets.items():
    for p, records in enumerate(log.parts):
        while pos[p] < len(records):
            print(name, "reads partition", p, "offset", pos[p], records[pos[p]])
            pos[p] += 1
$ python3 log.py
alice add book -> (1, 0)
bob add pen -> (0, 0)
alice add mug -> (1, 1)
bob remove pen -> (0, 1)
alice checkout -> (1, 2)
billing reads partition 0 offset 0 ('bob', 'add pen')
billing reads partition 0 offset 1 ('bob', 'remove pen')
billing reads partition 1 offset 0 ('alice', 'add book')
billing reads partition 1 offset 1 ('alice', 'add mug')
billing reads partition 1 offset 2 ('alice', 'checkout')
search reads partition 0 offset 0 ('bob', 'add pen')
search reads partition 0 offset 1 ('bob', 'remove pen')
search reads partition 1 offset 0 ('alice', 'add book')
search reads partition 1 offset 1 ('alice', 'add mug')
search reads partition 1 offset 2 ('alice', 'checkout')

Notice that Alice's events are in order and Bob's events are in order, but the program says nothing about whether "bob add pen" came before or after "alice add book". Both consumers saw identical per-partition order because both just walked the same lists, which is state machine replication in miniature. Try changing partitions=2 to 1: now everything has one total order, and you have built the bottleneck as well as the guarantee.

Distributed systems connection

Chapter 17 said you cannot ask "what time is it?" and get a trustworthy answer across machines, so you either track causality (vector clocks, as in case study 9's Dynamo) or you manufacture order. This case study shows the two ways to manufacture it. Spanner narrows physical time until waiting out the uncertainty is cheap enough. The log skips physical time and lets a leader's append order be the order, which is chapter 20's consensus put to work. Dynamo (case study 9) sits at the other extreme: it accepts that no single order exists and spends effort merging.

Key takeaways

  • External consistency (linearizability): if T1 commits before T2 starts, T1's timestamp is smaller.
  • TrueTime returns an interval [earliest, latest] guaranteed to contain true time; GPS and atomic clocks keep it narrow (the paper: typically ε of about 1 to 7 ms, generally under 10 ms).
  • Commit wait converts clock uncertainty into latency: wait until the chosen timestamp is definitely in the past before revealing a commit.
  • Each Spanner shard is a Paxos group; transactions across shards use two-phase commit over Paxos.
  • A log is an append-only, totally ordered sequence; its entry numbers act as timestamps independent of any physical clock.
  • State machine replication: same deterministic process, same inputs, same order, same result.
  • Kafka-style logs scale by partitioning: order within a partition, none between partitions; consumers keep their own offsets and can rewind.

Check your understanding

What does TrueTime's TT.now() return?

  1. The exact current time, accurate to the microsecond
  2. An interval [earliest, latest] guaranteed to contain the actual time of the call
  3. A counter that increases with every call
  4. The time according to the nearest GPS satellite
TrueTime exposes uncertainty instead of hiding it. Spanner's correctness relies on the guarantee that real time lies inside the interval.

Why does Spanner perform "commit wait"?

  1. To give the Paxos replicas time to elect a leader
  2. To let GPS receivers resynchronise
  3. To allow other transactions to finish first
  4. So the commit timestamp is definitely in the past before the commit is visible, making later transactions get larger timestamps
Waiting until TT.after(s) is true guarantees s is earlier than the real commit time. Any transaction starting afterwards then gets a larger timestamp, which is external consistency.

In a Kafka-style partitioned log, what ordering do you get?

  1. Events within one partition are read in the order they were written; there is no global order across partitions
  2. A single global order across all events
  3. No ordering at all
  4. Order by the producers' wall-clock timestamps
Each partition is a totally ordered log. Putting events that must stay in order under the same key puts them in the same partition.

Which statement best contrasts the two approaches?

  1. Spanner avoids all waiting; a log must always wait for clocks
  2. Both rely on GPS receivers
  3. Spanner derives order from bounded-uncertainty clocks at the price of commit wait; a log derives order from a sequencer's append position, at the price of a single leader per log
  4. A log orders events across partitions by wall-clock time
One pays in waiting and clock hardware, the other in a single ordering point per log. Neither is free; which suits you depends on whether you need cross-shard transactions or ordered streams.
