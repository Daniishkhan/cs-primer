Caches Inside Your ISP

When you press play on a Netflix show, the video very likely does not come from Netflix's data centers. It comes from a box that Netflix gave to your internet provider, sitting a few hops from your home, loaded with the show the night before. Why would a company put its servers inside other companies' networks, and why do those companies say yes?

The pattern in one sentence

Push content to the edge: when you can predict what people will want, copy it close to them in advance, during quiet hours, so that at peak time almost nothing has to cross the expensive long-distance links.

Where the facts come from

The Netflix details in this page come from Netflix's own Open Connect documents (listed under Sources). Sentences about how other CDNs work, or about the general pattern, are marked as general industry background.

The cast

Open Connect
Netflix's name for its content delivery network (CDN, chapter 14). Netflix says it started the program in 2011 and that it serves Netflix video using servers placed close to viewers.
OCA (Open Connect Appliance)
One of Netflix's purpose-built cache servers. According to Netflix, an OCA temporarily stores encoded video and image files and serves them over HTTP/HTTPS. It doesn't store viewer data such as viewing history or account details; it just stores and serves files.
ISP
The company you pay for internet access. Its network connects to the rest of the Internet through transit (a paid connection to a bigger network) and peering (a direct link with another network, often free).
IXP
An Internet Exchange Point: a building where many networks plug in next to each other to exchange traffic directly.
The control plane
Netflix software running in Amazon Web Services that tracks what every OCA holds, decides what to load, and decides which OCA each viewer should use. (The same "control plane" idea as in case study 6.)

Two ways to place the boxes

Netflix's overview says it deploys "thousands" of OCAs in two ways:

At exchange points

OCAs sit in IXPs in significant Netflix markets. ISPs that are present there connect to them with settlement-free peering, a direct link with no payment either way.

Inside the ISP

Netflix provides OCAs free of charge to qualifying ISPs, who host them in their own network: Netflix supplies the server hardware, and the ISP provides power, space and connectivity.

The second kind is the interesting one. A server inside the ISP is as close to you as content can get without being in your house, and the ISP decides which of its customers are routed to it. Netflix says ISPs work with it to configure BGP sessions (chapter 5) that steer traffic to the OCAs.

A day in the life of an OCA

Step through

Night fill, evening playback

  1. Plan. Netflix's control plane decides which files each OCA should hold. Netflix says it can "predict with high accuracy" what members will watch and when, and its control plane uses popularity algorithms to place content so that as much traffic as possible is served from the OCAs.
  2. Fill at night. Netflix describes deploying most content and software updates "proactively during off-peak fill windows" that are configurable. An ISP can set when the refresh happens so it doesn't add to peak load. OCAs can also copy from each other, which avoids using more "backbone" capacity.
  3. You press play. Your app contacts Netflix's playback services in AWS. These check your account and licence and work out which files your device needs. No video moves yet.
  4. The steering reply. A steering service picks OCAs based on what files they hold, their health, and their network proximity to you, then hands back URLs. This is how the client is steered: the application tells it exactly which server's URL to fetch.
  5. Video flows locally. Your device fetches from the OCA inside your ISP. The long-distance link carried the small request and reply, not the film.

How do they choose "nearby"?

Netflix's documents give two layers. First, the OCAs report to the control plane which BGP routes they have learned, which is how the ISP's routing choices reach the steering system. Second, a 2018 article by a Netflix partner-engagement manager describes the order in which the control plane compares candidates: longest prefix match, then shortest AS path, then lowest MED (a BGP preference value), then geolocation, with traffic balanced across appliances if everything ties. (You met "longest prefix wins" in chapter 5.) The same article says an embedded OCA's route has a shorter AS path than the route through an IXP, which is why embedded appliances are preferred. It is a 2018 description, so treat the details as a snapshot.

Compare this with anycast (case 1) and DNS steering (case 3). Both of those try to steer packets before the application knows anything. Netflix has a much stronger tool: the player already talks to an application service before any video flows, so that service can simply return the URL of a good server.

Why ISPs say yes

Hosting someone else's servers costs an ISP rack space and power. Why do it? Netflix's briefing paper lists the reasons:

Less transit

Traffic served from an OCA doesn't need to be fetched over a paid transit link. Netflix's paper says they avoid transit charges, and, for remote regions also avoid expensive international backhaul to reach a transit provider.

Less core traffic

With a nearby server, video doesn't have to cross the ISP's own core network either. Netflix cites several ISPs saying that caching lowers costs and improves customers' experience.

Fill costs almost nothing

Networks are built for their peak. Filling at off-peak hours means the capacity used is, in Netflix's words, "often without any cost to the ISP".

Control and quality

The ISP decides which customers are served from which OCA and when content is refreshed, and customers get less buffering, which Netflix argues helps ISPs keep them.

Netflix estimates in the same paper that the saving for ISPs that deploy embedded servers was $1.2 billion in 2020. Treat that as Netflix's own estimate in an advocacy document: it was written in the context of a debate about whether ISPs should charge content providers for traffic. The underlying logic is simple, though, and you can check it with arithmetic from chapter 13: if a thousand households in the evening each stream the same popular show over a transit link, the ISP pays for a thousand copies. With a cache inside, it pays for one copy, at night.

Push versus pull

Most CDNs you meet are pull-through caches, as described in chapter 14: an edge server starts empty, and the first user who asks for a file causes a cache miss, so the edge fetches the file from the origin and keeps a copy. This is general industry background, and it is simple and works for any content.

Pull-through cache (typical CDN)Proactive push (Open Connect)
Who decides what's cachedWhoever asks firstThe operator's control plane, using forecasts

Netflix's paper makes the same comparison: it says Netflix is "uniquely able to pre-position copies of all content in advance" because it has a defined, limited catalogue, whereas "many content providers store a copy of a file only after it has been requested by a user, which may occur during peak hours." A live sports stream or a web page that changes every minute can't be pre-positioned this way, because the content doesn't exist yet.

Analogy: the corner shop restocked overnight

A pull cache is a shop that orders an item the first time someone asks for it; the first customer waits. A pushed cache is a shop whose supplier studies what the neighbourhood will want tomorrow and restocks it overnight, when the roads are empty. The shelves are ready at opening time.

Where it breaks down: a shop's shelf space is physical and small; an OCA holds a lot, but it still can't hold everything, so popularity forecasting decides what earns space. Also, a real shop can't make a delivery vanish when it fails; OCAs report their health, and the control plane routes around the broken ones.

Common misconception: "the ISP is a Netflix customer here"

Embedded OCAs are provided free to qualifying ISPs, and Netflix says it isn't a sale: the appliances aren't for sale to other parties. The deal works because each side saves something: Netflix gets short, high-quality paths to its viewers, and the ISP carries less traffic.

What can go wrong

  • Cold or missing content. In general, if a viewer needs a file the nearby appliance doesn't hold, it has to come from somewhere farther away, which is slower and uses long-distance capacity. Netflix says its steering takes file availability into account, which is one reason fill planning matters.
  • A failed appliance. Netflix's briefing paper says CDNs are more resilient because, if one element fails, traffic can be redirected to other servers. Netflix also says ISPs with embedded OCAs use peering for resiliency.
  • Fill timing is a trade-off. Filling needs spare bandwidth, so the window matters. Netflix's documents describe it as configurable, and the ISP's control over timing is one of the benefits it advertises.

Try it yourself

See where a big site's content comes from

You can't easily see Netflix's OCAs from outside, but you can see the edge-caching idea in action on any CDN-fronted site. These commands are read-only. Your output will differ.

$ curl -sI https://www.example.com | head -n 12   # response headers only
HTTP/2 200
content-type: text/html
age: 4310
cache-control: max-age=604800
...
$ traceroute -n www.example.com              # how far away is the answering server?
  • An age header means the response came out of a cache that has held it for that many seconds (if the site sends one). cache-control: max-age says how long it may be kept. This is the pull-through pattern from chapter 14.
  • Open Connect publishes a page for ISPs with details of how the program works; it is aimed at network engineers but readable. See the first source below.

Distributed systems connection

Open Connect is replication with a planned placement policy: it copies data to many places before it is needed, which turns a hard latency problem into a scheduling problem (chapter 18). Replicas of immutable files, such as a movie, never conflict, so none of the hard consistency issues from the later chapters arise. Try the same design for a changing document and everything gets harder: when the original changes, every copy is stale. Cache invalidation, and the TTL trade-off from chapter 9, is what you would be back to.

Key takeaways

  • Open Connect puts caches (OCAs) at internet exchanges and inside ISP networks, close to viewers.
  • Content is loaded proactively, mostly in configurable off-peak windows, guided by popularity forecasts; a pull-through cache loads on the first miss.
  • When you press play, Netflix's services reply with the URLs of OCAs chosen by file availability, health and network proximity; the ISP's BGP announcements control which customers an OCA serves.
  • ISPs host the boxes because they cut transit and core traffic, fill during quiet hours, and give customers a smoother experience.
  • Pre-positioning works because the catalogue is limited and demand is predictable; it doesn't fit live or fast-changing content.

Check your understanding

What is the main difference between a pull-through CDN cache and Open Connect's approach?

  1. A pull-through cache is located inside the ISP; Open Connect is not
  2. A pull-through cache fills when someone first asks; Open Connect pre-positions content, mostly off-peak, based on forecasts
  3. A pull-through cache uses UDP; Open Connect uses TCP
  4. There is no difference
Pull caches wait for a miss, which may happen at peak time. Open Connect's control plane decides in advance what each appliance should hold and loads it in configurable off-peak windows.

Why does an ISP benefit from hosting an OCA?

  1. Netflix pays a fee for each gigabyte served
  2. It lets the ISP read its customers' viewing history
  3. It makes the ISP's network use less electricity than any other server
  4. Popular video is served locally instead of arriving over paid transit and through the core network, and filling happens at quiet times
The savings come from avoided transit and core traffic. OCAs don't store viewer data, and Netflix provides the appliances free to qualifying ISPs, who supply power, space and connectivity.

How does the Netflix app learn which OCA to fetch video from?

  1. Netflix's steering service returns URLs of OCAs chosen using file availability, health and network proximity
  2. The app pings every OCA in the world and chooses the fastest
  3. The ISP edits the app's settings
  4. The viewer picks a server from a list
The playback services check authorisation, then a steering service builds URLs for suitable OCAs. Because there is an application-level conversation first, no routing trick is needed to find a good server.

Which kind of content would be a poor fit for overnight pre-positioning?

  1. A film released last month
  2. An episode that comes out at a known time
  3. A live event that is only being created as it happens
  4. A large file that many people want tomorrow
You can't copy in advance something that doesn't exist yet. Proactive fill works for a limited catalogue with predictable demand; live or constantly changing content needs different techniques.
