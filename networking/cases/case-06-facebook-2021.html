When the Map Disappears: Facebook, October 2021

On 4 October 2021, Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp vanished from the Internet for about six hours. No server was hacked and no data center burned down. A routine maintenance command cut Facebook's private network, a safety mechanism did exactly what it was designed to do, and the tools engineers needed to fix it were on the wrong side of the outage.

The pattern in one sentence

Control-plane dependencies: the systems that steer and repair a network must not depend on the network they steer and repair, or one failure can take away both the service and the means to fix it.

The cast

You've met every piece of this story already. Here is how they fit together at Facebook (which now operates as Meta):

The backbone
Facebook's private network of fiber links connecting its data centers to each other and to its smaller edge sites around the world. Meta describes it as "tens of thousands of miles" of fiber.
Edge DNS servers
The authoritative nameservers for facebook.com and friends (chapter 9) run in Facebook's smaller edge facilities, close to users.
BGP announcements
Those edge sites announce, using BGP (chapter 5), "the IP addresses of our DNS servers can be reached through us". That's how every router on the Internet knows where to send DNS questions for Facebook.
A health check
Each DNS site keeps checking whether it can reach Facebook's data centers. If it can't, it concludes its own connection is unhealthy and withdraws its BGP announcement, so users get sent to a healthier site instead.

That last behaviour is good engineering. If one edge site loses its link to the data centers, you don't want it answering DNS and sending users towards a dead end. Withdrawing the route quietly moves traffic to other sites. This is the anycast pattern from case study 1, working as intended.

What happened, step by step

Step through

How Facebook disappeared

  1. A normal day. Your phone asks a DNS resolver for facebook.com. The resolver asks Facebook's authoritative DNS servers, which it can find because Facebook's edge sites announce the route to them with BGP. Behind the edge sites, the backbone links them to the data centers.
  2. ~15:40 UTC: the backbone goes down. During routine maintenance, a command meant to assess backbone capacity unintentionally took down all the backbone connections. A built-in audit tool should have blocked the command, but a bug in it let the command through. The data centers are now cut off from each other and from the edge.
  3. The safety mechanism fires everywhere at once. Every edge DNS site runs its health check, can't reach any data center, and concludes "my connection is unhealthy". Each one withdraws its BGP announcement. Individually that's the right call. But all of them made it at the same moment, so there was no healthy site left to take the traffic.
  4. The Internet forgets the way. Within minutes, routers worldwide drop the routes to Facebook's DNS addresses. The DNS servers are still running but can't be reached. Resolvers return SERVFAIL ("I couldn't get an answer"). Apps and people retry, and retry again. Cloudflare reported public resolvers handling around 30 times their usual query volume.
  5. Locked out. Engineers could not reach the data centers through their normal means because those networks were down, and Meta says its out-of-band network access was down too. The loss of DNS also broke many of the internal tools normally used to investigate outages like this. So people had to travel to the data centers, where hardened physical security, deliberately hard to bypass, slowed things further.
  6. ~21:00–21:30 UTC: recovery, gradually. Once the backbone was restored, routes came back and DNS answered again. Meta then brought services back in stages rather than all at once. Their data centers' power draw had dropped sharply, and a sudden surge could have tripped electrical systems or overwhelmed cold caches.

Common misconception: "Facebook's DNS servers crashed"

They didn't. Meta's own write-up says the DNS servers "became unreachable even though they were still operational." Many of Facebook's other IP addresses were still being announced too. They were just useless, because nobody could look up which address to use. DNS is the map; the territory was still there, but nobody could read the map.

The pattern: data plane vs control plane

Engineers split a network (or any large system) into two parts:

Data plane

The machinery that does the actual work: routers forwarding packets, servers answering requests. Fast, high volume.

Control plane

The machinery that decides how the work is done: routing protocols like BGP, DNS, health checks, configuration and deployment tools, and the access engineers use to fix things.

Most outages hit the data plane: a server dies, a link is cut. Those are survivable because the control plane notices and routes around the damage. The scary outages are the ones where the control plane breaks, or worse, depends on the thing that broke. In this case:

  1. The health check (control plane) depended on the backbone (data plane), so a backbone failure made every DNS site withdraw at once.
  2. DNS (control plane for everyone else) disappeared, so the healthy parts of the network were unreachable too.
  3. The repair tools and emergency access (control plane for engineers) needed the backbone and DNS, so the means of fixing the failure failed with it.

Analogy: the keys locked in the car

A car that locks itself automatically when you walk away is a sensible safety feature. But if the spare key is kept inside the car, one mistake locks you out of both the car and the way back in. Facebook's emergency access was, in effect, a spare key kept inside the car.

Where it breaks down: a car has one lock. A big network has thousands of components with hidden chains of dependencies, and nobody can list them all from memory. That's why these dependencies usually surface only during an outage.

Why a good safety check made it worse

Withdrawing a route when you're unhealthy is exactly what chapter 14's load balancers do with health checks. The trouble is correlated failure (chapter 15): every checker used the same rule and saw the same symptom at the same moment, so all of them reached the same conclusion together. A check designed to remove one bad site removed every site.

A common defence is to make health-based removal fail static or "fail open": if everything looks unhealthy, assume the checker is wrong and keep serving. Some widely used load balancers do this. Envoy, for example, has a "panic threshold": if too large a share of backends fail their health checks, it ignores the checks and spreads traffic across all of them. Serving from a possibly-degraded site is often better than serving from none.

Go deeper: the DNS retry storm optional

With Facebook's nameservers unreachable, resolvers had nothing to cache except failures. Every app on billions of phones kept asking again, and resolvers kept trying Facebook's servers, waiting for each attempt to time out. Cloudflare saw query volumes around 30 times normal. Cloudflare said the vast majority of lookups on 1.1.1.1 kept resolving in under 10 ms, though it warned the extra load could cause latency and timeouts for other platforms. It's a live example of chapter 16's retry amplification: the failure of one system produced a flood of extra load on other systems that had nothing to do with it. Case study 8 looks at retry storms in depth.

The lessons engineers took away

Truly independent out-of-band access

Emergency access must not share the network, DNS or authentication systems with production. If it can fail together, it isn't out-of-band.

Map your hidden dependencies

Ask "if X is completely gone, can we still fix X?" for DNS, the backbone, identity and config tools. The answer is often surprising.

Guard the big red buttons

A command that can take down the whole backbone needs strong safety checks, and those checks need tests too. Here, the audit tool's bug was the last line of defence that failed.

Recover gradually

Turning everything back on at once can cause a second outage: cold caches, a thundering herd of reconnecting clients, even power surges. Meta credited its "storm" drills, which practise bringing back data centers, for a controlled recovery.

The security trade-off

Meta noted that the physical and digital security protecting its data centers made recovery slower, and argued that this trade-off was worth it. That's an honest and common tension. Every emergency bypass is also a potential way in for an attacker. Good designs make emergency access independent without making it easy to abuse, and they rehearse using it.

Try it yourself

Look at the pieces that disappeared

These commands show today's healthy version of what vanished that day. Your output will differ.

$ dig +short NS facebook.com          # Facebook's own authoritative nameservers
a.ns.facebook.com.
b.ns.facebook.com.
c.ns.facebook.com.
d.ns.facebook.com.
$ dig +short a.ns.facebook.com       # the addresses whose routes were withdrawn
129.134.30.12
$ traceroute -n 129.134.30.12       # a path the Internet forgot for six hours

Notice the nameservers live inside facebook.com itself. Resolvers can still find them because the .com servers hand out their addresses as "glue" (chapter 9). But it also means every route to Facebook's DNS runs through Facebook's own network. You can browse the prefixes Facebook announces (its network is AS32934) on public BGP looking glasses such as bgp.he.net/AS32934.

Distributed systems connection

This is partial failure (chapter 15) at its most dramatic: the servers were fine, the network between them wasn't, and the system couldn't tell the difference. It also shows that "redundancy" only helps when failures are independent. Many DNS sites, all depending on one backbone and one shared health rule, behaved like a single site. When you design replication (chapter 18) or consensus (chapter 20), always ask: what do all my replicas have in common?

Key takeaways

  • A maintenance command cut Facebook's backbone. DNS sites saw no backbone, judged themselves unhealthy, and all withdrew their BGP routes at once.
  • DNS servers that are running but unreachable look exactly like DNS servers that are down.
  • The control plane (routing, DNS, health checks, repair tools) must not depend on what it controls.
  • Health checks need a "fail static" rule for when everything looks unhealthy at once.
  • Recover gradually: turning everything on at once can cause a second outage.

Check your understanding

During the outage, what was the state of Facebook's authoritative DNS servers?

  1. They had crashed and were rebooting
  2. They were running, but no route to them existed on the Internet
  3. They were returning wrong IP addresses
  4. They had been hacked
The servers were operational, but their BGP routes had been withdrawn, so no packet could reach them. From outside, unreachable and dead look identical: the core problem of partial failure.

Why did a sensible health check (withdraw your route if you can't reach the data centers) cause a global outage?

  1. The health check had a bug and withdrew routes randomly
  2. BGP doesn't support withdrawing routes safely
  3. The check ran too slowly
  4. Every site saw the same failure at the same time, so all of them withdrew and no healthy site was left
The check was correct for one site failing alone. Because the backbone failure affected all sites at once, the same correct rule fired everywhere: a correlated failure. A "fail static" rule (if everyone looks unhealthy, keep serving) guards against this.

Which of these is part of the control plane?

  1. BGP route announcements
  2. A web server sending a photo to a user
  3. A router forwarding a packet according to its table
  4. A database returning a row
BGP decides where traffic should go: that's control. Forwarding packets and serving data is the data plane. Outages that break the control plane are the hardest to recover from, because they also break the ability to steer around damage.

Once the backbone was fixed, why didn't Meta switch every service back on at once?

  1. Because BGP only allows one route per hour
  2. To wait for DNS TTLs to expire everywhere
  3. A sudden surge of traffic and power demand could crash services and strain electrical systems
  4. Because users had to re-register their accounts
After hours offline, caches were cold, every client was waiting to reconnect, and power draw had dropped sharply. Restoring everything instantly risked a thundering herd and electrical overload, so they brought services back in stages, as practised in their storm drills.
