Lab 6: Toy Raft

Five servers, a network that delays and drops messages, and a clock you control tick by tick. You'll write the heart of the Raft consensus algorithm (elections, a replicated log, the commit rule) and then watch a test harness try to break it with fifty different seeds, crashes and partitions. When every safety property holds on every seed, you'll know why the algorithm is built the way it is.

Why this lab matters

Chapter 20 told you what Raft does. This lab makes you responsible for each rule, and the checker will tell you which one you forgot. That is the best way to learn consensus: take a rule out and watch the bug it prevents appear. The simulation is deterministic (no sockets, no real time, no threads), so every failure comes with a seed you can replay and step through. It is also the way real consensus implementations are tested: simulate the network, inject faults, check the invariants.

What you'll build

You'll implement Raft the way the paper structures it, in six milestones, each with its own check:

  1. Elections: terms, the follower / candidate / leader states, randomized election timeouts and RequestVote. At most one leader per term.
  2. Heartbeats: a leader's empty AppendEntries keeps followers quiet; if it crashes, a new election starts.
  3. Log replication: client commands become log entries; the prevLogIndex/prevLogTerm consistency check and conflict truncation make follower logs match the leader's.
  4. The commit rule: an entry is committed when a majority stores it and it is from the current term. Committed entries are applied to a key-value state machine.
  5. The voting restriction: a candidate with an out-of-date log can't win, so a new leader never lacks a committed entry.
  6. Partitions: a leader cut off with a minority can't commit; after healing it steps down and its logs converge.

Keep the paper open

The source of everything here is Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout, "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm" (the Raft paper, 2014). Sections 5.2, 5.3 and 5.4 cover elections, replication and safety, in that order, matching milestones 1 to 5. Chapter 20 is the gentler version of the same story, and its vocabulary (terms, RequestVote, AppendEntries, committed, majority) is what this lab uses.

How the simulator works

Real Raft runs on real machines with real clocks (chapter 17 explained why those are slippery). Here, time is a counter. One tick is the smallest unit of time. Each call to cluster.step() does exactly this, in this order:

  1. Advance the clock by one tick.
  2. Hand every message that is due to its destination node (node.on_message(src, msg)).
  3. Call node.tick() on every live node, in id order.

The Network decides what happens to every message: it draws a random delay of 1 to 3 ticks, may drop the message entirely (with a probability the scenario sets), and refuses to carry it between two sides of a partition. All the randomness comes from one seeded generator, and each node gets its own seeded rng for its election timeouts. So seed 17 always plays out the same way. When the checker says "seed 17 failed", you can replay seed 17 and watch it.

Three timing constants live at the top of the file. They have the relationship the paper asks for in section 5.6: messages are much faster than heartbeats, and heartbeats are much faster than election timeouts:

ConstantValueMeaning
NET_DELAY1 to 3 tickshow long a message takes to arrive
HEARTBEAT_INTERVAL5 ticksa leader sends AppendEntries at least this often
ELECTION_TIMEOUT20 to 40 ticksa follower waits a random time in this range before starting an election

The four things the checker watches

The Raft paper's Figure 3 lists five properties that hold at all times. check.py verifies four after every single tick of every scenario (the fifth, Leader Append-Only, is a rule your leader follows by construction):

PropertyIn plain wordsHow the checker tests it
Election SafetyAt most one leader can win a given term.Records which node is leader of each term; two different nodes for one term is a failure.
Log MatchingIf two logs have an entry with the same index and the same term, the logs are identical up to that index.Compares every pair of live nodes.
Leader CompletenessA leader of a later term already holds every entry committed earlier.When a node becomes leader, its log must contain everything any node had committed so far.
State Machine SafetyNo two servers ever apply different commands at the same index.Every sm.apply(index, command) is recorded; a clash is a failure.

These are safety properties ("nothing bad ever happens", chapter 20): they must hold even during crashes and partitions. The checker also tests liveness ("something good eventually happens"): after faults stop, a new command must commit on every node. Liveness is only promised when a majority is connected, which is why the scenarios heal the cluster first.

Milestone 1: elections

Every server starts as a follower, waiting to hear from a leader. If the election timeout expires with silence, it concludes there may be no leader and campaigns. Time in Raft is cut into numbered terms (chapter 17's logical clocks, specialised): a term is a counter that only goes up, begins with an election, and has at most one leader.

The rules you need, all from Figure 2:

  • Start an election on timeout: increment current_term, become a candidate, vote for yourself, restart the election timer, and send RequestVote to every peer.
  • One vote per term, first come first served. A server remembers whom it voted for in voted_for and grants a vote only if it hasn't voted for someone else in this term. (Milestone 5 adds a second condition.)
  • A majority wins. With 5 servers, 3 votes (including your own). Any two majorities overlap, so two candidates can't both win the same term: that is Election Safety, straight from the arithmetic in chapter 20.
  • A higher term always wins the argument. If any message carries a term greater than yours, adopt it, forget your vote (it was for an older term), and become a follower. If it carries a smaller term, the sender is out of date and is refused.

Analogy: a leaderless classroom

A teacher steps out and the room goes quiet. Each student waits a different, random time, then stands up and says "I'll lead round 5, vote for me!" Everyone gives their vote to the first one who asks in each round. If two stand up at once and the votes split, nobody wins, and after another random pause someone tries again in round 6. The random pauses are the whole trick: identical pauses would mean everyone stands up together, forever.

Where it breaks down: students can see each other. Servers only know what messages happen to arrive, and the "round number" must travel on every message so that latecomers realise they've missed a round.

Why does the timeout have to be random? Try it later: set the timeout to a constant and the checker will report that no leader is ever elected (all five servers time out at the same tick, each votes for itself, nobody gets a majority, and they repeat in lock step). A random timeout makes one server the first to wake up; it asks for votes before the others have even stirred.

def reset_election_timer(self):
    self.election_elapsed = 0
    self.election_timeout = self.rng.randint(*ELECTION_TIMEOUT)
def become_follower(self, term):
    if term > self.current_term:
        self.current_term = term
        self.voted_for = None
    self.state = FOLLOWER
    self.votes = set()
def start_election(self):
    self.current_term += 1
    self.state = CANDIDATE
    self.voted_for = self.id
    self.votes = {self.id}
    self.leader_id = None
    self.reset_election_timer()
    for peer in self.peers:
        self.send(peer, {"type": REQUEST_VOTE, "term": self.current_term,
                         "candidate_id": self.id,
                         "last_log_index": self.last_log_index(),
                         "last_log_term": self.last_log_term()})
    if len(self.votes) >= self.majority():          # a cluster of one
        self.become_leader()
def become_leader(self):
    self.state = LEADER
    self.leader_id = self.id
    self.next_index = {p: self.last_log_index() + 1 for p in self.peers}
    self.match_index = {p: 0 for p in self.peers}
    self.broadcast_append_entries()
def on_message(self, src, msg):
    if msg["term"] > self.current_term:             # rule for all servers (Figure 2)
        self.become_follower(msg["term"])
    kind = msg["type"]
    if kind == REQUEST_VOTE:
        self.on_request_vote(src, msg)
    elif kind == VOTE_REPLY:
        self.on_request_vote_reply(src, msg)
    elif kind == APPEND_ENTRIES:
        self.on_append_entries(src, msg)
    elif kind == APPEND_REPLY:
        self.on_append_entries_reply(src, msg)
def on_request_vote(self, src, msg):
    grant = (msg["term"] == self.current_term
             and self.voted_for in (None, msg["candidate_id"])
             and self.candidate_log_ok(msg))
    if grant:
        self.voted_for = msg["candidate_id"]
        self.reset_election_timer()                 # we just backed a candidate: don't race it
    self.send(src, {"type": VOTE_REPLY, "term": self.current_term, "vote_granted": grant})
def on_request_vote_reply(self, src, msg):
    if self.state != CANDIDATE or msg["term"] != self.current_term:
        return                                      # a late reply from an old election
    if msg["vote_granted"]:
        self.votes.add(src)
        if len(self.votes) >= self.majority():
            self.become_leader()

Milestone 2: heartbeats and re-election

Once there is a leader it must keep telling everyone, otherwise the followers' timers will expire and they'll start elections for no reason. The mechanism is the same message that will later carry log entries: AppendEntries. With no entries inside, it is a heartbeat (chapter 16's failure detector, given a job).

  • The leader sends AppendEntries to every follower immediately on winning, then every HEARTBEAT_INTERVAL ticks. (tick() already counts; you write broadcast_append_entries and send_append_entries.)
  • A follower that receives AppendEntries with a term at least as large as its own accepts the sender as leader and resets its election timer. A candidate that receives one from a leader of its own term gives up and becomes a follower. An AppendEntries from an older term is refused and must not reset the timer: it comes from a leader that has already been deposed.
  • Leader crashes: heartbeats stop, the followers' timers run out, one of them wins a new election in a higher term. When the old leader comes back it's a follower and the first heartbeat it sees (higher term) confirms it.

For this milestone the messages can be minimal: no entries, and a follower that always answers "success". Milestone 3 gives them real content.

Common misconception: "heartbeat interval doesn't matter much"

It must be comfortably smaller than the election timeout, with room for message delay. Try setting HEARTBEAT_INTERVAL to 30 (inside the 20–40 election range) and rerun milestone 2. Followers will time out between heartbeats and the cluster will elect leaders constantly, even though nothing has failed. This is the thought experiment from chapter 20's "Try it yourself", running.

def broadcast_append_entries(self):
    self.heartbeat_elapsed = 0
    for peer in self.peers:
        self.send_append_entries(peer)
def send_append_entries(self, peer):
    prev = self.next_index[peer] - 1
    self.send(peer, {"type": APPEND_ENTRIES, "term": self.current_term,
                     "leader_id": self.id,
                     "prev_log_index": prev, "prev_log_term": self.term_at(prev),
                     "entries": self.log[prev:],
                     "leader_commit": self.commit_index})
def on_append_entries(self, src, msg):
    reply = {"type": APPEND_REPLY, "term": self.current_term, "success": False, "match_index": 0}
    if msg["term"] < self.current_term:             # from a deposed leader
        self.send(src, reply)
        return
    self.become_follower(msg["term"])               # a candidate of this term steps down
    self.leader_id = msg["leader_id"]
    self.reset_election_timer()                     # a live leader: stay quiet
    # Milestone 3: the consistency check, and conflict truncation (section 5.3)
    prev = msg["prev_log_index"]
    if prev > self.last_log_index() or self.term_at(prev) != msg["prev_log_term"]:
        self.send(src, reply)                       # we don't have the entry before the new ones
        return
    index = prev
    for entry in msg["entries"]:
        index += 1
        entry = tuple(entry)
        if index <= self.last_log_index():
            if self.log[index - 1][0] == entry[0]:
                continue                            # already have it
            del self.log[index - 1:]                # conflict: drop it and everything after
        self.log.append(entry)
    match = prev + len(msg["entries"])
    # Milestone 4: learn what is committed, but never beyond what this message verified
    if msg["leader_commit"] > self.commit_index:
        self.commit_index = min(msg["leader_commit"], match)
        self.apply_committed()
    reply.update(success=True, match_index=match)
    self.send(src, reply)

Milestone 3: log replication

A leader's real job is to be the one place where the order of commands is decided (the single-leader replication of chapter 18, with consensus protecting the choice of leader). A client sends a command; the leader appends it to its own log as a pair (current_term, command), and ships it to the followers inside AppendEntries.

The hard part is what happens after leaders change. A follower may be missing entries (it was down), or may have extra entries from an old leader that never reached a majority. The leader must make the follower's log identical to its own, without ever deleting something that matters. Raft does it with one cheap check that gives the Log Matching property:

  • Every AppendEntries says: "these entries go right after index prev_log_index, and the entry at that index has term prev_log_term."
  • The follower refuses unless its own log has exactly that entry at that index. If it does, then by induction everything before it matches too (each earlier append passed the same check).
  • On refusal, the leader steps next_index for that follower back by one and tries again, until it finds the point where the logs agree.
  • Then the follower appends the new entries. If an existing entry conflicts with a new one (same index, different term), it deletes that entry and everything after it. If the entry is already there with the same term, it leaves it alone.

Read the figure as a story:

  1. The leader (term 3) first sends AppendEntries(prevLogIndex=4, prevLogTerm=3). Follower A has no entry 4. Follower B has an entry 4, but from term 2, not 3. Both refuse.
  2. The leader backs up one step: prevLogIndex=3, prevLogTerm=2, carrying entries 4 and 5. Both followers have a term-2 entry at index 3, so both accept.
  3. A simply appends. B finds a conflict at index 4 (term 2 against term 3), deletes its entries 4 and 5, and appends the leader's. The old term-2 entries had never been committed, so nothing of value was lost.

Pitfall: don't truncate on every AppendEntries

Messages can be delayed, reordered and duplicated by the network. An old AppendEntries carrying entries 1 to 2 can arrive after a newer one that delivered entries 1 to 5. If the follower blindly replaces its log with "prev + entries", it deletes entries 3 to 5. Only truncate when an entry conflicts (same index, different term). The checker tests exactly this.

def client_request(self, command):
    if self.state != LEADER:
        return None
    self.log.append((self.current_term, command))
    self.broadcast_append_entries()
    return self.last_log_index()
def on_append_entries_reply(self, src, msg):
    if self.state != LEADER or msg["term"] != self.current_term:
        return
    if msg["success"]:
        self.match_index[src] = max(self.match_index[src], msg["match_index"])
        self.next_index[src] = self.match_index[src] + 1
        self.advance_commit_index()
    else:                                           # logs differ: back up one and try again
        self.next_index[src] = max(self.match_index[src] + 1, self.next_index[src] - 1)
        self.send_append_entries(src)

Milestone 4: the commit rule

An entry in a leader's log is only a proposal. It becomes committed, meaning Raft promises it will never be lost or changed, when the leader knows it is stored on a majority. Committed entries are then applied, in order, to the state machine (here KVStateMachine: ("set", key, value) and ("del", key)). This is the state machine replication of chapter 20: identical logs applied in the same order give identical key-value stores, with no further coordination.

  • Leader: after each successful AppendEntries reply, look for the highest index N that is stored on a majority (count yourself plus peers whose match_index >= N) and whose entry has the current term. Set commit_index = N and apply.
  • Follower: learn leader_commit from AppendEntries. Set commit_index = min(leader_commit, index of the last entry this message verified), and apply. The min matters: a follower may hold extra stale entries beyond what the leader has checked, and those must not be treated as committed.

The "Figure 8" subtlety, explained simply

The words and the current term are the strangest rule in Raft, so here is the story behind it (the paper's Figure 8). Suppose an entry from an older term is stored on a majority. It feels committed. It isn't safe yet:

In step 1 the term-2 entry (index 2) is on S1, S2 and S3, which is a majority. If S1 had declared it committed and told the client "done", then step 2 would erase it from S2, S3 and S4: a committed entry, lost.

The rule that fixes this: a leader never commits an entry from an earlier term by counting copies. It commits entries from its own term; committing one of those commits everything before it too (by the Log Matching property). In practice a new leader gets there by appending something in its own term. The checker has this exact situation as a unit test: it sets up a leader whose old-term entry is on three of five servers and requires commit_index to stay 0.

def advance_commit_index(self):
    for n in range(self.last_log_index(), self.commit_index, -1):
        if self.term_at(n) != self.current_term:
            break                                   # older-term entries commit only indirectly
        copies = 1 + sum(1 for p in self.peers if self.match_index[p] >= n)
        if copies >= self.majority():
            self.commit_index = n
            break
    self.apply_committed()
def apply_committed(self):
    while self.last_applied < self.commit_index:
        self.last_applied += 1
        self.sm.apply(self.last_applied, self.log[self.last_applied - 1][1])

Milestone 5: the voting restriction

The commit rule decides when an entry is safe. The second half of the safety argument is about the next leader: when a leader dies, its successor must already hold everything that was committed. Imagine it didn't. An entry is committed on three of five servers; the other two, S4 and S5, never received it. The leader crashes. S5 times out, becomes a candidate with a short log, and asks for votes. If servers handed out votes freely, S4 and one of the servers that has the entry could vote for S5; S5 would become leader and overwrite the committed entry. Raft closes that door at the ballot box: a server grants its vote only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own. "Up to date" is decided like this (paper section 5.4.1):

  1. Compare the terms of the last entries. The log whose last entry has the higher term is more up to date.
  2. If the last terms are equal, the longer log is more up to date.

Combine this with the majority rule and you get Leader Completeness. An entry is committed only when a majority stores it. To win, a candidate needs votes from a majority. Any two majorities share a server (the overlap argument from chapter 18 and 20). That shared server has the committed entry, so it refuses any candidate that lacks it. Hence every leader's log already contains every committed entry, and leaders never need to ask for old entries back.

Common misconception: "the highest term wins"

Chapter 20 warned about this one. A server that has been partitioned away keeps timing out and raising its term, so it has a big term and a short log. When it returns, its RequestVote forces everyone to adopt its term (they must, or terms mean nothing), but they must not vote for it. Terms say whose authority is current; they don't say whose log is complete. Your on_request_vote adopts the term (in on_message) and still refuses the vote.

Milestone 6: partitions

The last milestone puts everything under the worst ordinary stress: the network splits. Chapter 15 called this a partition, and chapter 20 said what Raft chooses: consistency over availability. Here is the scenario the checker runs, 50 times with different seeds:

  1. Elect a leader and commit 3 commands.
  2. Cut the cluster: the leader goes with 0 or 1 other node (on alternating seeds), the remaining 3 or 4 nodes are the majority side.
  3. The old leader keeps accepting commands. It must not commit any of them: it can't reach a majority.
  4. The majority side times out, elects a new leader in a higher term, and commits its own commands.
  5. Heal the network. The old leader receives an AppendEntries with a higher term and steps down; the entries it accepted but never committed are overwritten by the conflict rule from milestone 3. All logs converge and no state machine ever applied the old leader's commands.

This is the payoff milestone, and there is no new method to write. If your milestones 1 to 5 are right, it should pass at once, because each piece you wrote is one of the reasons it works: on_message demotes the stale leader (milestone 1), conflict truncation clears its bad entries (3), the commit rule stops it from committing in the first place (4), and the voting restriction keeps the new leader complete (5). If it fails, the message names the seed and which property broke, and that points you back to the earlier milestone.

After the 50 targeted scenarios, the checker runs 50 more seeds of partition chaos: random partitions, heals, crashes and restarts with commands arriving the whole time, all four safety properties checked each tick. Finally it heals everything and requires that the cluster still works: a new command commits on all five nodes (liveness).

If it fails debugging help

  • "ELECTION SAFETY": look at voted_for handling in (1): one vote per term, and a vote for a new term must reset first.
  • "logs did not converge": check conflict truncation and the leader's next_index back-off (3). A very common cause is truncating too much or too little.
  • "LIVENESS": after healing nothing commits. Look for a node that never resets its election timer, or a leader that never retries after a failed AppendEntries.
  • "commit_index is beyond its log": the follower's min(leader_commit, match) from (4).

Stretch goals

  • Append a no-op on election. A new leader can't commit older-term entries until it has a current-term entry on a majority. Real implementations append an empty entry in their own term as soon as they win (paper section 8). Add it in become_leader and see what changes in the commit logs.
  • Faster log back-off. Backing up one entry per round trip is slow for a follower that is far behind. Make the failure reply carry the follower's last index (or the first index of its conflicting term) so the leader can jump back (paper section 5.3, near the end).
  • Pre-vote and check-quorum. A node that returns from a partition with an inflated term forces a healthy leader to step down. Add a "pre-vote" round that doesn't bump the term unless a majority would grant the vote, or make a leader step down if it hasn't heard from a majority recently.
  • Client sessions. A client that retries a command after a leader crash can end up with it in the log twice. Give each command a client id and sequence number and have the state machine skip duplicates. This is Lab 5's idempotency key, applied to a log.
  • Linearizable reads. A leader that served a read from its state machine might be a stale leader in a partition. Make reads go through the log, or have the leader confirm a heartbeat round with a majority before answering (chapter 20, "Go deeper: reads").
  • Snapshots. The log grows forever. Let a node replace a committed prefix with a snapshot of its key-value store, and add an InstallSnapshot message for followers that are behind it.
  • Persistence. The simulator's "disk" is just the fields that restart() doesn't reset. Make it real: write current_term, voted_for and the log to a file before replying to any message, and explain why before replying matters (a node that forgot its vote could vote twice in one term).

Distributed systems connection

What you've built is the core of etcd (the store behind Kubernetes), Consul and many database replication layers: a small group of servers that agree on an ordered log, with leader election built from terms, majorities and a voting restriction. Compare it with Lab 5: there, any replica could be read and any write could land on any W nodes, and nothing guaranteed a single order of concurrent writes. Here, one leader orders every command, a majority must hold it before it's committed, and losing a minority changes nothing visible to clients. The price is the one chapter 20 listed: every write waits for a majority round trip, and a minority partition goes quiet. Real systems pair a small Raft group like this one (for leader identities, locks and configuration) with cheaper, eventually consistent machinery for bulk data, which chapter 21 picks up.

What you learned

  • Terms are Raft's logical clock: any message from a higher term makes a server adopt it and step down; any from a lower term is refused.
  • A majority vote, one vote per term, makes Election Safety follow from arithmetic; randomized timeouts make elections finish.
  • Heartbeats are empty AppendEntries; their interval must be well below the election timeout.
  • The prevLogIndex/prevLogTerm check plus conflict truncation gives Log Matching; the leader's log is the truth and followers are repaired to match it.
  • An entry is committed when a majority stores it and it is from the leader's current term; earlier entries commit with it. Followers may only commit what a message verified.
  • The voting restriction (compare last terms, then lengths) means leaders never miss a committed entry.
  • A minority leader cannot commit; after a partition heals its uncommitted entries are overwritten. Safety is never at risk, only availability on the small side.
  • A deterministic simulator with seeded faults and invariant checks is a powerful way to test distributed algorithms.

Check your understanding

Why does each Raft server pick a random election timeout?

  1. So that servers with slower clocks get a fair chance
  2. To make the leader's heartbeats unpredictable
  3. So that usually one server times out first and wins before the others become candidates, instead of everyone splitting the vote
  4. Because terms must be random to be unique
With identical timeouts all servers become candidates in the same tick, vote for themselves, and nobody gets a majority; the stalemate repeats in lock step. Random timeouts break the symmetry: someone wakes first. Collisions can still happen, but each retry draws new random numbers, so repeated stalemates are unlikely.

A leader of term 5 finds an entry from term 3 stored on 3 of 5 servers. May it mark that entry committed because it is on a majority?

  1. Yes, a majority is all that matters
  2. No: it commits only entries from its own term by counting replicas. Older entries become committed when a term-5 entry after them is, because logs match up to that point
  3. No: entries from older terms can never be committed
  4. Yes, but only if the leader has also applied it
Figure 8 in the paper shows an old-term entry on a majority being overwritten by a later leader whose last log term is higher. Entries from the current term don't have this problem. Older entries aren't doomed: they commit "indirectly" once a current-term entry above them is committed.

A voter's log ends with an entry (term 3, index 2). A candidate in term 6 has a log ending (term 2, index 9). What does the voter do?

  1. Grants the vote: the candidate's log is longer
  2. Grants the vote: the candidate's term is higher
  3. Ignores the request, because its own term is lower
  4. Adopts term 6 but refuses the vote: the candidate's last entry has an older term, so its log is less up to date despite being longer
The up-to-date test compares the terms of the last entries first, and only compares lengths on a tie. The higher term (6) is adopted for everything else, but the vote is refused. If this candidate won, it could overwrite an entry that may already be committed.

A five-node cluster is split 2 | 3. The old leader is on the side with 2 and a client sends it a command. What happens?

  1. The leader appends and replicates to its one neighbour but can't commit it. After the partition heals, the entry is overwritten if the majority side committed different entries at those indexes
  2. The leader commits it, because it still thinks it's the leader
  3. The leader commits it using the other side's vote once the partition heals, keeping the entry
  4. The leader steps down immediately because it can't reach everyone
A leader only steps down when it sees a higher term, which it can't while cut off. It can't commit without a majority (3 of 5), so the client never gets a success. The majority side elects a new leader and commits entries; when the network heals, the old leader sees the higher term, steps down, and the conflict rule replaces its uncommitted entries. Raft sacrifices availability on the small side to keep safety.
