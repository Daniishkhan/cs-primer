Deep Research Agents

A deep-research agent takes a question that no single search can answer, spends minutes searching and reading, and returns a long report with citations. This chapter takes the pipeline apart: planning, parallel search, reading, cross-checking, citing and writing. It shows where each stage goes wrong and how to build checks so a reader can trust, or distrust, what comes out.

Why this matters

Research is the most popular long-running agent task, and the one where fluent output is most dangerous. A report with forty confident citations looks authoritative, whether or not the citations support the sentences beside them. Earlier chapters gave you the parts: a tool loop (chapter 2), context budgets (chapter 10), sub-agents (chapter 14) and durable runs (chapter 15). This chapter assembles them into a research product and adds what is specific to research: deciding what counts as evidence, noticing disagreement, and refusing to assert what you cannot trace to a source. You will build the pipeline, run it over a small corpus with a planted fabrication, and see the checks catch it. Lab 6 turns it into your own implementation.

What a deep-research agent is

A deep-research agent is an agent whose task is to answer an open-ended question with a written, sourced report, by iterating between planning, searching and reading for minutes rather than seconds. Three neighbours are easy to confuse with it.

  • A search-grounded chat answer runs one or two searches and writes a paragraph. It is a single pass.
  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG; A5 chapter 13) answers from a corpus you indexed in advance, usually with one retrieval step per question. Deep research searches an open or very large collection, decides what to look for next from what it has read, and stops when it judges it has enough. Agentic retrieval in A5 is the same loop at smaller scale.
  • A multi-agent team (chapter 14) is one way to organise the work. A single agent can do deep research; teams are a way to widen it.

State of play, October 2026

Google announced Deep Research in Gemini on 11 December 2024, rolling out to Gemini Advanced subscribers: it makes a multi-step research plan and produces a report that can be exported to a Google Doc. OpenAI's deep research in ChatGPT followed in early February 2025, and Anthropic described its Research feature and the multi-agent system behind it in a June 2025 engineering post. Other assistants and open-source projects offer similar modes. Names, models, limits and prices of these products change often, so treat this list as a dated snapshot and read each vendor's current documentation. The pipeline below is what they have in common; it does not depend on any one of them.

The pipeline

Every deep-research system differs in detail, and nearly all contain the same seven stages. Learn the stages first, because each is a place to put a check.

The rest of this chapter walks through one small, concrete case so every stage has numbers. The question: how much did the (fictional) Northvale council spend on road repair in 2024, and how does that compare with 2023? The corpus is eight short documents, listed below. It has the problems real corpora have: primary sources and copies of them, a stale blog post, an unsupported forum comment, and a news story whose number differs from the official one.

Step through

One question through the whole pipeline

  1. Plan. The planner decomposes the question into four searches, with two phrasings of the main need and one for the reason behind the change.
  2. Search. The queries run in parallel. Each returns a ranked list; merged by document id there are eight distinct documents. Searching is cheap and read-only, so it can fan out.
  3. Triage. Reading a page costs thousands of tokens; a snippet costs a hundred or so. The agent scores each hit and discounts by source kind, and skips a stale blog and a forum thread.
  4. Read and verify. The reading step extracts claims and verbatim quotes. Code checks each quote against the page text. One claim fails: a number that looks plausible and appears nowhere in the source.
  5. Cross-check. Claims are grouped by what they measure. Agreement counts independent origins, not documents. A conflict is not averaged away; the agent looks for an explanation in other verified claims and finds one.
  6. Write and audit. The draft is audited sentence by sentence. A number with no support in the cited claims, and a sentence with no citation, are both flagged. The report that reaches the reader has been through this gate.

Planning: from a question to sub-questions

Clarify first. "Compare the leading approaches to grid storage" can mean a market survey for an investor or a technical review for an engineer. A short clarifying exchange (audience, time range, geography, what counts as "leading") is far cheaper than ten minutes of research aimed wrongly. Many products do this before they start. If the system is asynchronous, ask once at the start; do not interrupt a half-hour run with questions the user will not see until later.

Decompose. The planner turns the question into sub-questions that can be searched independently: one per entity, period, or angle. Our plan has four queries. Two of them (road repair spending 2024 and Northvale spent on roads in 2024) ask for the same thing in different words, which looks wasteful and is deliberate. Search engines and the retrieval systems of A5 match words (A5 chapter 2), and documents say "spent" as often as "spending". The simple search function in the demo below cannot tell that "spent" and "spending" are the same word, so the second phrasing ranks the Gazette article first (score 1.0) where the first ranks it third. Real engines are better than that, but vocabulary mismatch never vanishes.

Start broad, then narrow. Anthropic's engineering post on its research system reports that its agents defaulted to long, over-specific queries that returned few results, and that prompting them to start with short, broad queries, look at what exists, and then narrow worked better. The same post reports that early versions preferred search-optimised content-farm pages over authoritative sources that ranked lower, and that adding source-quality heuristics to the prompts helped. Both are planning-stage lessons.

Scale effort to the question. A simple factual lookup needs a few tool calls. A comparison needs a handful of parallel branches. A broad survey needs many. Chapter 14 gives Anthropic's published guidance on this, and the reason is cost: the research system in that post used about 15 times the tokens of a chat interaction. A planner with no sense of proportion is the quickest way to an expensive wrong answer.

Make the plan a visible, editable artefact. Showing the plan before searching lets the user fix a misunderstanding for pennies. Storing the plan outside the context (a notes file, as in chapter 11, or an event in the journal of chapter 15) means the agent can re-read it when its working memory is full, and a crash does not lose it.

Searching in parallel, and what it costs

Search calls are read-only and independent, which is the easiest kind of work to parallelise. The plan's four queries can run at once, and each result list is returned in one round trip. The code below runs the demo's plan with a thread pool and a tiny word-overlap search (a stand-in for a real search API), then does the triage described next.

# research.py: plan -> parallel search -> triage -> read -> verify quotes -> cross-check -> audit the draft
import json, re
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
from llm import ScriptedModel, text_of
from corpus import DOCS

STOP = set("the a an of in on to was were is for and by at as from up".split())
words = lambda s: [w.rstrip("s") for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower()) if w not in STOP]
PRIOR = {"primary": 1.0, "news": 0.6, "blog": 0.3, "forum": 0.1}       # source-quality prior (a heuristic, not truth)

def search(q, k=6):
    qs = set(words(q))
    scored = [(len(qs & set(words(d["title"] + " " + d["text"]))) / len(qs), i) for i, d in DOCS.items()]
    return [(i, round(s, 2)) for s, i in sorted(scored, reverse=True)[:k] if s > 0]

def origin(i): return DOCS[i]["derived_from"] or i      # a news story that quotes a report is not a second witness

# 1. PLAN: the planner model returns sub-questions as JSON (scripted here)
planner = ScriptedModel([json.dumps(["road repair spending 2024", "Northvale spent on roads in 2024", "road repair spending 2023", "storm damage road repair increase"])])
subqs = json.loads(text_of(planner.complete([{"role": "user", "content": "Plan: How much did Northvale spend on roads in 2024 vs 2023?"}])))
print("PLAN:", subqs)

# 2. SEARCH the sub-questions in parallel; 3. TRIAGE: keep documents above a score x prior threshold
with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=4) as pool:
    hits = list(pool.map(search, subqs))
for q, h in zip(subqs, hits): print(f"  {q!r:<38} -> {h}")
best = {}
for h in hits:
    for i, s in h: best[i] = max(best.get(i, 0), s * (0.5 + PRIOR[DOCS[i]["kind"]] / 2))
to_read = sorted([i for i, s in best.items() if s >= 0.35])
print("READ:", to_read, " skipped:", sorted(set(best) - set(to_read)))

Searching is cheap; reading is where the budget goes. A fetched page of a few thousand words is thousands of tokens, and the agent may find dozens of candidate pages. Three habits keep this under control.

  1. Deduplicate by document. The same page comes up under several queries; read it once. (In the run above, four queries return 24 hits for eight documents.) Also merge near-duplicates: syndicated copies, mirrored pages.
  2. Triage on snippets before fetching. A search result's title and snippet cost around a hundred tokens. Score each hit by relevance times a source prior (the PRIOR table above gives a primary record more weight than a forum post), and open only the best. The prior is a heuristic: a forum thread may be the only source for a niche technical fact, and an official page may be out of date. Treat the prior as a tie-breaker that you can override with evidence.
  3. Read in separate contexts. Have each reader (a sub-agent, chapter 14) fetch its pages in its own window and return compact notes, so the lead's context holds claims and references rather than page text.

The next script prices those habits. Every number is an assumption: six sub-questions, eight results each, 35% of results duplicates, 6,000 tokens a page, 150 for a snippet, 400 for a reader's notes, 40% of unique results worth opening, eight seconds to fetch and read a page, six parallel readers.

# research_cost.py: what reading costs, under stated ASSUMPTIONS (not measurements of any product)
Q, K = 6, 8                  # sub-questions, search results per sub-question
OVERLAP = 0.35               # share of results that duplicate a page another sub-question already found
PAGE, SNIP, NOTE = 6_000, 150, 400    # tokens: a fetched page, a search snippet, the notes a reader returns
KEEP = 0.4                   # share of unique results worth opening after reading snippets
SEC_READ, WORKERS = 8, 6     # seconds to fetch and read one page; parallel readers

raw = Q * K
unique = round(raw * (1 - OVERLAP))
opened = round(unique * KEEP)
def row(label, pages, snippets, notes_in_lead, workers):
    tokens = pages * PAGE + snippets * SNIP
    lead = notes_in_lead
    secs = -(-pages // workers) * SEC_READ
    print(f"{label:<46}{pages:>7}{tokens:>14,}{lead:>12,}{secs:>7}")
print(f"{'strategy':<46}{"pages":>7}{"read tokens":>14}{"lead ctx":>12}{"secs":>7}")
row("read every result, one agent, in order", raw, 0, raw * PAGE, 1)
row("dedupe, then read all, one agent", unique, 0, unique * PAGE, 1)
row("dedupe + snippet triage, one agent", opened, raw, opened * PAGE, 1)
row("dedupe + triage, readers in own contexts", opened, raw, opened * NOTE, WORKERS)
strategy                                        pages   read tokens    lead ctx   secs
read every result, one agent, in order             48       288,000     288,000    384
dedupe, then read all, one agent                   31       186,000     186,000    248
dedupe + snippet triage, one agent                 12        79,200      72,000     96
dedupe + triage, readers in own contexts           12        79,200       4,800     16

Deduplicating saves about a third. Triage on snippets saves more than half of what remains, at the price of occasionally skipping a page that mattered. Separate reader contexts do not change the tokens read but change two things that matter: the lead agent's context drops from 72,000 to 4,800 tokens (far below the point where recall degrades, chapter 12), and the wall-clock time falls from 96 to 16 seconds. Two cautions. Wall-clock time in a fan-out is set by the slowest branch, so tail latency applies, and the serial parts (planning, writing) limit the speed-up, as Amdahl's law says.

When to stop. A research loop has no natural end, so give it two. A hard budget (tokens, pages, minutes, money) bounds the worst case. A soft stopping rule ends the loop when more work is not paying: for example, when the last round of searches returned mostly documents already seen and added no new claims. Report which one fired. "Stopped at the budget; two sub-questions remain open" is an honest result, and a report that hides it is not.

Systems connection

The fetch tool takes a URL chosen by a model, which makes it a server-side request forger waiting to happen: a poisoned page can point it at http://169.254.169.254/ or an internal admin panel. Everything known about server-side request forgery applies: resolve and validate destinations, block private ranges, cap size and time. The search side is a ranked-retrieval system of the kind described in A5. And because the agent loops over many pages, treat its HTTP client like any other crawler: honour robots.txt and rate limits, and set timeouts and retries as for any network client.

Reading: claims with evidence, not summaries

A reader that returns a paragraph of summary has already lost the thing you need later: where each statement came from. Ask instead for claims: small, checkable statements, each with the supporting quote, the document id, and enough metadata to judge it (date, publisher, kind). In the demo a claim has a metric, a year, a value and a quote:

{"doc": "d1", "metric": "road repair spend", "year": 2024, "value": 4.2,
 "quote": "expenditure in 2024 was 4.2 million pounds"}

This shape does three jobs. It makes cross-checking mechanical (claims with the same metric and year can be compared). It makes citation verification mechanical (the quote either appears in the page or it does not). And it keeps the reader honest, because a model asked for a verbatim quote has to point at text.

Things to decide at reading time:

  • Date and scope. The blog post in the corpus says Northvale "spends about 3.1 million pounds a year": undated in the sentence, written in March 2024, before the 2024 accounts existed. A claim without a period is a trap. Ask the reader to attach one, or to say "unspecified".
  • Primary versus derived. Record whether the page is the origin of a figure or reports someone else's. A news story that begins "according to the council's annual accounts" is a pointer to the accounts, not a second source.
  • Page access. Paywalled pages, pages that need JavaScript, scanned PDFs and tables flattened into nonsense are common. A reader should say "could not read" rather than fill the gap from memory. Document parsing is its own discipline (A5 chapter 18).

Common misconception

"The agent reads the web, so it can only report what it found." A reading model fills gaps from its training data without announcing it. It will paraphrase a page and add a plausible detail from memory, or produce a perfect-looking quote that is a blend of two pages. That is why the quote is checked by code against the fetched text, and why claims that fail the check are dropped rather than corrected by asking the model again.

Pages are untrusted input

Everything a reader fetches is text written by someone else, and some of it is written to manipulate the agent: hidden text telling it to ignore its instructions, to include a link, to leave out a competitor. Chapter 19 covers prompt injection in depth. The design consequence for research is simple and strict. The component that reads untrusted pages should hold no sensitive tools and no private data: it can fetch, extract and return text, and nothing else. A research agent that can also read the user's private files and send web requests has the combination that Simon Willison named the lethal trifecta (private data, untrusted content, a way to communicate outward), and a search query can itself be the exfiltration channel. Keep reading in a low-privilege sandbox (chapter 20) and treat what comes back as data, never as instructions.

Analogy: the legal research memo

A junior lawyer asked "can we terminate this contract?" does not answer from memory. They clarify the question, list the issues, pull cases and statutes, read them, note where authorities disagree, write a memo with a citation for each proposition, and then a colleague cite-checks it: every citation is opened and read to see whether it says what the memo claims. Research agents have the same stages, and the cite-check is the one that gets skipped.

Where it breaks down: a lawyer knows what they have not understood and flags it; a model reads a case it has misunderstood with the same fluency as one it has understood. A lawyer also knows the hierarchy of authority (a supreme court outranks a blog); the agent knows only the priors you give it. And the cite-checker in a firm is a person who can read; ours must be code and a second model, each with limits.

Cross-checking: agreement, conflict and independence

Once claims are extracted, the system can do something a single-pass summariser cannot: compare them. Corroboration means that independent sources report the same thing. The word that matters is independent. Five articles that all quote one press release are one witness. In the demo each document carries a derived_from field, and the cross-checker counts independent origins, mapping every document to the origin it quotes. Real systems have no such field and must infer it, from phrases such as "according to" and "reported by", from links, from near-identical wording, and from publication order. It is imperfect, and still better than counting pages.

The second script reads the six pages, verifies quotes, groups claims, counts origins and audits a draft. Here the "reading model" is a scripted stand-in whose output includes one invented quote, so you can see the check fire. It imports research.py, whose first six lines of output (the plan, searches and triage shown earlier) are left out below.

# research2.py: read -> verify quotes -> cross-check -> audit a draft (continues research.py)
import json, re
from collections import defaultdict
from llm import ScriptedModel, text_of
from research import DOCS, to_read, origin

C = lambda doc, year, value, quote, metric="road repair spend": dict(doc=doc, metric=metric, year=year, value=value, quote=quote)
READER = {   # what the reading model returned for each document (scripted; d3's second claim is invented)
 "d1": [C("d1", 2024, 4.2, "expenditure in 2024 was 4.2 million pounds"), C("d1", 2023, 3.4, "Expenditure in 2023 was 3.4 million pounds")],
 "d2": [C("d2", 2024, 4.2, "2024 road repair spending of 4.2 million pounds"), C("d2", 2023, 3.4, "2023 comparator of 3.4 million pounds")],
 "d3": [C("d3", 2024, 4.2, "road repair spending reached 4.2 million pounds in 2024"), C("d3", 2023, 3.4, "the council confirmed a 24 per cent rise")],
 "d6": [C("d6", 2024, 0.8, "extra 0.8 million pounds for storm damage", "storm programme extra")],
 "d7": [C("d7", 2024, 0.3, "0.3 million pound government grant", "grant")],
 "d8": [C("d8", 2024, 4.5, "spent 4.5 million pounds on roads in 2024"), C("d8", 2024, 0.3, "0.3 million pound government grant", "grant")]}
reader = ScriptedModel(lambda msgs, tools, system: json.dumps(READER[re.search(r"DOC (d\d)", msgs[-1]["content"]).group(1)]))

claims = []
for i in to_read:
    claims += json.loads(text_of(reader.complete([{"role": "user", "content": f"DOC {i}: extract claims with verbatim quotes\n{DOCS[i]['text']}"}])))
norm = lambda s: re.sub(r"\s+", " ", s.lower())
good = [c for c in claims if norm(c["quote"]) in norm(DOCS[c["doc"]]["text"])]      # the quote must really be in the source
for c in claims:
    if c not in good: print(f"REJECTED  {c['doc']}: quote not found in source: {c['quote']!r}")

groups = defaultdict(list)
for c in good: groups[(c["metric"], c["year"])].append(c)
print(f"\n{'claim':<28}{'value':>6}  supported by (independent origins)   status")
for (metric, year), cs in sorted(groups.items()):
    by_value = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(set))
    for c in cs: by_value[c["value"]][origin(c["doc"])].add(c["doc"])
    status = "CONFLICT" if len(by_value) > 1 else ("corroborated" if len(by_value[cs[0]["value"]]) > 1 else "single source")
    for v, origins in sorted(by_value.items()):
        docs = ", ".join(f"{o}" + (f"(+{','.join(sorted(d - {o}))})" if d - {o} else "") for o, d in sorted(origins.items()))
        print(f"{metric + ' ' + str(year):<28}{v:>6}  {docs:<36} {status}")
    if status == "CONFLICT":
        vs = sorted(by_value); gap = round(vs[-1] - vs[0], 2)
        match = [c for c in good if c["metric"] != metric and c["value"] == gap]
        print(f"  -> values differ by {gap}; a verified claim of {gap} exists in {sorted({c['doc'] for c in match})} ({match[0]['metric']})")

# the writer's draft is audited sentence by sentence before anyone sees it
NUM = {d: n for n, d in enumerate(sorted({c["doc"] for c in good}), 1)}
print("\nSOURCES:", {f"[{n}]": DOCS[d]["title"] for d, n in NUM.items()})
draft = ["Northvale spent 4.2 million pounds on road repair in 2024 [1][2].", "That is up from 3.4 million pounds in 2023 [1][2].",
         "The rise is 24 per cent [1].", "A regional paper reports 4.5 million pounds, which includes a 0.3 million pound grant [6][5].",
         "Spending was driven by storm damage."]

    print(("FLAG " if problems else "ok   ") + s + (f"   <- {'; '.join(problems)}" if problems else ""))

Read the output against the corpus.

  • The fabricated claim was rejected before it reached the cross-check. The reading step produced a claim from the Courier page with the quote "the council confirmed a 24 per cent rise". That sentence is not in the page. (The Courier says spending was "up from 3.4 million"; the 24 per cent is arithmetic the model did, and then dressed as a quote.)
  • The 2023 figure is corroborated by two independent primary sources. The Courier's 3.4 is not counted as a third.
  • The 2024 figure is in conflict. The accounts, the audit office and (through the accounts) the Courier say 4.2; the Gazette says 4.5. The list shows 4.2 supported by two origins, and 4.5 by one. A good agent neither picks the majority silently nor averages the two. It notices that the difference, 0.3, equals the grant reported in two other documents, and concludes that the figures probably differ in definition (council-funded spending versus total including a grant). It then says so in the report, with both numbers and their sources. It does not assert the explanation as fact, because no document states it.
  • The forum and blog pages never entered. Triage skipped them. Had the forum's "6 million" been read, it would have appeared as a single-source value in conflict, outranked by the origins above it.

What to do with disagreement

  1. Check that the claims measure the same thing. Same metric, same period, same unit, same scope (the 4.2 versus 4.5 case), same date of publication (a figure from before a restatement).
  2. Prefer closeness to the source. A primary record outranks a report of it, and an audit of the record outranks the record alone for "is it accurate", though not for "what did the council say".
  3. Look for an explanation in the evidence you already hold, as the demo does with the 0.3. If one fits, present it as a hypothesis with its support.
  4. If nothing resolves it, report the disagreement. "Sources differ: A says X, B says Y" is a legitimate finding, and often the most useful sentence in a report.
  5. Search again, targeted. A conflict is the best trigger for a new round of searching, with a specific question ("how does the Gazette define 'spent on roads'?"). This is the loop back from cross-check in the pipeline diagram.

Citing, and checking that the citation is true

A citation is a promise: the sentence you are reading is supported by the cited source. A research product is worth only as much as that promise. Breaking it comes in three grades of increasing subtlety.

Fabricated

The source does not exist, or the quote does not appear in it. A string comparison catches this. It is the grade the demo's check addresses.

Misattributed

The source is real and the quote is present, but the sentence claims more than the quote says ("up 24 per cent" next to a source with two raw figures), or the quote is from a different context (a figure for the county, cited for the town).

Unsupported framing

Every fact is cited, but the synthesis, "spending was driven by storm damage", states a cause that no source states. The audit's "no citation" rule catches the uncited version; a cited version needs a judge.

How common is this? Liu, Zhang and Liang (2023), in "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines", audited four generative search engines (Bing Chat, NeevaAI, Perplexity.ai and YouChat) with human annotators. They found that on average only 51.5% of generated sentences were fully supported by their citations, and only 74.5% of citations supported the sentence they were attached to. Those systems and that year are not today's, and a figure from 2023 should not be taken as the rate for a current product. The finding still shapes the design: a citation is a claim to be verified, not evidence of quality, and a response densely decorated with links can have both low precision (citations that do not support the sentence) and low recall (supported sentences with no citation).

A practical audit has three layers, ordered from cheap and certain to expensive and fuzzy.

  1. Mechanical checks, in code. Every citation marker resolves to a fetched document. Every quoted string appears in the document (after normalising whitespace and case). Every number in a sentence appears in a verified claim attached to the cited sources. Every factual sentence has a citation. The demo's audit does the last three. These checks are deterministic, cheap and run on every report.
  2. Derived numbers computed by code. Percentages, differences and totals should be computed from the verified inputs by a function, and cited to the inputs. (4.2 - 3.4) / 3.4 is 23.5 per cent, and the sentence should say "about 24 per cent, calculated from [1]". Never let the model do arithmetic and cite it as if the source said so.
  3. Entailment checks by a second model. For each (sentence, cited passage) pair, ask a separate model call: "does this passage support this sentence, partly, or not at all?" This is the layer that catches the misattributed and the unsupported framing. It is itself a model and can be wrong, so measure it against human-labelled examples (course A6, and the groundedness material in A5 chapter 16).

Anthropic's post describes a dedicated citation agent that runs after research finishes: it processes the documents and the report and identifies the specific locations in the sources where each claim should be cited. Attaching citations after writing, by a separate step with its own checks, keeps the writer from inventing them. Whichever way you build it, resist the shortcut of asking the writing model to "include citations" in one pass and trusting the result.

Writing the long report

By this stage the system holds a set of verified claims with sources, a list of conflicts and open questions, and a plan. Writing is the stage most like ordinary generation, which is also why it is the stage where unsupported text slips in. Some design rules.

  • Write from the claims, not from memory. The writer's input is the verified claim set and the outline, not the raw pages and not the original question alone. The instruction is "use only these claims; if a point is not covered, say it is not covered."
  • Go section by section for long reports. A 5,000-word report written in one call strains output limits and attention. Write an outline, then each section from the claims assigned to it, and keep claim ids in the text ([c17]) until a final pass turns them into numbered citations. This also lets you regenerate one section without touching the others.
  • Separate findings from interpretation. Label what sources say, what the agent infers (with the reasoning), and what is unknown. The storm-damage sentence in the demo is an inference from two documents that mention storms near the increase; the report should present it as such.
  • Surface the conflicts and the gaps. A short "Where sources disagree" and "What I could not find" section raises trust more than a smooth narrative.
  • Calibrate language to evidence. Corroborated by independent primary sources: "spent". Single source: "according to the Gazette". Conflict: "reported as either". The status from the cross-check maps directly to the verb.
  • Attach the evidence table. A final appendix listing each claim, its sources and its status lets a reader audit the report without redoing it.

Evaluating a research agent

Evaluating research is hard because the answer is long, partly subjective and changes as the web changes. Three kinds of evidence are useful, each with limits.

Benchmarks of searching ability. BrowseComp, released by OpenAI in April 2025, has 1,266 questions that require persistently browsing the internet for hard-to-find, entangled information. Its answers are short and easy to check, so grading is simple. The authors compare it to programming-competition benchmarks for coding agents: incomplete but useful. They also say what it leaves out, which matters here: it does not measure long-form answer generation or the handling of ambiguous user queries, so it does not reflect the full distribution of real requests. It tells you something about whether an agent can find a needle; it does not tell you whether a 3,000-word report is correct, balanced and honestly cited. In Anthropic's account, token usage alone explained about 80% of the variance in BrowseComp performance (chapter 14), so a benchmark score is also partly a measure of how much compute was spent.

Rubric-based LLM judging of reports. Anthropic's post describes scoring outputs on factual accuracy (do claims match sources), citation accuracy (do cited sources match the claims), completeness, source quality (primary over lower-quality secondary sources) and tool efficiency (the right tools a reasonable number of times). The authors report that a single model call with one prompt, giving 0 to 1 scores and a pass or fail, was the most consistent and closest to human judgement. They also report that human testing found edge cases that automated evaluation missed, giving the example of agents choosing SEO-optimised pages over authoritative ones. Those criteria are a good starting rubric for your own evaluations: notice they separate factual accuracy from citation accuracy, because a report can be right for the wrong reasons.

Your own gold set. Build twenty to fifty questions from your domain with a known answer, a list of sources that must be found, and some sources that must be avoided. Track four numbers over time: answer accuracy, citation precision (cited sources that support the sentence), citation recall (supported sentences that have a citation) and cost per report. Re-run after every change to prompts, tools or models, as with any other component (course A6). Re-check periodically too, because pages move and a gold answer from last year may be wrong today.

State of play, October 2026

Benchmark scores for research agents rise quickly and the leaderboards are crowded with vendor-reported numbers that use different tools, budgets and graders. This chapter deliberately gives no current scores. BrowseComp's design (1,266 questions, short checkable answers) is from its April 2025 paper; for current results, read the benchmark's own page and the model providers' technical reports, and note the budget each result used. A published figure for a research product is a statement about that product, that benchmark and that week.

Product concerns that engineering has to meet

  • Latency is minutes, so show progress. Stream the plan, then "searching (4 of 6 sub-questions)", then sources found. A user who sees evidence of work waits; a user who sees a spinner leaves. Allow cancellation, and refund or stop the spend when they do.
  • Runs must be resumable. A fifteen-minute job will meet a deploy. Journal the plan, the claims and the fetched page text so that the run resumes at the interrupted stage and does not re-fetch pages that may since have changed (chapter 15). Storing the fetched text also lets you re-verify quotes later.
  • Cost is per report. Budget it in tokens, searches and dollars; show an estimate; cap it. A research system that can spend without limit will, on an ambiguous question.
  • Provenance is part of the output. Keep the retrieval date with every source; the web changes, and "as of 14 October 2026" belongs in the report.
  • Respect the sites you read. Observe access rules and terms, identify your crawler, and do not paywall-bypass. These are legal and ethical limits as well as engineering ones, and they vary by jurisdiction and by use; ask the people responsible for compliance, do not guess.
  • Private plus public data is dangerous. If the same agent can read internal documents and the open web, queries can leak what the documents contain, and pages can instruct the agent to do so. Separate the roles, or keep internal and external research in different runs, and see chapter 19.

Studio 2 asks you to design a research product end to end, with the cost controls, progress updates and quality checks above. Case study 1 reads Anthropic's account of its multi-agent research system in detail.

Key takeaways

  • Deep research is a loop of plan, search, read and cross-check that ends in a cited report. The loop back from cross-check, triggered by gaps and conflicts, is what separates it from a single search.
  • Search is cheap and parallel; reading is expensive. Deduplicate, triage on snippets with a source prior, and read in separate contexts so the lead holds claims, not pages. Give the loop a hard budget and a soft stopping rule, and report which fired.
  • Make readers return claims with verbatim quotes and metadata, not summaries. Check each quote against the page text in code and drop claims that fail.
  • Count independent origins, not documents. Do not average conflicting figures: check they measure the same thing, look for an explanation in the evidence, and report the disagreement when unresolved.
  • A citation is a claim to be verified. Audit mechanically (markers resolve, quotes appear, numbers are in cited claims, every factual sentence is cited), compute derived numbers in code, and use a second model for entailment.
  • Pages are untrusted input. Keep the reading component low-privilege and never combine private data, untrusted pages and an outbound channel in one agent.
  • Evaluate on rubric dimensions (factual accuracy, citation accuracy, completeness, source quality, efficiency) plus your own gold set. A search benchmark such as BrowseComp measures finding things, not writing a trustworthy report.

Check your understanding

Four news sites each report "the agency spent 12 million" and each says "according to the agency's press release". How many independent sources support the figure?

  1. Four, because four publishers wrote it
  2. One: the press release, which is the origin the others quote
  3. Zero, because news is never a source
  4. Five, counting the press release as well as the four stories
Independence is about origin. All four stories trace to the same document, so they add reach and no extra evidence. News is not worthless (it may add context or be the only record), but it does not corroborate its own source. Counting documents inflates confidence, which is what the evidence board in this chapter shows.

A reading model returns the claim "sales rose 18 per cent" with the quote "revenue grew by nearly a fifth". The quote does appear in the page, and the page gives 17.6 per cent in a table. What should the pipeline do?

  1. Accept it: the quote check passed
  2. Reject the claim because the model rounded
  3. Average 17.6 and 18
  4. Store the claim with the table's 17.6 as the value; compute any rounding in code and cite the source, and let an entailment check judge whether "18" is supported
Passing the substring test shows the quote is real, not that the claim follows from it. A number that is not in the cited source should come from code operating on source values, with the inputs cited. Rejecting every rounding is too strict, and averaging a rounded figure with its own source is meaningless.

Two primary documents say a project cost 4.2 million; a newspaper says 4.5. The 0.3 gap equals a grant described in two other documents. What should the report do?

  1. State 4.2, mention 4.5 with its source, and offer the grant as a possible reason for the difference, labelled as an inference
  2. Report 4.35, the average
  3. Report only 4.2 because most sources agree
  4. Report 4.5 because it is the larger and so more complete figure
The evidence supports preferring 4.2 and suggests a definitional explanation, which no document states outright, so it is presented as a hypothesis. Averaging invents a number nobody reported. Silent majority-rule hides a disagreement the reader would want to know about. "Larger is more complete" assumes the explanation as fact.

In the cost model, giving each reader its own context left the tokens read unchanged but cut the lead agent's context from 72,000 to 4,800 tokens and the time from 96 to 16 seconds. Which statement about this result is correct?

  1. It shows that parallel readers lower the bill
  2. It shows that the time saving is unlimited as readers are added
  3. The gain comes from isolating page text and running reads at the same time; the total tokens are the same, and the slowest reader and the serial stages limit how far it goes
  4. It proves that snippet triage is unnecessary
Both numbers are assumptions, not measurements. Total tokens read were 79,200 in rows three and four. Wall-clock time in a fan-out is set by the slowest branch plus the serial planning and writing, so it does not fall without limit. Triage is what cut the tokens from 186,000 to 79,200.

A research agent can read the user's private files, browse the web and make web requests. A web page tells it to put the contents of a private file into a search query. Which design change addresses the root cause?

  1. Add a sentence to the system prompt saying to ignore instructions in web pages
  2. Split roles so the component that reads untrusted pages has no access to private data, or run private and web research as separate jobs
  3. Use shorter search queries
  4. Increase the number of citations in each report
The harm needs private data, untrusted content and an outbound channel in one place; removing a leg breaks the attack. A prompt instruction helps but cannot be relied on, since the model cannot reliably tell instructions from data. Query length and citation counts are unrelated to the leak.

An evaluation shows your agent scores well on a browsing benchmark with short answers, yet users complain that reports contain unsupported claims. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. The benchmark is wrong and should be ignored
  2. Users are mistaken, since the benchmark is objective
  3. The agent needs a larger model
  4. The benchmark measures finding hard-to-find facts, not whether a long report's citations support its sentences; add citation-precision and recall checks and a rubric over reports
BrowseComp's authors say it leaves out long-form generation, so a high score and an unreliable report can coexist. The benchmark is useful for what it measures. A larger model may help or not; measure the failure first with a citation audit, then choose the fix.
