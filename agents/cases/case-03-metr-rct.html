Measuring AI Coding Productivity

In July 2025 the research group METR published a randomised controlled trial of experienced open-source developers working on their own repositories. Before the study they expected AI tools to make them about 24% faster. After it they still believed they had been about 20% faster. The measured result was that tasks with AI allowed took 19% longer. This case is about what the trial did, what it can and cannot show, and why the gap between feeling fast and being fast is the real finding.

What I could and could not read

I read METR's blog post of 10 July 2025, the paper (Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity", arXiv:2507.09089) as text from its HTML and PDF versions, and METR's follow-up post of 24 February 2026. The paper is long and I read the sections with the design, results, factors and caveats, not every appendix. The confidence interval for the main estimate I took from the 2026 post, because the paper's own interval was in a figure and appendix I could not extract. I did not re-run anything; the simulation in "Try it yourself" is my own and uses made-up task times.

The pattern in one sentence

Do not rely on how a tool feels, or on what experts predict, to know whether it helps: randomise the work, measure the outcome you care about, and report the uncertainty.

Context: a claim everyone had an opinion on

By 2025 AI coding tools were widely used, and most evidence for their benefit was either a benchmark (can the model solve a self-contained task?), a vendor study on tasks chosen for the tool, or a developer's self-report. Each has a known weakness. Benchmarks like those in the SWE-agent case do not measure a person working in a codebase they know well. Vendor tasks are chosen to suit the tool. Self-report is what this case turns on. METR designed a study that avoids all three: real work, no task selection by the tool maker, and an objective-ish outcome, time to finish.

Randomised controlled trial (RCT)
An experiment where units (here, tasks) are assigned to conditions by chance, so that other differences average out and the difference in outcome can be attributed to the condition.
Treatment and control
The two conditions compared. Here the treatment is "AI tools allowed" and the control is "AI tools not allowed" on a given task.
Confidence interval
A range of effect sizes consistent with the data under the study's assumptions. A wide interval means the study does not pin the number down. See statistics for evals.
Perception gap
The difference between what people believe a tool did for them and what a measurement shows.
External validity
How far a result can be carried to other people, tasks, tools and times. A well-run trial can have limited external validity.

The design

According to METR's post and paper, the study recruited 16 experienced open-source developers. Each worked on large, mature repositories they already contributed to: on average about 23,000 stars, 1,100,000 lines of code, and around 5 years of the developer's own history in the project (59% of its lifetime, with about 1,500 commits). The developers supplied 246 real issues (bug fixes, features, refactors) from their own backlogs, averaging about two hours each. Each issue was randomly assigned to "AI allowed" (136 issues) or "AI disallowed" (110). When AI was allowed, developers could use any tools; the primary one was Cursor Pro, an AI-assisted editor, with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet. Developers were paid $150 an hour, recorded their screens, and reported their own implementation time. Of the developers, 93% had used LLM tools before but only 44% had used Cursor.

Randomising per issue is the key idea. A developer's skill, the repository and the day of the week do not differ systematically between the two groups, because every developer contributes to both. What can differ is the issues themselves: by chance, the AI-allowed set can be slightly harder. The paper reports that developers' own forecasts of how long each issue would take, made before the coin flip, were a little longer for the AI-allowed issues, and it uses those forecasts in the regression to correct for the imbalance (see below).

The results

The headline from METR: with AI allowed, developers took 19% longer. The 2026 follow-up post gives the 95% confidence interval for that estimate as +2% to +39%, so the data are consistent with a small or a large slowdown but, under the study's assumptions, not with no effect. The paper notes that the raw ratio of average times was a larger 34% slowdown; the regression corrects for the harder-by-chance AI-allowed issues using developers' pre-assignment time forecasts, which is why the headline is 19%.

What makes the study memorable is the comparison with expectations. Everything below is as stated by METR.

The paper reports that 34 economics experts forecast a 39% reduction in completion time and 54 machine-learning experts 38%. The developers' own forecast of 24% before the study fell only to a belief of 20% after it, which the paper calls a belief that AI had sped them up even though they were measurably slowed down. So three groups with expertise, plus the participants who had just lived the experience, were wrong in the same direction. That is the finding about perception that gives the case its title.

Why might it be slower? The factors

The paper examines around 20 hypotheses (my count of its summary list is 21) for why the result looks as it does and sorts them by how plausibly each contributes. It marks five as likely contributing to the slowdown: developers' over-optimism about how useful AI would be; their high familiarity with their repositories; the repositories being large and complex; the AI's low reliability; and implicit repository context that the AI lacked. It lists ten as mixed or unclear and six as unlikely, among them that the developers lacked experience with the environment, that they cheated or under-used the tool, or that the outcome measure was not robust. The paper's activity data add texture: in recordings, developers accepted fewer than 44% of Cursor's generations, spent about 9% of their time reviewing and cleaning AI output, and 56% said they often made major changes to what the AI produced.

Notice what this suggests. The developers were experts in code they knew intimately; the AI's value is plausibly greatest where the human lacks context, and least where the human has it all in their head. That is a hypothesis the study does not test, so treat it as one, and as a pointer to how the result may differ for newcomers or unfamiliar codebases. It matches the advice in the working with AI coding tools chapter about giving the tool the context you hold implicitly (instruction files, plans) and checking its output.

What the study does not show

METR is careful about this, and so should readers be. The blog post and paper say the study does not show that AI slows most developers; that it fails to speed up work in other settings, for less experienced developers or in unfamiliar codebases; or that future tools or better usage cannot produce a speed-up. They also note that:

  • The sample is small. Sixteen developers is a small, possibly unrepresentative group, and recruitment could have selected for developers with particular views.
  • The study was not blinded. Nobody could be unaware of which condition they were in; the paper says so.
  • The outcome is self-reported. Total time came from the developers, with screen recordings as a check. METR lists self-report inaccuracy as a limitation.
  • Learning effects. Only 44% had used Cursor before. The paper finds no clear learning effect over the first 30 to 50 hours of use but says it cannot rule out larger effects beyond that; the blog post flags the same limit.
  • Subgroups are underpowered. The paper says it is not powered for significant results when the data are cut into subsets, so splits by developer or task type are suggestive only.

Common misconception

"A randomised trial proves AI coding tools slow developers down." It shows that, for these 16 developers on these 246 tasks with early-2025 tools, tasks took longer, with an interval from +2% to +39%. Randomisation gives you a trustworthy comparison inside the study; it does not give you generality beyond it. The opposite error, "it was only 16 people so ignore it", also misses the point: a small, well-run trial is better evidence than a large survey of feelings.

What happened next

METR planned a follow-up: from August 2025 it ran a larger experiment with late-2025 tools. In its post of 24 February 2026 it reports that the new design was compromised by selection effects. More developers declined to take part because they would not work without AI, which METR says likely biases the estimate of speed-up downwards; and in surveys 30% to 50% of developers said they skipped submitting some tasks they did not want to do without AI, so high-uplift tasks were under-represented. Pay had also fallen, from $150 to $50 an hour, which METR thinks may have contributed.

The post still gives estimates, with strong caveats: about -18% time (a speed-up) for the 10 returning developers, with an interval of -38% to +9%, and about -4% for 47 newly recruited developers, interval -15% to +9%, across 800 or more tasks and 143 repositories. Both intervals include zero, and METR says the data are weak evidence. METR also writes that it believes developers are likely more sped up in early 2026 than in early 2025, but that the compromised data is very weak support for how much. It labels the original study as out of date.

State of play, October 2026

The 19% slowdown is a snapshot of early-2025 tools and 16 specific developers; METR itself marks it as superseded and has not produced a clean replacement. At the time of writing I found no controlled estimate for current tools that METR considers reliable. Treat any single productivity number you see quoted, for or against, as dated and conditional, and check whether the study randomised, what it measured and over what population.

Try it yourself

Try it yourself

The script simulates trials shaped like METR's: 136 AI-allowed and 110 control tasks, task times that are heavy-tailed (a few tasks take far longer than most), and a true slowdown of 19% that I put in by hand. The spread parameter is my assumption, not METR's. It asks how often one trial of this size gets the answer roughly right. Change sigma to 0.5 and then 1.2 and watch the intervals tighten and widen.

"""Simulate a randomised trial shaped like METR's: 246 tasks, heavy-tailed times,
a TRUE slowdown of 19%, then ask how well one trial pins the number down. Illustrative."""
import random, math, statistics as st
random.seed(7)
def trial(true_ratio=1.19, n_ai=136, n_no=110, sigma=0.9, mu=math.log(2.0)):
    ai  = [random.lognormvariate(mu, sigma) * true_ratio for _ in range(n_ai)]
    no  = [random.lognormvariate(mu, sigma) for _ in range(n_no)]
    lr = math.log(st.mean(ai) / st.mean(no))
    return lr, ai, no
def boot(ai, no, B=400):
    out = []
    for _ in range(B):
        a = [random.choice(ai) for _ in ai]; b = [random.choice(no) for _ in no]
        out.append(math.log(st.mean(a) / st.mean(b)))
    out.sort(); return math.exp(out[int(.025*B)]) - 1, math.exp(out[int(.975*B)]) - 1
lr, ai, no = trial()
lo, hi = boot(ai, no)
print(f"one simulated trial: estimated change in time {math.exp(lr)-1:+.0%}, 95% interval {lo:+.0%} to {hi:+.0%}")
est, covers_zero, wrong_sign = [], 0, 0
for _ in range(300):
    lr, ai, no = trial()
    est.append(math.exp(lr) - 1)
    lo, hi = boot(ai, no, B=100)
    covers_zero += lo <= 0
    wrong_sign += (math.exp(lr) - 1) < 0
est.sort()
print(f"300 repeats: estimates ranged {est[7]:+.0%} to {est[292]:+.0%} (middle 95%), median {st.median(est):+.0%}")
print(f"  intervals that include 'no effect': {covers_zero/300:.0%}; trials that got the sign wrong: {wrong_sign/300:.0%}")
# the perception gap, using the paper's reported belief numbers
actual = 1.19
believed_speedup = 0.20         # developers' reported belief after the study
believed_time = 1 / (1 + believed_speedup)   # time ratio if "20% faster" means 20% more work per hour
print(f"believed time ratio {believed_time:.2f}, measured {actual:.2f}: tasks took {actual/believed_time:.2f} times as long as developers thought")
one simulated trial: estimated change in time +11%, 95% interval -16% to +49%
300 repeats: estimates ranged -10% to +53% (middle 95%), median +21%
  intervals that include 'no effect': 69%; trials that got the sign wrong: 10%
believed time ratio 0.83, measured 1.19: tasks took 1.43 times as long as developers thought

Even though the slowdown is real by construction, a single trial of this size produces estimates that range from a 10% speed-up to a 53% slowdown, and about two trials in three have an interval that includes "no effect", with one in ten getting the sign wrong. METR's reported interval (+2% to +39%) is narrower than my simulated one, plausibly because its analysis adjusts for task difficulty, but the lesson holds: with heavy-tailed task times and a few hundred tasks, intervals are wide, and a point estimate alone misleads. The last line of the output shows the gap that matters. Developers who believed they were about 20% faster (time ratio 0.83) had in fact taken 1.19 times as long, so tasks took about 1.4 times as long as they believed.

Systems connection

This is the discipline of the model to value chapter: offline impressions do not stand in for an A/B test on the outcome the business cares about. Stopping an experiment early because the number looks good is the trap in the peeking case; the heavy tails that widen the interval here are the same ones that make averages of latency misleading in metrics and percentiles. For measuring agent systems in general, see evaluating agents.

Lessons

  • Distrust felt productivity. Feeling fast and being fast diverged by a wide margin, in the people who had done the work.
  • Randomise at the unit that varies. Assigning tasks, not people, let each developer be their own control.
  • Report the interval, not only the point. The same trial supports "small slowdown" and "large slowdown"; it does not support "no effect".
  • State the population. Experienced developers in repositories they know are one corner of software work.
  • Expect measurement to change behaviour. The follow-up shows how an experiment can be undermined when participants will not work without the tool.
  • Measure your own team. If you roll out a coding tool, compare like with like on your own tasks; the AI coding tools chapter suggests how, and the coding-assistant rollout studio practises it.

Check your understanding

Why did METR randomise individual issues to "AI allowed" or "AI disallowed" rather than assigning some developers to each condition?

  1. To make sure all developers used Cursor
  2. So each developer contributes to both conditions, removing differences in skill and repository from the comparison
  3. Because only 16 developers took part, so splitting them would leave too few tasks
  4. To blind developers to the condition
Within-developer comparison is the point of per-issue randomisation. The study was not blinded, and the sample size is a limitation but is not the reason for the design.

After the study, developers still believed AI had made them about 20% faster. What does this show?

  1. The measurement must be wrong, because the developers were there
  2. AI tools only help on tasks developers enjoy
  3. Developers were lying to the researchers
  4. Self-reported impressions of productivity can diverge sharply from measured time, so they are weak evidence for whether a tool helps
The result is a perception gap, not dishonesty. It does not mean the measurement is infallible either (METR lists self-reported time as a limitation), only that feelings were the worse guide here.

Which statement is best supported by the study?

  1. For these developers, on these tasks, with early-2025 tools, tasks with AI took longer; the study does not show this holds for other developers, tools or later times
  2. AI coding tools slow down all experienced developers
  3. AI coding tools will never speed up experienced developers
  4. Less experienced developers are slowed down more
METR states explicitly that the study does not show the second, third or fourth claims. The fourth was not tested.

In the simulation, why do about two thirds of trials produce an interval that includes "no effect" although the true slowdown is 19%?

  1. The simulation has a bug
  2. Randomisation is unreliable
  3. With heavy-tailed task times and a few hundred tasks, the averages are noisy, so intervals are wide compared with the effect
  4. A 19% slowdown is not a real effect
The effect is real by construction; the sample just cannot always resolve it. Randomisation protects against bias, not against noise. METR's reported interval is narrower than this toy's, but its interval of +2% to +39% is still wide.
