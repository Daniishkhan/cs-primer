Lab 4: Workflow Patterns & Budgets

Chapter 13 gave you the vocabulary: chains, routers, parallel fan-out and orchestrator-workers are ordinary programs with model calls inside them. Chapter 14 added the warning that every extra call costs tokens, money and time. In this lab you build all four patterns as plain Python over a deterministic stand-in model, put a meter under every call, and then use the meter to answer the questions that decide real designs: which pattern is cheap, which is fast, what a gate saves, what a confidence threshold costs, why voting stops helping, and how to make an orchestrator stay inside a budget it cannot argue with.

Why this lab matters

Arguments about agent architecture are usually arguments about taste. The numbers settle most of them, and the numbers are cheap to collect if the meter exists from the start. A chain looks like "two calls instead of one", but the gate in the middle means one of the failure paths costs half as much as the success path. A router looks like "an extra call per ticket", but it moves the expensive work onto a cheaper tier for most tickets. A vote looks like "more accuracy for more tokens", until you measure it on items that fool every judge alike. An orchestrator looks like "parallel workers", but the number of workers is chosen by a model, so the bill is partly under the model's control until your code takes it back. The aim is the habit of the workflow chapter, made concrete: write the least autonomous program that works, measure it, and let the measurements, not the diagram, tell you where to spend. Along the way you meet the practical forms of multi-agent cost: fan-out shortens the clock without shrinking the bill, and a cap that lives in code is the only cap that holds.

Deterministic, offline, and the numbers are assumptions

The "model" is a stand-in function in fixtures.py that behaves differently depending on the first word of the system prompt (EXTRACT, CLASSIFY, JUDGE-3 and so on). It is not a language model, so nothing here measures how good a real model is; it makes every token count, price and time exact and repeatable, which is what a budget lab needs. Prices and latencies are illustrative assumptions, set in two tables at the top of your file: a "small" and a "large" tier with a twelve-fold price gap and a slower large tier. They are not any vendor's rates, which change often; replace the two tables with your own provider's numbers (and their tokeniser's counts) to size a real system. Tokens come from the stand-in tokeniser in llm.py, about four characters per token. Time is simulated by the meter, never slept, so the whole check takes a fraction of a second. Property checks print their seed on failure (python3 check.py solution 5 12 replays milestone 5 with seed 12). Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only.

What you'll build

The desk handles customer-support tickets, the running example of chapter 13: find the order a customer means, write a reply, route refunds and account problems to specialists, check drafts in parallel, and compare refund policies across branches. Six milestones:

  1. The meter: price and time for one call, a Meter that records every call with its tier, and fan_out, which makes the clock stop at the slowest branch.
  2. A chain with gates: extract, check, look up in plain code, draft, check; a failed gate stops the chain before the next model call is paid for. Plus the arithmetic of compounding errors.
  3. A router: classify with a confidence, hand the ticket to a specialist on the right tier or to a human, and choose the threshold from an evaluation.
  4. Parallel fan-out: sectioning (independent checks at once) and voting (the same question to several judges), and the gap between the promised and the measured gain.
  5. Orchestrator-workers inside a budget: a plan whose size comes from the input, a cap on workers, a budget that refuses calls, and an answer that says what it left out.
  6. The whole desk: route, then chain or hand off, under a per-ticket budget, summarised as totals and percentiles.

Milestone 1: the meter

Before any pattern, a measuring instrument. Every model call has four numbers worth recording: tokens in (the system prompt plus everything sent), tokens out, the dollars they cost, and the seconds they take. The first two come from the response's usage. The other two are functions of the model tier, because providers sell a cheap fast model and an expensive slow one and workflows are largely the art of using each where it pays.

Cost is linear in tokens with different prices for input and output: call_cost = (tokens_in × input price + tokens_out × output price) / 1,000,000. With this lab's assumed small-tier prices ($0.25 and $1.25 per million tokens), a call with 1,000 tokens in and 200 out costs (1,000 × 0.25 + 200 × 1.25) / 1,000,000 = $0.0005. Latency is an overhead plus a term per token, larger for output (a model generates one token at a time) than for input.

The subtle method is fan_out. Work that runs in parallel does not add up on the clock; the wait is set by the slowest branch, the effect behind tail latency at scale. The simulation runs the branches one after another in Python, but it rewinds the clock to the same starting point for each, and afterwards leaves the clock at the latest finish. Two numbers fall out and you should keep both: seconds, the critical path (the longest chain of calls that depend on each other, which is what the user waits for), and work_seconds, the sum over all calls (what you would wait with no parallelism, and a proxy for the capacity you consume).

def call_cost(tier: str, tokens_in: int, tokens_out: int) -> float:
    """Dollars for one call: (tokens_in x input price + tokens_out x output price) / 1,000,000."""
    pin, pout = PRICES[tier]
    return (tokens_in * pin + tokens_out * pout) / 1_000_000

def call_seconds(tier: str, tokens_in: int, tokens_out: int) -> float:
    """Simulated seconds for one call: overhead + tokens_in x per-input + tokens_out x per-output."""
    base, per_in, per_out = LATENCY[tier]
    return base + tokens_in * per_in + tokens_out * per_out

class Meter:
    """Wraps a model so that every call is recorded, and keeps a simulated clock."""

    def __init__(self, model):
        self.model = model
        self.calls: List[Call] = []
        self.clock = 0.0                     # simulated seconds elapsed on the critical path

    def ask(self, step: str, system: str, user: str, tier: str = "small") -> str:
        """One model call with `user` as the only message. Record a Call from the response's usage,
        advance the clock by the call's seconds, and return the reply text."""
        r = self.model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": user}], system=system)
        tin, tout = r.usage["input_tokens"], r.usage["output_tokens"]
        secs = call_seconds(tier, tin, tout)
        self.calls.append(Call(step, tier, tin, tout, secs, call_cost(tier, tin, tout)))
        self.clock += secs
        return text_of(r)

    def fan_out(self, thunks: List[Callable[[], Any]]) -> List[Any]:
        """Run the thunks as if in parallel and return their results in order.

        They run one after another in this simulation, but each starts at the same clock reading,
        and afterwards the clock stands at the latest finish time among them: the slowest branch
        sets the wait. An empty list leaves the clock alone.
        """
        start, ends, results = self.clock, [], []
        for thunk in thunks:
            self.clock = start
            results.append(thunk())
            ends.append(self.clock)
        self.clock = max(ends, default=start)
        return results

    def totals(self) -> Dict[str, Any]:
        """calls, tokens_in, tokens_out, cost_usd, seconds (the clock: wall time on the critical path)
        and work_seconds (the sum of every call's seconds: what you would wait with no parallelism)."""
        return {"calls": len(self.calls), "tokens_in": sum(c.tokens_in for c in self.calls),
                "tokens_out": sum(c.tokens_out for c in self.calls),
                "cost_usd": sum(c.cost_usd for c in self.calls), "seconds": self.clock,
                "work_seconds": sum(c.seconds for c in self.calls)}

    def by_step(self) -> Dict[str, Dict[str, Any]]:
        """Per step name, in first-seen order: calls, tokens_in, tokens_out, cost_usd."""
        out: Dict[str, Dict[str, Any]] = {}
        for c in self.calls:
            row = out.setdefault(c.step, {"calls": 0, "tokens_in": 0, "tokens_out": 0, "cost_usd": 0.0})
            row["calls"] += 1
            row["tokens_in"] += c.tokens_in
            row["tokens_out"] += c.tokens_out
            row["cost_usd"] += c.cost_usd
        return out

Milestone 2: a chain with gates

A chain splits a task into steps in a fixed order, each model call working on the previous one's output. A gate is a check in plain code between two steps that decides whether to continue; it is cheap, exact and testable, and it is where everything you can verify without a model belongs. The support chain has two model steps with a lookup between them:

  1. extract (model): read the order id and the issue from the free-text ticket, as JSON.
  2. Gate 1 (code): is the id a real order? If not, stop. No later model call is made.
  3. lookup (code): fetch the order. A database read is not a language task.
  4. draft (model): write a reply from the facts only.
  5. Gate 2 (code): does the reply name the order and stay under 41 words?

Token accounting is the point of the milestone, so the contract fixes exactly what each call sends: the extract call sends the ticket text unchanged; the draft call sends "FACTS:" followed by json.dumps(facts, sort_keys=True). That makes your token counts reproducible and lets the checker compute them independently. Here is a real run on two tickets, one that names an order and one that does not:

T01 ok     calls=2 in= 81 out= 38 $0.000068  ok
T04 gate1  calls=1 in= 41 out=  9 $0.000022  no valid order id

The second ticket costs about a third as much as the first (41 tokens in and 9 out, against 81 and 38), and it ends with a clean "no valid order id" instead of a draft about nothing. Without gate 1 the draft step would be asked to write a reply about None, and a model asked to be helpful about None will often invent an order. A gate turns a silent wrong answer into a loud, cheap, handled one.

The arithmetic of compounding errors

Suppose each of n independent steps is right with probability p. All of them are right with probability pn: 0.95 = 0.590, so a five-step chain of 90% steps is wrong 41% of the time. Gates do not change that 59%. What they change is how the other 41% looks. Let c be the fraction of wrong outputs a gate catches. At each step the output is right (p), wrong and caught ((1 − p)c, the chain stops, loudly) or wrong and missed ((1 − p)(1 − c), the chain carries on). The chain finishes without any gate firing with probability (p + (1 − p)(1 − c))n, so:

correct = pn    detected = 1 − (p + (1 − p)(1 − c))n    silent = (p + (1 − p)(1 − c))n − pn

Silent failures (the chain finished and the answer is wrong) are the expensive ones, because nobody retries them or routes them to a person. Here are the numbers for p = 0.9 and n = 5, from your chain_outcomes:

gate catches   0%: correct 0.590  detected 0.000  silent 0.410
gate catches  50%: correct 0.590  detected 0.226  silent 0.183
gate catches  80%: correct 0.590  detected 0.341  silent 0.069
gate catches 100%: correct 0.590  detected 0.410  silent 0.000

With no gates all 41% of failures are silent; with gates that catch 80% of bad outputs the silent share falls to 6.9% and 34.1% become detected failures you can retry or hand to a human. This is the quantitative case for the advice in chapter 13 to put a check wherever you can verify something without a model, and for the eval harnesses of course A6 that measure how many failures your checks catch.

def chain(meter: Meter, ticket_text: str) -> Outcome:
    """extract (model) -> gate 1 -> lookup (plain code) -> draft (model) -> gate 2.

    1. ask("extract", SYSTEM_EXTRACT, ticket_text); the reply is JSON with "order_id" and "issue".
       Not valid JSON: Outcome("gate1", None, "extract was not valid JSON").
    2. Gate 1: the order_id must be a key of ORDERS, else Outcome("gate1", None, "no valid order id").
       No further model call is made.
    3. facts = dict(ORDERS[order_id], order_id=order_id); then
       ask("draft", SYSTEM_DRAFT, "FACTS:" + json.dumps(facts, sort_keys=True)).
    4. Gate 2: the reply must contain the order id and have at most 40 words,
       else Outcome("gate2", None, "draft rejected").
    5. Outcome("ok", reply, "ok").
    """
    raw = meter.ask("extract", SYSTEM_EXTRACT, ticket_text)
    try:
        fields = json.loads(raw)
        oid = fields.get("order_id")
    except (ValueError, AttributeError):
        return Outcome("gate1", None, "extract was not valid JSON")
    if oid not in ORDERS:
        return Outcome("gate1", None, "no valid order id")
    facts = dict(ORDERS[oid], order_id=oid)
    reply = meter.ask("draft", SYSTEM_DRAFT, "FACTS:" + json.dumps(facts, sort_keys=True))
    if oid not in reply or len(reply.split()) > 40:
        return Outcome("gate2", None, "draft rejected")
    return Outcome("ok", reply, "ok")

def chain_outcomes(p_step: float, n_steps: int, catch: float) -> Dict[str, float]:
    """What happens to a chain of n independent steps, each right with probability p_step,
    when a gate after each step stops the chain on a fraction `catch` of the wrong outputs.

    Returns probabilities that sum to 1:
      "correct"  every step was right,
      "detected" some wrong output was caught by a gate (the chain stopped, loudly),
      "silent"   the chain finished but at least one wrong output slipped through.
    """
    correct = p_step ** n_steps
    survives = (p_step + (1 - p_step) * (1 - catch)) ** n_steps      # nothing was caught
    return {"correct": correct, "detected": 1 - survives, "silent": survives - correct}

Milestone 3: a router and its threshold

Routing classifies an input and sends it down a specialised path. It earns its extra call when categories need different handling or different cost tiers. Here a small classifier call returns label|confidence; refunds go to a careful handler on the large tier, account and tracking tickets to cheaper handlers on the small tier, and everything else to a human. Four details are the whole craft, and each is something the check can see:

  • A fallback exists. A label outside the table, or a confidence below the threshold (the minimum confidence at which the router acts without a human), goes to a person. "Your driver was lovely" is not a support request.
  • The threshold is a dial, set from an evaluation. Raise it and fewer tickets are handled automatically but fewer are misrouted; lower it and the reverse. evaluate_router measures that trade on the labelled tickets and pick_threshold picks the highest automatic share inside an error cap you choose.
  • Confidence is not correctness. T23 ("Refund please, and reset my password") gets 0.93 from the classifier and is half wrong. No threshold in the sweep removes it, because a threshold can only filter out the cases the classifier is unsure about. Multi-intent inputs need a different fix: split the input first, or have each handler say what it did not cover.
  • The router must be cheaper than what it saves. It is a model call on every ticket. A tiny classifier call on the small tier moves refund-only work to the large tier and the rest away from it.

The tier is where the money is. The same 24 tickets at threshold 0.7 cost $0.00327 when each route uses its own tier; forcing every handled ticket onto the large tier costs $0.00874, 2.7 times as much, for exactly the same calls and tokens. Prices, not token counts, moved the bill, which is the lesson the last milestone returns to. (Chapter 19 of the evals course, Cost, Latency & Quality Trade-offs, turns this into Pareto fronts.)

def classify(meter: Meter, text: str) -> Tuple[str, float]:
    """One small call: ask("classify", SYSTEM_CLASSIFY, text) replies 'label|confidence'.
    Return (label, confidence as a float); a malformed reply is ("other", 0.0)."""
    raw = meter.ask("classify", SYSTEM_CLASSIFY, text)
    try:
        label, conf = raw.split("|")
        return label.strip(), float(conf)
    except ValueError:
        return "other", 0.0

def route(meter: Meter, text: str, threshold: float = 0.7, tier_override: Optional[str] = None) -> Routed:
    """Classify, then either hand the ticket to a specialist or to a human.

    A label that is not in ROUTES, or a confidence below `threshold`, goes to "human" with no reply
    and no further call. Otherwise ask("handle", HANDLER_SYSTEMS[label], text, tier=...) with the
    tier ROUTES gives for that label (or `tier_override` when it is not None).
    """
    label, conf = classify(meter, text)
    if label not in ROUTES or conf < threshold:
        return Routed(label, conf, "human", None)
    tier = tier_override or ROUTES[label]
    return Routed(label, conf, label, meter.ask("handle", HANDLER_SYSTEMS[label], text, tier=tier))

def evaluate_router(tickets: List[Dict[str, Any]], threshold: float = 0.7, tier_override: Optional[str] = None) -> Dict[str, Any]:
    """Route every ticket through one Meter(make_model()) and score the result against the labels.

    Returns: threshold, n, auto (tickets not sent to a human), auto_rate = auto / n,
    misrouted (auto tickets whose destination differs from the true intent),
    misroute_rate = misrouted / n, plus the meter's calls, tokens (in + out) and cost_usd.
    """
    meter = Meter(make_model())
    auto = misrouted = 0
    for t in tickets:
        r = route(meter, t["text"], threshold, tier_override)
        if r.destination != "human":
            auto += 1
            misrouted += r.destination != t["intent"]
    tot, n = meter.totals(), len(tickets)
    return {"threshold": threshold, "n": n, "auto": auto, "auto_rate": auto / n, "misrouted": misrouted,
            "misroute_rate": misrouted / n, "calls": tot["calls"], "tokens": tot["tokens_in"] + tot["tokens_out"],
            "cost_usd": tot["cost_usd"]}

def pick_threshold(rows: List[Dict[str, Any]], max_misroute_rate: float) -> float:
    """From evaluate_router rows, the threshold with the highest auto_rate among those whose
    misroute_rate is at most the cap; ties go to the HIGHER threshold. No row qualifies: ValueError."""
    ok = [r for r in rows if r["misroute_rate"] <= max_misroute_rate]
    if not ok:
        raise ValueError(f"no threshold keeps misrouting at or below {max_misroute_rate}")
    return max(ok, key=lambda r: (r["auto_rate"], r["threshold"]))["threshold"]

Milestone 4: parallel fan-out, sectioning and voting

When work divides into pieces that do not depend on each other, run them at once and combine the results in code. Chapter 13 names two variants, and they answer different questions.

Sectioning
Different independent subtasks run in parallel: here a check for personal data, a check of tone and a check for policy matters on the same message. Each call is narrow, and the wait is that of the slowest. The tokens are the same as running them in a row.
Voting
The same question goes to several judges and the answers are combined, usually by majority. It trades tokens for confidence on a noisy decision.

Sectioning is a measurement you have already seen in the timeline of milestone 1: the same three calls, the same $0.000047, a clock reading of 1.072 s one at a time and 0.358 s together. Voting needs more thought, because its promise is a formula and the formula has a condition.

If one judge is right with probability p and judges err independently, a majority of n (odd) is right with probability

P(majority right) = ∑k > n/2 C(n, k) pk (1 − p)n−k

For p = 0.7 that is 0.784 for three judges, 0.837 for five and 0.901 for nine, which is why voting is tempting. The condition is independence. The 200 items in fixtures.py are built so that one judge is right 70% of the time, but a fifth of the items are "hard" in a way that fools every judge the same way (the same ambiguity, the same missing context), and on the rest each judge is right 87.5% of the time. A majority can never beat the 80% of items that are not hard, however many judges you pay for.

Go deeper optional: why the ceiling is exactly 0.80

Let h = 0.2 be the share of hard items. On a hard item every judge gives the same wrong answer, so the majority is wrong with probability 1. On the other 80%, each judge is right with q = 0.7 / 0.8 = 0.875, independently, so the majority is right with probability P(q, n) from the formula above. The panel's accuracy is therefore 0.8 × P(0.875, n): 0.7 for one judge, and 0.8 × 0.9981 = 0.7985 for nine. The extra judges only improve the part of the problem that was already easy.

def section(meter: Meter, text: str, parallel: bool = True) -> Dict[str, str]:
    """Run the three independent checks of CHECK_SYSTEMS (pii, tone, policy, in that order) on `text`.

    Each is ask("check-<name>", CHECK_SYSTEMS[name], text). With parallel=True they run inside
    meter.fan_out; with parallel=False one after another. Return {name: reply}.
    """
    names = list(CHECK_SYSTEMS)
    thunks = [lambda n=n: meter.ask("check-" + n, CHECK_SYSTEMS[n], text) for n in names]
    replies = meter.fan_out(thunks) if parallel else [t() for t in thunks]
    return dict(zip(names, replies))

def majority(votes: List[str]) -> str:
    """The most common vote. A tie goes to the vote that appeared first. Empty: ValueError."""
    if not votes:
        raise ValueError("no votes")
    counts: Dict[str, int] = {}
    for v in votes:
        counts[v] = counts.get(v, 0) + 1
    best = max(counts.values())
    return next(v for v in votes if counts[v] == best)

def vote(meter: Meter, text: str, n: int, tier: str = "small") -> Tuple[str, List[str]]:
    """Ask n judges (k = 0 .. n-1) at once: inside fan_out, ask("judge", judge_system(k), text, tier).
    Return (majority label, the list of votes in judge order)."""
    votes = meter.fan_out([lambda k=k: meter.ask("judge", judge_system(k), text, tier=tier).strip() for k in range(n)])
    return majority(votes), votes

def majority_accuracy(p: float, n: int) -> float:
    """Chance that a majority of n independent judges, each right with probability p, is right.
    n must be odd (ValueError otherwise)."""
    if n % 2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be odd so there are no ties")
    return sum(math.comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k) for k in range(n // 2 + 1, n + 1))

def evaluate_voting(items: List[Dict[str, Any]], n: int) -> Dict[str, Any]:
    """Run vote(...) with n judges on every item (one Meter(make_model()) for all) and score the
    majorities against item["truth"]. Returns n, accuracy, calls, tokens (in + out), cost_usd, seconds."""
    meter = Meter(make_model())
    right = sum(vote(meter, it["text"], n)[0] == it["truth"] for it in items)
    tot = meter.totals()
    return {"n": n, "accuracy": right / len(items), "calls": tot["calls"],
            "tokens": tot["tokens_in"] + tot["tokens_out"], "cost_usd": tot["cost_usd"], "seconds": tot["seconds"]}

Milestone 5: orchestrator-workers inside a budget

Parallelisation needs you to know the parts when you write the program. Often you do not: "compare the refund windows of the branches in this email" has two subtasks if the email names two branches and nine if it names nine. In orchestrator-workers a planning call reads the input and decides the subtasks, a worker call handles each, and a final call synthesises the results. The number of workers is now chosen by a model, so cost is partly under the model's control. Anthropic's write-up of its multi-agent research system reports that early versions spawned dozens of sub-agents for simple queries before the prompts taught the lead how much effort a query deserved (chapter 14). Prompts are advice; limits in code are law. This milestone builds two such limits, and a rule about honesty.

  1. A cap on workers (max_subtasks). The plan may be longer; only the first few run.
  2. A budget on calls, tokens and dollars. BudgetedMeter checks before every call: if a limit has already been reached it raises BudgetExceeded and makes no call. So a call can push the total past a token or dollar limit, by at most one call's worth, and the next call is refused; a call limit is exact. (A hard stop that must never overshoot needs an estimate of the next call's size; this lab does not guess.)
  3. The answer must say what it left out. Dropped subtasks are reported with a reason (limit or budget) and named in the answer. A summary that silently covers three of five branches reads like a summary of five.

class BudgetedMeter(Meter):
    """A Meter that refuses to make a call once a limit has been reached."""

    def __init__(self, model, budget: Budget):
        super().__init__(model)
        self.budget = budget

    def check_budget(self) -> None:
        """Raise BudgetExceeded("calls"), ("tokens") or ("usd") (checked in that order) if the calls
        made so far have already REACHED a limit that is not None: calls >= max_calls,
        tokens_in + tokens_out >= max_tokens, cost >= max_usd. Otherwise return."""
        b, t = self.budget, self.totals()
        if b.max_calls is not None and t["calls"] >= b.max_calls:
            raise BudgetExceeded("calls")
        if b.max_tokens is not None and t["tokens_in"] + t["tokens_out"] >= b.max_tokens:
            raise BudgetExceeded("tokens")
        if b.max_usd is not None and t["cost_usd"] >= b.max_usd:
            raise BudgetExceeded("usd")

    def ask(self, step: str, system: str, user: str, tier: str = "small") -> str:
        self.check_budget()                      # given: the check happens before every call
        return super().ask(step, system, user, tier)

def plan(meter: Meter, request: str) -> List[Dict[str, str]]:
    """ask("plan", SYSTEM_PLAN, request, tier="large"); the reply is a JSON list of subtasks
    ({"task": ..., "city": ...}). Not valid JSON, or not a list: return []."""
    try:
        tasks = json.loads(meter.ask("plan", SYSTEM_PLAN, request, tier="large"))
    except ValueError:
        return []
    return tasks if isinstance(tasks, list) else []

def orchestrate(meter: Meter, request: str, max_subtasks: int = 6) -> Orchestration:
    """Plan, fan out one worker per subtask, synthesise, and say what was left out.

    1. tasks = plan(...). None: Orchestration("No subtasks found for this request.", 0, 0, []).
    2. Only the first `max_subtasks` run; the rest are dropped with reason "limit".
    3. Workers run inside meter.fan_out: ask("worker", SYSTEM_WORK, task["task"]) at tier "small".
       A worker that raises BudgetExceeded is dropped with reason "budget" (the others carry on).
       (A BudgetExceeded from the plan call itself is not caught here.)
    4. No worker finished: answer "No subtask finished: budget exhausted.". Otherwise
       ask("synth", SYSTEM_SYNTH, "\\n".join(results), tier="large"); if that raises BudgetExceeded the
       answer is "Budget reached before synthesis: " + "; ".join(results) + ".".
    5. If anything was dropped, append " Not covered: " + ", ".join(dropped task texts) + "."
    `dropped` lists the dropped subtasks in plan order; `ran` counts finished workers.
    """
    tasks = plan(meter, request)
    if not tasks:
        return Orchestration("No subtasks found for this request.", 0, 0, [])
    runnable = tasks[:max_subtasks]

    def worker(t):
        try:
            return meter.ask("worker", SYSTEM_WORK, t["task"], tier="small")
        except BudgetExceeded:
            return None

    outputs = meter.fan_out([lambda t=t: worker(t) for t in runnable]) + [None] * (len(tasks) - len(runnable))
    results = [o for o in outputs if o is not None]
    dropped = []
    for i, (t, o) in enumerate(zip(tasks, outputs)):
        if i >= max_subtasks:
            dropped.append({"task": t["task"], "reason": "limit"})
        elif o is None:
            dropped.append({"task": t["task"], "reason": "budget"})
    if not results:
        answer = "No subtask finished: budget exhausted."
    else:
        try:
            answer = meter.ask("synth", SYSTEM_SYNTH, "\n".join(results), tier="large")
        except BudgetExceeded:
            answer = "Budget reached before synthesis: " + "; ".join(results) + "."
    if dropped:
        answer += " Not covered: " + ", ".join(d["task"] for d in dropped) + "."
    return Orchestration(answer, len(tasks), len(results), dropped)

Milestone 6: the whole desk

Patterns compose, and the composition is where budgets earn their keep. The desk handles a ticket like this: classify (one small call); if the router is unsure or the ticket is not a support request, hand it to a human; if it is a tracking question, run the chain (extract, gate, draft, gate) instead of a free-text handler, because the answer must be built from real order data; otherwise call the specialist for that label on its tier. Each ticket gets its own BudgetedMeter, and a ticket that hits its budget does not fail: it goes to a human with the reason budget: calls (or tokens or usd), exactly like a failed gate or a low-confidence route.

Two reports then matter more than the total. The first is where the tokens and dollars go. Here is the ledger for the 24 tickets at threshold 0.7 (aggregated over every call from the per-ticket meters, with your Meter.by_step):

def process_ticket(ticket: Dict[str, Any], model, threshold: float = 0.7, budget: Optional[Budget] = None) -> TicketResult:
    """Handle one ticket with a BudgetedMeter(model, budget or Budget()).

    classify first. A label not in ROUTES or a confidence below `threshold`: status "human",
    reason "router". Label "tracking": run chain(...): "answered" if it returned "ok", else "human"
    with reason "gate: " + the Outcome's reason. Other labels: one ask("handle", ...) at the ROUTES
    tier, status "answered", reason "ok". BudgetExceeded anywhere: status "human", reason "budget: <which>".
    The result carries the meter's calls, tokens (in + out), cost_usd and seconds.
    """
    meter = BudgetedMeter(model, budget or Budget())
    status, reason = "answered", "ok"
    try:
        label, conf = classify(meter, ticket["text"])
        if label not in ROUTES or conf < threshold:
            status, reason = "human", "router"
        elif label == "tracking":
            out = chain(meter, ticket["text"])
            if out.status != "ok":
                status, reason = "human", "gate: " + out.reason
        else:
            meter.ask("handle", HANDLER_SYSTEMS[label], ticket["text"], tier=ROUTES[label])
    except BudgetExceeded as e:
        status, reason = "human", f"budget: {e.reason}"
    t = meter.totals()
    return TicketResult(ticket["id"], status, reason, t["calls"], t["tokens_in"] + t["tokens_out"], t["cost_usd"], t["seconds"])

def percentile(values: List[float], q: float) -> float:
    """Nearest-rank percentile, 0 < q <= 100: sort, take the value at 1-based rank ceil(q/100 x n).
    percentile([1..10], 50) is 5 and (.., 95) is 10. Empty list or q out of range: ValueError."""
    if not values or not 0 < q <= 100:
        raise ValueError("need values and 0 < q <= 100")
    s = sorted(values)
    return s[math.ceil(q / 100 * len(s)) - 1]

def run_desk(tickets: List[Dict[str, Any]], threshold: float = 0.7, budget: Optional[Budget] = None) -> Dict[str, Any]:
    """Process every ticket (one shared make_model(), a fresh budget per ticket) and summarise:
    results (list of TicketResult), answered, human, reasons ({reason: count}), tokens_total,
    cost_usd (sum), p50_tokens and p95_tokens (percentile of per-ticket tokens)."""
    model = make_model()
    results = [process_ticket(t, model, threshold, budget) for t in tickets]
    reasons: Dict[str, int] = {}
    for r in results:
        if r.status == "human":
            reasons[r.reason] = reasons.get(r.reason, 0) + 1
    toks = [r.tokens for r in results]
    return {"results": results, "answered": sum(r.status == "answered" for r in results),
            "human": sum(r.status == "human" for r in results), "reasons": reasons,
            "tokens_total": sum(toks), "cost_usd": sum(r.cost_usd for r in results),
            "p50_tokens": percentile(toks, 50), "p95_tokens": percentile(toks, 95)}

Going further

  • Reserve budget for the last step. Make orchestrate keep enough calls in hand for the synthesiser, running fewer workers rather than none of the synthesis. How does the answer change with max_calls=4?
  • An evaluator-optimiser loop. Add the fifth pattern of chapter 13: a draft, a rubric check in plain code, feedback to the writer, and two limits (rounds and a no-improvement stop). Meter it, and find the rubric score at which a second round stops paying for itself.
  • Split multi-intent tickets. Add a step before the router that splits "refund please, and reset my password" into two tickets, route each, and measure whether the extra call lowers the misroute rate enough to be worth its tokens.
  • A cascade. Try the small tier first and escalate to the large tier only when a gate fails or confidence is low. Compare cost and quality with always-large and always-small using your run_desk table.
  • Prompt caching. Real providers charge less for a repeated prompt prefix. Add a cached-input price to the meter and a flag on ask, and see which step's cost falls most. (The classify prompt is identical on every ticket.)
  • Tail latency from fan-out. Give each worker a random latency (seeded) and plot the clock against the number of workers. At what fan-out does the slowest branch dominate? Compare with the model in tail latency at scale.
  • Turn the desk durable. Store each ticket's step outputs as they complete, so a crash after extract does not repay for extract. That is the starting point of the next part of the course, durable execution.

Systems connection

The patterns are old ideas wearing new components. A chain with gates is a pipeline with validation between stages; the early exit is fail-fast. Sectioning is fork-join, with the tail-latency consequences of fan-out amplification: the slowest branch sets the wait. Orchestrator-workers is the master-worker shape of batch systems. A budget is admission control and load shedding aimed at your own spending (overload), and its fallback answers are graceful degradation. Retrying a failed step needs the idempotency discipline of timeouts and retries and exactly-once, and a multi-step workflow that must resume at step six is a saga. The meter itself is the span-per-call instrumentation of distributed tracing, with tokens and dollars as attributes.

What you learned

  • Meter first: tokens in and out, dollars at the call's tier and simulated seconds for every call. Parallel work shortens the critical path but never the bill, so keep seconds and work_seconds apart.
  • A gate in a chain is plain code that stops the run before the next model call is paid for. Gates do not raise the chance of a correct answer (pn); they turn silent failures into detected ones: at 90% per step over five steps and a gate catching 80%, silent failures fall from 41% to 6.9%.
  • A router needs a fallback and a threshold chosen from a labelled evaluation. Confidence filters unsure cases, not confident mistakes, and the tier assignment is usually the biggest cost lever.
  • Voting gains what the formula says only if errors are independent. With shared blind spots the ceiling is the share of items that are not hard, however many judges you add, while tokens grow linearly.
  • When a model chooses the number of workers, the cap and the budget live in code, are checked before every call, and the answer names what was left out and why.
  • Look at dollars as well as tokens: in the desk, the handle step was a fifth of the tokens and three quarters of the cost. And report percentiles, because budgets promise worst cases.

Check your understanding

A chain has five independent steps, each right 90% of the time, and a gate after each step that catches 80% of wrong outputs. Roughly how often does the chain finish with a wrong answer that nobody noticed?

  1. 41%: gates only add latency
  2. 59%: that is the chance everything went right
  3. 7%: the finish-without-a-gate-firing chance (about 66%) minus the chance everything was right (59%)
  4. 34%: that is the chance some gate fires
Each step passes without a gate firing with probability 0.9 + 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.92, so the chain finishes quietly with probability 0.925 = 0.659. All-correct is 0.95 = 0.590, so the silent failures are the difference, 0.069. The 34% is real but it is the detected failure rate: loud failures that can be retried or handed to a person. Gates do not touch the 59%; they change what the other 41% looks like.

In the router sweep the number of misrouted tickets falls as the threshold rises but never reaches zero, even at 0.9. Why?

  1. The threshold comparison has an off-by-one error at the top of the range
  2. The classifier is simply too small, and a larger one would reach zero
  3. Misrouted tickets are always low-confidence ones, so a threshold of 1.0 would be needed
  4. One ticket holds two requests and gets a confident label (0.93), and a threshold only removes cases the classifier is unsure about
A confidence threshold filters out uncertainty, not confident mistakes. T23 ("Refund please, and reset my password") is classified as a refund at 0.93, above every threshold in the sweep, and half of it is wrong. The fix is structural (split the input, or let each handler report what it left uncovered), not a higher number. Nine independent-looking judges each have 70% accuracy, but 20% of the items are hard in a way that fools every judge identically. What is the best accuracy a majority vote can reach?

  1. At most 80%, however many judges there are
  2. About 90%, from the binomial formula for nine judges
  3. 70%, because a majority cannot beat its members
  4. It approaches 100% as the number of judges grows
On hard items every judge gives the same wrong answer, so the majority is wrong whatever the panel size; only the other 80% of items can be won. The 90% figure assumes independent errors, which is exactly what fails here. A majority can beat its members when errors are independent (three judges at 70% give 78%), so the third option is wrong too, but the ceiling is set by the shared blind spot, not by the individual accuracy.

  2. The call is made, but its reply is discarded and an exception is raised afterwards
  3. The call is made and the total becomes 139; the call after it is refused
  4. The budget rounds up to the next multiple of the call size, so nothing happens
The check runs before the call and asks only "has a limit already been reached?" The meter cannot know the next call's size in advance, so token and dollar limits can be overshot by at most one call, while a call-count limit is exact. A guaranteed hard stop needs an estimate (or a max_tokens cap on the call itself). Discarding a reply after paying for it is the worst of both worlds.
