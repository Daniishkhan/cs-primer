Framing Problems & Choosing Metrics

Most failed machine learning projects fail before any model is trained, in the translation from a business wish to a prediction task. This chapter shows how to make that translation explicit (what exactly is predicted, for whom, when, and what happens next) and how to choose a metric whose mistakes cost what the real mistakes cost.

Why this matters

A model is optimised against whatever number you hand it. Hand it the wrong number (accuracy on a 1% problem, average error when big misses are what hurt, a proxy label that differs from the thing you care about) and a flawless training run produces a flawless answer to the wrong question. The previous chapter, What Learning Is, taught you to estimate error honestly; this one decides which error to estimate. After it you will be able to write a one-paragraph problem statement, pick between precision, recall, AUC and cost-based metrics for a real decision, derive a threshold from costs, and say why a metric might be lying to you. The framing exercise is practised end to end in Studio 1: Frame a Churn Prediction Problem.

From a business question to a prediction task

Someone says: "We want to reduce churn", "Catch more fraud", "Help the support team". None of these is a machine learning problem yet. A prediction task has five parts, and you cannot start modelling until each has a concrete answer:

  1. The decision. What will someone do differently because of the prediction? A retention offer is sent, a transaction is held for review, a ticket is routed. If nothing changes, the prediction has no value, however accurate.
  2. The unit of prediction. One row is one what: a customer, a customer-month, a transaction, a ticket? This sets how you split data and how you count (a customer-month table has twelve rows per customer per year).
  3. The target (label) and how it is observed. The exact definition: "cancelled within 30 days of the prediction date", not "churn". Who records it, and how long after the fact does the truth arrive? Labels that arrive months later shape how fast you can retrain and evaluate.
  4. The prediction time. At what moment is the prediction made, and what is known at that moment? Every feature must be available then. A feature recorded after the event (a refund issued after the fraud was discovered) is a leak, covered in chapter 14.
  5. The metric and the bar. How good is good enough, measured how, against what baseline? This is the rest of the chapter, and chapter 4 supplies the baseline.

A worked framing: "reduce churn"

A subscription company wants to reduce churn. Written as a task: once a month, for every active customer, predict whether they will cancel within the next 30 days, using only data up to that day, so that the retention team can contact the 500 customers most at risk with an offer. That last remark hides a deeper point. The retention team does not benefit from contacting people who will churn; it benefits from contacting people whose behaviour the offer changes. A model predicting churn finds the people most likely to leave, some of whom cannot be persuaded and others who would have stayed anyway. Predicting who to contact is a causal question (an uplift model), and you only learn the answer by running an experiment: chapter 21 returns to this. For now: a prediction is an input to a decision, and a good predictor does not guarantee a good decision.

When the label is a proxy

Often the quantity you care about cannot be observed, so you predict a stand-in, a proxy label. Then the proxy's flaws become the model's flaws. The sharpest published example is Obermeyer et al. (2019), "Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations", in Science. A widely used US commercial algorithm helps decide which patients get extra care management. It was trained to predict future healthcare cost as a stand-in for health need. The authors found that, at the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker than White patients, because less money is spent on Black patients with the same level of need, so cost understated their need. They estimated that correcting the disparity would raise the share of Black patients flagged for extra help from 17.7% to 46.5%, and that reformulating the algorithm to stop using cost as a proxy for need removed the bias in who was identified. Nobody wrote a biased line of code. The target was a poor stand-in for the intent, and the metric rewarded predicting it faithfully.

The habit to build: for every target, ask "what is the gap between this label and what I actually want, and who falls into the gap?" A related case, a recruiting model that learned past hiring bias from past hiring labels, is the subject of Case 4.

Is this a prediction problem at all?

Before modelling, run three quick tests. Is there a decision that changes with the prediction? If not, you are building a dashboard. Would a rule do? If "flag any transaction above £5,000 from a new device" captures nearly all of the value, a rule is cheaper to run, explain and audit; baselines make this test quantitative. Will the labels exist when you need them? If outcomes are never recorded, or only for cases you acted on (you only learn whether a loan defaults for applicants you approved), the training data is a censored view of the world, and that is its own chapter of trouble.

What kind of prediction?

The output type determines the family of metrics. A short map:

Classification
Predict a category. Binary (fraud or not), multiclass (which of 12 ticket queues), multilabel (which of several tags apply). Most models output a score or probability per class, and a threshold turns it into a decision.
Regression
Predict a number: delivery time, house price, demand. A model can return a single best guess or a distribution (quantiles, an interval).
Ranking
Order a list so that the best items come first: search results, a retention list. Only the order matters, and usually only the top. See Relevance Metrics for nDCG and friends.
Forecasting
Regression where time is central: next week's demand. Evaluation must respect time order (chapter 18).
Unsupervised tasks
No labels: clustering, dimensionality reduction, anomaly detection (chapters 10 to 12). Evaluation is harder and more tied to the downstream use.

Many business questions can be framed more than one way. "Which customers should the team call?" is a binary classification with a threshold, a ranking with a cut-off at 500, or a regression on expected lost revenue. The ranking frame matches the capacity constraint best; the regression frame lets you weight by customer value. Picking the frame is a design decision, not a given.

The confusion matrix and the accuracy trap

For binary classification, every prediction lands in one of four boxes, by what the model said and what was true. Call the thing you are looking for the positive class (fraud, churn, disease). The 2×2 table is the confusion matrix.

Now see why accuracy, the fraction of rows right, is dangerous. Take 10,000 card transactions of which 100 (1%) are fraud. A model gives each a suspicion score; fraud scores average 2.0 and legitimate ones 0.0, both with standard deviation 1. We apply several thresholds, and compare against the laziest "model" imaginable: say "legit" every time.

"""Card fraud, 10,000 transactions, 1% fraud: why accuracy alone is a trap."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N, FRAUD = 10_000, 100
y = np.zeros(N, dtype=int)
y[:FRAUD] = 1                                              # 100 fraudulent transactions
score = np.where(y == 1, rng.normal(2.0, 1.0, N), rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, N))   # a model's suspicion score

def report(name, pred):
    tp = int(((pred == 1) & (y == 1)).sum()); fp = int(((pred == 1) & (y == 0)).sum())
    fn = int(((pred == 0) & (y == 1)).sum()); tn = int(((pred == 0) & (y == 0)).sum())
    acc = (tp + tn) / N
    prec = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else float("nan")
    rec = tp / (tp + fn)
    f1 = 2 * prec * rec / (prec + rec) if tp else 0.0
    print(f"{name:<22} TP={tp:<3} FP={fp:<4} FN={fn:<3} TN={tn:<5} acc={acc:.3f} precision={prec:.3f} recall={rec:.3f} F1={f1:.3f}")

report("always say 'legit'", np.zeros(N, dtype=int))
report("model, threshold 1.0", (score > 1.0).astype(int))
report("model, threshold 2.0", (score > 2.0).astype(int))
report("model, threshold 3.0", (score > 3.0).astype(int))

model, threshold 1.0   TP=89  FP=1684 FN=11  TN=8216  acc=0.831 precision=0.050 recall=0.890 F1=0.095
model, threshold 2.0   TP=50  FP=238  FN=50  TN=9662  acc=0.971 precision=0.174 recall=0.500 F1=0.258
model, threshold 3.0   TP=5   FP=9    FN=95  TN=9891  acc=0.990 precision=0.357 recall=0.050 F1=0.088

Do not skip the first line. The do-nothing model is 99.0% accurate and catches no fraud at all (recall 0). The last row, a genuine model at a high threshold, has the same 99.0% accuracy and catches 5 frauds. The middle rows, which do far more useful work, have lower accuracy (97.1% and 83.1%) because every false alarm counts against them. Accuracy is dominated by the majority class whenever classes are imbalanced, and in most real problems they are: fraud, disease, churn, defects, outages.

Precision and recall tell the real story and pull in opposite directions. Lowering the threshold to 1.0 raises recall to 89% (89 of 100 caught) but precision falls to 5%: of 1,773 flagged transactions, 1,684 are false alarms. Raising it to 3.0 gives 36% precision but recall of 5%. The F1 score combines the two into one number (their harmonic mean, which is low whenever either is low), and among these thresholds it peaks at the middle one, 0.258. F1 is a useful summary but it silently assumes that precision and recall matter equally, which they rarely do. Whether a false alarm or a miss is worse is a fact about your business, not a statistical property.

Common misconception

"My classes are roughly balanced, so accuracy is fine." Balance of the classes is only one of the conditions. Accuracy also treats a false alarm and a miss as equally bad, which is false for almost every real decision: telling a healthy patient to have another test is not equivalent to missing a tumour. Even on a 50/50 problem, ask what each kind of error costs before accepting accuracy as the target.

Scores, thresholds and threshold-free metrics

Most classifiers output a score, and the threshold is a separate choice you make later. Two metrics summarise a scorer across all thresholds.

  • ROC AUC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) plots recall against false positive rate as the threshold sweeps. The area has a clean meaning: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect separation. It measures ranking quality and does not depend on the threshold or (in expectation) on the class balance.
  • Average precision (AP, close to the area under the precision-recall curve) averages precision at each rank where a true positive appears. Because precision depends on how many negatives are mixed in, AP does depend on the base rate, and that is a feature: it describes what the person reading the list will experience.

The script computes both from scratch (the ROC AUC through the rank-sum formula, which is the probability interpretation made literal) for one fixed scorer, positives ~ N(1.5, 1) against negatives ~ N(0, 1), while the number of positives falls from equal-to-negatives down to 1 in 1,000.

"""Same scorer, different base rates: ROC AUC barely moves, average precision collapses."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

def roc_auc(s, y):
    """Probability that a random positive outscores a random negative (rank formulation)."""
    order = np.argsort(s, kind="mergesort")
    ranks = np.empty(len(s)); ranks[order] = np.arange(1, len(s) + 1)
    n_pos = y.sum(); n_neg = len(y) - n_pos
    return (ranks[y == 1].sum() - n_pos * (n_pos + 1) / 2) / (n_pos * n_neg)

def average_precision(s, y):
    """Mean of precision at each rank where a positive appears (area under the PR curve)."""
    y_sorted = y[np.argsort(-s, kind="mergesort")]
    tp = np.cumsum(y_sorted)
    precision_at_k = tp / np.arange(1, len(y) + 1)
    return precision_at_k[y_sorted == 1].mean()

N_NEG = 50_000
print(f"{'positives':>9} {'base rate':>10} {'ROC AUC':>8} {'avg precision':>14} {'precision @ recall 0.8':>23}")
for n_pos in [50_000, 5_000, 500, 50]:
    y = np.r_[np.ones(n_pos, dtype=int), np.zeros(N_NEG, dtype=int)]
    s = np.r_[rng.normal(1.5, 1.0, n_pos), rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, N_NEG)]
    thr = np.quantile(s[y == 1], 0.2)                     # threshold that keeps 80% of positives
    prec = (y[s >= thr]).mean()
    print(f"{n_pos:>9} {n_pos/(n_pos+N_NEG):>10.4f} {roc_auc(s, y):>8.3f} {average_precision(s, y):>14.3f} {prec:>23.3f}")
positives  base rate  ROC AUC  avg precision  precision @ recall 0.8
    50000     0.5000    0.858          0.856                   0.761
     5000     0.0909    0.858          0.461                   0.240
      500     0.0099    0.852          0.118                   0.030
       50     0.0010    0.824          0.038                   0.002

The scorer never changed, and ROC AUC barely moved (0.858, 0.858, 0.852, 0.824; the small slide at the end is sampling noise with only 50 positives). Average precision fell from 0.856 to 0.038, and the precision you get when you catch 80% of positives fell from 76% to 0.2%. Same model, wildly different experience for the analyst reading the queue. This is the argument made by Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015) in "The Precision-Recall Plot Is More Informative than the ROC Plot When Evaluating Binary Classifiers on Imbalanced Datasets": ROC AUC can look comfortable when false positive rates that sound small (1%) still swamp the rare positives.

Let the costs choose the threshold

If a missed fraud loses £100 and a needless manual review costs £5, then treating mistakes equally is irrational. The principled method is to price each outcome and pick the threshold that minimises expected cost. Here is the derivation, and it is short.

Suppose the model's score for a case is its estimated probability p of being positive (assume for now that it is accurate; chapter 15 shows how to make it so). Let CFP be the cost of a false alarm and CFN the cost of a miss, with correct decisions costing nothing. For one case:

  • If you flag it, you pay CFP when it is actually negative (probability 1 − p). Expected cost: (1 − p) · CFP.
  • If you pass it, you pay CFN when it is actually positive (probability p). Expected cost: p · CFN.

Flag when flagging is cheaper: (1 − p) · CFP < p · CFN. Rearranging gives the rule:

flag when   p > CFP / (CFP + CFN)

With CFN = 100 and CFP = 5 the threshold is 5/105 ≈ 0.048. Flag anything with more than about a 5% chance of being fraud. This is not 0.5, the default in many libraries, and it is not the threshold that maximises F1. The simulation below tests it: 100,000 transactions whose true fraud probabilities follow a skewed distribution (average 4.8%), a model whose score equals that true probability, and five policies.

"""Choosing a threshold by cost: a fraud queue where a miss costs 100 and a review costs 5."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N = 100_000
C_FN, C_FP = 100.0, 5.0                      # missed fraud loses 100; a needless manual review costs 5
p = rng.beta(0.4, 8.0, N)                    # each transaction's TRUE fraud probability (mean about 4.8%)
y = (rng.random(N) < p).astype(int)          # outcomes drawn from those probabilities
score = p                                    # a perfectly calibrated model: score == true probability

def evaluate(t):
    flag = score >= t
    tp = (flag & (y == 1)).sum(); fp = (flag & (y == 0)).sum(); fn = (~flag & (y == 1)).sum()
    prec = tp / flag.sum() if flag.any() else float("nan")
    rec = tp / y.sum()
    f1 = 2 * tp / (2 * tp + fp + fn)
    return flag.sum(), prec, rec, f1, fp * C_FP + fn * C_FN

grid = np.linspace(0.001, 0.9, 900)
best_f1 = grid[np.argmax([evaluate(t)[3] for t in grid])]
t_cost = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)                # theory: flag when p >= C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)
best_cost = grid[np.argmin([evaluate(t)[4] for t in grid])]
print(f"fraud rate {y.mean():.3%}; theoretical cost-optimal threshold {t_cost:.4f}; grid search {best_cost:.3f}")
print(f"{'policy':<26}{'threshold':>9}{'flagged':>9}{'precision':>10}{'recall':>8}{'F1':>7}{'total cost':>12}")
for name, t in [("flag nothing", 1.01), ("default 0.5", 0.5), ("maximise F1", best_f1), ("minimise cost", t_cost), ("flag everything", 0.0)]:
    n, pr, rc, f1, cost = evaluate(t)
    print(f"{name:<26}{t:>9.3f}{n:>9}{pr:>10.3f}{rc:>8.3f}{f1:>7.3f}{cost:>12,.0f}")
fraud rate 4.833%; theoretical cost-optimal threshold 0.0476; grid search 0.046

flag nothing                  1.010        0       nan   0.000  0.000     483,300
default 0.5                   0.500       63     0.587   0.008  0.015     479,730

Study the last column. The default 0.5 threshold flags 63 transactions and saves almost nothing (479,730 against 483,300 for doing nothing). The F1-maximising threshold (0.157) cuts the cost to 325,570. The cost-minimising threshold from the formula, 0.048, gets 222,950 (within a rounding of the grid search's 0.046), a 31% saving over the F1 choice, by flagging four times as many transactions at much lower precision (12.7% against 24.1%). "Maximise F1" was the wrong goal; "minimise expected cost" was the right one. Notice also that flagging everything costs 475,835, barely better than flagging nothing: the model's value is entirely in how it prioritises.

When capacity limits the action

The cost formula assumes you can act on everything above the threshold. Often you cannot: the retention team can call 500 people, fraud analysts can review 200 cases a day. Then the question changes from "what threshold?" to "how good is the top of the list?", and the right metrics are precision at k (of the top k, how many are positive?) and recall at k. You choose k from the capacity, and the threshold falls out as the score of the k-th item. This is why a ranking frame often suits operational problems better than a classification frame, and why the right model for the top 200 can differ from the best model on average.

Metrics for numbers

For regression the main choices are squared error (reported as RMSE, root mean squared error, in the units of the target), absolute error (MAE, mean absolute error), and percentage errors (MAPE, mean absolute percentage error). They are not interchangeable: each rewards a different prediction. A clean way to see this is to ask, for one fixed set of outcomes, which single constant prediction each metric prefers. Take delivery times in minutes, drawn from a skewed distribution: most deliveries take about 20 minutes, a long tail take much longer.

"""Which constant prediction does each metric prefer? Skewed delivery times in minutes."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
y = rng.lognormal(mean=3.0, sigma=0.7, size=5_000)          # most deliveries ~20 minutes, a long tail of slow ones
grid = np.linspace(5, 60, 1101)

def best(loss):
    return grid[np.argmin([loss(c) for c in grid])]

print(f"mean {y.mean():.2f}   median {np.median(y):.2f}   90th percentile {np.quantile(y, 0.9):.2f}")
print(f"best constant for RMSE (squared error)        : {best(lambda c: np.sqrt(np.mean((y - c) ** 2))):.2f}")
print(f"best constant for MAE (absolute error)        : {best(lambda c: np.mean(np.abs(y - c))):.2f}")
print(f"best constant for MAPE (percentage error)     : {best(lambda c: np.mean(np.abs(y - c) / y)):.2f}")
q = 0.9
print(f"best constant for pinball loss at q=0.9       : {best(lambda c: np.mean(np.maximum(q * (y - c), (q - 1) * (y - c)))):.2f}")
mean 25.66   median 20.24   90th percentile 48.89
best constant for RMSE (squared error)        : 25.65
best constant for MAE (absolute error)        : 20.25
best constant for MAPE (percentage error)     : 12.20
best constant for pinball loss at q=0.9       : 48.90

Four metrics, four different answers from the same data. RMSE is minimised by the mean (25.65, pulled up by the long tail). MAE is minimised by the median (20.25). MAPE, which divides by the true value, is minimised far lower (12.20), because an under-prediction of a short trip is penalised less than an over-prediction of the same size: it rewards low forecasts, which is rarely what anyone wants. And pinball loss at the 0.9 quantile, which penalises under-prediction nine times as much as over-prediction, is minimised by the 90th percentile (48.90): the right target if you promise customers an arrival time they should beat 90% of the time.

So the choice of regression metric is a statement about what you want the model to predict and which errors you fear:

R2 deserves a remark because it already contains a baseline: R2 = 1 − SSE/SST, where SSE is your model's sum of squared errors and SST is that of always predicting the mean. R2 = 0 means "no better than the mean", and negative values mean worse. Forecasting adds scale-free measures such as MASE that compare against a naive forecast (Hyndman and Koehler, 2006; chapter 18).

Choosing, checking and distrusting a metric

A workable procedure:

  1. Write the cost of each kind of error in the decision's own units. Money, minutes, harm. Rough numbers are fine; an order of magnitude settles most disputes. If nobody can say, that is itself a finding.
  2. Pick a primary metric that matches the action. A threshold with prices (expected cost), a fixed capacity (precision at k), a promise (a quantile), a ranking (nDCG, AP).
  3. Add guardrail metrics. Things that must not get worse while you optimise the primary: performance on important subgroups, latency, the false alarm rate seen by customers. Chapter 21 and Case 7 show how metrics that look equally reasonable (calibration, error balance) can conflict.
  4. Report with an error bar. A single decimal on a small test set is false precision (chapter 1).
  5. Compare to a baseline, always. "AUC 0.81" means little until you know a one-line rule achieves 0.79 (chapter 4).
  6. Plan the online check. An offline metric is a proxy for the business outcome. Decide how you will confirm the outcome moves, by an experiment if possible (chapter 21).

When the metric becomes the target

The more you optimise a metric, the more it separates from what it stood for. This is often called Goodhart's law, popularly summarised as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) separate several distinct ways this happens, including the plain statistical one (the extreme values of a noisy proxy are partly noise, the same selection effect as the best-of-1,000 experiment in chapter 1) and the adversarial one (people change behaviour to game the measure). A model optimised for click-through learns to prefer clickbait; a support model graded by resolution time learns to close tickets quickly. Defences are practical: several metrics rather than one, a metric you do not train on directly, and human review of examples. The Improvement Flywheel in the evals course develops the last of these.

Systems connection

Choosing a metric is the same discipline as choosing a service-level indicator. An SLI is a measurement chosen to reflect what users experience, and an SLO is the target for it; you would not set an availability SLI that stays green while users see errors. SLIs, SLOs & Error Budgets argues for measuring what users feel and for budgeting the cost of failure, which maps one-to-one onto "price the errors, then choose the threshold". Likewise a precision-at-k target with a review queue is a capacity-planning problem, covered in Queueing Theory for Engineers: a model that floods the analysts with false alarms creates a backlog, which delays real cases.

Analogy

A smoke alarm is tuned by costs, not by accuracy. Nearly every minute of every day there is no fire, so an alarm that stayed silent forever would be 99.99% accurate. Manufacturers accept occasional false alarms from burnt toast because a missed fire costs lives; the "threshold" is set by pricing the two mistakes. And if the alarm sounds too often, people remove the battery, so false alarms have a cost that grows with their number.

Where it breaks down: a smoke alarm's costs are fixed by physics and regulation, while a business model's costs change with prices, staffing and how the customers respond, so the threshold needs revisiting.

Key takeaways

  • A prediction task has five parts: the decision, the unit, the target and when its label arrives, the prediction time (and the features known then), and the metric. Write them down before modelling.
  • Proxy labels carry their flaws into the model. Obermeyer et al. (2019) found that predicting cost in place of health need under-identified Black patients at the same level of illness.
  • Accuracy is dominated by the majority class: on a 1% problem a model that never fires is 99% accurate. Use precision, recall and costs instead.
  • ROC AUC measures ranking quality and is stable across base rates; average precision and precision at k describe what a reader of the list experiences, and collapse as positives become rare.
  • With priced errors, flag when p > CFP / (CFP + CFN). In the simulation this beat the F1-maximising threshold by 31% in cost and the default 0.5 by far more.
  • RMSE targets the mean, MAE the median, pinball loss a quantile, and MAPE a low value: the metric chooses what the model learns to predict.
  • Metrics drift away from their purpose under optimisation. Use guardrails, error bars and baselines, and check the outcome online.

  • Elkan, "The Foundations of Cost-Sensitive Learning" (2001). IJCAI. The cost-based decision rule and how to adapt models to it. (The PDF was not machine-readable when I checked it, so the attribution is from my knowledge of the paper.)

Check your understanding

A hospital tool screens for a condition affecting 0.5% of patients. A model that tells every patient "healthy" has 99.5% accuracy. A second model catches 80% of cases with 2% false positives and has accuracy 98%. Which statement is right?

  1. The first model is better, because its accuracy is higher.
  2. The second is likely more useful, because accuracy hides that the first model never finds a case; compare recall, precision and the cost of each error.
  3. The models are equivalent because both exceed 90% accuracy.
  4. Neither can be evaluated without ROC AUC.
With a 0.5% base rate the always-healthy model gets 99.5% accuracy and zero recall. The second model's lower accuracy comes from false alarms, which may well be an acceptable price for finding 80% of cases, depending on costs. Option 4 is wrong because AUC is one summary, not a prerequisite; the confusion matrix and costs already show the difference.

A missed fraud costs £200 and a manual review costs £4. The model outputs well-calibrated probabilities. What is the cost-minimising threshold for flagging?

  1. 0.5
  2. 0.98, so that only near-certain cases are flagged
  3. 0.20
  4. About 0.0196, which is 4/204
The rule is CFP/(CFP + CFN) = 4/204 ≈ 0.0196, so flag anything with about a 2% chance. Option 1 ignores costs. Option 2 is the right idea pointed the wrong way: because misses are expensive you flag more, not fewer. Option 3 would be right only if a miss cost about four times a false alarm.

You hold a scorer fixed and the share of positives in the incoming data drops from 10% to 0.1%. Which metric would you expect to change least, and which most?

  1. Least: ROC AUC. Most: precision at a fixed threshold (or average precision).
  2. Least: precision. Most: ROC AUC.
  3. All change by the same factor.
  4. None change, because the model is the same.
ROC AUC depends on how positives rank against negatives, which is unchanged. Precision depends on how many negatives sit among the flagged, which grows when positives are rare, as the auc02.py table shows. Option 4 confuses an unchanged model with unchanged metrics.

A team trains a delivery-time model with MAPE as the loss and finds it systematically under-predicts. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. A bug in the data pipeline, since MAPE is unbiased.
  2. The model is overfit.
  3. MAPE penalises over-prediction more than the same-sized under-prediction, so it favours low forecasts.
  4. MAPE targets the median, which is always lower than the mean.
Percentage error is bounded at 100% when you under-predict but unbounded when you over-predict, so minimising it pulls forecasts down (12.2 minutes against a median of 20.25 in the chapter's script). Option 4 is true of MAE but not of MAPE, and the median is not always below the mean. Option 2 would show up as a train-validation gap, not a consistent directional bias.

A bank wants to predict which applicants will repay loans. It trains only on loans it approved in the past, with label "defaulted within 12 months". What is the main framing problem?

  1. Default is too rare to model.
  2. The labels exist only for people previously approved, so the training data is a selected sample and the model may behave unpredictably on applicants who would have been declined.
  3. The metric should be accuracy.
  4. Twelve months is too short.
The outcome is observed only where the old policy said yes, so the model learns about a filtered population and is then applied to everyone. Rarity affects metric choice, not validity of the frame; accuracy is no remedy; and the horizon is a separate decision.

A retention team can call 300 customers per month. Which evaluation best matches the real decision?

  1. Accuracy across all customers at a threshold of 0.5.
  2. ROC AUC alone, since it is threshold-free.
  3. Precision (or lift over the baseline) among the 300 highest-scored customers, ideally with the value of each retained customer.
  4. RMSE of the predicted churn probability.
The action is bounded by capacity, so the quality of the top 300 is what produces value. ROC AUC can be excellent while the top of the list is mediocre. Accuracy at 0.5 ignores both capacity and imbalance, and RMSE of probabilities is a calibration measure, not a statement about the top of the list.
