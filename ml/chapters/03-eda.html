Exploratory Data Analysis

Before a model sees the data, you should. This chapter is a field guide to the first hours with a new table: what a row is, what the distributions and the gaps look like, whether duplicates and shifts will make your validation lie, and which questions to put to the people who produced the data.

Why this matters

Models are obedient: they learn whatever the data contains, including sentinel values, duplicated rows, silently missing groups and a test period that does not resemble training. Most of the expensive surprises in applied machine learning are visible in an hour of looking, and invisible to a metric. After this chapter you will have a repeatable audit for a new dataset, know why summary statistics are not enough, understand the three kinds of missing data and what each does to a model, and be able to test whether your test set has moved away from your training set. The tooling for loading and reshaping tables is in Data Wrangling: pandas, SQL & Tidy Data; this chapter is about what to look for.

What exploratory analysis is for

Exploratory data analysis (EDA), a phrase popularised by the statistician John Tukey in his 1977 book of that name, is looking at data with an open question, "what is here?", before committing to a model. It differs from modelling in intent: you are not trying to get a score, you are trying to find out what you do not know. Four kinds of discovery justify the time.

  • Mistakes in the data: impossible values, wrong units, a column that changed meaning in March.
  • Structure that shapes the method: heavy tails, groups, time trends, strong seasonality, clusters.
  • Threats to evaluation: duplicates, repeated entities, leaks, and differences between the data you train on and the data you will meet.
  • Questions for humans: why is this field empty for one region? who decides this label? A paragraph of answers from the data's owner is worth more than an hour of plotting.

Analogy

Surveying land before you build. You walk the boundary, note where the ground is soft, check old maps against what is on the ground, and ask the neighbours where the pipes run. Nobody pours concrete first. A data scientist who starts fitting models without this walk is building on land they have not seen.

Where it breaks down: land stays still while you survey it, whereas data keeps arriving and a feed can change shape next week, so EDA is a habit to repeat, ideally as automated checks, not a one-off.

First questions: what is a row, and what is the target?

Start with the plainest facts. How many rows and columns? What does one row represent: a customer, a customer-month, a transaction, a ticket, a sensor reading? The answer sets your counting (twelve rows per customer per year is not twelve customers), your splitting (rows from one customer must stay together, chapter 1) and your sense of how much independent data you have. Next, what is the time span, and is it complete? A table that covers January to September and is described as "last year" is a common surprise.

Then the target. Count it: 19.7% positives in the example below is a very different problem from 0.2%. Check how it is defined and when it becomes known (chapter 2). Look at its distribution over time: if the positive rate halves in the last two months, either the world changed or the labels there are not yet complete (a customer who will cancel next week has not cancelled yet, so recent rows look better than they are). That second effect, label lag, silently corrupts recent data in churn, fraud and default problems.

Summaries lie; plots do not

The reflex is to call describe() and read means, standard deviations and correlations. Those summaries discard shape, and shape is where the problems live. The statistician Francis Anscombe (1973) made the point with four small datasets, now called Anscombe's quartet. Each has 11 points. The script below types them in and computes the summaries.

"""Anscombe's quartet (1973): four datasets with near-identical summary statistics."""
import numpy as np

    "III": (x123, [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]),
    "IV":  ([8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8],
            [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89]),
}

To two decimals the four sets share the same mean of x (9.00), mean of y (7.50), variance of y (about 4.1), correlation (0.82) and fitted line (y = 3.00 + 0.50x). A table of statistics cannot tell them apart. The plot can, instantly:

Matejka and Fitzmaurice (2017) extended the idea in "Same Stats, Different Graphs", generating datasets of wildly different shapes (including a dinosaur) with matching summary statistics. The working rule: never trust a summary you have not also seen as a picture. For each numeric column, look at a histogram and the quantiles (minimum, 1st, 25th, 50th, 75th, 99th percentile, maximum). For each pair that matters, a scatter plot, or a binned version when there are many points. For the target, plot its rate against each of the top features and against time.

What to look for in a distribution:

  • Skew and heavy tails. Incomes, prices and counts pile up near zero with a long right tail. A model that minimises squared error will be pulled by the tail; a log transform often turns such a column into something closer to a bell shape.
  • Spikes at special values. A tall bar at exactly 0, at 999 or at 1 January 1970 is usually a default or sentinel, not a real measurement.
  • Impossible or implausible values. Negative ages, heights in the wrong unit, dates in the future.
  • Mixed populations. Two humps usually mean two groups mixed in one column (two currencies, two sensor models).
  • Rounding and heaping. If ages cluster at multiples of five, people are reporting rather than measuring.

An audit you can run in seconds

Plots are indispensable but slow. A quick tabular audit finds the cheap problems first. The script builds a synthetic customer table, 5,000 customers plus 100 accidentally repeated rows, with problems planted that are typical of real data, and then runs a short audit function over it. The fields are: an ID, age, annual income (some missing), tenure in months, a country string, a currency, and whether the customer churned. Pure Python is enough here; in practice you would use pandas (listing further down).

"""A first-look audit of a synthetic customer table: 5,000 rows with planted problems."""
import random
from collections import Counter

rng = random.Random(3)
rows, true_income = [], []
for i in range(5000):
    income = rng.lognormvariate(10.3, 0.5)                       # annual income, pounds
    hidden = rng.random() < (0.05 + 0.25 * (income > 45_000))    # higher earners skip the field more often
    tenure = rng.randint(1, 72)
    churn = rng.random() < (0.30 - 0.003 * tenure + (0.10 if hidden else 0))   # and are a little less engaged
    true_income.append(income)
    rows.append({
        "customer_id": 10_000 + i,
        "age": rng.randint(18, 80) if rng.random() > 0.01 else 999,        # 999 = "unknown" sentinel
        "income": None if hidden else round(income),
        "tenure_months": tenure,
        "country": rng.choice(["UK", "UK", "UK", "uk", "DE", "FR", "United Kingdom"]),            # inconsistent spellings
        "currency": "GBP",                                                    # constant column
        "churned": int(churn),
    })
rows_unique = list(rows)
rows += [dict(r) for r in rng.sample(rows, 100)]                  # 100 exact duplicate rows appended

def audit(rows):
    print(f"rows: {len(rows)}   exact duplicate rows: {len(rows) - len({tuple(r.values()) for r in rows})}")
    print(f"{'column':<14}{'missing':>8}{'unique':>8}  notes")
    for col in rows[0]:
        vals = [r[col] for r in rows]
        present = [v for v in vals if v is not None]
        notes = []
        if len(set(present)) == 1: notes.append("constant: no information")
        if len(set(present)) > 0.95 * len(present) and col != "churned": notes.append("nearly all unique: an ID?")
        if all(isinstance(v, (int, float)) for v in present):
            notes.append(f"min {min(present):,} max {max(present):,}")
            if col == "age" and max(present) > 120: notes.append("impossible ages: sentinel?")
        else:
            notes.append("values: " + ", ".join(f"{k}({n})" for k, n in Counter(present).most_common(8)))
        print(f"{col:<14}{vals.count(None) / len(vals):>8.1%}{len(set(present)):>8}  {'; '.join(notes)}")
    print(f"target 'churned' rate: {sum(r['churned'] for r in rows) / len(rows):.1%}")
audit(rows)

miss = [r["churned"] for r in rows if r["income"] is None]; seen = [r["churned"] for r in rows if r["income"] is not None]
print(f"churn rate where income is missing: {sum(miss)/len(miss):.1%}  where present: {sum(seen)/len(seen):.1%}")

obs = [r["income"] for r in rows_unique if r["income"] is not None]
mean_obs = sum(obs) / len(obs); mean_true = sum(true_income) / len(true_income)
sd = lambda v: (sum((x - sum(v) / len(v)) ** 2 for x in v) / len(v)) ** 0.5
imputed = [r["income"] if r["income"] is not None else mean_obs for r in rows_unique]
print(f"mean income: true {mean_true:,.0f}, observed rows only {mean_obs:,.0f}  (bias {mean_obs - mean_true:+,.0f})")
print(f"std of income: true {sd(true_income):,.0f}, after mean imputation {sd(imputed):,.0f}")

Read the output as a list of questions for the data's owner.

  • Exact duplicates: 100. Someone's join or export repeated rows. If they land on both sides of a random split, the validation score is flattered (see below).
  • customer_id is nearly unique. An identifier carries no generalisable signal; if it is time-ordered, it can still leak the past. Exclude it from features but keep it for grouping.
  • age has a maximum of 999. The minimum of 18 looks fine, and the 999 is a sentinel for "unknown". Left in, it would distort any model that treats age as a number. Convert to missing.
  • country has "UK", "uk" and "United Kingdom". Three spellings of one value split its counts and give a model three separate categories. Normalise.
  • currency is constant. It carries no information; drop it. (But ask why: the company sells in one currency, and if that changes, the column becomes informative at the worst time.)
  • income is 10.2% missing. That needs its own section.

Missing data: how it is missing matters

An empty cell is a fact about the world, not merely an absence of information. Rubin (1976) classified why values go missing, and the classes have different consequences.

MCAR, missing completely at random
The chance of being missing is unrelated to anything, observed or not: a sensor drops a reading at random. Dropping or filling these rows is mostly harmless, apart from lost data.
MAR, missing at random
Missingness depends on other observed columns: younger users skip the income field more often, and we know ages. Models can account for this by using those columns.
MNAR, missing not at random
Missingness depends on the missing value itself: people with high incomes decline to say. The data cannot tell you this from the observed values alone, so you need domain knowledge.

The planted problem in the audit data is MNAR: customers with income above £45,000 are far more likely to leave the field empty (30% against 5%), and customers who leave it empty are also a little more likely to churn. The final lines of the output show the consequences. The mean of the observed incomes is £32,218 against a true mean of £33,685, a bias of −£1,467 (about 4%), because the high earners are the ones who vanish. Filling every gap with that mean shrinks the standard deviation from £17,953 to £15,870 (about 12% lower), since the imputed rows add no spread. And the missing-ness itself is informative: 26.0% of customers with no income churn, against 19.0% of those who gave it.

What that implies for practice:

  • Add a "was missing" indicator next to any filled column. When missingness is informative the indicator is a feature in its own right, and in the data above it carries a seven-point churn difference.
  • Do not delete rows with any missing value unless the fraction is small and the pattern random; you will usually drop a biased slice and, in a 20-column table, most of the table.
  • Impute inside the validation loop. Computing a mean on the whole table before splitting lets validation rows influence the training features, a small preprocessing leak. Fit the imputer on training folds only.
  • Prefer methods that handle missing values natively when you can: modern gradient-boosted trees send missing values down a learned branch (chapter 9).
  • Ask why. "Not applicable" (no spouse's income for single people) is different from "not collected" and from "refused".

Common misconception

"Fill the gaps with the mean and move on." Mean imputation keeps the average right only when values are missing at random, it shrinks the variance, it hides the informative missingness pattern, and done before splitting it leaks. It is a reasonable first baseline, but it is a decision, so record it and compare against alternatives (a missing indicator, a model-based imputer, native handling).

Duplicates and repeated entities

Duplicates matter for evaluation more than for training. If the same record, or a near-copy, is in both your training and test sets, the test score measures memory, not generalisation. There are three levels to check:

  1. Exact duplicate rows. Cheap to find; decide whether they are errors (drop) or real repeated events (keep, but group them).
  2. Near duplicates. The same item with trivial differences: a re-saved image, a text with a changed timestamp, a customer with two nearly identical snapshots. Barz and Denzler (2019), in "Do We Train on Test Data? Purging CIFAR of Near-Duplicates", found that about 3.3% of CIFAR-10 test images and 10% of CIFAR-100 test images have a duplicate in the training set, and that replacing those with fresh images lowered the accuracy of the state-of-the-art networks they tested by roughly 9 to 14% relative to their original accuracy. Even a benchmark studied by thousands of papers had this problem.
  3. Repeated entities. Many rows about the same customer, patient or device. The rows are not duplicates but they are not independent either, which breaks the i.i.d. assumption of chapter 1.

The effect is large and easy to reproduce. In the script, 1,500 distinct entities have noisy labels; 600 of them (40%) get a second row, a near-copy with a tiny perturbation, as if a customer had two snapshots. A 1-nearest-neighbour classifier, which is little more than a lookup, is evaluated in two ways: a random split of rows, and a split by entity so that copies stay together.

"""Near-duplicate rows make a random split look better than it is."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n_unique = 1500
X = rng.normal(0, 1, (n_unique, 5))
y = ((X[:, 0] + 0.5 * X[:, 1] + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n_unique)) > 0).astype(int)   # a noisy rule

# Real tables repeat entities: 40% of rows get one slightly perturbed copy (same customer, a new snapshot)
dup_idx = rng.choice(n_unique, int(0.4 * n_unique), replace=False)
X_all = np.vstack([X, X[dup_idx] + rng.normal(0, 0.01, (len(dup_idx), 5))])
y_all = np.r_[y, y[dup_idx]]
group = np.r_[np.arange(n_unique), dup_idx]                  # which original entity each row came from

def one_nn_accuracy(train_idx, test_idx):
    d = ((X_all[test_idx, None, :] - X_all[None, train_idx, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
    return float((y_all[train_idx][d.argmin(1)] == y_all[test_idx]).mean())

order = rng.permutation(len(y_all))
cut = int(0.7 * len(order))
random_tr, random_te = order[:cut], order[cut:]
print(f"rows {len(y_all)}, distinct entities {n_unique}")
print(f"random row split:    1-NN accuracy {one_nn_accuracy(random_tr, random_te):.3f}")

ents = rng.permutation(n_unique); ent_tr = set(ents[: int(0.7 * n_unique)])
group_tr = np.array([i for i in range(len(y_all)) if group[i] in ent_tr]); group_te = np.array([i for i in range(len(y_all)) if group[i] not in ent_tr])
print(f"grouped split:       1-NN accuracy {one_nn_accuracy(group_tr, group_te):.3f}")
rows 2100, distinct entities 1500
random row split:    1-NN accuracy 0.763
grouped split:       1-NN accuracy 0.587

The lookup model scores 0.763 under the random row split and 0.587 once entities are kept together. Nothing about the model changed, and the extra 17.6 points were pure memory of near-copies. Whenever your table has an entity column, split by it. The same applies to time: a model that sees a customer's January row and is tested on their February row has seen the answer's neighbourhood.

Does the test data look like the training data?

Your validation score is a promise about future data only if the future resembles the past. Dataset shift is the umbrella name for when it does not. Three kinds matter:

  • Covariate shift: the inputs change, the relationship between inputs and target does not. A new marketing channel brings younger users; the way age predicts churn is unchanged.
  • Label shift (prior shift): the share of each class changes. A fraud campaign raises the positive rate.
  • Concept drift: the relationship itself changes. After a price rise, the same customer profile now churns more often.

Two cheap checks find covariate shift before you train. The first scores each feature separately. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic is the largest gap between the two empirical cumulative distributions (0 means identical, 1 means disjoint). The population stability index (PSI) bins the training values by quantile and sums (test share − train share) × ln(test share ÷ train share) over the bins. A common industry convention reads PSI below 0.1 as stable and above 0.25 as a major shift, but that is a rule of thumb from credit scoring, not a theorem.

The second check is adversarial validation: train a classifier to predict whether a row came from the training set or from the test set. If the two are drawn from the same distribution the task is impossible and the AUC is 0.5; the higher the AUC, the more the sets differ, and the classifier's largest coefficients point at which features give the game away. The script builds a training period (customers with average tenure of 24 months, 30% signed up on mobile) and a later test period (average tenure 14 months, 55% on mobile), then applies both checks.

"""Has the test data moved? Per-feature drift scores, then adversarial validation."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n = 4000
def make(n, tenure_mean, mobile_share):
    return np.c_[rng.normal(40, 12, n),                    # age
                 rng.exponential(tenure_mean, n),          # tenure in months
                 rng.lognormal(3.0, 0.8, n),               # monthly spend
                 (rng.random(n) < mobile_share).astype(float)]   # signed up on mobile?
names = ["age", "tenure", "spend", "mobile"]
train, test = make(n, 24, 0.30), make(n, 14, 0.55)         # test period: newer customers, more mobile

def ks(a, b):                                              # biggest gap between the two empirical CDFs
    grid = np.sort(np.r_[a, b])
    return np.abs(np.searchsorted(np.sort(a), grid, side="right") / len(a) - np.searchsorted(np.sort(b), grid, side="right") / len(b)).max()

def psi(a, b, bins=10):                                    # population stability index, bins from the training quantiles
    edges = np.quantile(a, np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1)); edges[0], edges[-1] = -np.inf, np.inf
    pa = np.histogram(a, edges)[0] / len(a) + 1e-6; pb = np.histogram(b, edges)[0] / len(b) + 1e-6
    return float(((pb - pa) * np.log(pb / pa)).sum())

print(f"{'feature':<8}{'KS':>7}{'PSI':>8}")
for j, name in enumerate(names):
    print(f"{name:<8}{ks(train[:, j], test[:, j]):>7.3f}{psi(train[:, j], test[:, j]):>8.3f}")

# Adversarial validation: can a model tell train rows from test rows?
X = np.vstack([train, test]); y = np.r_[np.zeros(n), np.ones(n)]
Z = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)
idx = rng.permutation(2 * n); fit, held = idx[: n], idx[n:]
w, b = np.zeros(4), 0.0
for _ in range(500):                                       # logistic regression by gradient descent
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(Z[fit] @ w + b)))
    w -= 0.5 * Z[fit].T @ (p - y[fit]) / n; b -= 0.5 * (p - y[fit]).mean()
s = Z[held] @ w + b
ranks = np.empty(n); ranks[np.argsort(s)] = np.arange(1, n + 1)
pos = y[held] == 1
auc = (ranks[pos].sum() - pos.sum() * (pos.sum() + 1) / 2) / (pos.sum() * (~pos).sum())
print(f"adversarial AUC (0.5 = indistinguishable): {auc:.3f}")
print("largest coefficients:", ", ".join(f"{names[j]} {w[j]:+.2f}" for j in np.argsort(-np.abs(w))[:2]))
feature      KS     PSI
age       0.018   0.005
tenure    0.202   0.272
spend     0.020   0.002
mobile    0.251   0.265
adversarial AUC (0.5 = indistinguishable): 0.698
largest coefficients: tenure -0.63, mobile +0.47

Age and spend have KS statistics near 0.02 and PSI near zero: unchanged. Tenure (KS 0.202, PSI 0.272) and the mobile flag (KS 0.251, PSI 0.265) have moved, and the adversarial classifier confirms it with an AUC of 0.698, pointing at exactly those two features, tenure with a negative coefficient (the test rows have shorter tenure) and mobile with a positive one. Finding shift does not tell you what to do, but it changes what you do next. You may choose a validation set from the most recent period so that it resembles deployment, reweight training rows, drop the unstable feature, or accept the shift and plan to monitor and retrain (chapter 21). Strong evidence of shift is also the first thing to report when a model underperforms in production. The case of search queries that predicted flu until the world changed is the subject of Case 2.

Relationships, and the feature that is too good

Last, look at how features relate to the target. Plot the target rate by bins of each numeric feature and by each category, and rank features by a simple association (correlation, or the AUC of a single feature). Most real features are weak: single-feature AUCs of 0.55 to 0.65 are typical. A feature with an AUC of 0.99 should not make you celebrate; it should make you suspicious. Ask whether it could be known at prediction time. A "cancellation reason" field, a refund recorded after the fraud report, or a status updated by the very process you are predicting will produce a stunning score and a model that is useless on the day. This is target leakage, and chapter 14 and Lab 4 teach you to hunt it systematically. EDA is where you meet it first.

Also look at relationships among features: two columns that are almost the same (correlation 0.99) add instability to linear models and split importance in tree models. And look at relationships with time: a feature that drifts steadily, or that was only populated after a system change, may separate old rows from new ones better than it separates your classes.

Systems connection

Everything on the audit list is a data-quality check, and in production it should run automatically on every new batch: schema and type checks, null-rate bounds, duplicate counts, value ranges, freshness. Data Platforms: Warehouses, Lakehouses & Pipelines covers where those checks live and why a quality failure should stop a pipeline rather than reach a model. Shift checks are the ML counterpart of monitoring: the same PSI and KS numbers you computed here, run weekly on live inputs, are an early warning that a deployed model is operating outside its experience.

Try it yourself

Run the audit on a table of your own, or alter the synthetic one.

$ python3 audit03.py

  2. Change the income-missing rule so that it no longer depends on income (a constant 10% for everyone). How do the observed mean and the churn difference between missing and present change? Which class of missingness is that now?
  3. In shift03.py, set the test tenure_mean to 24 and the mobile share to 0.30. What adversarial AUC do you expect, and what do you get? Run it with two different seeds to see the noise.
  4. In dupes03.py, raise the duplicate share from 40% to 0%. Do the two accuracies agree? What does this tell you about when grouped splitting matters?

Key takeaways

  • Start every project by answering: what is a row, what is the target and when is it known, what is missing, what repeats, what do values look like, what changes in time, does test match train, and which feature looks too good.
  • Summaries are not enough: Anscombe's four datasets share mean, variance, correlation and fitted line to two decimals and look nothing alike. Plot before you trust.
  • A short tabular audit finds duplicates, sentinels (999), inconsistent categories, constant and ID-like columns in seconds.
  • Missingness has a mechanism. Under MNAR, the observed mean was 4% off and mean imputation cut the spread by 12%, while missingness itself predicted churn (26.0% against 19.0%). Add indicators, impute inside folds.
  • Duplicates and repeated entities inflate validation: a lookup model scored 0.763 on a random row split and 0.587 when entities were kept together.
  • Check shift with per-feature KS and PSI and with an adversarial classifier (AUC near 0.5 means similar; 0.698 here, driven by tenure and mobile).
  • A feature that is far too predictive is a leak suspect until you show it is known at prediction time.

  • Anscombe, "Graphs in Statistical Analysis" (1973). The American Statistician. The original four-dataset paper, three pages long.
  • Matejka and Fitzmaurice, "Same Stats, Different Graphs" (2017). CHI. A method for generating datasets of any shape with fixed summary statistics.
  • Rubin, "Inference and Missing Data" (1976). Biometrika. The origin of the MCAR, MAR and MNAR classification.
  • Barz and Denzler, "Do We Train on Test Data? Purging CIFAR of Near-Duplicates" (2019). How much duplication a famous benchmark contained and what removing it changed.
  • scikit-learn user guide, "Imputation of missing values". Practical imputers, including the missing-indicator option.

Check your understanding

Two datasets have identical means, variances, correlation and best-fit line. What can you conclude about their relationship between x and y?

  1. They have the same relationship, because the summary statistics agree.
  2. Nothing about their shape: one could be a noisy line, another a curve or a line with an outlier, so you need to plot them.
  3. That both are linear, because the correlation is 0.82.
  4. That the correlation is spurious.
This is Anscombe's lesson: low-order summaries discard shape. A correlation of 0.82 can arise from a curve, from one lever point, or from a clean linear trend with one outlier. Option 1 is the trap the quartet was built to expose.

In a loan dataset, applicants with the lowest incomes are the most likely to leave the income field blank, and income affects default. Which kind of missingness is this, and what is a sensible first move?

  1. MCAR; drop the rows.
  2. MAR; fill with the global mean and stop.
  3. MCAR; add noise to the filled values.
  4. MNAR; add a missing indicator and impute within each training fold, and ask the data's owner why it is missing.
The probability of being missing depends on the missing value itself, so it is MNAR. Dropping rows or mean-filling bias the data in a known direction. An indicator preserves the signal, fold-wise imputation avoids leakage, and domain knowledge is the only way to understand a MNAR mechanism. MAR would require missingness to depend on observed columns instead.

A table has 3,000 rows from 800 patients. A model scores 96% on a random 80/20 row split but 78% when whole patients are held out. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. The model recognises individual patients from rows in training, so the random split overestimates performance on new patients.
  2. The held-out patients are mislabelled.
  3. The model is underfit.
  4. Accuracy is not defined for grouped data.
Rows from the same patient are strongly correlated, so a random split leaks identity-specific information into validation. If the model will be applied to new patients, the grouped number is the honest one. Underfitting would lower both scores, not widen the gap. Option 2 is not suggested by the setup.

An adversarial validation model, trained to separate training rows from test rows, reaches AUC 0.50. How do you read this?

  1. The test set is useless.
  2. The features are all uninformative about the target.
  3. The model cannot tell the sets apart, which is consistent with train and test having the same input distribution.
  4. The model is overfit.
AUC 0.5 means the classifier does no better than chance at identifying which set a row came from, so there is no evidence of covariate shift in the inputs it could use. It says nothing about whether the features predict the target (option 2) and does not rule out label shift or concept drift.

While exploring, you find a feature with a single-feature AUC of 0.99 for predicting loan default. What is the right next step?

  1. Keep it and report the model, since it is clearly the strongest signal.
  2. Ask whether the value is known at the time of prediction and how it is produced; treat it as a leak suspect until shown otherwise.
  3. Remove all other features so the model is simple.
  4. Transform it with a logarithm to reduce its influence.
Real predictors are rarely that strong. Features that encode the outcome (a collections status, a write-off flag) or are set after it are leaks, so check availability at prediction time. A transform does not remove a leak, and dropping the other features makes things worse.

In the shift example, tenure had PSI 0.272 and age 0.005 between train and test. Which action fits best?

  1. Ignore both; PSI is only for credit scoring.
  2. Drop age, since it is stable.
  3. Conclude that the target rate has changed.
  4. Investigate why tenure moved, consider validating on recent data or reweighting, and monitor tenure in production.
A PSI above the conventional 0.25 for tenure signals the input distribution has moved materially, so the validation score from an older period may not describe the new one. PSI says nothing directly about the target rate (option 3), and stable features are not a reason to drop them.
