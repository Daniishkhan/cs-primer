Decision Trees

A decision tree is a flowchart that a computer writes for you: a sequence of yes/no questions about the features, ending in a prediction. This chapter shows how the questions are chosen, why a tree must be stopped, and why the same property that makes a single tree easy to read (a few crisp rules) also makes it unstable, which is the opening for the next chapter.

Why this matters

On the tabular data of most businesses (customers, transactions, claims, sensors), the best-performing models are not linear models or neural networks: they are ensembles of decision trees (chapter 9). Every one of those ensembles is built from the pieces in this chapter, so understanding a single tree, how it chooses a split, how it overfits and where it fails, is the prerequisite for random forests, gradient boosting, XGBoost and LightGBM. A single tree is also worth knowing for its own sake: it is the one model whose entire logic can be printed on a page and read by a compliance officer. Trees handle thresholds and interactions that a linear model misses, need no feature scaling, and work with mixed data. After this chapter you will be able to write a tree learner from scratch, read the output, choose its depth honestly and recognise its characteristic failures.

A tree is a flowchart learned from data

A decision tree predicts by asking a series of questions, each about one feature compared with a threshold. A question sends the example down the left or right branch, where another question awaits, until it reaches a box with no further questions: a leaf, which holds the prediction. The boxes with questions are internal nodes, and the first one is the root. A leaf predicts the majority class of the training examples that reached it, and the proportion of that class is a crude probability.

Here is one fitted to a synthetic churn dataset. It has 1,500 customers, with tenure (months), spend (monthly), tickets (support tickets last quarter) and basic_plan (1 if on the basic plan). 1,000 customers are for training and 500 for testing. The generating rule, which the tree must discover, is that a customer is "risky" if they have at least 3 tickets and under 24 months of tenure, or are on the basic plan with spend above 65 and under 40 months of tenure; risky customers churn with probability 0.88 and others with 0.12. So even a perfect model is right about 88 percent of the time.

Reading the tree is straightforward and that is its great virtue. A customer with 1 ticket, spend 80 and a basic plan goes left at the root (tickets <= 2.5), right at spend <= 66.1, right at basic_plan <= 0.5 and lands in a leaf where 60 of 92 training customers churned: predict churn. Every leaf is a rule one can state in a sentence ("three or more tickets and under 24 months of tenure"), and the tree has found, unprompted, the two ingredients of the hidden rule.

Choosing a question: impurity

The central problem is picking the questions. For each candidate question the tree needs a score for how much it helps, and the score has to be about how mixed the resulting groups are. A group of 100 customers who all churned is easy to predict; a group split 50-50 is as hard as it gets. The impurity of a node measures this mixing, and there are three standard choices, for a node in which a fraction p of the examples are positive:

  • Gini impurity: 1 − p2 − (1 − p)2 = 2p(1 − p). It is the probability that two examples drawn at random from the node (with replacement) have different labels. It is 0 for a pure node and 0.5 at an even split. It is the default in CART and in scikit-learn.
  • Entropy: −p log2 p − (1 − p) log2(1 − p), the surprise measure of A0 chapter 14, in bits: 0 for a pure node, 1 bit at an even split. The reduction in entropy produced by a split is the information gain, the criterion of Quinlan's ID3 and C4.5 algorithms.
  • Misclassification rate: 1 − max(p, 1 − p), the fraction of the node a majority-vote leaf would get wrong.

Gini and entropy are nearly proportional, and in practice they pick the same splits most of the time. Misclassification rate is a poor guide for growing a tree, because it is a straight line on each side of 0.5: a split that turns a 70/30 node into a 100/0 child and a 50/50 child can leave the total misclassification count unchanged, although it has clearly made progress. The smooth curves reward purity even when the majority class does not change.

A split is scored by the weighted impurity of its two children, compared with the parent's. Each child is weighted by its share of the rows. The reduction is the impurity decrease (or gain), and the tree takes the question with the biggest decrease. Here is the search by hand on a toy problem: ten customers, their tenure in months and whether they churned. The parent node has 5 churners and 5 stayers, so its gini is 0.5.

import numpy as np

def gini(y):
    p = np.bincount(y, minlength=2) / len(y)
    return 1 - np.sum(p ** 2)

def entropy(y):
    p = np.bincount(y, minlength=2) / len(y); p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

# ten customers: tenure in months and whether they churned (1) or stayed (0)
tenure = np.array([2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15, 20, 26, 34])
churn  = np.array([1, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0])
print("node of 10 customers: 5 churned, 5 stayed")
print(f"  gini = {gini(churn):.3f}   entropy = {entropy(churn):.3f} bits   misclassification = {min(churn.mean(), 1 - churn.mean()):.3f}\n")

print("threshold   left (n, churn)  right (n, churn)   weighted gini   gain")
parent = gini(churn); best = None
for t in (tenure[:-1] + tenure[1:]) / 2:                   # candidate cut points: midpoints between sorted values
    L, R = churn[tenure <= t], churn[tenure > t]
    w = (len(L) * gini(L) + len(R) * gini(R)) / len(churn)
    print(f"  <= {t:<6.1f}  {len(L):>2}, {int(L.sum())}            {len(R):>2}, {int(R.sum())}               {w:.3f}        {parent - w:+.3f}")
    if best is None or w < best[1]: best = (t, w)
print(f"\nbest cut: tenure <= {best[0]}  (weighted gini {best[1]:.3f}, gain {parent - best[1]:.3f})")

The candidate thresholds are the midpoints between consecutive sorted values (nothing else can change a split's outcome). The best is tenure <= 6.0: the left child has 3 customers who all churned (gini 0), the right has 7 with 2 churners (gini 0.408), and the weighted average is 0.286, a gain of 0.214. A cut at 10.5 has a gain of 0.180, and a cut at 23 gains nothing: it moves one churner into a tiny pure-looking group while the big group keeps its original mix. Note how a single churner at tenure 9 and another at 26 stop any threshold from separating the classes perfectly. That is noise at work, and the tree has no way of knowing which points are noise: it will fit them if allowed.

For a numerical target (a regression tree), the same machinery applies with a different impurity: the variance of the target in the node, or equivalently the sum of squared errors around the node's mean. A split is good when it reduces the weighted variance, and each leaf predicts the mean of its training targets.

Growing the tree

Given the scoring rule, the learning algorithm (CART, short for "classification and regression trees", by Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, 1984, in the book Classification and Regression Trees) is a few lines of recursion:

  1. Start with all training rows in one node.
  2. Search every feature and every candidate threshold for the split with the largest impurity decrease. Sort the feature once; then the class counts on each side of every cut come from running sums, so scoring all cuts costs one pass.
  3. Split the node into two children by the winning question.
  4. Recurse on each child independently, ignoring the rest of the data.
  5. Stop a branch when the node is pure, or a limit applies (a maximum depth, a minimum number of rows to attempt a split, a minimum size for a leaf, or a minimum gain).

The algorithm is greedy: it picks the best split now without looking ahead, because finding the globally best tree is computationally intractable (optimal decision tree learning is NP-complete, Hyafil and Rivest, 1976).   3. Split 2. The 59 rows that reached this node are searched again, from scratch, ignoring the rest of the data. Best question: tenure <= 36.1; gini 0.282 at the node falls to a weighted 0.232 after the split.
  4. Split 3. The 26 rows that reached this node are searched again, from scratch, ignoring the rest of the data. Best question: tickets <= 2.8; gini 0.453 at the node falls to a weighted 0.296 after the split.
  5. Stop, and read the leaves. The tree stops after 3 splits (a depth limit and a minimum leaf size are the usual stopping rules). The plane is now 4 rectangles; each predicts its majority class, and together they classify 64 of 70 training rows correctly (91%). Each leaf box shows how many of its customers churned, out of how many arrived. More splits would raise that number, partly by fitting noise: depth is the knob the next sections examine.

Here is the whole learner in NumPy, about 45 lines. It is the code behind the tree figure above and the experiments below. It stores a tree as nested dictionaries, uses the sorted-running-sums trick, and respects a maximum depth and a minimum leaf size. (There is no output to show for the module itself; the experiments that import it follow.)

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 1500
tenure = rng.uniform(1, 60, n); spend = np.clip(rng.normal(50, 20, n), 5, None)
tickets = rng.poisson(1.5, n).astype(float); basic = rng.integers(0, 2, n).astype(float)
risky = ((tickets >= 3) & (tenure < 24)) | ((basic == 1) & (spend > 65) & (tenure < 40))
y = (rng.random(n) < np.where(risky, 0.88, 0.12)).astype(int)         # 12% label noise either way
X = np.column_stack([tenure, spend, tickets, basic]); names = ["tenure", "spend", "tickets", "basic_plan"]
X_tr, y_tr, X_te, y_te = X[:1000], y[:1000], X[1000:], y[1000:]

def gini_from_counts(c1, n):                      # n rows, c1 of them positive
    p = c1 / n; return 2 * p * (1 - p)

def best_split(X, y, min_leaf):
    n, best = len(y), (0.0, None, None)                          # (gain, feature, threshold)
    parent = gini_from_counts(y.sum(), n)
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):
        order = np.argsort(X[:, j], kind="stable"); xs, ys = X[order, j], y[order]
        left_n = np.arange(1, n); left_pos = np.cumsum(ys)[:-1]   # stats of "first i rows" for every cut i
        right_n, right_pos = n - left_n, ys.sum() - left_pos
        w = (left_n * gini_from_counts(left_pos, left_n) + right_n * gini_from_counts(right_pos, right_n)) / n
        ok = (xs[1:] != xs[:-1]) & (left_n >= min_leaf) & (right_n >= min_leaf)   # only real gaps, big enough leaves
        if ok.any():
            i = np.where(ok, w, np.inf).argmin()
            if parent - w[i] > best[0]: best = (parent - w[i], j, (xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2)
    return best

def grow(X, y, depth, max_depth, min_leaf):
    node = {"n": len(y), "pos": int(y.sum())}
    if depth < max_depth and 0 < y.sum() < len(y):
        gain, j, t = best_split(X, y, min_leaf)
        if j is not None:
            m = X[:, j] <= t
            node.update(feat=j, thr=t, left=grow(X[m], y[m], depth + 1, max_depth, min_leaf), right=grow(X[~m], y[~m], depth + 1, max_depth, min_leaf))
    return node

def predict(node, x):
    while "feat" in node: node = node["left"] if x[node["feat"]] <= node["thr"] else node["right"]
    return node["pos"] / node["n"] > 0.5

def show(node, indent=""):
    if "feat" not in node: print(f"{indent}-> {'CHURN' if node['pos'] / node['n'] > 0.5 else 'stay'}  ({node['pos']} of {node['n']} churned)"); return
    print(f"{indent}if {names[node['feat']]} <= {node['thr']:.1f}:"); show(node["left"], indent + "    ")
    print(f"{indent}else:"); show(node["right"], indent + "    ")

acc = lambda t, X, y: float(np.mean([predict(t, x) == yy for x, yy in zip(X, y)]))
def leaves(t): return 1 if "feat" not in t else leaves(t["left"]) + leaves(t["right"])

Read the baseline first: the logistic regression scores 0.802 on the test set and the majority-class guess 0.772, so the linear model barely improves on a constant. The hidden rule is made of interactions (tickets and tenure, plan and spend and tenure), and no weighted sum of the four features expresses an "and". The depth-3 tree gets 0.850, and the best of the sweep, at depth 4, gets 0.882, which is the ceiling set by the label noise. Trees find interactions without being told about them, because each branch below the root is automatically conditional on the answers above it.

Analogy: twenty questions

The tree learner plays a game of twenty questions against the training data. At each step it asks the question that most reduces its uncertainty about the answer, then continues in whichever branch the answer opens, asking a fresh best question there. Like a good player it often finds the answer in a handful of questions, and the questions it asks depend on the answers it has already had.

Where it breaks down: in the game, the answers are fixed and the question-asker could in principle plan ahead. A tree has to choose its first question knowing only a noisy sample, and it cannot revise it later; a slightly different sample may lead it to a different first question and a different game altogether.

How deep? Overfitting in a tree

A tree that is allowed to grow until every leaf is pure will always succeed in fitting the training data, because in the worst case each training row gets its own leaf. The last rows of the depth table show it: at depth 20 there are 167 leaves, the training accuracy is 1.000 and the test accuracy is 0.792, worse than the depth-3 tree and no better than guessing the majority class. The deep splits are carving up noise: with 12 percent of labels flipped at random, a leaf of four customers has little to learn from.

The curve is the familiar shape of chapter 6, with depth as the complexity axis. Training accuracy climbs to 1.0 and test accuracy peaks near depth 4 (0.882) and then declines. Depth is only one way to hold a tree back; the usual controls, and the scikit-learn parameters that implement them (documented for version 1.9.1, read on 14 October 2026), are:

max_depth (default none)
The longest path from root to leaf. The blunt instrument: it limits every branch equally, including the ones that are learning something real.
min_samples_leaf (default 1)
No leaf may have fewer than this many training rows. Leaves of one or two rows are almost always noise, so this is often the better control. The scikit-learn documentation suggests trying 5 as a starting value. In our run, with min_samples_leaf=20 and no depth limit, the tree stopped on its own at 33 leaves with train accuracy 0.886 and test accuracy 0.882: as good as the best depth, found without choosing a depth.
min_samples_split (default 2), min_impurity_decrease (default 0.0)
Do not split a node that is too small, or whose best split gains too little.
ccp_alpha (default 0.0)
Cost-complexity pruning. Grow a large tree, then cut back the branches that earn their keep least. It minimises R(T) + α|leaves(T)|, the tree's training error plus a price α per leaf: exactly the penalty idea of chapter 6, with the number of leaves in place of the size of the weights. Larger α gives a smaller tree.

Whichever control you use, it is a hyperparameter, and it must be chosen on data the tree has not seen: cross-validation (chapter 13), not the training loss, which would always prefer the deepest tree.

The instability of a single tree

The first question a tree asks is decided by a comparison of a few thousand impurity numbers, and a small change to the data can flip which candidate is best. That is a high-variance model in the sense of chapter 6, and it can be measured. The script below draws 30 bootstrap samples of the training rows (1,000 rows drawn with replacement, so about 63 percent of the distinct rows appear in each), fits a tree to each, and looks at what changes.

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
from tree import X_tr, y_tr, X_te, y_te, names, grow, predict, acc

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = len(y_tr)
boot = [rng.integers(0, n, n) for _ in range(30)]                   # 30 bootstrap resamples of the training rows

# 1. How stable is the first question the tree asks?
roots = Counter()
for idx in boot:
    t = grow(X_tr[idx], y_tr[idx], 0, 3, 20)
    roots[(names[t["feat"]], round(t["thr"]))] += 1
print("root split of a depth-3 tree, over 30 bootstrap samples:")
for (f, thr), c in roots.most_common(): print(f"   {f} <= about {thr}: {c} times")

# 2. How much do deep trees disagree with each other on the same test rows?
trees = [grow(X_tr[idx], y_tr[idx], 0, 20, 5) for idx in boot]
P = np.array([[predict(t, x) for x in X_te] for t in trees])          # (30 trees, 500 test rows)
pairs = [(P[i] != P[j]).mean() for i in range(30) for j in range(i + 1, 30)]
print(f"\nfully grown trees: on average two trees disagree on {np.mean(pairs):.1%} of test rows")
print(f"  accuracy of a single such tree: {np.mean([(P[i] == y_te).mean() for i in range(30)]):.3f}")
print(f"  accuracy of the majority vote of all 30: {((P.mean(0) > 0.5) == y_te).mean():.3f}")
shallow = np.array([[predict(grow(X_tr[idx], y_tr[idx], 0, 3, 20), x) for x in X_te] for idx in boot])
print(f"depth-3 trees disagree on {np.mean([(shallow[i] != shallow[j]).mean() for i in range(30) for j in range(i + 1, 30)]):.1%} of test rows")

Two fully grown trees fitted to different bootstrap samples disagree with each other on 14.7 percent of the test rows, even though each is individually about 82 percent accurate (0.816). Shallow trees are more stable (3.7 percent disagreement at depth 3) but still change.

The last two lines of output are a preview of the next chapter. If you average the opinions of the 30 trees (a majority vote) the accuracy rises from 0.816 for a typical single tree to 0.880, with no change to the algorithm, just by repeating it on resampled data and voting. High variance means the errors are partly random from tree to tree, and averaging random errors cancels them. Chapter 9 turns that observation into random forests, and then shows a different route to the same destination, gradient boosting.

Common misconception

"A decision tree is easy to interpret, so I can trust its explanations." A tree is easy to read. Whether the reading is stable is another matter: as the experiment shows, the first split may change if the training data changes slightly, and with it the story ("tickets are the key driver" in one refit, "tenure" in the next). Treat the structure of a single deep tree as one sample of many equally plausible trees, not as a discovery about the world. The importances that libraries print for trees (how much impurity each feature removed in total) are also biased towards features with many distinct values (an ID column can look important); chapter 16 introduces permutation importance, which does not share that bias.

What trees cannot do

Diagonal boundaries cost a staircase

Every question concerns one feature at a time, so every boundary is parallel to an axis. A linear model expresses "feature 1 greater than feature 2" with two weights. A tree has to approximate it with steps.

import numpy as np
from tree import grow, predict, acc, leaves

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)

# 1. A regression tree predicts the mean of the training targets in its leaf: it cannot extrapolate
x = rng.uniform(0, 10, 200); y = 2 * x + rng.normal(0, 1, 200)
def reg_tree(x, y, depth):
    if depth == 0 or len(y) < 10: return ("leaf", y.mean())
    o = np.argsort(x); xs, ys = x[o], y[o]
    csum, csq = np.cumsum(ys), np.cumsum(ys ** 2); n = len(ys); i = np.arange(1, n)
    sse = (csq[:-1] - csum[:-1] ** 2 / i) + ((csq[-1] - csq[:-1]) - (csum[-1] - csum[:-1]) ** 2 / (n - i))   # SSE after each cut
    k = int(sse.argmin()); thr = (xs[k] + xs[k + 1]) / 2
    return ("split", thr, reg_tree(x[x <= thr], y[x <= thr], depth - 1), reg_tree(x[x > thr], y[x > thr], depth - 1))
def reg_pred(t, v):
    while t[0] == "split": t = t[2] if v <= t[1] else t[3]
    return t[1]
tree = reg_tree(x, y, 5); slope, icpt = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
print("true rule: y = 2x.  training x ranges over 0 to 10")
print("     x   truth   tree   line")
for v in (2.5, 7.5, 10, 15, 20):
    print(f"{v:>6}  {2 * v:6.1f}  {reg_pred(tree, v):5.1f}  {icpt + slope * v:5.1f}")

# 2. Axis-aligned splits approximate a diagonal boundary with a staircase
X = rng.uniform(0, 1, (600, 2)); lab = (X[:, 0] > X[:, 1]).astype(int)        # the true boundary is x1 = x2
Xt = rng.uniform(0, 1, (2000, 2)); labt = (Xt[:, 0] > Xt[:, 1]).astype(int)
print("\ndiagonal boundary x1 > x2, noise-free labels:")
print("depth  leaves  test accuracy")
for d in (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12):
    t = grow(X, lab, 0, d, 1); print(f"{d:>5}  {leaves(t):>6}  {acc(t, Xt, labt):13.3f}")
print("a single linear boundary (the true one) would score 1.000 with 2 parameters")

The second block of the output sets a tree against a noise-free diagonal boundary. At depth 2 (four leaves) it gets 0.867; at depth 4 (14 leaves) 0.943; and it plateaus around 0.944 at 29 leaves, where every training point is classified and extra depth has nothing to fix. A straight line with two parameters gets 1.000. This is the weakness that features such as the difference of two columns, a ratio or a rotated pair (chapter 17) are designed to repair.

Trees cannot extrapolate

A leaf predicts the average of the training targets in its region, so the model's output is piecewise constant, and it never exceeds the range of values it has seen. The first block shows a regression tree and a straight line fitted to y = 2x plus a little noise, with x between 0 and 10 in training. Inside the range both do well (at 7.5: truth 15.0, tree 14.7, line 14.9). Outside it the tree is flat: at x = 15 and at x = 20 it predicts 17.7, the mean of the last leaf, where the truth is 30 and 40 and the line says 29.8 and 39.8. That matters for any quantity that trends over time (prices, demand, user counts): a tree fitted to last year cannot predict a number higher than any it saw. Chapter 18 returns to this in forecasting.

Scikit-learn's guide states both limits: tree predictions are "piecewise constant approximations" and "therefore, they are not good at extrapolation", and small variations in the data can produce "a completely different tree". It also notes that its implementation (an optimised CART) does not support categorical variables natively, so categories must be encoded first (chapter 17), and that tree learners "create biased trees if some classes dominate", so imbalanced data wants class weights or resampling (chapter 15).

What trees do well

  • No feature scaling. A split compares a feature with a threshold, so only the order of values matters. Multiplying spend by 1,000, or taking its logarithm, leaves the tree's predictions unchanged (the thresholds shift to match). The standardising that distance-based models and penalised linear models require is unnecessary.
  • Thresholds and interactions for free. "More than 2 tickets", "under 24 months" and the conjunction of the two are exactly what a tree is made of.
  • Mixed types and missing values. Numerical and binary features sit side by side. Recent scikit-learn versions have built-in support for missing values in DecisionTreeClassifier and DecisionTreeRegressor (with the default splitter='best'); other libraries have their own conventions, and chapter 9 covers how the boosted-tree libraries treat them.
  • Cheap prediction. A prediction is a walk from the root, costing a number of comparisons equal to the depth, independent of the training set size.
  • Readable, auditable logic. The structure can be printed, reviewed, turned into rules and compared with expert expectations: a real advantage where a decision must be explained (credit, insurance, medicine), provided you remember the instability caveat above.

Systems connection

A fitted tree is a nested if statement, and that has operational consequences. It can be compiled into a few lines of code with no dependency on a machine-learning library, which makes it easy to run inside a database query, a rules engine or a latency-sensitive service; a prediction is a handful of comparisons that sit in a few cache lines, though branch-heavy code behaves differently from the arithmetic of a linear model on a GPU or SIMD unit. A rule set that regulators or customers can read is also easier to review for unfair behaviour: a leaf that routes on a proxy for a protected attribute is visible in a tree and much harder to spot in a neural network. Finally, the instability above has a deployment form: retrain a deep tree weekly and its rules, and so its explanations, can change from one week to the next even if its accuracy does not.

Key takeaways

  • A decision tree is a learned flowchart of threshold questions on single features; each leaf predicts the majority class (or mean) of the training rows that reach it. Trees need no scaling, handle thresholds and interactions naturally, and can be printed and read.
  • The learner is greedy and recursive (CART): at each node it tries every feature and every midpoint threshold, picks the split that most reduces the weighted impurity of the two children (gini or entropy for classification, variance for regression), and recurses.
  • Left alone, a tree fits every training row, noise included. Depth, minimum leaf size, minimum gain and cost-complexity pruning are the complexity knobs, to be chosen by validation, not training accuracy.
  • A single tree is a high-variance model: on bootstrap samples the root split changed in 4 of 30 fits and deep trees disagreed on 14.7 percent of test rows. Treat the structure of one tree as one of many plausible ones.
  • Averaging many trees fitted to resampled data cut the error markedly (0.816 to 0.880): the foundation of random forests in the next chapter.
  • Trees cut along the axes, so diagonal boundaries need staircases; and their output is piecewise constant, so they cannot extrapolate beyond the range of the training targets.

Check your understanding

A node contains 100 training customers, 20 of whom churned. What is its Gini impurity?

  1. 0.20
  2. 0.16
  3. 0.32
  4. 0.50
Gini = 2p(1 − p) with p = 0.2 gives 2 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.32. 0.20 is the churn rate itself, 0.16 is just 0.2 × 0.8 (missing the factor 2), and 0.50 would be an even 50/50 node, the maximum for two classes.

A fully grown tree has training accuracy 1.000 and test accuracy 0.79, while a depth-4 tree scores 0.89 on training and 0.88 on test. What is the most sensible fix for the first tree?

  1. Standardise the features, so that the splits are fairer
  2. Add more depth, so that it can learn the remaining 21 percent
  3. Switch the criterion from gini to entropy
  4. Limit its complexity, for example with a minimum leaf size or pruning, chosen by cross-validation
The tree has memorised noise, which is a variance problem; restricting leaf size, depth or the number of leaves (or pruning) trades a little training accuracy for much better generalisation. Trees do not need standardised features. More depth would worsen the problem, and gini versus entropy rarely changes the outcome.

You refit a tree on a bootstrap resample of the same data and its root question changes from "tickets <= 2" to "tenure <= 27". Which technique directly exploits this kind of instability to improve accuracy?

  1. Using a single deeper tree, so the root matters less
  2. Fitting many trees on resampled data and averaging their predictions
  3. Using the root question from the first fit and fixing it
  4. Removing the tenure feature from the data
Instability is high variance: different samples give different trees whose errors are partly independent, so averaging many of them cancels the random parts (our majority vote of 30 trees lifted accuracy from 0.816 to 0.880). This is bagging, the basis of random forests. A deeper tree would be more unstable, not less, and fixing or removing features throws information away rather than reducing variance.

A regression tree is trained on delivery times for orders of 1 to 50 items. A customer orders 120 items. What does the tree predict?

  1. About the average delivery time of the largest orders in training, because the last leaf's mean is the prediction for everything beyond it
  2. A value that continues the upward trend, as a straight line would
  3. An error, because 120 is outside the training range
  4. The overall average delivery time across all training orders
A leaf predicts the mean of the training targets in its region, and the rightmost leaf covers every value above its threshold, so the prediction is flat beyond the data (17.7 at x = 15 and at x = 20 in our example). Trees do not continue trends, and they do not raise errors for out-of-range values; and the overall mean is only the prediction of a tree with no splits.

You rescale the feature spend by converting pounds to pence (multiplying by 100) and retrain a decision tree with identical settings. What happens?

  1. The tree becomes dominated by spend, as in k-nearest neighbours
  2. The tree needs a lower learning rate
  3. The tree's predictions are unchanged, because only the ordering of values matters and the thresholds simply scale
  4. Training fails because pence are integers
Splits depend only on how a feature orders the rows. A monotonic rescaling gives the same orderings, hence the same best splits, with thresholds multiplied by 100. Distance-based methods such as kNN do care about scale, but trees do not; and there is no learning rate in a single tree.

The target is the exclusive-or of two features (positive when exactly one of x1 > 0 and x2 > 0 holds), with data spread evenly over all four quadrants. A greedy tree considers the root split. What is the problem?

  1. Trees cannot represent exclusive-or at any depth
  2. No single first split improves impurity much, so the greedy search sees little reason to split, though a tree of depth 2 would separate the classes perfectly
  3. The tree would split on x1 and x2 simultaneously and double count
  4. The criterion becomes negative, so the tree stops
Splitting on x1 alone leaves each side 50/50, and the same for x2, so the gain at the root is close to zero: a greedy learner cannot see that two questions together are decisive. A depth-2 tree represents exclusive-or exactly. Trees split on one feature at a time, and impurity decrease is never negative for a valid split. In practice noise breaks the tie and the tree usually finds the structure anyway, but this shows the limitation of greedy search.
