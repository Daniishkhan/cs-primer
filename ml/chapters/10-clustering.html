Clustering

Clustering asks a table of rows to organise itself into groups, with no labels to say what a group should be. This chapter builds the three standard methods (k-means, hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN), shows what each one assumes about the shape of a group, and then faces the awkward question: how do you know the clusters you found are real?

Why this matters

Everything so far in this course was supervised: each row had a target to predict, and validation told you whether you had done well. Clustering is unsupervised learning: there is no target and therefore no score to check against. That makes it useful (customer segments, document groups, sensor states, a first look at an unfamiliar dataset, the first step of a vector index) and dangerous, because every algorithm will return clusters whether or not the data contains any. After this chapter you will be able to pick a method from the shape of your data, choose the number of clusters with evidence rather than a guess, and run the checks that separate a real structure from a pattern the algorithm imposed.

What clustering is, and what it cannot be

A cluster is a set of rows that are more similar to each other than to the rest. That sentence hides a decision: similar in what sense? Every method starts from a distance between rows, usually the Euclidean distance of A0 chapter 5 on the feature vector. So the features, and their scales, are the definition of similarity. If one column is income in pounds and another is age in years, distance is almost entirely income, and the clusters will be income bands. The standard first step is to standardise columns (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation), and to think about which columns should count at all.

The second hidden decision is what a cluster looks like. Different algorithms assume different answers, and the same data can be clustered correctly by one method and nonsensically by another. Keep a picture of two datasets in mind throughout the chapter. The first is three round blobs of points, like three towns on a map. The second is two interlocking crescent moons. A person sees the groups instantly in both. The first is easy for most algorithms; the second defeats the most popular one.

Analogy: post offices for a town

Imagine opening three post offices for a town. A natural procedure is: put the three offices anywhere, assign every house to its nearest office, then move each office to the middle of the houses that use it, and repeat until nothing moves. The result is a neat division of the town into three catchment areas, each round-ish and centred on its office. That is k-means exactly.

Where it breaks down: catchment areas are the right answer when the town is a few clumps of houses. If the houses really sit along two curved streets, the same procedure still cuts the town into three round areas, because the method only knows how to draw lines halfway between offices. It has no concept of a street.

k-means

k-means divides n rows into k groups by minimising the inertia, also called the within-cluster sum of squares: the total squared distance from each point to the centre (mean) of its cluster. The user supplies k. The standard procedure, Lloyd's algorithm (Lloyd, 1982, written in 1957 and published later; MacQueen, 1967, coined the name), alternates two steps:

  1. Assign. Give each point to the nearest of the current k centres.
  2. Update. Move each centre to the mean of the points assigned to it.

Repeat until the assignments stop changing. Each step can only lower the inertia or leave it unchanged: reassigning a point to a nearer centre cannot raise its distance, and the mean of a set is the point that minimises the sum of squared distances to the set. The inertia cannot go below zero and there are finitely many assignments, so the loop must stop. It stops at a local minimum, a solution no single step can improve, which is not always the best one.

Starting positions matter

Because the result is a local minimum, it depends on where the centres start. Picking k random rows works often and fails badly enough to matter. k-means++ (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007, "k-means++: The Advantages of Careful Seeding") picks the first centre at random and each later centre from the rows with probability proportional to the squared distance to the nearest existing centre, so new centres tend to land far from old ones. The standard practice is to run the algorithm from several starts (the n_init parameter in scikit-learn) and keep the run with the lowest inertia.

The script below is a complete NumPy implementation with both initialisations and a silhouette function (explained in the next section). The first experiment clusters a dataset of three overlapping Gaussian blobs, 150 points each, with k from 2 to 7, keeping the best of 5 starts. The second builds six well-separated groups of 60 points and records the inertia of 300 single runs per initialisation.

import numpy as np

def kmeans(X, k, rng, init="plusplus", n_init=1, iters=100):
    best = None
    for _ in range(n_init):
        if init == "plusplus":                     # k-means++: spread the starting centres out
            C = [X[rng.integers(len(X))]]
            for _ in range(k - 1):
                d2 = np.min([((X - c) ** 2).sum(1) for c in C], axis=0)
                C.append(X[rng.choice(len(X), p=d2 / d2.sum())])
            C = np.array(C)
        else:                                      # plain random rows
            C = X[rng.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]
        for _ in range(iters):
            lab = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(2).argmin(1)    # assignment step
            newC = np.array([X[lab == j].mean(0) if (lab == j).any() else C[j] for j in range(k)])
            if np.allclose(newC, C): break
            C = newC                                                   # update step
        inertia = ((X - C[lab]) ** 2).sum()
        if best is None or inertia < best[0]: best = (inertia, lab, C)
    return best

def silhouette(X, lab):
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None] - X[None]) ** 2).sum(2)); s = np.zeros(len(X))
    for i in range(len(X)):
        same = (lab == lab[i]); same[i] = False
        if not same.any(): continue
        a = D[i, same].mean()
        b = min(D[i, lab == c].mean() for c in set(lab) if c != lab[i])
        s[i] = (b - a) / max(a, b)
    return s.mean()

What k-means assumes

Because it summarises each cluster by a mean and assigns by nearest mean, k-means implicitly assumes that clusters are roughly round (equal spread in every direction), similar in size and similar in density, and that every point belongs to some cluster. It breaks in predictable ways. Elongated clusters get cut across their short direction. A tight cluster next to a large diffuse one may be absorbed into it. Outliers pull a mean, and with no "noise" label, each outlier is forced into a cluster. The method is also sensitive to the units of the data, which is why standardising first matters. A variant, k-medoids, uses actual rows as centres and tolerates outliers better at a higher cost, and Gaussian mixture models allow elliptical clusters and give soft membership probabilities.

Choosing the number of clusters

k-means needs k in advance, and inertia cannot choose it: more clusters always give lower inertia, ending at zero when every point is its own cluster. In the output above, inertia falls from 2,667 at k = 2 to 785 at k = 3, then more slowly (696, 599, 525, 465). The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the bend after which improvements are small. Here the bend is clear at 3. On messier data the bend is often a curve with no corner, and the method is more a prompt for judgement than a test.

The silhouette score (Rousseeuw, 1987) is a better-defined alternative. For each point, let a be its average distance to the other points in its own cluster and b its average distance to the points of the nearest other cluster. The point's silhouette is (b − a) / max(a, b): close to +1 when the point is far nearer its own cluster than any other, near 0 when it sits on a boundary, and negative when it is probably in the wrong cluster. The score for a clustering is the average over all points. In our run it peaks at k = 3 (0.646) and falls to 0.513 at k = 4 and about 0.3 by k = 6, agreeing with the elbow. The gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001) compares inertia with that of uniformly random data of the same extent, which handles the "no structure at all" case that a bare silhouette cannot; the last section returns to why that matters.

Also ask a question the numbers cannot answer: what will you do with the groups? A marketing team that can run four campaigns wants four segments, even if the silhouette prefers three. The statistically best k and the useful k can differ, and the useful one is a decision, not a measurement.

Hierarchical clustering

Hierarchical clustering sidesteps choosing k in advance by producing a whole family of clusterings at once. The common form, agglomerative clustering, starts with every row as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one cluster remains. The record of merges, drawn as a tree, is a dendrogram; cutting the tree at any height gives a clustering, and a lower cut gives more, smaller clusters.

"Closest" between two groups of points needs a rule, the linkage:

Single linkage
Distance between the two nearest members. Can chain: a line of points links everything into one long cluster, which is a weakness on noisy data and a strength on curved shapes.
Complete linkage
Distance between the two farthest members. Prefers compact clusters and resists chaining.
Average linkage
Mean distance over all cross-pairs. A compromise between the two.
Ward linkage
Merge the pair that increases total within-cluster variance the least. It is the hierarchical cousin of k-means and tends to give round clusters of similar size.

Different linkages find different structures, which is the point of the next experiment. The script implements agglomerative clustering from scratch (with the Lance-Williams formula to update distances after each merge), cuts the tree at a chosen k, and scores the result against the known groups with the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 1.0 for identical partitions, around 0 for what random labelling gives. It uses the moons and the Gaussian blobs, and imports the moons and ari helpers from the DBSCAN listing in the next section (save that listing as dbscan.py; the __main__ guard keeps it quiet when imported).

import numpy as np
from dbscan import moons, ari          # helpers from the previous listing

def agglomerate(X, linkage):
    """Naive agglomerative clustering. Returns merges (a, b, height) as clusters are joined."""
    n = len(X)
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None] - X[None]) ** 2).sum(2)); np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
    size = {i: 1 for i in range(n)}; merges = []
    D = {(i, j): D[i, j] for i in range(n) for j in range(i + 1, n)}
    nxt = n
    while len(size) > 1:
        (a, b), h = min(D.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1])       # closest pair of clusters
        na, nb = size.pop(a), size.pop(b)
        for c in size:                                          # Lance-Williams update to the new cluster
            dac, dbc = D.pop((min(a, c), max(a, c))), D.pop((min(b, c), max(b, c)))
            if linkage == "single":     d = min(dac, dbc)
            elif linkage == "complete": d = max(dac, dbc)
            elif linkage == "average":  d = (na * dac + nb * dbc) / (na + nb)
            else:                       # ward, written for squared distances
                nc = size[c]; d = np.sqrt(((na + nc) * dac**2 + (nb + nc) * dbc**2 - nc * h**2) / (na + nb + nc))
            D[(c, nxt)] = d
        del D[(a, b)]
        size[nxt] = na + nb; merges.append((a, b, h)); nxt += 1
    return merges

def cut(merges, n, k):
    parent = list(range(2 * n))
    def find(i):
        while parent[i] != i: i = parent[i]
        return i
    for m, (a, b, _) in enumerate(merges[: n - k]):
        parent[find(a)] = parent[find(b)] = n + m
    roots = {r: i for i, r in enumerate(sorted({find(i) for i in range(n)}))}
    return np.array([roots[find(i)] for i in range(n)])

    Xb = np.vstack([c + rng.normal(0, 0.9, (53, 2)) for c in ([0, 0], [5, 0], [2.5, 4.2])])
    yb = np.repeat([0, 1, 2], 53)
    print("ARI against the true groups (1.0 = perfect, 0 = chance)")
    print(f"{'linkage':9s} {'two moons (k=2)':>16s} {'three blobs (k=3)':>18s}")

    h = [m[2] for m in agglomerate(Xb, "ward")]
    print("\nward on the blobs, last 6 merge heights:", np.round(h[-6:], 2))

Single linkage is perfect on the moons (1.000), because the nearest-neighbour chain follows each crescent, and worst on the blobs (0.559), where stray points in the tails chain the blobs together. Ward and average linkage are perfect on the blobs and fail on the moons (0.016 and 0.246). No linkage wins both, which restates the chapter's theme: the method encodes an assumption about shape. The last line shows the merge heights of the Ward run on the blobs: 6.88, 7.48, 8.28, 8.52, then a jump to 34.81 and 36.7. The costs are real: the naive algorithm needs the full table of pairwise distances, so memory grows as n2 and time at best the same, which limits it to thousands, not millions, of rows.

DBSCAN: clusters as dense regions

DBSCAN (Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu, 1996, "A Density-Based Algorithm for Discovering Clusters in Large Spatial Databases with Noise") defines a cluster as a region where points are packed densely, separated from other regions by sparse space. It does not need k. It needs two numbers: a radius eps and a count min_pts. A point is a core point if at least min_pts points (counting itself) lie within eps of it. A cluster grows from a core point by absorbing everything within eps, and continuing from every core point it reaches. Points inside a cluster's reach that are not themselves core points are border points. Points that no core point reaches are labelled noise (label −1), which is the feature k-means lacks: DBSCAN is willing to say "this point belongs to nothing".

The script implements DBSCAN from scratch on 400 points of the two-moons dataset (noise standard deviation 0.07) and sweeps eps with min_pts fixed at 5. It also defines the ARI and a k-means with 10 restarts for comparison.

import numpy as np
from math import comb

def moons(n, noise, rng):
    t = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, n // 2)
    a = np.c_[np.cos(t), np.sin(t)]; b = np.c_[1 - np.cos(t), 0.5 - np.sin(t)]
    X = np.vstack([a, b]) + rng.normal(0, noise, (n // 2 * 2, 2))
    return X, np.r_[np.zeros(n // 2, int), np.ones(n // 2, int)]

def dbscan(X, eps, min_pts):
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None] - X[None]) ** 2).sum(2))
    nbrs = [np.flatnonzero(D[i] <= eps) for i in range(len(X))]
    core = np.array([len(nb) >= min_pts for nb in nbrs])      # a point counts itself
    lab = np.full(len(X), -1); c = 0
    for i in range(len(X)):
        if lab[i] != -1 or not core[i]: continue
        lab[i] = c; stack = [i]
        while stack:                                           # grow the cluster through core points
            j = stack.pop()
            for q in nbrs[j]:
                if lab[q] == -1:
                    lab[q] = c
                    if core[q]: stack.append(q)
        c += 1
    return lab                                                 # -1 means noise

def ari(a, b):                                                 # adjusted Rand index
    ct = np.array([[np.sum((a == i) & (b == j)) for j in np.unique(b)] for i in np.unique(a)])
    s = sum(comb(int(v), 2) for v in ct.ravel())
    sa = sum(comb(int(v), 2) for v in ct.sum(1)); sb = sum(comb(int(v), 2) for v in ct.sum(0))
    exp = sa * sb / comb(len(a), 2)
    return (s - exp) / (0.5 * (sa + sb) - exp)

def lloyd(X, k, seed):                                         # k-means, 10 restarts, best inertia
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); best = None
    for _ in range(10):
        C = X[rng.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]
        for _ in range(100):
            lab = ((X[:, None] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(2).argmin(1)
            C = np.array([X[lab == j].mean(0) for j in range(k)])
        inertia = ((X - C[lab]) ** 2).sum()
        if best is None or inertia < best[0]: best = (inertia, lab)
    return best[1]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
    X, y = moons(400, 0.07, rng)
    print(f"k-means (k=2):                 ARI vs truth {ari(y, lloyd(X, 2, 0)):.3f}")
    print("DBSCAN, min_pts=5, varying eps:")
    for eps in (0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.25, 0.40, 0.60):
        lab = dbscan(X, eps, 5); k = lab.max() + 1
        print(f"  eps={eps:.2f}: {k:2d} clusters, {np.sum(lab == -1):3d} noise points, ARI {ari(y, lab):.3f}")

k-means with the correct k = 2 scores only 0.269. DBSCAN's result depends on eps in a way worth studying. At 0.05 the radius is smaller than the typical gap between neighbours, so almost nothing is dense: 15 tiny clusters and 293 of 400 points called noise (ARI 0.008). At 0.10 the moons are fragmented into 6 pieces (0.514). A range from 0.15 to 0.25 recovers the two moons almost perfectly (0.995, 1.000). At 0.40 and above the gap between the moons is bridged and everything merges into one cluster (ARI 0). So there is a comfortable window here, but its edges are sharp, and in real data you find it by looking: a common method plots each point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (sorted) and picks eps near the knee.

DBSCAN's main weakness is that one eps describes all clusters, so it struggles when clusters have very different densities: a radius that separates a sparse cluster merges two dense neighbours, and a radius that separates the dense ones calls the sparse cluster noise. HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi and Sander, 2013) removes eps by building a hierarchy over all radii and extracting the clusters that persist; scikit-learn has included an implementation since version 1.3 (2023). It is a good first choice when you want density-based clusters and do not know the right scale. In high dimensions all distances tend to become similar, so density-based methods lose their footing; that is a reason to reduce dimension first (chapter 11).

Are the clusters real?

Every method above returns clusters on any input. Give k-means a square of uniformly scattered points and it hands back k neat regions, a tessellation, not a discovery. So a clustering needs evidence beyond "the algorithm finished". Three kinds of evidence are available, in rough order of strength.

  • Internal measures such as the silhouette use only the data. They describe how well separated the groups are but cannot tell you that separation exists in nature rather than in the algorithm's output.
  • Stability. If the clusters reflect real structure, resampling the data should reproduce them. Cluster two bootstrap resamples, label all rows with each fitted model, and compare the two labellings with the ARI. A value near 1 means the grouping survives resampling; a low value means it is an accident of the sample.
  • External validation. Compare the clusters with something you know independently: a label you held back, behaviour you did not cluster on (do the segments have different retention?), or the judgement of a domain expert. This is the strongest test, and it is the one that connects clustering to an actual use.

The script runs the first two on three datasets of 240 points each: three clean blobs (standard deviation 0.9), three heavily overlapping blobs (standard deviation 1.6, centres 3 apart), and uniform noise with no structure. For each it reports the best k by silhouette over 2 to 6, and the stability (mean ARI over 20 pairs of bootstrap clusterings) at k = 3. It imports kmeans and silhouette from the first listing and ari from the DBSCAN one.

import numpy as np
from kmeans import kmeans, silhouette     # the listing from the k-means section, saved as kmeans.py
from dbscan import ari                    # adjusted Rand index, from the DBSCAN listing

def best_k(X, rng):
    scores = {k: silhouette(X, kmeans(X, k, rng, n_init=5)[1]) for k in range(2, 7)}
    k = max(scores, key=scores.get); return k, scores[k]

def stability(X, k, rng, rounds=20):
    """Cluster two bootstrap resamples, label ALL rows by nearest centre, compare the two labelings."""
    out = []
    for _ in range(rounds):
        labs = []
        for _ in range(2):
            idx = rng.integers(0, len(X), len(X))
            C = kmeans(X[idx], k, rng, n_init=3)[2]
            labs.append(((X[:, None] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(2).argmin(1))
        out.append(ari(labs[0], labs[1]))
    return np.mean(out)

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
blobs = np.vstack([c + rng.normal(0, 0.9, (80, 2)) for c in ([0, 0], [5, 0], [2.5, 4.2])])
uniform = rng.uniform(0, 5, (240, 2))                              # no structure at all
weak = np.vstack([c + rng.normal(0, 1.6, (80, 2)) for c in ([0, 0], [3, 0], [1.5, 2.6])])  # overlapping

print(f"{'data':16s} best k  silhouette  stability (ARI between bootstrap clusterings, at k=3)")
for name, X in [("three blobs", blobs), ("overlapping", weak), ("uniform noise", uniform)]:
    k, s = best_k(X, rng)
    print(f"{name:16s} {k:^6d} {s:^11.3f} {stability(X, 3, rng):.3f}")

The clean blobs give a silhouette of 0.682 at k = 3 and a stability of 0.999: nearly identical clusters from every resample. The overlapping blobs are weaker (0.373, stability 0.704): there is some structure but the borders are fuzzy and move with the sample. The uniform noise is the instructive row. Its best silhouette, 0.406, would look respectable in many reports (Kaufman and Rousseeuw's rule of thumb reads 0.26 to 0.50 as weak structure that may be artificial), and it picks k = 6, the largest value offered, because slicing a square into more pieces keeps scoring about the same. But its stability is only 0.500: resample and the pieces change. Silhouette alone could not distinguish this from the overlapping blobs; stability did, because the noise clusters do not survive. Compare against the gap statistic's idea too: always cluster a structureless reference of the same shape and see what score it gets before reading yours.

Common misconception

"The algorithm found three clusters, so there are three kinds of customer." The algorithm was asked for three. A clustering is a summary of the data under a particular distance and a particular method, and its usefulness has to be shown: the groups should be stable under resampling, interpretable (you can describe what distinguishes them), different on something you did not cluster on, and large enough to act on. Hennig (2015, "What are the true clusters?") makes the broader point that "the true clusters" often does not exist as a single answer; what exists is a purpose. Names like "price-sensitive families" are stories told about the groups afterwards, and they need to be checked as hypotheses.

Try it yourself

Clustering experiments are cheap, so break things deliberately. In real.py, change the standard deviation of the "overlapping" dataset to 1.0, 1.3, 2.0 and 2.5 and watch the stability fall as the blobs merge into one cloud, and note at what spread you would stop trusting the clusters. Systems connection

k-means is also a workhorse inside other systems. Vector search engines use it to partition millions of embeddings into cells (an "inverted file" or IVF index), so a query scans only the few cells nearest to it; Approximate Nearest-Neighbour Search explains the recall and speed trade-off, which is exactly the "assign to the nearest centre" step of this chapter, run at scale. For large tables, mini-batch k-means updates centres from small random batches and trades a little inertia for a large speed-up. Clustering text embeddings to find topics in a corpus is a common first step in the document pipelines of RAG End to End, with the same caution about verifying the topics.

Key takeaways

  • Clustering has no ground truth; the features, their scales and the algorithm together define what a cluster is. Standardise features first and decide which columns should count.
  • k-means alternates assign and update steps, lowers inertia every step and stops at a local minimum. Use k-means++ and several restarts; it assumes round, similar-sized, similar-density groups.
  • Inertia always falls with k. Use the elbow, the silhouette and, above all, the purpose to choose k.
  • Hierarchical clustering gives a tree to cut at any level; the linkage (single, complete, average, Ward) decides what shapes it finds, and memory grows as n2.
  • DBSCAN follows dense regions, needs no k, labels noise, and finds non-round shapes; it depends on eps and struggles with varying density. HDBSCAN relaxes that.
  • A silhouette near 0.4 can occur on pure noise. Check stability under resampling, compare with a structureless reference, and validate against something outside the clustering.

Further reading

  • Arthur and Vassilvitskii, "k-means++: The Advantages of Careful Seeding" (2007). The seeding rule used by default in most libraries, with a proof that it is competitive with the optimal clustering.
  • Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu, "A Density-Based Algorithm for Discovering Clusters in Large Spatial Databases with Noise" (1996). The original DBSCAN paper, including how to choose eps from a k-distance plot.
  • Rousseeuw, "Silhouettes: a graphical aid to the interpretation and validation of cluster analysis" (1987). Defines the silhouette and the plot that goes with it.
  • Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, "Estimating the number of clusters in a data set via the gap statistic" (2001). Comparing a clustering with a structureless reference.
  • Hennig, "What are the true clusters?" (2015). A careful discussion of why cluster validity depends on purpose.
  • scikit-learn user guide, "Clustering". A side-by-side comparison of the common algorithms on toy shapes, with the practical parameters.

Check your understanding

You cluster customers with k-means on two columns: annual spend in pounds (range 0 to 50,000) and number of orders (range 1 to 40). The resulting segments look like horizontal bands of spend. What is the most likely fix?

  1. Increase k until the bands split along orders as well
  2. Standardise both columns so that each contributes comparably to distance
  3. Switch to single-linkage hierarchical clustering, which ignores scale
  4. Remove the orders column, since spend dominates
Euclidean distance is dominated by the column with the largest numbers, so spend decides everything. Standardising puts the columns on comparable scales. More clusters would only slice spend more finely. Single linkage uses the same distances and is equally affected, and dropping orders throws away the information you wanted.

In the k-means experiment on six well-separated groups, plain random starts gave 131 of 300 single runs more than 10 percent above the best inertia, and k-means++ gave 79. What does this tell you about practice?

  1. k-means++ always finds the global optimum, so a single run is enough
  2. Random starts are better because their best run is lower
  3. The best run was different for the two methods, so one of them is wrong
  4. Better seeding helps but does not remove bad local minima, so run several restarts and keep the lowest inertia
Both methods reached the same best inertia (485), so neither is wrong, and k-means++ reduced but did not eliminate the bad runs (79 of 300 is still more than a quarter). That is why n_init exists. Claiming a single run suffices ignores those 79 runs.

You run DBSCAN with min_pts = 5 on a dataset and find that 70 percent of points are labelled noise and the rest form many tiny clusters. The most likely cause and fix?

  1. eps is too small for the data's spacing; increase it, guided by a k-distance plot
  2. eps is too large; decrease it
  3. The data has too many clusters; reduce k
  4. DBSCAN cannot handle noise, so use k-means
At eps = 0.05 in the moons experiment, 293 of 400 points were noise and there were 15 tiny clusters: the radius was smaller than the usual gap to the nearest neighbours. Too large an eps would merge everything into one cluster instead. DBSCAN has no k, and handling noise is one of its strengths.

A colleague reports that k-means with k = 6 on a dataset gave a silhouette of 0.41 and concludes the data has six segments. You suspect it might be structureless. Which check is most informative?

  1. Re-run with k = 7 and see if the silhouette rises
  2. Compute the inertia, which should be low if the clusters are real
  3. Cluster two bootstrap resamples and compare their labellings (ARI), and cluster a structureless reference of the same shape for comparison
  4. Check whether the clusters have exactly equal sizes
In our experiment, uniform noise scored a silhouette of 0.406 and still had a stability of only 0.50, against 0.999 for real blobs. Inertia always decreases with k and says nothing about reality, and a change in k shows how the score moves, not whether structure exists. Equal sizes are not required of real clusters.

Which dataset is the best fit for single-linkage hierarchical clustering, and why might it fail elsewhere?

  1. Three round blobs with some noise points between them, because single linkage is robust to noise
  2. Two thin crescent-shaped groups, because chains of nearest neighbours follow the curve; but noise bridges can chain distinct groups together
  3. Groups of very different sizes, because the farthest-point rule balances them
  4. Any dataset, because it makes no assumptions
Single linkage merges by the nearest members, so it follows elongated or curved structure (ARI 1.000 on the moons) but chains separate blobs through stray points (0.559 on the blobs). Option 1 states the opposite of its noise behaviour, option 3 describes complete-linkage reasoning loosely, and every linkage makes an assumption about shape.

A Ward dendrogram on 500 customers shows merge heights of 4, 5, 5.5, 6, 6.2 for the last five merges before the final one at 6.4, with no obvious gap anywhere. What is the most defensible conclusion?

  1. There are exactly two clusters, because the last merge is the largest
  2. There are exactly five clusters, one for each of the last merges
  3. The tree must be wrong, because a dendrogram should always show a gap
  4. The data has no clear cluster structure at this level; any cut is a choice driven by how many groups you can use, not a discovery
A clear structure shows up as a large jump before the final merges, like the 8.52 to 34.81 jump in the blob experiment. Heights that rise smoothly mean no level of the tree is naturally preferred. Dendrograms of unstructured data often look like this, so no gap is a legitimate finding and not an error.
