Dimensionality Reduction

A table with 200 columns cannot be drawn, and many of its columns repeat each other. Dimensionality reduction maps rows into a few coordinates while keeping what you care about. This chapter derives PCA, the linear workhorse, then explains t-SNE and UMAP, the pictures you see in papers, and, just as important, what those pictures do and do not tell you.

Why this matters

High-dimensional data is awkward in three ways: distances between points stop being informative (so the nearest-neighbour and density methods of chapter 7 and chapter 10 degrade), models with many correlated columns are unstable (chapter 6), and people cannot look at it. Reduction addresses all three. It also produces some of the most misread graphics in data science: a two-dimensional scatter of embeddings, with tidy islands, presented as evidence of structure. You will learn what PCA computes and how to choose the number of components, how t-SNE and UMAP are built, which features of their plots are meaningful (neighbourhoods) and which are artefacts of the method (cluster sizes, gaps and the distances between islands), and how to avoid leaking information when reduction is part of a pipeline.

Why many dimensions are a problem

Intuition built in two and three dimensions fails in a hundred. One symptom, the curse of dimensionality, is that distances concentrate: in a high-dimensional cube almost every pair of points is about the same distance apart, so "nearest" barely differs from "farthest". The script below draws 1,000 uniform random points in cubes of increasing dimension, picks one as a query, and looks at its distances to the other 999 points.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
print("dims   mean dist  (max-min)/min   share of points within 10% of the mean distance")
for d in (2, 10, 100, 1000):
    X = rng.uniform(0, 1, (1000, d))
    q = X[0]                                   # one query point; distances to the other 999
    dist = np.sqrt(((X[1:] - q) ** 2).sum(1))
    share = np.mean(np.abs(dist - dist.mean()) < 0.1 * dist.mean())
    print(f"{d:<6d} {dist.mean():8.2f}   {(dist.max() - dist.min()) / dist.min():10.2f}      {share:.2f}")

In 2 dimensions the farthest point is 164 times as far as the nearest (some point lies very close), and only 10 percent of points are within 10 percent of the mean distance. In 1,000 dimensions the farthest point is only 1.11 times as far as the nearest, and every point lies within 10 percent of the mean. A method that relies on "which points are close" has little to work with, even though nothing about the data is wrong. Real data is usually not uniform: its rows tend to lie near a lower-dimensional surface (a handful of causes generate hundreds of columns), and that is the structure reduction tries to find. The reduction recipe works when the data has such structure, and cannot create it when it does not.

PCA: the best flat summary

Principal component analysis (PCA) looks for the line (then the plane, then the 3-D subspace) through the data that keeps the most information, where "information" means variance. Take a cloud of points, centre it at the origin, and ask: in which direction does the cloud spread the most? That direction is the first principal component (PC1). Among directions at right angles to it, the one with the most remaining spread is PC2, and so on. Projecting each point onto the first k components gives a k-number summary of the row.

Two views of the same line

There are two ways to define the best line, and PCA's elegance is that they agree. Projecting a point onto a unit direction v splits it, by Pythagoras, into a part along v and a part perpendicular to v. For a fixed point, the squared length of the whole vector is fixed, so the bigger the along-part, the smaller the perpendicular part. Summed over all points: maximising the variance kept along v is the same as minimising the squared distance to the line. The yellow segment in the figure is the perpendicular part.

The algebra is short. Centre the data matrix X (n rows, p columns) by subtracting each column's mean. The sample covariance matrix is C = XTX/(n − 1). The variance of the data projected on a unit vector v is vTCv. Maximising that subject to vTv = 1 gives the eigenvector problem Cv = λv (a Lagrange multiplier step), so the best direction is the eigenvector of C with the largest eigenvalue, and the eigenvalue is the variance along it. The next components are the next eigenvectors, which are automatically at right angles because C is symmetric (A0 chapter 7). In practice one takes the SVD of the centred matrix, X = UΣVT: the rows of VT are the principal axes and the variance along axis j is σj2/(n − 1). The SVD is numerically steadier than forming C explicitly, and it is what libraries use.

Analogy: the best angle for a shadow

Hold a long, flat object under a lamp and look at its shadow on a wall. Turn it until the shadow is as spread out as possible: the shadow then keeps the most information about the object's shape. PCA is that search for the viewing direction (or plane, for a 2-D shadow) with the most spread, done for any number of dimensions at once.

Where it breaks down: spread is not the same as importance. A viewing angle that spreads the shadow most might hide the one small feature you wanted to see. If the signal that separates two classes lies along a low-variance direction, PCA will discard it first, because PCA never looks at labels.

A worked run

The data here are 500 rows with 10 columns, generated from 3 hidden factors with standard deviations 3, 2 and 1. Each column is a random mix of the factors, plus noise with standard deviation 0.5. So the truth is a 3-dimensional structure seen through 10 noisy windows. The script computes PCA by SVD, checks that the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix gives the same answer, and measures how well k components rebuild the 10 columns.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 500
Z = rng.normal(size=(n, 3)) * [3.0, 2.0, 1.0]            # 3 hidden factors with different strengths
W = rng.normal(size=(3, 10))                             # each of 10 columns mixes the factors
X = Z @ W + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (n, 10))                  # plus noise on every column

def pca(X, scale=False):
    Xc = X - X.mean(0)                                   # centre (and optionally scale)
    if scale: Xc = Xc / Xc.std(0)
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)    # rows of Vt are the principal axes
    var = S ** 2 / (len(X) - 1)                          # variance along each axis
    return Xc, Vt, var

Xc, Vt, var = pca(X)
print("explained variance ratio:", np.round(var / var.sum(), 3))
print("cumulative:              ", np.round(np.cumsum(var) / var.sum(), 3))

# same answer from the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(np.cov(Xc.T))
print("eigh matches SVD variances:", np.allclose(np.sort(vals)[::-1], var),
      "| same axes up to sign:", np.allclose(np.abs(vecs[:, ::-1].T), np.abs(Vt)))

print("\nk   reconstruction RMSE   (noise sd was 0.5)")
for k in (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 10):
    Zk = Xc @ Vt[:k].T                                   # project onto the first k axes
    Xhat = Zk @ Vt[:k]                                   # map back to 10 columns
    print(f"{k:<3d} {np.sqrt(np.mean((Xc - Xhat) ** 2)):.3f}")

# units matter: express column 0 in different units (x1000) and PCA again
X2 = X.copy(); X2[:, 0] *= 1000
for scale in (False, True):
    _, V2, v2 = pca(X2, scale)
    print(f"\nscale={scale!s:5}: PC1 explains {v2[0] / v2.sum():.3f}; weight of column 0 on PC1 = {abs(V2[0, 0]):.3f}")

Read it in four steps. First, the explained variance ratios (0.78, 0.16, 0.048) then a floor of 0.002 per component: three components carry 98.8 percent of the variance and the other seven are noise, matching how the data was built. Second, the check eigh matches SVD confirms the two routes agree (axes match up to sign, since an eigenvector and its negative are the same direction). Third, the reconstruction error falls from 1.747 at one component to 0.416 at three and then flattens (0.380 at four, 0.300 at six, 0 at ten). Why 0.416 and not 0.5, the noise level? The three kept components capture the signal and throw away the noise in the other seven directions: the discarded noise is spread over seven of the ten directions, so the RMSE is about 0.5 × √(7/10) = 0.42. Reconstruction through a few components is partly a denoiser. Fourth, the unit experiment: multiplying one column by 1,000 (as if it had been recorded in millimetres rather than metres) makes PC1 equal to that column alone, explaining 100 percent of the variance. After standardising columns first, PC1 explains 66.6 percent and column 0 carries a weight of 0.219. PCA is variance-hungry, so standardise columns first unless the units are already comparable.

Using PCA well

  • Choosing the number of components. Look at the scree plot, keep enough to reach a variance target, or treat the number as a hyperparameter and pick it by cross-validated performance of the model that follows. A variance threshold (for example 95 percent) is a heuristic, because the discarded low-variance directions can carry the signal.
  • Fit inside the validation fold. The mean and axes are estimated from data. If you compute them on the whole dataset before splitting, information from the validation rows has leaked into the features: the same preprocessing leak as in chapter 14. Put PCA in a pipeline so that it is fitted on each training fold only.
  • Loadings are not importances. Each component is a weighted mix of columns. The weights (loadings) show which columns move together, and are useful for interpreting a component, but a column with a small loading on PC1 can matter a great deal elsewhere, and signs are arbitrary.
  • What it is good for. Removing redundancy before a model that suffers from correlated columns; compressing wide numeric data (for example embeddings) with little loss; decorrelating features; denoising; and making a first 2-D picture. It is linear only: a curved surface (a spiral, a rolled sheet) is not captured by a flat plane, and that is the opening for t-SNE and UMAP.
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

# X: (n_samples, n_features), y: labels. PCA is refitted inside every training fold.
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=0.95), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
scores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5)
print(scores.mean())

That listing shows the conventions (n_components=0.95 keeps enough components for 95 percent of the variance) but was not run here, since scikit-learn is not installed on the machine that built this page, so there is no output to show. The scaling and PCA steps are what the NumPy script above does, wrapped in a pipeline so that they are refitted inside each training fold.

t-SNE: keeping neighbours together

When the structure is curved or clustered, a linear projection flattens it badly and overlapping clusters pile up. t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding; van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008, "Visualizing Data using t-SNE") takes a different goal: place the points in 2-D so that points that were neighbours in the original space stay neighbours. It does not care about preserving variance or global distances. It works in four moves:

  1. Neighbour probabilities in the original space. For each point i, turn distances to every other point j into probabilities pj|i proportional to exp(−dij2/2σi2): nearby points get high probability. The bandwidth σi is set separately for each point so that the entropy of its distribution equals a chosen perplexity, which you can read as "the effective number of neighbours" (typical values 5 to 50). Dense regions get a small σ, sparse regions a large one. The probabilities are then symmetrised: pij = (pj|i + pi|j)/2n.
  2. Neighbour probabilities in the map. Give every point a 2-D position yi and define qij proportional to (1 + ‖yi − yj‖2)−1, normalised over all pairs. This is a Student-t kernel with one degree of freedom, which has much heavier tails than a Gaussian.
  3. Make the two agree. Minimise the Kullback-Leibler divergence KL(P ‖ Q) = Σ pij log(pij/qij) (A0 chapter 14) by gradient descent on the positions. The gradient for point i is 4 Σj (pij − qij)(yi − yj)(1 + ‖yi − yj‖2)−1: pairs that are neighbours in the data but far apart in the map attract, and pairs that are close in the map but not neighbours repel.
  4. Tricks that make it work. Positions start tiny; an early exaggeration multiplies P by about 12 for the first stretch so that clusters form compactly; momentum speeds descent.

Why the heavy-tailed kernel? Squeezing a high-dimensional neighbourhood into 2-D leaves too little room (the crowding problem): there is not enough area around a point for all its moderately distant neighbours. With heavy tails, moderately distant points can sit relatively far away in the map at little cost, which gives clusters room to separate. The direction of the KL divergence also matters. It punishes placing true neighbours far apart (p large, q small) heavily, but placing non-neighbours close together only lightly. So t-SNE is built to keep local structure; what it does with the far-apart parts of the map is decided mostly by chance and by the optimisation, not by the data.

What t-SNE keeps and what it destroys

The script implements exact t-SNE in about 50 lines of NumPy (no Barnes-Hut approximation, so it handles a few hundred points). The data: 300 points in 20 dimensions in three clusters. Each cluster is a 3-D sheet with a random orientation plus a little noise, which gives it real neighbourhood structure to preserve. Cluster A is tight (spread 1), cluster B is wide (spread 3) and sits close to A (centre distance 6.9), and cluster C is tight and far away (23.2 from B, 30.0 from A). We compare PCA to 2-D with t-SNE at three perplexities on three numbers: how many of each point's 10 nearest neighbours in 20-D are still among its 10 nearest in 2-D; the three distances between cluster centres; and the spread inside each cluster.

import numpy as np

def make_data(rng):
    """300 points in 20-D. Each cluster is a 3-D sheet (random orientation) plus a little noise:
    A tight, B wide and close to A, C tight and far away."""
    def blob(shift, sd, n):
        basis = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(20, 3)))[0].T     # 3 orthonormal directions in 20-D
        c = np.zeros(20); c[0] = shift
        return c + rng.normal(0, sd, (n, 3)) @ basis + rng.normal(0, 0.2, (n, 20))
    X = np.vstack([blob(0, 1.0, 100), blob(7, 3.0, 100), blob(30, 1.0, 100)])
    return X, np.repeat([0, 1, 2], 100)

def sq_dists(X):
    s = (X ** 2).sum(1)
    return np.maximum(s[:, None] + s[None] - 2 * X @ X.T, 0)

def affinities(X, perplexity):
    D, n = sq_dists(X), len(X); P = np.zeros((n, n)); target = np.log(perplexity)
    for i in range(n):                                   # find sigma_i so that entropy = log(perplexity)
        lo, hi, beta = 0.0, np.inf, 1.0
        d = np.delete(D[i], i)
        for _ in range(60):
            p = np.exp(-(d - d.min()) * beta); p /= p.sum()
            H = -(p * np.log(p + 1e-12)).sum()
            if H > target: lo, beta = beta, (beta * 2 if hi == np.inf else (beta + hi) / 2)
            else:          hi, beta = beta, (beta + lo) / 2
        P[i, np.arange(n) != i] = p
    return (P + P.T) / (2 * n)                           # symmetrise

def tsne(X, perplexity=30, iters=600, seed=0, init=None):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); n = len(X)
    P = affinities(X, perplexity)
    Y = rng.normal(0, 1e-2, (n, 2)) if init is None else init.copy()
    vel = np.zeros_like(Y); lr = 100.0
    for t in range(iters):
        ex = 12.0 if t < 150 else 1.0                    # early exaggeration: pull clusters together first
        num = 1.0 / (1.0 + sq_dists(Y)); np.fill_diagonal(num, 0)
        Q = np.maximum(num / num.sum(), 1e-12)
        grad = 4 * (((ex * P - Q) * num)[:, :, None] * (Y[:, None] - Y[None])).sum(1)
        vel = (0.5 if t < 150 else 0.8) * vel - lr * grad
        Y = Y + vel; Y -= Y.mean(0)
    kl = (P * np.log((P + 1e-12) / Q)).sum()
    return Y, kl

def knn_overlap(A, B, k=10):                             # share of A's k nearest neighbours that B keeps
    ia = np.argsort(sq_dists(A) + np.diag([np.inf] * len(A)), 1)[:, :k]
    ib = np.argsort(sq_dists(B) + np.diag([np.inf] * len(B)), 1)[:, :k]
    return np.mean([len(set(a) & set(b)) / k for a, b in zip(ia, ib)])

    dd = lambda C: [np.linalg.norm(C[0] - C[1]), np.linalg.norm(C[1] - C[2]), np.linalg.norm(C[0] - C[2])]

Read the table. PCA keeps the global geometry exactly (centre distances 6.9, 23.2 and 30.0 match the original, and B is still the widest) but only 31 percent of nearest neighbours survive, because 20 dimensions squeezed into 2 mix up points within each cluster. t-SNE at perplexity 30 keeps 68 percent of neighbours, so its local structure is much better. In exchange it has lost the global story: the three cluster distances become 24.2, 26.5 and 25.8, nearly equal, although in truth A and B are close and C is far; and the three spreads become 3.6, 3.7 and 3.6, although B is really roughly 2.5 to 3 times wider than A. The heavy-tailed kernel and per-point bandwidths equalise density, so a cluster's apparent size in a t-SNE map says nothing about its real size or density. The perplexity changes the picture: at 5 the neighbourhoods are tiny, the clusters look fragmented and the distances are random-looking (37.1, 35.1, 38.7); at 100, which exceeds the cluster size of 100, the islands collapse towards each other (4.5, 19.8, 16.7) and the spreads shrink to about 0.5.

One more check, which is the one people forget: is the picture reproducible? A second script reruns t-SNE with different random initial positions, and with an initialisation taken from the first two principal components (the practice recommended by Kobak and Linderman, 2021, "Initialization is critical for preserving global data structure in both t-SNE and UMAP"). It imports the functions from the previous listing, saved as tsne.py.

    return [np.linalg.norm(C[i] - C[j]) for i, j in ((0, 1), (1, 2), (0, 2))]

Common misconception

"In the t-SNE plot the clusters are far apart, so they are very different, and the big cluster has more variety." None of that follows. Distances between islands are not meaningful (our three equally spaced islands hide one near pair and one distant), the size of an island is not its real spread, and two islands may be one cluster that the optimiser tore apart. The safe readings are local: points that are near each other in the map are likely to be near in the data, and an island that persists across several perplexities and random seeds is more credible than one that appears once. Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson's interactive essay "How to Use t-SNE Effectively" (2016, Distill) shows each of these artefacts with pictures and is worth an hour.

UMAP

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection; McInnes, Healy and Melville, 2018) is the other embedding you will meet constantly. The mechanism is similar in spirit and different in detail. It first builds a weighted graph of each point's k nearest neighbours (edges are fuzzy: nearer neighbours get weights close to 1, with the scale set per point), then finds a 2-D layout whose own neighbour graph matches the original graph, by minimising a cross-entropy between the two sets of edge weights. Because it uses a sparse neighbour graph and a cheaper optimisation than t-SNE's all-pairs sums, it is typically faster on large data, and it has a transform method for new points, which scikit-learn's t-SNE lacks.

Two parameters control it. n_neighbors (default 15 in the umap-learn library) sets how local the view is: small values emphasise fine detail, large values give a broader view. min_dist (default 0.1) controls how tightly points may be packed in the map: small values give clumpy, well-separated islands, larger values spread things out. Like t-SNE, a UMAP plot has caveats. It often shows more global structure than a default t-SNE, but the layout is still driven by the neighbour graph and the optimiser; cluster sizes and gaps remain unreliable (Chari and Pachter, 2023, "The specious art of single-cell genomics", argue that both methods distort distances and neighbourhoods in ways that are easy to over-interpret). Treat both as visualisation and exploration, and check any claimed structure with something that does not depend on the 2-D picture.

import umap
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)                    # X: (n_samples, n_features)
reducer = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1, n_components=2, random_state=0)
Z = reducer.fit_transform(Xs)                             # shape (n_samples, 2)
Z_new = reducer.transform(X_new_scaled)                   # new rows can be mapped, unlike scikit-learn's TSNE

State of play, October 2026

Defaults worth knowing as of this writing (documentation checked 14 October 2026): scikit-learn's TSNE uses perplexity=30, initialises from PCA and chooses its learning rate automatically (both changes arrived in version 1.2), uses the Barnes-Hut approximation by default, and has no transform method for new data. The umap-learn defaults are n_neighbors=15 and min_dist=0.1. Defaults and implementations (openTSNE, GPU versions, newer neighbour-graph methods) change; verify against the documentation for your version and record the settings next to any figure you publish.

Systems connection

Embeddings from language and image models are hundreds to thousands of dimensions, and plotting them in 2-D is the standard way to inspect them; the caveats of this chapter apply in full, because a "cluster" in a UMAP of embeddings is only as trustworthy as the neighbour graph underneath it (Dense Retrieval covers how such embeddings are trained). Reduction also appears inside retrieval systems: Approximate Nearest-Neighbour Search uses compression such as product quantisation (and sometimes PCA) to shrink vectors so that billions fit in memory, trading a little accuracy for a large saving. The cost model matters too: exact t-SNE needs an n × n table of pairwise affinities, which is why it needs the Barnes-Hut approximation or a neighbour-graph shortcut beyond tens of thousands of points, a scaling question of the kind in Performance Engineering.

Key takeaways

  • In high dimensions distances concentrate, so neighbour-based methods weaken; real data usually lies near a lower-dimensional structure that reduction can exploit.
  • PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, computed via the SVD. It simultaneously minimises squared reconstruction error.
  • Standardise columns before PCA, choose the number of components from a scree plot or by cross-validated downstream performance, and fit PCA inside each training fold to avoid leakage.
  • PCA is linear and ignores labels: variance is not importance, and a curved structure needs a non-linear method.
  • t-SNE matches neighbour probabilities (Gaussian in the data, heavy-tailed in the map) by minimising KL divergence; it preserves local neighbourhoods (68 percent of neighbours in our run, against 31 percent for PCA).
  • In a t-SNE or UMAP plot, trust neighbourhoods that persist across seeds and parameters. Do not read cluster sizes, gaps or distances between islands.
  • UMAP builds a fuzzy neighbour graph and is faster, with a transform for new data; it carries the same interpretive caveats.

Check your understanding

You run PCA on a table with columns in very different units (income in pounds, age in years, a 0/1 flag) and PC1 explains 99.9 percent of the variance with a loading of almost 1 on income. What does this most likely mean, and what should you do?

  1. Income is by far the most important feature; keep only PC1
  2. Income dominates because of its units; standardise the columns and rerun
  3. PCA failed to converge; use more iterations
  4. The 0/1 flag should be removed because binary columns break PCA
PCA maximises variance, and variance depends on units: in the chapter's experiment, scaling one column by 1,000 made PC1 equal to that column and explain 100 percent. After standardising, PC1 explained 66.6 percent and no single column dominated. Variance is not importance, so option 1 confuses the two. PCA via SVD has no iterations to run out of, and binary columns are allowed.

A colleague fits PCA on all 50,000 rows to reduce 300 features to 40, then runs 5-fold cross-validation on a classifier using the 40 components and reports an excellent score. What is the problem?

  1. 40 components are too few for 300 features
  2. PCA should have been run after cross-validation on the test fold only
  3. Cross-validation cannot be used with PCA
  4. PCA was fitted using the validation rows, so information from them leaked into the features; it should be refitted inside each training fold
The axes and means were estimated from data that includes each validation fold, a preprocessing leak of the kind covered in chapter 14. The fix is a pipeline that fits the scaler and PCA on the training part of each fold. Fitting on the test fold only (option 2) is wrong in the opposite way, and cross-validation works fine with PCA when it is inside the pipeline.

In the t-SNE experiment, cluster B was really roughly 2.5 to 3 times wider than cluster A, but in the t-SNE map the two had nearly equal spread (3.7 and 3.6). Why?

  1. Each point's bandwidth is set to match the same perplexity, so dense and sparse regions are equalised and apparent size carries no information about real size
  2. t-SNE averages the spread of all clusters at the end
  3. The perplexity was too high, and a lower value would have preserved the widths
  4. PCA initialisation always equalises cluster sizes
t-SNE picks a bandwidth per point so that every point has the same effective number of neighbours; a wide, sparse cluster is therefore treated like a tight one. Changing the perplexity changes the picture but does not restore true spread: at 5 the spreads were about 11 each, at 100 about 0.5 or 1. Nothing averages clusters at the end, and PCA initialisation is only a starting layout.

A UMAP of text embeddings shows five clearly separated islands, and a manager wants to launch five products for the five "customer types". Which response is most appropriate?

  1. Agree: well-separated islands in a UMAP prove five clusters
  2. Disagree: UMAP can never show real clusters
  3. Check whether the five groups persist across seeds and parameter settings, are found by a clustering method run on the original embeddings, and differ in something not used to build them
  4. Increase min_dist until there are five islands
A UMAP plot is a visualisation; islands can be created or split by n_neighbors, min_dist and the seed. The evidence for real groups is stability and external validation (chapter 10), done on the original data. Rejecting UMAP outright goes too far, since neighbourhoods in the plot are informative, and tuning a parameter to reach a desired number of islands is exactly the wrong direction.

With 10 input columns generated from 3 hidden factors plus noise of standard deviation 0.5 per column, reconstruction RMSE with 3 components was 0.416, below the noise level of 0.5. What explains this?

  1. The reconstruction is overfitted to the training rows
  2. The 3 components keep the signal and discard the noise in the other 7 directions, so only about √(7/10) × 0.5 of the noise is in the reconstruction error
  3. PCA adds information that was not in the original data
  4. The noise was not random, so PCA removed it perfectly
The error is measured against the noisy data. The discarded part is noise spread over the 7 unused directions, so RMSE ≈ 0.5 × √(7/10) = 0.42. Noise inside the 3 kept directions stays in the reconstruction and counts as part of the "signal" it keeps. PCA adds no information, and the noise was random but isotropic, so most of it lies outside the kept subspace.

Which statement about "distances in the 2-D map" is correct for t-SNE?

  1. Distances between all pairs of points are approximately preserved
  2. Distances between cluster centres are preserved but within-cluster distances are not
  3. Larger distances between islands always mean more different clusters
  4. Small distances between nearby points are fairly reliable, but distances between islands are not
The KL objective penalises separating true neighbours heavily and is lenient about the placement of distant points, so the local picture is reliable (68 percent of 10-NN kept in our run) and the global one is not (the three centre distances came out nearly equal when the truth was 6.9, 23.2 and 30.0). Option 2 reverses this, and option 3 relies on exactly the distances that are not meaningful.
