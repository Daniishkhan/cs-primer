Linear & Logistic Regression

The two models that most of machine learning is built on. A linear model predicts a number as a weighted sum of features; a logistic model turns the same weighted sum into a probability. This chapter shows how both are fitted, by a formula and by gradient descent, what their coefficients really say, and the ways they quietly mislead.

Why this matters

In chapter 4 you set a bar that every model must clear. A linear or logistic model is usually the first thing that clears it, and in many production systems (credit scoring, ad click prediction, clinical risk scores) it is also the last. It trains in milliseconds, serves in microseconds, can be audited line by line, and gives a strong baseline against which boosted trees and neural networks have to justify their cost. It is also the single best place to learn the machinery that every later chapter reuses: a loss function, a gradient, a step size, and the discipline of reading what a fitted parameter does and does not mean. After this chapter you will be able to fit both models from scratch, tell when gradient descent is struggling and why, read coefficients without overclaiming, and recognise the situations (collinearity, separation) where the numbers look fine and are not.

Throughout, the notation follows scikit-learn: X is a matrix of shape (n_samples, n_features), one row per example; y is a vector of length n_samples; a model has fit(X, y) and predict(X). The mathematics leans on three earlier chapters of A0, which we recall where needed: matrices as functions, gradients and maximum likelihood.

A line is a model

Suppose you run a lettings agency and want to estimate monthly rent from a flat's size, its distance from the city centre and the age of the building. We will use a small synthetic dataset throughout this chapter so that we know the truth and can check what the model recovers: 200 flats, with area drawn between 25 and 120 square metres, dist (kilometres to the centre) between 0.5 and 15, and age of the building between 0 and 80 years. The generating rule, which the model never sees, is

rent = 300 + 11 × area − 25 × dist − 2 × age + noise,

where the noise is normally distributed with standard deviation 60. The first 150 flats are the training set and the last 50 are held back for testing, as in chapter 1.

A linear model predicts the target (the quantity we want, here rent) as a weighted sum of the features (the measured inputs) plus a constant:

ŷ = b + w1x1 + w2x2 + … + wdxd = b + w · x

The numbers w are the weights (also called coefficients), and b is the intercept (or bias): the prediction when every feature is zero. Fitting the model means choosing b and w. For a whole dataset the predictions are one matrix-vector product, X @ w + b, which is why the model is so cheap to evaluate: one pass over d numbers per example.

The word "linear" is about the parameters, not about the features. A model b + w1*area + w2*area**2 is still a linear model, because the prediction is a weighted sum of whatever columns you hand it, and you are free to hand it squares, logarithms or interactions. That is the main lever for bending a straight line to fit curved data, and we return to it in chapter 17.

Measuring a fit

To choose parameters we need a number that says how bad a set of predictions is. For each example the residual is the actual value minus the prediction, y − ŷ. The standard score is the mean squared error (MSE): square every residual, then average. Choosing the parameters that minimise it is called ordinary least squares.

Plug in numbers. Four flats and two candidate lines (made up for the table); each column of the printout is one step above.

The idea

  1. 4 flats: area x, rent y; two candidate lines, each with a (b, w)
  2. per flat: pred ← b + w × x; resid ← y − pred; sq ← resid²
  3. MSE ← (sum of sq) ÷ n
  4. compare the two lines, and the typical miss √MSE
"""Mean squared error by hand: four flats, two candidate lines.
Change a number and re-run to see the score move."""

# ── 1. Four flats and two candidate lines ────────────────────────────────
# One feature (floor area) keeps w . x down to a single product.
AREA = [30, 45, 60, 80]                   # x_i, square metres
RENT = [640, 810, 940, 1220]              # y_i, pounds a month
LINES = {"line A": (300, 11.0),           # (b, w): rent = b + w * area
         "line B": (500, 6.0)}


def mse_table(b, w):
    """Print one row per flat and return the mean squared error."""
    total = 0.0
    print("   x     y   b+w*x  residual  squared")
    for x, y in zip(AREA, RENT):
        pred = b + w * x                  # step 1: prediction b + w . x
        resid = y - pred                  # step 1: y_i minus the prediction
        sq = resid ** 2                   # step 2: square it
        total += sq                       # step 3: sum over i
        print(f"{x:4d} {y:5d} {pred:7.1f} {resid:+8.1f} {sq:8.1f}")
    return total / len(AREA)              # step 4: divide by n


# ── 2. Score each line ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
for name, (b, w) in LINES.items():
    print(f"{name}: b = {b}, w = {w}")
    mse = mse_table(b, w)
    print(f"  MSE = {mse:.1f}   (typical miss ~ {mse ** 0.5:.1f} pounds)")
    print()

Why square? Three reasons, in decreasing order of depth. First, the loss is smooth and bowl-shaped in the parameters, so it has one minimum and calculus finds it. Second, if you believe the noise around the true line is normally distributed, then minimising squared error is exactly maximum likelihood, the argument from A0 chapter 13. Third, squaring punishes a large miss much more than a small one, which is sometimes what you want and sometimes a trap: one flat listed at ten times its true rent (a data-entry error) contributes a hundred times more to the loss than a flat that is 10 percent off, and it will drag the whole line towards itself. If your data has such points, look at them first (chapter 3); a loss such as the absolute error or the Huber loss, which grows only linearly for large residuals, is the other remedy.

The closed-form answer

Because the MSE is a smooth bowl, its minimum is where the gradient is zero. Collect the intercept into the weights by adding a column of ones to X (call the result A, shape (n, d+1)) and set the derivative to zero. The result is the normal equations:

(ATA) w = ATy

Geometrically, Aw is the point in the space spanned by your feature columns that lies closest to y, and the residual vector is perpendicular to every column (which is why the residuals of a model with an intercept always sum to zero). Try it by hand on three points, (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5). The mean of x is 2 and of y is 10/3. The slope is the sum of products of deviations over the sum of squared deviations: w = ((−1)(−4/3) + 0 + (1)(5/3)) / (1 + 0 + 1) = 3/2 = 1.5, and the intercept is b = 10/3 − 1.5 × 2 = 1/3. Every prediction line you ever fit is this calculation, scaled up.

On the rent data, in NumPy. We solve the normal equations and also call np.linalg.lstsq, which is what libraries actually use, and compare against the baseline from chapter 4 (predict the training mean for everyone).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 200
area = rng.uniform(25, 120, n)       # m^2
dist = rng.uniform(0.5, 15, n)       # km to the centre
age = rng.uniform(0, 80, n)          # building age, years
rent = 300 + 11 * area - 25 * dist - 2 * age + rng.normal(0, 60, n)

X = np.column_stack([area, dist, age])          # shape (n_samples, n_features)
y = rent
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = X[:150], X[150:], y[:150], y[150:]

def add_intercept(X):
    return np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])

A = add_intercept(X_tr)
w = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ y_tr)         # normal equations
w_lstsq = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y_tr, rcond=None)[0]  # what libraries do (SVD based)
print("normal eq :", np.round(w, 2))
print("lstsq     :", np.round(w_lstsq, 2))

def rmse(y, p): return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((y - p) ** 2)))
pred = add_intercept(X_te) @ w
base = np.full(len(y_te), y_tr.mean())
print("baseline RMSE (predict the mean):", round(rmse(y_te, base), 1))
print("linear model RMSE               :", round(rmse(y_te, pred), 1))
ss_res = np.sum((y_te - pred) ** 2); ss_tot = np.sum((y_te - y_te.mean()) ** 2)
print("R^2 on test:", round(1 - ss_res / ss_tot, 3))
print("residual mean on train:", round(float(np.mean(y_tr - A @ w)), 6))

The two solvers agree to two decimals, the model recovers the generating rule (true weights 300, 11, −25, −2 against fitted 317, 10.7, −24.0, −1.9) from only 150 noisy rows, and the test error falls from 345 to 61. That 61 is close to the noise standard deviation of 60, which is the best any model could do here: the remaining error is irreducible. The test R2 of 0.968 says the model explains 96.8 percent of the variance in rent that the mean-predictor cannot.

Two practical notes. Library solvers use a matrix factorisation (QR or the SVD from A0 chapter 7) instead of forming ATA and inverting it: squaring the matrix squares its condition number, and an ill-conditioned matrix turns small rounding errors into large errors in w. And the cost is roughly n·d2 to build ATA plus d3 to solve it: trivial for ten features, painful for ten million.

Gradient descent: the same answer, found by walking

The closed form exists for linear regression and almost nowhere else. Logistic regression has no formula, neither do boosted trees, and neural networks certainly do not. The tool that does work everywhere is gradient descent: start with any weights, compute the direction in which the loss rises fastest (the gradient), and take a small step the other way. Repeat. A0 chapter 10 covers the optimisers in depth; here we need the plain version.

For the MSE the gradient has a tidy form. With residuals r = Aw − y,

∇MSE = (2/n) ATr,    update: w ← w − η ∇MSE

Read the gradient in words: for each feature, multiply its column by the residuals and average. If large flats are consistently under-predicted (positive residuals, large area), the area weight gets pushed up. The factor η is the learning rate or step size, and choosing it is the only real decision. Too small and you crawl; too large and each step overshoots the valley, landing higher on the opposite wall, and the loss grows instead of shrinking.

The surprise is that the step size that works depends on the units of your features. Area runs up to 120, age up to 80, distance up to 15. The loss changes enormously per unit change in the area weight and very little per unit change in the distance weight, so the loss surface is a long narrow canyon, and a step size small enough not to bounce off the steep walls is far too small to make progress along the floor. Standardising each feature (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) turns the canyon into a round bowl. Here are the two runs side by side, with the same 2,000 steps:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 200
area = rng.uniform(25, 120, n); dist = rng.uniform(0.5, 15, n); age = rng.uniform(0, 80, n)
y = 300 + 11 * area - 25 * dist - 2 * age + rng.normal(0, 60, n)
X = np.column_stack([area, dist, age])

def gd(X, y, lr, steps):
    """Batch gradient descent on mean squared error. Returns weights, loss history."""
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])
    w = np.zeros(A.shape[1])
    hist = []
    for t in range(steps):
        resid = A @ w - y
        hist.append(float(np.mean(resid ** 2)))
        w -= lr * (2 / len(y)) * (A.T @ resid)      # gradient of the mean squared error
    return w, hist

mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0)
Xs = (X - mu) / sd                                   # standardise: mean 0, sd 1 per column

w_raw, h_raw = gd(X, y, lr=1e-4, steps=2000)
w_std, h_std = gd(Xs, y, lr=0.1, steps=2000)
print("raw features, lr=1e-4   : loss after 10/100/2000 steps =", [round(h_raw[i]) for i in (9, 99, 1999)])
print("standardised, lr=0.1    : loss after 10/100/2000 steps =", [round(h_std[i]) for i in (9, 99, 1999)])
print("closed-form (best possible) loss on these rows:", round(float(np.mean((y - np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X]) @ np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X]), y, rcond=None)[0]) ** 2))))

# a learning rate that is too large on raw features: the loss explodes
_, h_big = gd(X, y, lr=1e-3, steps=8)
print("raw features, lr=1e-3   : first losses =", [f"{v:.3g}" for v in h_big])

# convert standardised weights back to the original units
slope = w_std[1:] / sd
intercept = w_std[0] - np.sum(slope * mu)
print("recovered slopes:", np.round(slope, 2), "intercept:", round(float(intercept), 1))

With raw features we had to use a step size of 0.0001, and after 2,000 steps the loss is still 10,225, nearly three times the minimum. With standardised features a step size of 0.1 reaches the closed-form minimum, 3,626, within about 100 steps. Raise the raw-feature step size by a factor of ten, to 0.001, and the loss explodes: 794,000, then 139 million, then 25 billion, growing roughly 180-fold per step. And since we standardised, the weights are in units of "per standard deviation", so the last two lines convert them back: dividing each slope by the feature's standard deviation and adjusting the intercept recovers rent per square metre and so on. The numbers differ slightly from the 150-row fit above because this run used all 200 rows.

Three things to try. Raw features at a step size of about 1e-4: slow, with the path creeping along the canyon floor. Raise the step size past the limit shown under the plots and watch it diverge. Then standardise and push the step size up towards 1: now it converges in a handful of steps. The "largest stable step size" printed under the plots is 2 divided by the largest curvature of the surface, which is why the units of the features set it.

Analogy: walking down a valley in fog

You are on a hillside in thick fog and want the lowest point. You cannot see it, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your boots. You take a step downhill, feel again, and repeat. Your stride is the learning rate. In a round bowl, any sensible stride works. In a long narrow gorge the ground slopes steeply towards the nearest wall and only gently along the floor, so you stagger from wall to wall while barely moving along the valley.

Where it breaks down: a walker can see the whole slope under their feet at once and stops when it is flat. Gradient descent knows only the local slope, never how far away the bottom is; and for the convex losses in this chapter there is exactly one bottom, but for the neural networks of later courses there are many hollows and the analogy stops being comforting.

Go deeper optional: why the step-size limit is 2 / λmax

Near the minimum the MSE is exactly a quadratic: loss = lossmin + (Δw)T H (Δw) / 2, with Hessian H = (2/n) ATA. The gradient is H Δw, so one step maps Δw to (I − ηH) Δw. Along an eigenvector of H with eigenvalue λ, the error is multiplied by (1 − ηλ) each step. That shrinks if |1 − ηλ| < 1, i.e. η < 2/λ, for every eigenvalue, so the largest one (λmax) sets the limit. The slowest direction shrinks by (1 − ηλmin) per step, so the number of steps needed is of the order λmax/λmin, the condition number. Standardising the features moves the eigenvalues close together, which shrinks the condition number from thousands to near one. This is the same quantity that made the normal equations numerically fragile above.

Stochastic and mini-batch versions

The gradient above averages over all n rows before taking one step. With ten million rows that is wasteful: a random batch of 256 rows gives a noisy but unbiased estimate of the same gradient for a fraction of the cost. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) takes one such step per batch. The noise means the loss jitters instead of settling, so the step size is usually decayed over time. This is the engine inside every large model you will meet, from scikit-learn's SGDClassifier to the training of language models, and A0 chapter 10 covers momentum, Adam and schedules.

When a linear model misleads

A linear regression always returns an answer. It will fit a straight line through a parabola, a staircase or a cloud, report coefficients to six decimal places and offer no warning. The classical assumptions are not formalities; each one fails in a recognisable way.

Non-linearity
The true relationship bends. Plot residuals against each feature and against the prediction: a curve in the plot means the model is missing structure. The fix is a transformed feature (log of area, area squared) or a different model family.
Outliers and leverage
A few extreme points can pull the line, because squared error rewards moving towards them. Points far from the others in feature space have extra leverage. Inspect the largest residuals and the most extreme feature values before trusting the fit.
Unequal noise
If the spread of residuals grows with the prediction (expensive flats vary more than cheap ones), the point predictions can still be fine, but any uncertainty statement based on one global noise level is wrong. Modelling the logarithm of the target often helps.
Extrapolation
A line goes on forever. A model fitted on flats of 25 to 120 square metres will happily price a 400 square metre flat, and the further from the data, the less the answer means.
Collinearity
Two or more features carry nearly the same information. The predictions stay fine, but the individual coefficients become unstable. This one is invisible from accuracy alone, so we demonstrate it next.

Collinearity: good predictions, untrustworthy coefficients

Multicollinearity means that one feature is nearly a linear combination of others. Suppose the lettings system also records floor area in square feet, entered by a different team with some rounding noise. The new column is almost perfectly correlated with area. We refit on 200 bootstrap resamples of the data (draw 200 rows with replacement, fit, repeat; A0 chapter 15) and watch how much the coefficients move.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 200
area = rng.uniform(25, 120, n); dist = rng.uniform(0.5, 15, n); age = rng.uniform(0, 80, n)
y = 300 + 11 * area - 25 * dist - 2 * age + rng.normal(0, 60, n)
area_ft2 = area * 10.764 + rng.normal(0, 8, n)       # the same size again, in square feet, with noise
X = np.column_stack([area, area_ft2, dist, age])

def fit(X, y):
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])
    return np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]

print("corr(area, area_ft2) =", round(float(np.corrcoef(area, area_ft2)[0, 1]), 4))
coefs, preds = [], []
probe = np.array([60.0, 60 * 10.764, 5.0, 30.0])      # one fixed flat to predict
for b in range(200):
    idx = rng.integers(0, n, n)                         # bootstrap resample
    w = fit(X[idx], y[idx])
    coefs.append(w[1:3]); preds.append(float(w[0] + probe @ w[1:]))
coefs = np.array(coefs)
print("bootstrap sd of the area coefficient (m^2)  :", round(float(coefs[:, 0].std()), 2))
print("bootstrap sd of the area coefficient (ft^2) :", round(float(coefs[:, 1].std()), 2))
print("they move together: corr =", round(float(np.corrcoef(coefs[:, 0], coefs[:, 1])[0, 1]), 3))
print("prediction for the probe flat: mean", round(np.mean(preds), 1), "sd", round(float(np.std(preds)), 2))

c2, p2 = [], []
X3 = np.column_stack([area, dist, age])
for b in range(200):
    idx = rng.integers(0, n, n)
    w3 = fit(X3[idx], y[idx])
    c2.append(w3[1]); p2.append(float(w3[0] + probe[[0, 2, 3]] @ w3[1:]))
print("area coefficient sd with no duplicate column:", round(float(np.std(c2)), 2))
print("prediction sd with no duplicate column      :", round(float(np.std(p2)), 2))

With the duplicate column, the area coefficient wanders with a standard deviation of 5.5 across resamples, about 35 times more than without it (0.16), and the two area coefficients move in exactly opposite directions (correlation −1.0): the model can only determine the combination "m2 weight plus 10.764 times the ft2 weight", not each one alone. Yet the prediction for a fixed flat is just as steady as before (standard deviation 5.65 against 5.31). The model predicts fine and explains nothing. Anyone who reads a single area coefficient off such a fit, and reports it as the value of a square metre, is mostly reading noise.

Common misconception

"A bigger coefficient means a more important feature." A coefficient depends on the units of its feature: measure area in square feet and its coefficient shrinks by a factor of 10.764 while the model is unchanged. Even after standardising, collinear features share credit arbitrarily, and the coefficient says nothing about whether intervening on that feature would change the outcome. Importance has its own tools, covered in chapter 16.

Reading coefficients carefully

Used with care, the coefficients are the point of a linear model: a human can read the whole model off a page. The careful reading has four parts.

  1. Units. The area weight, 10.7, means "10.7 more rent per extra square metre". It has the units of the target per unit of the feature. To compare features on a common scale, standardise them first; then each weight is "change in target for a one-standard-deviation change in the feature".
  2. Holding the others fixed. In a multiple regression each weight is the association between that feature and the target after accounting for the other features in the model. Add or remove a correlated feature and the weight changes, sometimes even its sign. The age weight of −1.9 means "among flats of the same size and distance, each extra year of age goes with 1.9 less rent", not "age on its own".
  3. Association, not intervention. The model describes how rent differs across flats. It does not say what happens to a particular flat's rent if the owner adds a bedroom. The data may have been generated by a process where size and neighbourhood are chosen together. Moving from "associated with" to "caused by" needs an experiment or a causal argument, not a bigger dataset.
  4. Uncertainty. A fitted weight is an estimate from 150 rows. The bootstrap above is the most general way to put an error bar on it; for ordinary least squares there are also textbook standard errors, proportional to the noise level and to the inverse of ATA, which is exactly the matrix that goes nearly singular under collinearity.

From numbers to probabilities: logistic regression

Many targets are not quantities but yes/no outcomes: did the customer churn, was the transaction fraudulent, does the scan show a tumour. Code the outcome as 0 or 1 and a linear regression will still run, but the predictions are not probabilities: they can be −0.3 or 1.4, and a straight line is a poor shape for something that must flatten out at 0 and at 1. Logistic regression keeps the weighted sum and passes it through a function that squeezes any real number into the interval (0, 1).

The easiest route to the function is through odds. If an event has probability p, its odds are p / (1 − p): a probability of 0.75 is odds of 3 to 1. The log-odds (or logit) is the natural logarithm of that, and unlike a probability it runs over the whole real line, from −∞ for impossible to +∞ for certain. Logistic regression says: the log-odds are a linear function of the features.

log( p / (1 − p) ) = z = b + w · x   ⇔   p = 1 / (1 + e−z)

The right-hand form is the sigmoid function, also called the logistic function. It is the inverse of the logit.

The curve is steepest at z = 0, where p = 0.5, and flattens at both ends, so once the model is already confident, extra evidence moves the probability less and less. Each increase of 1 in z multiplies the odds by e ≈ 2.72, which is where the standard way of reading coefficients comes from.

The loss: log-loss is maximum likelihood

With a probability for each row we can score the model by how much probability it gave to what actually happened. If the true label is 1 the model's likelihood for that row is p; if 0, it is 1 − p. Multiply over rows, take the logarithm so the product becomes a sum, negate so that smaller is better, and average. The result is log-loss (also called binary cross-entropy):

L = −(1/n) ∑i [ yi ln pi + (1 − yi) ln(1 − pi) ]

This is the same move as with squared error: a probability model for the data, then the weights that make the observed data most likely (A0 chapter 13). It also behaves the way you would want. A confident wrong answer is punished hard: predicting 0.01 for something that happens costs −ln 0.01 = 4.6, while a hedged 0.4 costs 0.92. Predicting exactly 0 for something that happens costs infinity, which is why implementations clip probabilities away from 0 and 1. A model that always predicts the base rate has a loss equal to the entropy of the labels (A0 chapter 14), so log-loss tells you how many nats of surprise the model removed.

Go deeper optional: the gradient of log-loss has the same shape as the MSE gradient

Write p = σ(z) with z = a · w for one row a (the features with a leading 1). Two facts: dσ/dz = σ(1 − σ) = p(1 − p), and for one row the loss is −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)]. By the chain rule,

∂L/∂z = −[ y/p − (1 − y)/(1 − p) ] · p(1 − p) = −[ y(1 − p) − (1 − y)p ] = p − y.

The sigmoid's derivative cancels the denominators exactly. Then ∂L/∂w = (p − y) a, and averaging over rows gives ∇L = (1/n) AT(p − y). Compare with linear regression, ∇MSE = (2/n) AT(ŷ − y): "features times prediction error" in both. This is not a coincidence; it holds for every model in the generalised linear model family when the loss is the matching likelihood (normal noise with identity link, Bernoulli with logit link, Poisson with log link), and it is why the code for the two models differs by one line. Fitting it

There is no closed form, but the log-loss is convex, so gradient descent from zeros is guaranteed to approach the one minimum, and because the gradient has the familiar shape the code is the linear version with one extra function. The dataset is a synthetic churn problem: 1,000 customers, each with tenure (months, 1 to 60), spend (monthly spend, mean 50, standard deviation 20) and tickets (support tickets last quarter, Poisson with mean 1.5). Churn probability falls with tenure and rises with spend and tickets by a rule the model has to rediscover. 700 rows train, 300 test. We standardise with training statistics only, a habit that matters enormously in chapter 14.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n = 1000
tenure = rng.uniform(1, 60, n)               # months as a customer
spend = rng.normal(50, 20, n)                # monthly spend
tickets = rng.poisson(1.5, n)                # support tickets last quarter
z = -0.3 - 0.06 * (tenure - 24) + 0.02 * (spend - 50) + 0.5 * (tickets - 1.5)
y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))).astype(float)     # 1 = churned
X = np.column_stack([tenure, spend, tickets])
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = X[:700], X[700:], y[:700], y[700:]
mu, sd = X_tr.mean(0), X_tr.std(0)           # statistics from the TRAINING rows only
S_tr, S_te = (X_tr - mu) / sd, (X_te - mu) / sd

def sigmoid(z): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
def log_loss(y, p, eps=1e-12):
    p = np.clip(p, eps, 1 - eps)
    return float(-np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p)))

def fit_logistic(S, y, lr=0.5, steps=500):
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(S)), S])
    w = np.zeros(A.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = sigmoid(A @ w)
        w -= lr * A.T @ (p - y) / len(y)          # the gradient of the mean log-loss
    return w

w = fit_logistic(S_tr, y_tr)
A_te = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(S_te)), S_te])
p_te = sigmoid(A_te @ w)

rate = y_tr.mean()
print("churn rate in training data:", round(float(rate), 3))
print("log-loss, predict the base rate :", round(log_loss(y_te, np.full(len(y_te), rate)), 3))
print("log-loss, logistic regression   :", round(log_loss(y_te, p_te), 3))
print("accuracy, always 'stays'        :", round(float(np.mean(y_te == 0)), 3))
print("accuracy, logistic at 0.5       :", round(float(np.mean((p_te >= 0.5) == y_te)), 3))

# check the analytic gradient against finite differences
A_tr = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(S_tr)), S_tr])
g = A_tr.T @ (sigmoid(A_tr @ w) - y_tr) / len(y_tr)
num = np.zeros(4)
for j in range(4):
    e = np.zeros(4); e[j] = 1e-6
    num[j] = (log_loss(y_tr, sigmoid(A_tr @ (w + e))) - log_loss(y_tr, sigmoid(A_tr @ (w - e)))) / 2e-6
print("max |analytic - numeric| gradient:", f"{np.abs(g - num).max():.1e}", "(near zero at the optimum:", np.round(g, 4), ")")

names = ["tenure", "spend", "tickets"]
print()
print(f"{'feature':<8} {'weight/SD':>10} {'odds ratio per SD':>18} {'SD in units':>12}")
for nm, wj, s in zip(names, w[1:], sd):
    print(f"{nm:<8} {wj:>10.3f} {np.exp(wj):>18.2f} {s:>12.1f}")
x_new = (np.array([6, 80, 4]) - mu) / sd
print("\nnew customer (6 months, 80 spend, 4 tickets): P(churn) =", round(float(sigmoid(w[0] + x_new @ w[1:])), 3))

log-loss, predict the base rate : 0.667
log-loss, logistic regression   : 0.544
accuracy, always 'stays'        : 0.617
accuracy, logistic at 0.5       : 0.74
max |analytic - numeric| gradient: 5.0e-11 (near zero at the optimum: [ 0.  0. -0. -0.] )

Against the base rate (a model that says 35.7 percent for everyone), logistic regression cuts the test log-loss from 0.667 to 0.544 and lifts accuracy from 61.7 percent (always predicting "stays") to 74.0 percent. The gradient check passes: the analytic and numerical gradients agree to 5e-11, and at the fitted weights the gradient is zero to four decimals, which is the definition of having converged. Remember the lesson from chapter 2: accuracy is a decision-level number, log-loss a probability-level one, and the better model for a business that acts on probabilities is the one with the lower log-loss.

Step through

One logistic prediction, from raw features to a decision

  1. The raw record. A customer with 6 months of tenure, spending 80 a month, who raised 4 tickets. These are the units a human reads.
  2. Standardise. Subtract the training mean and divide by the training standard deviation, so each feature is measured in units of how unusual it is. The mean and SD come from the training rows only (chapter 14 explains why that matters).
  3. Weight each feature. Each standardised value is multiplied by its fitted weight. The tenure weight is negative and this customer's tenure is far below average, so the tenure term is positive and pushes towards churn.
  4. Add up to the log-odds. The three terms plus the intercept give z, which can be any real number. It is the model's score on the log-odds scale.
  5. Squash to a probability. The sigmoid maps z to a number between 0 and 1. Here the model says 94.8% chance of churn.
  6. Decide, and score. A threshold turns the probability into an action, and if the true label arrives later, log-loss scores the probability itself. Here it would be -ln(p) = 0.053 if the customer did churn and -ln(1-p) = 2.97 if not: small when confident and right, large when confident and wrong.

Notice the stepper's last step. Training minimises log-loss, not accuracy, for a reason: accuracy is flat almost everywhere (changing a weight by a tiny amount flips no predictions) so it gives gradient descent nothing to follow, while log-loss changes smoothly and keeps rewarding probabilities that are well calibrated, a theme taken up in chapter 15.

The decision boundary is a straight line

Where does the model switch from "stays" to "churns"? At p = 0.5, which is z = 0, which is the set of points where b + w · x = 0: a straight line in two features, a flat plane in three, a hyperplane in general. Lines of constant probability are parallel to it, because the probability depends on the features only through z.

The drawing uses two of the three features. The model cannot represent "churn is high for new customers with many tickets and also high for old customers with none", because no single line separates them; for that you need an interaction feature, a non-linear model or both. And the 0.5 threshold is a default, not a law: if a missed churner costs ten times what a pointless retention offer costs, the best threshold is nearer 0.1. The choice of threshold from costs is the subject of chapter 15.

Reading logistic coefficients

Because the log-odds are linear, exponentiating a weight gives the factor by which the odds change when the feature rises by one unit, with the others held fixed. From the table above: one standard deviation more tenure (17.2 months) multiplies the churn odds by 0.26, so a customer one SD longer-lived has about a quarter of the odds; one SD more spend (about 20) multiplies them by 1.56, and one SD more tickets (1.2) by 1.84. This is the odds ratio that clinical papers and credit reports quote.

Common misconception

"An odds ratio of 1.84 means the probability rises by 84 percent." It does not. Odds multiply by 1.84; what happens to the probability depends on where you start. From a probability of 0.05 (odds 0.053) the new odds are 0.097 and the probability becomes 0.088, a rise of under 4 points. From 0.50 (odds 1) the new odds are 1.84 and the probability becomes 0.648, a rise of 15 points. The effect on the probability is largest in the middle of the sigmoid and shrinks near 0 and 1. Report odds ratios as odds ratios, or compute the change in probability for a named customer.

Perfect separation, and why you will want a penalty

One failure of logistic regression is so common in practice that it deserves a demonstration. If a feature (or a combination of features) separates the two classes perfectly in the training set, the log-loss can always be reduced further by making the weights larger: a steeper sigmoid is more confident, and more confident is better when every call is right. There is no minimum; the best weights are at infinity.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([-3.0, -2.0, -1.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])      # perfectly separable at 0
y = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1], dtype=float)
A = np.column_stack([np.ones(6), x])
w = np.zeros(2)
for step in range(1, 100001):
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(A @ w)))
    w -= 0.5 * A.T @ (p - y) / 6
    if step in (10, 100, 1000, 10000, 100000):
        loss = -np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))
        print(f"step {step:>6}: slope {w[1]:6.2f}  intercept {round(float(w[0]), 4) + 0.0:7.4f}  log-loss {loss:.6f}")
step     10: slope   1.49  intercept  0.0000  log-loss 0.094497
step    100: slope   3.07  intercept  0.0000  log-loss 0.016056
step   1000: slope   5.16  intercept  0.0000  log-loss 0.001925
step  10000: slope   7.42  intercept  0.0000  log-loss 0.000199
step 100000: slope   9.72  intercept  0.0000  log-loss 0.000020

The loss keeps falling, from 0.094 to 0.00002, while the slope keeps climbing, 1.5, 3.1, 5.2, 7.4, 9.7: about 2.3 more for every tenfold increase in steps, a logarithmic crawl towards infinity. The data says "separable" and the weights say "infinitely certain". With real data this shows up as enormous coefficients, convergence warnings and predicted probabilities of 0.9999 on new data that the model has no right to be sure about. It also happens, more quietly, with many features and few rows. The cure is to penalise large weights, which is the subject of the next chapter; scikit-learn's LogisticRegression applies such a penalty by default, which is why its coefficients are not identical to those fitted here.

Where this fits, and where it stops

  • More than two classes. Replace the sigmoid by the softmax over one score per class; the loss is the cross-entropy and the gradient keeps its "features times (prediction minus truth)" shape. The final layer of a neural classifier is exactly this. See A2 chapter 1, where a single logistic unit is the neuron.
  • Other targets. Counts (calls per hour) suit Poisson regression, positive skewed amounts suit a log link or a model of the logarithm. The recipe is unchanged: pick the likelihood that matches the noise, then fit by gradient descent.
  • Curved relationships. Add squared terms, logarithms, interactions or spline bases as new columns. The model stays linear in its parameters and every tool in this chapter still applies. Chapter 17 develops this.
  • Interactions and thresholds in the data. If "premium plan and more than 3 tickets" matters and no single line expresses it, a tree-based model finds such rules automatically; chapter 8 introduces trees and chapter 9 their ensembles.

Systems connection

A fitted linear model is tiny: a vector of weights, an intercept and the means and standard deviations used to standardise. Serving it is one dot product, which is why it is the model of choice when a decision must be made in a few microseconds, as in a payments path. The risk is not speed but skew: if the training pipeline standardises with one mean and the serving code with another, or silently fills a missing value differently, the weights are applied to numbers they were never fitted to. Ship the preprocessing constants and the weights as one versioned artefact. The matrix products themselves are what vector units and BLAS libraries accelerate.

Key takeaways

  • A linear model predicts b + X @ w; fitting it by least squares has a closed form (the normal equations, solved in practice by a factorisation), and the fitted line is the one whose residuals are perpendicular to every feature.
  • Gradient descent finds the same answer and works for models with no formula. The step size that converges depends on the scale of the features: standardise first, and use the largest step size below 2 / λmax.
  • Linear regression always returns an answer. Check residuals for curvature, look at outliers, avoid extrapolating, and test for collinearity: it leaves predictions healthy and makes individual coefficients unreliable (35 times noisier in our example).
  • Read coefficients as associations, in the units of their feature, with the other features held fixed, and with an error bar. A larger coefficient does not mean a more important or more causal feature.
  • Logistic regression sets the log-odds equal to the weighted sum; the sigmoid turns that into a probability; training minimises log-loss, which is maximum likelihood; the gradient is again "features times (prediction minus truth)".
  • Exponentiated weights are odds ratios, not probability changes. The decision boundary is a hyperplane; the threshold is a business choice.
  • With separable data the unpenalised weights grow without limit. The remedy, a penalty on weight size, is the next chapter.

Check your understanding

Gradient descent on a linear regression with unscaled features (area up to 120, age up to 80) crawls at the step size you can afford, and a ten times larger step size makes the loss explode. What is the most direct fix?

  1. Use the normal equations instead, because gradient descent cannot fit unscaled features
  2. Standardise the features, then use a much larger step size
  3. Add more training rows so that the gradient is less noisy
  4. Switch the loss from squared error to absolute error
Standardising turns the long narrow loss valley into a round bowl, so one step size suits every weight and a far larger one is stable. The normal equations would also work but they are a different method, not the explanation of the problem (and gradient descent can fit unscaled data, slowly). More rows do not change the shape of the surface in this full-batch setting, and absolute error makes the gradient less smooth, not better conditioned.

You add a near-duplicate of the area column (the same size in square feet, plus a little noise) to a rent model. Which outcome should you expect?

  1. Test error rises sharply because the model now double counts area
  2. The fit fails with an error because the matrix cannot be inverted
  3. Both area coefficients become exactly zero
  4. Test error barely changes, but the individual area coefficients become unstable from sample to sample
With noise in the duplicate, the matrix is nearly but not exactly singular, so a solver still returns an answer and the predictions, which depend on the well-determined combination of the two weights, stay steady. The individual weights trade off against each other and swing from resample to resample (35 times more in our run). With an exactly duplicated column some solvers would complain or pick one solution arbitrarily, and no standard least-squares method sets both weights to zero.

A logistic model reports an odds ratio of 2.0 for a feature. A customer's churn probability is currently 0.50. After the feature rises by one unit (others fixed), what is the new probability, closest?

  1. 0.67
  2. 0.75
  3. 1.00
  4. 0.52
A probability of 0.50 is odds of 1. Doubling the odds gives 2, and a probability of odds/(1+odds) = 2/3 = 0.67. The tempting 0.75 treats the odds ratio as a ratio of probabilities (0.50 doubled would be 1.00, which is impossible, and 0.75 would need odds of 3). 0.52 would come from a small additive nudge on the probability scale, which is not how odds ratios act.

Why do we train logistic regression by minimising log-loss rather than directly minimising the error rate?

  1. Log-loss is always lower than the error rate, so training converges sooner
  2. The error rate cannot be computed on a training set
  3. The error rate is flat almost everywhere in the weights, so it gives gradient descent no direction, whereas log-loss changes smoothly and rewards good probabilities
  4. Log-loss guarantees the highest possible accuracy
Nudging a weight slightly flips no predictions, so the gradient of the error rate is zero almost everywhere. Log-loss is smooth, convex and is the maximum-likelihood objective. It does not guarantee the best accuracy (the best-accuracy weights may differ), and the error rate is perfectly computable; the issue is that it is not a useful training signal.

You fit an unpenalised logistic regression and see coefficients near 40, with probabilities of 0.9999 on the training rows and a convergence warning. What is the most likely cause?

  1. The learning rate is too small
  2. The classes are perfectly (or nearly) separable by the features, so the loss keeps falling as the weights grow
  3. The data is not standardised, so the intercept is wrong
  4. The target is balanced
With separable data there is no finite minimiser: a steeper sigmoid always lowers the loss, so the weights drift to large values and the optimiser stops by iteration limit. A small learning rate would slow growth, not cause huge weights. Lack of scaling affects speed, not whether a minimum exists, and class balance is irrelevant to separability. The usual fix is a penalty on the weights (chapter 6) or more data.

A regression says each extra bedroom is associated with 14,000 more in house price, holding floor area fixed. A seller proposes to add a bedroom by splitting a room. What does the model tell you about the effect on that house's price?

  1. It will rise by 14,000, because the coefficient measures the effect of adding a bedroom
  2. It will rise by 14,000 times the standard error
  3. It will fall, because floor area is unchanged
  4. Nothing reliable: the coefficient describes differences between houses in the data, not what happens when you change one house
A coefficient is an association estimated across different houses, adjusted for the other features in the model. Bedrooms may proxy for unmeasured things (garden, neighbourhood, layout), and splitting a room may reduce the living space buyers value. Turning an association into a prediction about an intervention needs causal assumptions or an experiment. The standard-error option mixes up the size of an effect with the uncertainty of its estimate; the "will fall" option is an unfounded guess in the other direction.
