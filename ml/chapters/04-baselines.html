Baselines First

A score means nothing until you know what an obvious method scores. This chapter builds a ladder of baselines: a constant, a rule, then a linear model. It shows how to measure whether a more complex model is genuinely better. It also explains why skipping this step is one of the most common ways to waste months.

Why this matters

"Our model scores 0.81" is a sentence without content. Maybe a rule written in one line gets 0.79. Maybe the pipeline has a bug and the real number is 0.60. Maybe the model is excellent and the baseline was a straw man. A baseline is the cheapest way to turn a number into a judgement, and it protects you from your own enthusiasm. After this chapter you will be able to do four things. Build the first three rungs of a baseline ladder in an afternoon. Report improvement with an error bar. Run sanity checks that catch broken pipelines. Recognise when a published or internal result has been compared against a weak opponent. This chapter closes Part 1. Part 2 then builds the models themselves.

What a baseline is for

A baseline is a simple method, evaluated the same way as your real model, that sets the level any serious model must clear. It does three jobs at once.

  • It gives a number meaning. The same accuracy can be impressive or embarrassing depending on what trivial prediction scores. On a problem with 9.3% positives, a model that always says "no" is 90.7% accurate.
  • It tests your pipeline. If a carefully tuned model is no better than a constant, something upstream is wrong: labels, features, a join. If it is wildly better than every baseline, suspect leakage (chapter 14).
  • It prices complexity. Every extra component (more features, a bigger model, a longer pipeline) has a cost in time, compute, bugs and maintenance. The baseline tells you what the extra complexity bought.

The best baseline is often what the organisation does today without machine learning: the current rule, a spreadsheet heuristic, a human reviewer's judgement. A model that cannot beat the current process has no business case, however good its score.

The cost of weak baselines

Literature offers sobering evidence that comparisons are often made against opponents that were not tried hard. Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach (2019), in "Are We Really Making Much Progress?", tried to reproduce 18 neural recommendation algorithms published at top conferences. Only 7 could be reproduced with reasonable effort. Six of those 7 were often outperformed by simple, well-tuned heuristic methods such as nearest-neighbour and graph-based approaches. The seventh beat the baselines, but not consistently a tuned linear ranking method. Rendle, Zhang and Koren (2019), in "On the Difficulty of Evaluating Baselines", reported that baseline numbers on the MovieLens 10M benchmark were suboptimal in many papers over five years. A carefully configured plain matrix-factorisation baseline outperformed the reported results of every newly proposed method. On tabular data, Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022) benchmarked tree-based and deep models across 45 datasets. In their study, tree-based models remained state of the art on medium-sized data, around 10,000 rows. None of these says that new methods are useless. They say the claim "better than the baseline" is only as strong as the baseline's tuning. So tune your baselines as hard as your model.

The ladder of baselines

Climb in order, and stop to record the score on each rung.

Rung 1 is the dummy baseline: predict the most common class, or the mean (or median) of the target, ignoring the features entirely. Rung 2 uses one or two obvious features or an existing business rule. Rung 3 is a regularised linear model (chapter 5), which is quick to train, hard to break and gives coefficients you can read. Rung 4 is a gradient-boosted tree model with default settings (chapter 9). Only rung 5, tuning and engineering, is where most of the time usually goes, and it should be spent knowing the rungs below.

Analogy

A clinical trial compares a new drug against a placebo or the current treatment, never against nothing. A drug that "works" in the sense that patients got better tells you little if untreated patients got better at the same rate. The baseline is the control arm of your modelling experiment.

Where it breaks down: a trial's control arm is assigned at random from one population at one time. Your baselines are evaluated on the same test rows. So you can compare them paired, as shown below. This is more powerful, but you must take care to keep the evaluation identical.

Classification baselines, in numbers

We need a problem. The synthetic table has 6,000 customers with three features: tenure in months, support tickets in the last 90 days and monthly spend. Churn within 30 days has a real but modest dependence on them: longer tenure and higher spend lower the risk, tickets raise it. The first 4,200 customers train and the last 1,800 test; 168 of those 1,800 (9.3%) churn. The retention team can contact 100 customers, so the metrics are average precision, the area under the precision-recall curve, and hits in the top 100. Lift is hits divided by what you would expect from picking 100 customers at random (100 × 9.3% = 9.3 hits). Reasons for these metric choices are in chapter 2.

The shared helpers (data generator, average precision, a small gradient-descent logistic regression from NumPy) are in data04.py; the experiment imports them.

"""Shared synthetic churn data and helpers for the baseline experiments."""
import numpy as np

def make_churn(seed=21, n=6000):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    tenure = rng.exponential(24, n).clip(1, 72)                  # months as a customer
    tickets = rng.poisson(1.2, n)                                # support tickets in the last 90 days
    spend = rng.lognormal(3.5, 0.6, n)                           # monthly spend in pounds
    logit = -2.3 - 0.03 * tenure + 0.5 * tickets - 0.004 * spend
    y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))).astype(int)   # churn within 30 days
    return np.c_[tenure, tickets, spend], y

def average_precision(s, y):
    ys = y[np.argsort(-s, kind="mergesort")]
    return (np.cumsum(ys) / np.arange(1, len(ys) + 1))[ys == 1].mean()

def logistic_fit(X, y, steps=2000, lr=0.5):
    """Plain gradient-descent logistic regression on standardised features; returns a scoring function."""
    mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0); Z = (X - mu) / sd
    w, b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), np.log(y.mean() / (1 - y.mean()))
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(Z @ w + b)))
        w -= lr * Z.T @ (p - y) / len(y); b -= lr * (p - y).mean()
    return lambda Xn: ((Xn - mu) / sd) @ w + b

def top_k_hits(s, y, k):
    return int(y[np.argsort(-s, kind="mergesort")[:k]].sum())
"""Baselines for churn: 6,000 customers, a team that can contact 100 of the 1,800 in the test period."""
import numpy as np
from data04 import make_churn, average_precision, logistic_fit, top_k_hits

X, y = make_churn()
cut = int(0.7 * len(y))                                      # first 70% of customers train, rest test
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = X[:cut], X[cut:], y[:cut], y[cut:]
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
K, prev = 100, yte.mean()

scores = {
    "random scores": rng.random(len(yte)),
    "rule: 3+ tickets": (Xte[:, 1] >= 3) + 1e-6 * rng.random(len(yte)),   # tiny noise breaks ties
    "rule: shortest tenure": -Xte[:, 0],
    "logistic regression": logistic_fit(Xtr, ytr)(Xte),
}
print(f"test rows {len(yte)}, churners {yte.sum()} ({prev:.1%}); 'nobody churns' is {1 - prev:.1%} accurate but finds 0")
print(f"{'baseline':<24}{'avg precision':>15}{'hits in top ' + str(K):>17}{'lift':>7}")
print(f"{'constant (prevalence)':<24}{prev:>15.3f}{K * prev:>17.1f}{1.0:>7.1f}")
for name, s in scores.items():
    hits = top_k_hits(s, yte, K)
    print(f"{name:<24}{average_precision(s, yte):>15.3f}{hits:>17}{hits / (K * prev):>7.1f}")
print(f"perfect ranking would give {min(K, int(yte.sum()))} hits")
test rows 1800, churners 168 (9.3%); 'nobody churns' is 90.7% accurate but finds 0
baseline                  avg precision  hits in top 100   lift
constant (prevalence)             0.093              9.3    1.0
random scores                     0.100                9    1.0
rule: 3+ tickets                  0.154               25    2.7
rule: shortest tenure             0.121               16    1.7
logistic regression               0.246               34    3.6
perfect ranking would give 100 hits

Read down the rungs. The constant has no ranking ability: its average precision is the prevalence (0.093), and it finds 9.3 customers in 100 by definition. Random scores land within noise of that (0.100, 9 hits). The business rule "3 or more tickets" already reaches 0.154 average precision and 25 hits, a lift of 2.7. The shortest-tenure rule is weaker, at 0.121 and 16 hits. Logistic regression, using all three features, reaches 0.246, 34 hits and a lift of 3.6. Note the first line of the output as well: "nobody churns" is 90.7% accurate and finds none of the 168 churners. Accuracy cannot see the difference between this and a real model, which is why it appears as a sentence and not as a column.

Regression baselines, in numbers

The same ladder for a numeric target. The synthetic table has 4,000 house sales across 8 areas. Price depends on an area premium, floor size (about £2,200 per square metre) and age (−£600 per year), plus noise with a standard deviation (SD) of £30,000. We train on the first 3,000 and test on 1,000. R2 here is computed against the training mean, so it reads as "how much of the error of the mean baseline is removed".

"""Regression baselines on synthetic house prices: 4,000 sales across 8 areas."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 4000
area = rng.integers(0, 8, n)                                  # neighbourhood id
area_premium = np.array([-40, -20, -10, 0, 10, 25, 60, 120])  # thousands of pounds
floor_m2 = rng.normal(95, 30, n).clip(30, 250)
age = rng.integers(0, 100, n)
price = 150 + area_premium[area] + 2.2 * floor_m2 - 0.6 * age + rng.normal(0, 30, n)   # thousands of pounds

cut = int(0.75 * n)
tr, te = slice(0, cut), slice(cut, n)

def report(name, pred):
    err = pred - price[te]
    rmse, mae = np.sqrt(np.mean(err ** 2)), np.mean(np.abs(err))
    r2 = 1 - np.sum(err ** 2) / np.sum((price[te] - price[tr].mean()) ** 2)
    print(f"{name:<34}{rmse:>8.1f}{mae:>8.1f}{r2:>8.3f}")

print(f"{'baseline':<34}{'RMSE':>8}{'MAE':>8}{'R^2':>8}   (prices in thousands of pounds)")
report("global mean", np.full(n - cut, price[tr].mean()))
report("global median", np.full(n - cut, np.median(price[tr])))
area_mean = np.array([price[tr][area[tr] == a].mean() for a in range(8)])
report("mean price of the area", area_mean[area[te]])
report("price per m2 of the area x size", np.array([(price[tr][area[tr] == a] / floor_m2[tr][area[tr] == a]).mean() for a in range(8)])[area[te]] * floor_m2[te])
A = np.c_[np.ones(n), np.eye(8)[area][:, 1:], floor_m2, age]   # intercept, area dummies, size, age
w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A[tr], price[tr], rcond=None)
report("linear regression (all features)", A[te] @ w)
print(f"noise floor: the data were made with noise SD 30, so no model can beat RMSE of about 30")
baseline                              RMSE     MAE     R^2   (prices in thousands of pounds)
global mean                           89.0    71.0   0.000
global median                         89.1    70.9  -0.001
mean price of the area                76.2    60.1   0.266
price per m2 of the area x size       66.9    51.7   0.435
linear regression (all features)      32.3    25.6   0.869
noise floor: the data were made with noise SD 30, so no model can beat RMSE of about 30

The mean and the median are interchangeable, with a root mean squared error (RMSE) of about 89.0 and R2 = 0 by construction. Knowing only the area cuts RMSE to 76.2. Multiplying the area's typical price per square metre by the floor size gets 66.9, a sensible hand-made rule. A linear regression on all features reaches RMSE 32.3 and R2 = 0.869. We generated the data with noise SD 30, so we know the floor. No model can do much better than 30, and a boosted ensemble would have at most about 2 units to gain. In real data you do not know the noise floor. You can still estimate how much is left to gain by watching how quickly the ladder's gains shrink.

Is the improvement real?

Logistic regression scored 0.246 against 0.121 for the tenure rule. Is that gap bigger than the luck of this particular test set? Both models are scored on the same 1,800 rows, so we can compare them paired. Resample the test rows with replacement 2,000 times, which is the bootstrap. Recompute both scores on every resample. Then look at the spread of the difference. Pairing cancels the noise common to both. This gives a tighter interval than comparing two separate error bars. The bootstrap itself is introduced in Statistics for Experiments.

"""Is the improvement over the best simple rule real? Paired bootstrap on the same test rows."""
import numpy as np
from data04 import make_churn, average_precision, logistic_fit

X, y = make_churn()
cut = int(0.7 * len(y))
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = X[:cut], X[cut:], y[:cut], y[cut:]
model = logistic_fit(Xtr, ytr)(Xte)
rule = -Xte[:, 0]                                            # baseline: shortest tenure first

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
diffs = []
for _ in range(2000):
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(yte), len(yte))                # resample test rows with replacement
    if yte[idx].sum() == 0: continue
    diffs.append(average_precision(model[idx], yte[idx]) - average_precision(rule[idx], yte[idx]))
lo, hi = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"AP model {average_precision(model, yte):.3f}, AP rule {average_precision(rule, yte):.3f}, "
      f"difference {average_precision(model, yte) - average_precision(rule, yte):+.3f}")
print(f"95% bootstrap interval for the difference: {lo:+.3f} to {hi:+.3f}   share of resamples where model wins: {np.mean(np.array(diffs) > 0):.1%}")
AP model 0.246, AP rule 0.121, difference +0.124
95% bootstrap interval for the difference: +0.070 to +0.182   share of resamples where model wins: 100.0%

The 95% interval for the difference in average precision runs from +0.070 to +0.182. That excludes zero, and the model wins in every resample. That is a real improvement over this rule. If the interval had been −0.02 to +0.15, you would conclude only that this test set cannot tell them apart. You would then prefer the simpler one. Two further questions turn a statistical win into a decision. Does the gain matter? 34 hits against 16 in the top 100 is 18 more customers reached. Multiply by the value of a saved customer and the cost of the offer. Does it hold on the data you will see? A paired bootstrap says nothing about shift (chapter 3) or leakage.

Common misconception

"Baselines are for beginners; experts go straight to the best model." Experienced practitioners are the ones who insist on baselines. They have been burned by an unexplained jump. Going straight to a complex model means you cannot tell three things. You cannot tell whether a result is good, whether the pipeline is correct, or whether you needed the complexity. Studies of published comparisons (above) show that skipping or under-tuning baselines is common even in top venues.

Sanity baselines: checking the pipeline itself

Some baselines test the machinery rather than the problem. They take minutes and catch bugs that would otherwise poison weeks of work.

  • Shuffle the labels. Train on randomly permuted labels. A sound pipeline must fall to chance on held-out data. If the score stays high, information is leaking from somewhere other than the real labels. Examples are an ID, a row order, or a preprocessing step fitted on all data.
  • Overfit a tiny subset. A flexible model should be able to memorise 20 rows. If it cannot, the training code is broken (a frozen layer, a wrong target, a learning rate of zero).
  • One feature at a time. Score each feature alone. The strongest single feature is a baseline in itself, and a single feature that is near-perfect is a leak suspect.
  • Remove the signal. Train with only the ID column or only row index. Anything above chance means the split or the ordering carries information.

The first check, run on the churn data:

"""Sanity baseline: with shuffled labels a sound pipeline must fall to chance."""
import numpy as np
from data04 import make_churn, average_precision, logistic_fit

X, y = make_churn()
cut = int(0.7 * len(y))
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
print(f"test prevalence (chance level for average precision): {y[cut:].mean():.3f}")
print(f"{'labels':<26}{'train AP':>10}{'test AP':>10}")
for name, labels in [("real labels", y), ("training labels shuffled", np.r_[rng.permutation(y[:cut]), y[cut:]])]:
    f = logistic_fit(X[:cut], labels[:cut])
    print(f"{name:<26}{average_precision(f(X[:cut]), labels[:cut]):>10.3f}{average_precision(f(X[cut:]), y[cut:]):>10.3f}")
test prevalence (chance level for average precision): 0.093
labels                      train AP   test AP
real labels                    0.214     0.246
training labels shuffled       0.090     0.103

With real labels the model reaches a test average precision of 0.246. With the training labels shuffled, the test score falls to 0.103, within noise of the 0.093 prevalence, which is what chance looks like. (Train average precision is lower than test with real labels, 0.214 against 0.246. At this size such differences are sampling noise, a reminder not to over-read single numbers.) If the shuffled run had given 0.20, we would have a leak to find. We would need to find it before trusting any result.

Baselines beyond tables

The idea transfers to every kind of model. For text classification, a keyword rule or logistic regression on TF-IDF weights is the baseline for a transformer. TF-IDF combines term frequency (TF) with inverse document frequency (IDF), so that rare words count for more. For forecasting, "the same as last week" and "the average of the last four same-weekdays" are baselines that are surprisingly hard to beat (chapter 18). For retrieval, BM25 (a keyword-matching score) is the baseline for any embedding model (Inverted Indexes & BM25). For an LLM application, the baseline is a plain prompt with no retrieval or tools. Compare them on the same labelled cases (Assertions & Eval Harnesses). In each case the question is the same: what does the elaborate system buy over the simple one, on the cases you care about?

Systems connection

Engineers already practise this under other names. A canary release compares a new version with the running one on live traffic. A shadow deployment runs the candidate beside the incumbent and logs the difference without affecting users. A model's offline baseline is the first rung of that same comparison, before any traffic is exposed. The advice to prefer the simplest thing that works also applies to operations. A rule has no training pipeline, no drift, and a one-line rollback. A learned model brings extra machinery that a rule does not.

Key takeaways

  • A baseline turns a score into a judgement, tests the pipeline and prices complexity. Build them before the model you care about.
  • Climb the ladder: constant, rule (or current process), linear model, default boosted trees, then tuning. Record the score on each rung.
  • On the churn data, a constant gave 0.093 average precision, a one-line rule 0.154, logistic regression 0.246. "Nobody churns" is 90.7% accurate and useless.
  • On house prices, a linear model reached RMSE 32.3 against a noise floor of about 30, leaving almost nothing for a complex model to win.
  • Compare models paired, with a bootstrap interval. The churn model's gain over the tenure rule was +0.070 to +0.182 in average precision, so it was real.
  • Sanity baselines (shuffled labels, tiny-subset overfitting, single features) test the pipeline. With shuffled labels the test score fell to chance, at 0.103 against 0.093 prevalence.
  • Comparisons are only as strong as the baseline's tuning, and published and internal comparisons are often weaker than they look.

Check your understanding

A fraud model scores 99.2% accuracy on a test set where 0.8% of transactions are fraud. What is the right reaction?

  1. Ship it: 99.2% is excellent.
  2. Ship it, but tune the threshold first.
  3. Compare against the constant "no fraud" baseline, which is also 99.2% accurate, and use recall, precision or cost to judge the model.
  4. Reject it: accuracy above 99% is always a sign of leakage.
The majority-class baseline scores 99.2% here, so accuracy shows no skill at all. The fix is a comparison in metrics that expose ranking and detection ability. Option 4 is too strong: high accuracy can be innocent, as here, only because the problem is imbalanced.

You shuffle the training labels, retrain the whole pipeline, and the test score is far above chance. What does that indicate?

  1. A leak or bug: the pipeline gets information from somewhere other than the training labels. Examples are an ID, row order, or preprocessing fitted on all data.
  2. The model is excellent at generalising.
  3. The labels were already random.
  4. The test set is too small to evaluate.
With shuffled training labels there is nothing real to learn, so a sound pipeline falls to chance. Anything above chance means information is flowing in another way. Option 3 would make real-label scores poor too, and test size affects noise, not a systematic gap above chance.

A new model has average precision 0.30 and the incumbent rule 0.27 on the same 2,000 test rows. A paired bootstrap gives a 95% interval of −0.02 to +0.08 for the difference. What should you conclude?

  1. The model is better, since 0.30 exceeds 0.27.
  2. The model is worse, since part of the interval is negative.
  3. The two are certainly identical.
  4. The test set cannot distinguish them with confidence; prefer the simpler option unless more data or other considerations favour the model.
The interval includes zero, so a difference of this size could be sampling luck. That is not proof of equality (option 3) nor of inferiority (option 2); it means the evidence is inconclusive. Option 1 treats a point estimate as certain.

A house-price linear model has RMSE 32 on data whose irreducible noise has standard deviation about 30. A colleague proposes a much larger model. What is a sensible response?

  1. Go ahead: bigger models always lower error.
  2. There is little left to gain: RMSE cannot go meaningfully below the noise floor, so the cost of complexity is hard to justify.
  3. The linear model must be overfit.
  4. RMSE cannot be compared with noise.
When a simple model is near the noise floor, additional capacity can only fit noise. In real data you rarely know the floor exactly, but diminishing gains up the ladder carry the same message. Option 3 confuses a good fit with overfitting; check the train-validation gap instead.

A paper reports that a neural method beats a baseline by 3 points. What is the most important question to ask first?

  1. How many layers does the network have?
  2. Which framework was used?
  3. How thoroughly was the baseline tuned and evaluated under the same protocol, and does the gap exceed the noise?
  4. Was the code released under an open licence?
Studies of recommender-system papers found untuned or weakly configured baselines were common, and well-tuned simple methods often matched or beat the new methods. Architecture details and licence are secondary to whether the comparison was fair.

On the churn problem the rule "3 or more tickets" has lift 2.7 in the top 100. What does that number mean?

  1. It is 2.7% accurate.
  2. Among the 100 customers it ranks highest, it finds 2.7 times as many churners as picking 100 customers at random would.
  3. It reduces churn by 2.7 times.
  4. Its area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is 2.7.
Lift compares hits in the selected group against the number expected by chance (here 25 against 9.3). It is not a causal effect on churn, since contacting those customers is a separate action, and an AUC cannot exceed 1.
