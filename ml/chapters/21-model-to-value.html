From Model to Value

A model with a fine offline score has done nothing yet. Value appears when its predictions change a decision and the decision changes an outcome, and it lasts only while the world still resembles the training data. This chapter connects offline metrics to business results, shows how to test a model in production with an A/B experiment, and explains monitoring, drift and retraining, with a simulation for each.

Why this matters

Most machine-learning projects that fail do so after the notebook succeeds: the metric was not what the business needed, the model was never tested against the status quo, the inputs changed quietly, or nobody noticed it stopped working. This chapter closes the course by following a model out of the notebook. You will be able to say what an offline number does and does not promise, design and read a basic A/B test, choose what to monitor and why a drift alarm and a performance alarm are different things, and set up retraining as a controlled release. It leans on the validation of chapter 13, the calibration of chapter 15 and the engineering of service-level objectives and deployment strategies.

The chain from score to outcome

A deployed model is one link in a chain. Data trains a model; the model produces a score; a decision rule (a threshold, a budget, a top-k list) turns scores into actions; the actions have effects; the effects are the outcome the business cares about. Offline metrics such as AUC, log loss or MASE evaluate only the first half. The second half, what the decisions change in the world, can fail even when the first is excellent.

Three gaps open between the metric and the value.

  1. The metric is not the objective. Chapter 2 argued for metrics that match the cost of errors. In practice the offline metric is a proxy: AUC measures ranking over all thresholds, while the business operates at one threshold with one review capacity.
  2. Prediction is not intervention. A model that predicts who will leave tells you nothing about whom a retention offer will keep. The two groups overlap but are not the same, as the next experiment shows.
  3. The world responds. Fraudsters adapt, customers game scores, and the model's own decisions change which data you see next: you only learn whether an approved loan defaults, never whether a rejected one would have. This is the selective labels problem (Lakkaraju et al., 2017) and one form of feedback loop.

Predicting churn is not retaining customers

A subscription business has a budget of 2,000 retention offers among 20,000 customers. An offer costs 5, and a customer who stays is worth 100. Suppose we have the best possible churn model: it knows each customer's true churn probability (an AUC of 0.798 against actual outcomes, which no real model can beat). The simulation also needs an assumption about what the offer does, and it is only an assumption: the offer helps undecided customers, does nothing for customers who have already decided to go, and slightly annoys loyal ones. It then compares sending offers to the 2,000 highest-risk customers with sending them to the 2,000 whose churn probability the offer would reduce most (the ranking an uplift model tries to estimate), and with 2,000 random customers.

"""A good churn model, a bad retention policy: ranking by churn risk versus ranking by what the offer changes."""
import numpy as np
from lib import roc_auc, sigmoid

rng = np.random.default_rng(31)
N, BUDGET, VALUE, COST = 20000, 2000, 100.0, 5.0            # customers, offers we can send, value of a kept customer, cost of one offer
x = rng.normal(size=(N, 3))
p0 = sigmoid(-1.4 + 1.2 * x[:, 0] + 0.6 * x[:, 1])           # churn probability if we do nothing
# What the offer does to churn probability: it helps customers who are undecided (x2 high), does nothing for the already-gone,
# and slightly annoys the loyal (x0 very low), who might have stayed anyway.
effect = -0.30 * sigmoid(2.5 * x[:, 2]) * (1 - p0) * (p0 > 0.12) * np.minimum(1, p0 / 0.35) + 0.04 * (p0 < 0.12)
churned = rng.random(N) < p0
print(f"{N} customers, average churn probability {p0.mean():.1%}; offers: {BUDGET}, cost {COST:.0f} each, a kept customer is worth {VALUE:.0f}")
print(f"AUC of the true churn probability against churn outcomes: {roc_auc(p0, churned.astype(int)):.3f} (the true probability: no risk model can do better)")

def value(selected):          # expected net value of sending the offer to `selected`: customers kept, minus offer costs
    return float(np.sum(-effect[selected] * VALUE - COST))
risk_pick = np.argsort(-p0)[:BUDGET]
uplift_pick = np.argsort(effect)[:BUDGET]                     # most negative effect = most churn avoided
random_pick = rng.choice(N, BUDGET, replace=False)
print(f"\n{'policy':<34}{'expected customers kept':>25}{'net value':>12}")
for name, sel in (("target the highest churn risk", risk_pick), ("target the largest effect of the offer", uplift_pick), ("random customers", random_pick)):
    print(f"{name:<34}{-effect[sel].sum():>25.1f}{value(sel):>12.0f}")
print(f"\nmean churn probability of the risk-ranked group: {p0[risk_pick].mean():.2f}; mean effect of the offer on them: {effect[risk_pick].mean():+.3f}")
print(f"mean churn probability of the effect-ranked group: {p0[uplift_pick].mean():.2f}; mean effect of the offer on them: {effect[uplift_pick].mean():+.3f}")

Targeting the highest-risk customers keeps about 88 customers and loses money (net −1,211): their churn probability averages 0.70, but the offer changes it by only 4.4 points because many have already decided. Ranking by the offer's effect keeps about 319 customers for a net gain of about 21,900, from customers whose own risk is only 0.34 but who respond. Random choice does worse than doing nothing. The best predictor in the world lost because it answered the wrong question. The lesson is not that churn models are useless (they are useful for forecasting revenue and for choosing whom to monitor) but that a decision needs an estimate of the effect of the action, and the only reliable way to get one is an experiment. Uplift modelling (reviewed by Gutierrez and Gérardy, 2017) estimates effects from experimental data; it is a causal problem and needs randomised treatment data to train.

Common misconception

"If the new model's AUC is higher, the business metric will improve." Sometimes, but nothing guarantees it. The metric measures ranking on historical data; the business metric depends on a decision rule, on how people react, and on conditions that differ from the historical ones. A higher AUC is a reason to test the model, not evidence that it is worth shipping.

Testing in production: the A/B test

The reliable way to learn what a model's decisions do is an A/B test, also called a randomised controlled experiment. Users (or accounts, or stores) are assigned at random to the current system (control) or the new one (treatment), and a pre-chosen outcome is compared. Randomisation is what makes the comparison fair: any other difference between the groups, known or unknown, is chance, and statistics can say how much chance to expect. The statistics are those of A0 chapter 15 (confidence intervals, the bootstrap and power) and the experimental practice is the subject of Kohavi, Tang and Xu's book Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020). The ingredients to decide before you start:

Unit of randomisation
What gets assigned: a user, a session, a store, a day. Pick the unit at which effects do not spill over (users who see each other's outcomes need grouped assignment).
Primary metric
One metric that defines success, chosen in advance (revenue per user, retained customers), usually closer to value than the offline metric.
Guardrail metrics
Metrics that must not get worse: latency, complaints, error rate, a fairness measure. A treatment that wins on the primary metric and breaks a guardrail does not ship.
Minimum detectable effect and power
The smallest improvement worth detecting, and the probability (power, commonly 80%) of detecting it if real. They fix the sample size.
Duration
Long enough to reach the sample size and to cover weekly cycles and any novelty effect (a new feature attracts curiosity clicks at first).

Three pieces of arithmetic come up constantly, and the script runs all of them. Sample size: to detect a lift in conversion from 5.00% to 5.25% with 80% power at the 5% significance level you need about 2(zα/2 + zβ)2 p(1 − p) / δ2 users per arm, where δ is the absolute lift. Peeking: checking the result every day and stopping at the first significant one inflates false positives far beyond 5%; the script simulates 4,000 A/A tests (no true difference) over 20 days. Sample ratio mismatch (SRM): if the split was meant to be 50/50 and the observed counts are not, something in the assignment or logging is broken and the result cannot be trusted, however good it looks.

"""A/B testing arithmetic: sample size, the cost of peeking, and a sample-ratio check."""
import math
import numpy as np

def norm_p_two_sided(z): return math.erfc(abs(z) / math.sqrt(2))

# 1. How many users per arm to detect a lift from 5.00% to 5.25% conversion with 80% power at the 5% level?
p, delta, z_a, z_b = 0.05, 0.0025, 1.96, 0.8416
n_arm = 2 * (z_a + z_b) ** 2 * p * (1 - p) / delta ** 2
print(f"sample size: {n_arm:,.0f} users per arm to detect +{delta * 100:.2f} points on a {p:.0%} base (80% power, alpha 0.05)")
print(f"  at 10,000 users a day split two ways that is {n_arm / 5000:.0f} days of traffic")

# 2. Peeking: an A/A test (no true difference) checked every day, stopping at the first p < 0.05.
rng = np.random.default_rng(32)
DAYS, PER_DAY, RUNS = 20, 1000, 4000                          # users per arm per day
a = rng.binomial(PER_DAY, p, (RUNS, DAYS)).cumsum(1); b = rng.binomial(PER_DAY, p, (RUNS, DAYS)).cumsum(1)
n = PER_DAY * np.arange(1, DAYS + 1)
pool = (a + b) / (2 * n); se = np.sqrt(2 * pool * (1 - pool) / n)
z = (a / n - b / n) / se
sig = np.abs(z) > 1.96
print(f"\nA/A test, {RUNS} runs, {DAYS} days, check once at the end: false positives {sig[:, -1].mean():.1%}")
print(f"A/A test, check every day and stop at the first p < 0.05: false positives {sig.any(1).mean():.1%}")

# 3. Sample-ratio mismatch: the split was meant to be 50/50.
for ctl, trt in ((500_000, 501_000), (500_000, 507_000)):
    tot = ctl + trt; z_srm = (ctl - tot / 2) / math.sqrt(tot / 4)
    print(f"\ncontrol {ctl:,}, treatment {trt:,}: p-value for 'the split really was 50/50' = {norm_p_two_sided(z_srm):.2g}")
sample size: 119,304 users per arm to detect +0.25 points on a 5% base (80% power, alpha 0.05)
  at 10,000 users a day split two ways that is 24 days of traffic

A/A test, 4000 runs, 20 days, check once at the end: false positives 5.2%
A/A test, check every day and stop at the first p < 0.05: false positives 24.7%

control 500,000, treatment 501,000: p-value for 'the split really was 50/50' = 0.32

Detecting a lift of a quarter of a percentage point needs 119,304 users per arm: about 24 days at 10,000 users a day. Small effects need large samples, because the required size grows with the inverse square of the effect. The peeking simulation is the more important result. A single check at the end of the 20 days produces false positives at about the nominal rate (5.2% of 4,000 runs), while checking daily and stopping at the first p < 0.05 produces them in 24.7% of runs, about one in four. Fix the sample size in advance and analyse once, or use a method designed for continuous monitoring (sequential tests). The third block shows the sample-ratio check: 500,000 against 501,000 is unremarkable (p = 0.32), but 500,000 against 507,000, a 0.7% imbalance, has a p-value of about 3 × 10−12. Such a gap means a bug (a crash on one arm, bots filtered differently, a redirect that loses users), and the metric comparison should be discarded until the cause is found. Kohavi and colleagues treat a very small SRM p-value as an alarm on its own.

Not everything can be randomised. Regulators may forbid it, the number of units may be tiny (a handful of warehouses), or the harm of a wrong decision may be too great for live testing. Then use a staged route: shadow mode, where the model scores live traffic silently and its outputs are compared with what actually happened; a canary release to a small share of traffic with guardrails watched (deployment strategies); and, where an experiment is impossible, careful before-and-after or matched comparisons with explicit caveats. Each step is weaker evidence than a randomised test, and the write-up should say so.

Analogy: the wind tunnel and the road

An aircraft design is tested in a wind tunnel first: cheap, fast, repeatable, and good at ranking designs. Nobody certifies a plane from the tunnel alone, because on the road (or in the air) there are gusts, passengers and maintenance that the tunnel left out. Offline evaluation is the wind tunnel; an A/B test is the flight test.

Where it breaks down: a wind tunnel's physics does not change while you test. The world a model is deployed into responds to the model and drifts away from the data it was tested on, so even a passed flight test has a shelf life; hence monitoring.

What to monitor, and what drift means

After launch, the model faces data that keeps changing. The general term is dataset shift (Quiñonero-Candela et al., 2009), and it comes in kinds that need different responses.

Covariate shift (input drift)

The distribution of the inputs P(x) changes while the relationship P(y | x) stays the same: a new customer mix, a new sensor. The model may be fine, or may be asked about regions of input space it never saw.

Prior (label) shift

The share of each class changes: fraud triples in a month, so probabilities are too low although the model still ranks well (chapter 15 covers the adjustment).

Concept drift

The relationship P(y | x) itself changes: a competitor alters what makes a customer leave; a fraud ring adopts a new method. The model is wrong in a way no input statistic reveals. Gama et al. (2014) survey the field.

Monitoring has four layers, in order of how quickly each can fire. Data quality (schema, nulls, ranges, freshness) catches broken pipelines within minutes and has no labels to wait for. Input and score distributions catch drift: the population stability index (PSI), which sums (current share − reference share) × ln(current share / reference share) over bins of the reference distribution, or a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, or the adversarial classifier of chapter 14. The common rules of thumb (PSI below 0.1 is stable, above 0.25 a major shift) come from credit-scoring practice; they are conventions, not tests. Outcome metrics with labels (accuracy, calibration, MASE) are the truth but arrive late: a loan's default takes months, a churn label a billing cycle. Business metrics (conversion, losses, complaints) say whether the whole chain still delivers value. Alert on symptoms in terms users feel, following alerting practice, and page a person only when action is needed.

The simulation separates the first two layers from the third. A logistic model is trained on weeks 0 to 3 of a stream with 4,000 rows a week, then frozen. In scenario A (input drift) from week 12 feature 1 shifts by 1.5 standard deviations and the rule linking features to the label stays the same. In scenario B (concept drift) the inputs do not change at all, but the coefficient of feature 2 slides from +1 to −1 over five weeks. The script prints the PSI of feature 1, the PSI of the model's score and the weekly AUC, then refits on four recent weeks.

"""Monitoring a deployed model: input drift versus concept drift, seen through PSI and through weekly AUC."""
import numpy as np
from lib import fit_logreg, predict_proba, roc_auc, sigmoid

W, PER, SHIFT = 26, 4000, 12                                  # weeks, rows per week, week the world changes
def week(w, scenario, rng):
    x = rng.normal(size=(PER, 3))
    if scenario == "input drift" and w >= SHIFT: x[:, 0] += 1.5          # customers change: feature 1 moves, the rule stays the same
    c2 = 1.0 if not (scenario == "concept drift" and w >= SHIFT) else 1.0 - 2.0 * min(1, (w - SHIFT + 1) / 5)   # the rule changes: feature 2 reverses
    y = (rng.random(PER) < sigmoid(-0.5 + 1.0 * x[:, 0] + c2 * x[:, 1] + 0.5 * x[:, 2])).astype(int)
    return x, y

def psi(ref, cur, bins=10):
    """Population stability index between a reference and a current sample, using the reference's decile edges."""
    edges = np.quantile(ref, np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1)[1:-1])
    r = np.bincount(np.searchsorted(edges, ref), minlength=bins) / len(ref) + 1e-4
    c = np.bincount(np.searchsorted(edges, cur), minlength=bins) / len(cur) + 1e-4
    return float(np.sum((c - r) * np.log(c / r)))

for scenario in ("input drift", "concept drift"):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(33)
    Xt, yt = zip(*[week(w, "none", rng) for w in range(4)]); Xt, yt = np.vstack(Xt), np.concatenate(yt)       # train on weeks 0-3
    beta = fit_logreg(Xt, yt, l2=1.0); ref_x1, ref_score = Xt[:, 0], predict_proba(beta, Xt)
    print(f"\nscenario: {scenario} from week {SHIFT}    (model fixed after training on weeks 0 to 3)")
    print(f"{'week':>5}{'PSI feature 1':>15}{'PSI score':>11}{'weekly AUC':>12}")
    for w in range(4, W, 2):
        x, y = week(w, scenario, rng); s = predict_proba(beta, x)
        flag = "  <- PSI above 0.25" if psi(ref_x1, x[:, 0]) > 0.25 else ""
        print(f"{w:>5}{psi(ref_x1, x[:, 0]):>15.3f}{psi(ref_score, s):>11.3f}{roc_auc(s, y):>12.3f}{flag}")
    # the response: refit on the four most recent labelled weeks (18 to 21) and score the weeks after them
    rng2 = np.random.default_rng(34)
    Xr, yr = zip(*[week(w, scenario, rng2) for w in range(18, 22)]); beta2 = fit_logreg(np.vstack(Xr), np.concatenate(yr), l2=1.0)
    xe, ye = week(24, scenario, rng2)
    print(f"  week 24 AUC: old model {roc_auc(predict_proba(beta, xe), ye):.3f}, model refit on weeks 18 to 21 {roc_auc(predict_proba(beta2, xe), ye):.3f}")

The two scenarios behave oppositely. With input drift the PSI of feature 1 goes from about 0.002 to 1.9, far over the 0.25 rule of thumb, and the score's PSI goes to about 0.9, while the weekly AUC stays between 0.80 and 0.82: the model is simply asked about a shifted population and its rule still holds. An alarm on drift fires, and the right response is to look, not to retrain in panic. With concept drift the PSI of feature 1 and of the score stays below 0.01 for the whole run, nothing in the inputs looks different, and the AUC falls from 0.81 to about 0.52, close to chance. Only a metric that uses labels sees it. The last line of each scenario shows the remedy where one is needed: refitting on four recent weeks restores the AUC to 0.816 in the concept-drift case (from 0.520), and changes nothing (0.827 against 0.826) in the input-drift case.

Two practical consequences. First, because labels are delayed, build proxies that arrive sooner (early-payment behaviour for a default model, a short-horizon outcome for a long one), and sample cases for human labelling. Second, monitor the model's own decisions: the share of cases flagged, the average score and the calibration. A flag rate that halves overnight is a symptom whatever its cause. For monitoring tools see the toolbox entry on drift monitoring; the idea matters more than the product.

Retraining as a release process

Models decay. The case studies in this course show it: Google Flu Trends drifted as search behaviour changed (Case 2), and Zillow's price forecasts met a turning market (Case 3). The remedy is to retrain, but retraining is itself a change to production and can break things. The loop treats it that way.

The decisions inside the loop are these.

  • What triggers a retrain? A schedule (weekly, monthly) is simple and predictable; a drift or performance trigger is responsive but noisy. Many teams use a schedule with a performance alarm as a backstop. The simulation shows why drift alone is a weak trigger: PSI fired in the case where nothing needed fixing and stayed silent in the case where everything did.
  • Which data? Recent data represents the present; old data adds volume. A sliding window forgets old regimes, an expanding one keeps them. Choose by backtest (chapter 18), and if the world changed abruptly, weight recent rows more.
  • Is the challenger better? Compare it with the current champion on a time-ordered backtest, with the same features and the same metric, and check calibration (chapter 15) because a retrained model may rank better but shift its probabilities and invalidate a threshold. Then compare on live traffic in shadow or canary, and use an A/B test when the decision is consequential.
  • Can we go back? Keep the previous champion deployable, version the data, code and parameters of every model, and rehearse the rollback.
  • Beware of feedback. If the model's decisions shape the training data (only approved applicants have outcomes), retraining on that data reinforces the model's past choices. Reserve a small random exploration slice, or use methods that correct for selection.

All of this is machinery around a model, and the machinery is where much of the cost lives. Sculley et al. (2015), "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems", describe how a small amount of model code sits in a large system of data collection, feature extraction, configuration, serving and monitoring, and how entanglement, undeclared consumers of a model's output and feedback loops accumulate maintenance debt. Budget for it when you decide to ship a model, not afterwards.

Systems connection

Everything in this section is reliability engineering applied to models. A model has an SLO (a calibration or accuracy target as well as latency), alerts that should page on symptoms, and a deployment path with canaries and fast rollback. Key takeaways

  • A model is one link in a chain from data to outcome. Offline metrics measure the model; only an experiment measures what its decisions change.
  • Prediction is not intervention: with the best possible churn model, targeting by risk lost 1,211 while targeting by the offer's effect gained about 21,900 (under the stated assumptions).
  • A/B tests need a metric, guardrails and a sample size fixed in advance (119,304 users per arm for a 0.25-point lift on a 5% base). Peeking daily inflated false positives from 5.2% to 24.7%, and a sample-ratio mismatch invalidates a result.
  • Drift comes in kinds. Input drift moved PSI to about 1.9 with AUC steady near 0.81; concept drift left PSI under 0.01 and cut AUC to about 0.52. Alarm on drift to investigate, on labelled performance and business metrics to act.
  • Monitor in layers: data quality, input and score distributions, labelled outcomes (late) and business metrics, plus the model's own decision rates.
  • Retraining is a release: trigger, challenger, offline backtest, shadow or canary, promotion and a ready rollback, with attention to feedback loops.
  • The model is a small part of the system. Budget for the surrounding data, serving and monitoring work before you promise value.

Check your understanding

A new fraud model has a higher AUC than the old one offline, but in a two-week A/B test the loss per transaction is unchanged. Which explanation is most plausible?

  1. The A/B test must be wrong, because AUC is the more rigorous measure.
  2. The new model is worse and the AUC is a coincidence.
  3. AUC measures ranking over all thresholds, while the business acts at one threshold and one review capacity; the improved ranking may not change who is actually blocked or reviewed in the region that matters.
  4. Offline and online metrics always agree.
An AUC gain can come from parts of the ranking that the operating threshold never reaches, and the outcome also depends on what happens after the flag. The A/B test measures the outcome directly and should be believed unless it fails its validity checks. There is no reason to call the offline result a coincidence, and the two kinds of metric do not always agree.

You peek at an A/B test every day for three weeks and stop when a daily z-test first shows p < 0.05. What is the effect?

  1. The probability of a false positive is well above 5%, since each look is another chance for noise to cross the threshold.
  2. The false-positive rate stays at 5% because each test is valid on its own.
  3. The test has more power with no downside.
  4. The effect size estimate becomes more accurate.
Each look at accumulating data is another draw. In the simulation, 20 daily looks gave false positives in 24.7% of A/A runs against 5.2% for a single final look. Each test is valid on its own, but the stopping rule makes the overall error rate far larger, and early stops also exaggerate the estimated effect.

A model's weekly AUC drops from 0.81 to 0.55 while the PSI of every input feature and of the score stays near zero. What is the likeliest cause?

  1. Covariate shift in a feature.
  2. A data pipeline failure that nulls out features.
  3. Label noise introduced by the metric.
  4. Concept drift: the relationship between the features and the outcome changed, which input distributions cannot reveal.
A pipeline failure would show up as shifted or missing inputs (large PSI and data-quality alerts), and covariate shift would also move the PSI. With stable inputs and a collapsing metric, the mapping from inputs to outcomes is the thing that changed. Retraining on recent labelled data is the usual response.

Why can a churn-risk model, even a perfect one, lose money when used to choose whom to send a retention offer?

  1. Churn models cannot be perfect.
  2. The customers most likely to churn may be the ones the offer cannot persuade, so ranking by risk is not ranking by the effect of the action.
  3. Offers always cost more than customers are worth.
  4. Retention offers should be sent at random.
Risk answers "who will leave", while the decision needs "whose behaviour will the offer change". In the simulation the highest-risk group averaged a churn probability of 0.70 but the offer moved it by only 4.4 points; random choice did even worse. Offers can be profitable when targeted by estimated effect, which is not the same as random targeting.

An experiment intended as a 50/50 split records 500,000 users in control and 507,000 in treatment, and treatment shows a 2% lift in revenue. What should you do?

  1. Ship the treatment; the lift is positive.
  2. Discard the extra 7,000 treatment users and recompute.
  3. Treat the result as untrustworthy until the cause of the sample-ratio mismatch (p about 3 × 10−12) is found and fixed.
  4. Rerun the analysis with a different metric.
An imbalance this unlikely under random assignment means assignment or logging is broken, and whatever broke it can also bias the metric. Trimming users to rebalance the numbers hides the symptom without removing the cause, and switching metrics is a fishing expedition.

Which retraining strategy is most defensible for a model that decides loan approvals?

  1. Retrain nightly on all newly approved loans and deploy automatically.
  2. Retrain on a schedule, backtest the challenger against the champion, canary it, keep a rollback path, and keep a small random slice so that rejected cases' outcomes can also be learned.
  3. Never retrain, to keep the model stable.
  4. Retrain only when PSI exceeds 0.25.
Automatic deployment without a challenger comparison risks promoting a worse model, and retraining only on approved loans bakes in selective labels. A drift-only trigger misses concept drift (the simulation's second scenario), and never retraining lets the model decay. The staged, reversible release with some exploration addresses these.
