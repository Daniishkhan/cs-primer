How Competitions Are Won

Prediction competitions are the one place where thousands of people attack the same problem and the winners tell you what they did. The pattern is consistent: a validation scheme they trust more than the leaderboard, features that carry domain insight, a boosted-tree backbone, and an ensemble of diverse models blended through out-of-fold predictions. This chapter explains why each part works, reproduces the statistical effects behind them, and says which habits transfer to real work and which do not.

Why this matters

Competitions compress the whole craft of this course into a fixed dataset, a fixed metric and a hidden test set, so they are an unusually clear mirror of what works. They also teach a skill that product work needs and rarely rewards: knowing whether a number can be trusted. After this chapter you will be able to explain why the public leaderboard of a competition misleads, quantify how much, build a stacked ensemble whose meta-model learns from honest predictions, and judge whether an extra point of accuracy from a 50-model blend is worth shipping. The earlier chapters on validation, leakage and features are the foundations; this one adds the last layer on top.

Running the examples

The setting, and how it differs from real work

In a typical competition (Kaggle is the best-known host) the organiser publishes a training set with labels and a test set without. Participants submit predictions for the test set and receive a score. The test set is split in secret: the public leaderboard shows scores on one part (say 30% of the test rows) while the private leaderboard, computed on the rest, is revealed only when the competition closes and decides the final ranking. Participants can submit several times a day, so the public score is a feedback signal that is tempting to climb.

That setting differs from deployment in ways that decide what transfers. The data is clean and fixed, the metric is given, and nobody has to maintain, serve, monitor or explain the winning model, or ask whether the metric was the right one. What transfers is the discipline of validation and the way of building strong, diverse predictors. What does not is the willingness to spend 50 models and 20 hours of compute on the fourth decimal place, a point we return to under deployability.

Trust your validation, not the leaderboard

The first rule winners give, in nearly every write-up, is to build a local validation scheme that mirrors the test split and to trust it over the public leaderboard. The leaderboard is a noisy measurement of a small sample, and climbing it is a form of repeated testing. The following simulation puts numbers on this. There are 1,000 teams whose true accuracies are spread as a normal distribution with mean 0.80 and standard deviation 0.01, so the best team is about 3 percentage points above the average. Each team's public score is its accuracy measured on n public rows (a binomial draw), and its private score the same measurement on 20,000 private rows. For each public size we repeat the competition 300 times and report the optimism of the public leader's score, the private rank of that team, and how often the eventual private winner was in the public top 10.

"""The leaderboard shake-up: how much does the public score of the best submission overstate it, and how often does the public winner stay on top?"""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(22)
TEAMS, PRIVATE_N, REPEATS = 1000, 20000, 300
print(f"{TEAMS} teams whose true accuracies are spread as N(0.800, 0.010); private test set {PRIVATE_N} rows")
print(f"{'public rows':>12}{'public best minus its true':>28}{'private rank of public #1':>27}{'public top-10 holds private #1':>32}")
for n_pub in (200, 1000, 5000, 20000):
    opt, rank1, hit = [], [], 0
    for _ in range(REPEATS):
        true = rng.normal(0.80, 0.01, TEAMS)
        pub = rng.binomial(n_pub, true) / n_pub + rng.random(TEAMS) * 1e-9            # tiny jitter breaks ties
        prv = rng.binomial(PRIVATE_N, true) / PRIVATE_N
        top = pub.argmax(); opt.append(pub[top] - true[top])
        rank1.append(1 + (prv > prv[top]).sum())
        hit += prv.argmax() in np.argsort(-pub)[:10]
    print(f"{n_pub:>12}{np.mean(opt):>28.4f}{np.median(rank1):>27.0f}{hit / REPEATS:>32.0%}")
1000 teams whose true accuracies are spread as N(0.800, 0.010); private test set 20000 rows
 public rows  public best minus its true  private rank of public #1  public top-10 holds private #1
         200                      0.0815                        170                              9%
        1000                      0.0296                         24                             32%
        5000                      0.0084                          4                             77%
       20000                      0.0024                          2                             90%

With a public set of 200 rows the team in first place on the public board scores on average 8.2 percentage points above its true accuracy, a pure artefact of picking the maximum among 1,000 noisy measurements, and finishes around 170th on the private board; the private winner was in the public top ten only 9% of the time. With 1,000 public rows the leader is still 3.0 points too optimistic and finishes about 24th. Even with 20,000 public rows (as many as the private set) the maximum is inflated by 0.24 points and the eventual winner is in the public top ten 90% of the time, not always. This is the winner's curse of chapter 13 on a grand scale: the more candidates you score on the same data, the more the best score overstates the best candidate.

Real leaderboards are, in one respect, kinder than this model, because participants' submissions are strongly correlated and each team does not submit independently random noise. Roelofs, Fridovich-Keil, Miller, Shankar, Hardt, Recht and Schmidt (2019), "A Meta-Analysis of Overfitting in Machine Learning", examined over a hundred Kaggle competitions and found little evidence of substantial adaptive overfitting to the public set: rankings on the public and private boards agreed more than the worst-case argument predicts. Their finding does not remove the simulation's lesson; it says that large, well-built test sets and sensible participants keep the damage moderate. Teams do still fall far in "shake-ups" when the public set is small, or differs from the private set (different time period, different customers), or when they have overfit by probing it.

Practical rules follow, and they are rules for any model selection, competition or not.

  1. Build local cross-validation first and make it match the test split. If the test set is a later period, use time-ordered folds; if it contains unseen customers, group by customer (chapter 13). Check the claim with adversarial validation (chapter 14).
  2. Fix the folds and the seeds. Every model is scored on identical splits, so differences between models are paired differences, much less noisy than comparing separate random splits.
  3. Treat the leaderboard as one more fold, a small and slightly biased one. When local CV and the leaderboard disagree, work out why before changing your mind: a mismatch usually means your validation does not mimic the test split.
  4. Do not select among many near-equal candidates by their top score. Prefer a robust choice (an ensemble, a simpler model) over the single highest noisy number.

Common misconception

"The competition winner has the best model for the problem." The winner has the best score on one hidden sample for one metric, achieved with a robust process and a measure of luck: the simulation above shows that with many close teams the identity of the winner changes from run to run. The gaps between the top teams are often smaller than the noise in the test set. Read winners' write-ups for their methods, not for proof that a particular model is best.

Why ensembles work

An ensemble combines the predictions of several models. The reason it helps is arithmetic. Suppose M models make errors with the same variance σ2 and with pairwise correlation ρ. The average of their predictions has error variance

σ2 × ( ρ + (1 − ρ) / M ).

When the models' errors are independent (ρ = 0) the variance falls by a factor of M; when they make identical mistakes (ρ = 1) averaging does nothing. This is the argument for bagging in chapter 9 in a more general form, and the figure shows how fast the benefit disappears as models become alike.

The practical conclusion is that diversity matters as much as accuracy. Three variants of one algorithm are nearly the same model; three algorithms with different inductive biases (the biases of chapter 19) disagree in useful ways. The experiment trains on a 10-column table with a target built from a linear part, a threshold, an interaction and a sine. It compares three families (a neural network, boosted trees, 40-nearest-neighbours) with three variants of boosted trees (depths 2, 3 and 4). Models are combined by averaging their ranks, which puts scores on a common scale and suits the AUC metric.

"""Why averaging helps: diverse models make different mistakes. Three families versus three variants of one family."""
import numpy as np
from lib import Boost, roc_auc, sigmoid
from mlp import mlp_fit_predict

rng = np.random.default_rng(20)
def make(n):
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, 10))
    z = 0.9 * X[:, 0] - 0.7 * X[:, 1] + 0.8 * (X[:, 2] > 0.3) + 1.1 * X[:, 3] * X[:, 4] + 0.9 * np.sin(2 * X[:, 5]) - 0.4
    return X, (rng.random(n) < sigmoid(z)).astype(int), z
Xtr, ytr, _ = make(3000); Xte, yte, zte = make(6000)
mu, sd = Xtr.mean(0), Xtr.std(0); Ztr, Zte = (Xtr - mu) / sd, (Xte - mu) / sd

def knn(A, y, B, k=40):                                   # brute-force k nearest neighbours: mean label of the k closest rows
    d = (B ** 2).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * B @ A.T + (A ** 2).sum(1)[None, :]
    return y[np.argpartition(d, k, axis=1)[:, :k]].mean(1)

def rank(s): return np.argsort(np.argsort(s)) / len(s)    # scores -> ranks in [0, 1], so models on different scales can be averaged
P = {"neural network (MLP)": mlp_fit_predict(Xtr, ytr.astype(float), Xte, np.random.default_rng(5)),
     "boosted trees": Boost(rounds=150, lr=0.1, depth=3, min_leaf=20).fit(Xtr, ytr.astype(float)).predict(Xte),
     "40-nearest-neighbours": knn(Ztr, ytr.astype(float), Zte)}
V = {f"boosted, depth {d}": Boost(rounds=150, lr=0.1, depth=d, min_leaf=20).fit(Xtr, ytr.astype(float)).predict(Xte) for d in (2, 3, 4)}
auc = lambda s: roc_auc(s, yte)
print(f"ceiling (true log-odds) AUC {auc(zte):.3f}\n")
print("three different families")
for k, v in P.items(): print(f"  {k:<24}AUC {auc(v):.3f}")
names = list(P); C = np.corrcoef([rank(P[n]) for n in names])
print(f"  rank correlations: MLP/trees {C[0,1]:.2f}, MLP/kNN {C[0,2]:.2f}, trees/kNN {C[1,2]:.2f}")
print(f"  average of the three ranks   AUC {auc(sum(rank(v) for v in P.values())):.3f}")
print("\nthree variants of one family")
for k, v in V.items(): print(f"  {k:<24}AUC {auc(v):.3f}")
vn = list(V); C = np.corrcoef([rank(V[n]) for n in vn])
print(f"  rank correlations: {C[0,1]:.2f}, {C[0,2]:.2f}, {C[1,2]:.2f}")
print(f"  average of the three ranks   AUC {auc(sum(rank(v) for v in V.values())):.3f}")

The three families have rank correlations of 0.74 to 0.83 and the average of their ranks scores an AUC of 0.810, better than the best of them (0.795) by 0.015. The three boosted-tree variants correlate at 0.90 to 0.97, and their average (0.791) is no better than the best single one (0.796). Same number of models, very different result. The ceiling for this data is 0.841, so the blend closes about a third of the gap between the best single model and what is possible; the rest requires better features or more data, not more models.

Analogy: a panel of doctors

Three specialists trained at different hospitals, each looking at the same scan, will disagree on some cases, and the majority view is right more often than any one of them. Three doctors who trained under the same professor will mostly make the same mistakes and add little as a panel. The value of the panel is in how differently they think, not how many there are.

Where it breaks down: doctors know when they are uncertain and can discuss it. Model outputs are numbers on different scales, and some models are confidently wrong. Combining them well needs the calibration and rank tricks below, and a weak model added with equal weight can drag the panel down.

Blending and stacking

There are three common ways to combine models, in increasing order of power and risk.

Averaging
Take the mean of predicted probabilities, or of ranks when the metric depends only on ordering (AUC) or the scales differ. Nothing is fitted, so nothing can overfit; the cost is that every model gets equal weight.
Weighted blending
Choose weights by optimising the metric on out-of-fold predictions, for example with non-negative weights summing to one (Breiman, 1996, "Stacked regressions"). Caruana, Niculescu-Mizil, Crew and Ksikes (2004), "Ensemble selection from libraries of models", instead build the ensemble by repeatedly adding whichever model most improves the validation score, allowing repeats, which gives weights as a by-product.
Stacking
Train a meta-model (often a regularised linear or logistic model, sometimes a small boosted model) whose inputs are the base models' predictions and whose target is the true label. Introduced as "stacked generalization" by Wolpert (1992). It can learn that model A is better for some ranges of score than others, at the price of more things to overfit.

The central rule of stacking is the same as for target encoding in chapter 17: the meta-model must be trained on predictions the base models made for rows they had not seen. Otherwise it learns from predictions that are too good. The recipe is the figure: split the training rows into k folds; for each base model and each fold, train on the other k − 1 folds and predict the held-out fold; stack those out-of-fold predictions into a feature matrix with one column per base model; fit the meta-model on it with the true labels. To predict new rows, refit each base model on all training rows and feed its predictions to the fitted meta-model. The base models are fitted k + 1 times in all, so stacking multiplies the training cost.

The script shows what goes wrong if the rule is broken. The three base models are a logistic regression, a 40-nearest-neighbour model and a deliberately overfit boosted-tree model (200 rounds of depth-6 trees with leaves as small as 3 rows). The meta-model is a logistic regression on the base models' logit-scaled predictions. It is trained twice: on the base models' predictions for their own training rows, and on out-of-fold predictions.

"""Stacking: a meta-model that learns how to combine base models, with out-of-fold versus in-sample base predictions."""
import numpy as np
from lib import Boost, fit_logreg, predict_proba, roc_auc, sigmoid

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
def make(n):
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, 10))
    z = 0.9 * X[:, 0] - 0.7 * X[:, 1] + 0.8 * (X[:, 2] > 0.3) + 1.1 * X[:, 3] * X[:, 4] + 0.9 * np.sin(2 * X[:, 5]) - 0.4
    return X, (rng.random(n) < sigmoid(z)).astype(int)
Xtr, ytr = make(2500); Xte, yte = make(6000)
mu, sd = Xtr.mean(0), Xtr.std(0)

def knn(A, y, B, k=40):
    A, B = (A - mu) / sd, (B - mu) / sd
    d = (B ** 2).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * B @ A.T + (A ** 2).sum(1)[None, :]
    return y[np.argpartition(d, k, axis=1)[:, :k]].mean(1)
def logit(p): p = np.clip(p, 0.02, 0.98); return np.log(p / (1 - p))
BASE = {   # name: function (train X, train y, X to predict) -> score
    "logistic": lambda A, y, B: predict_proba(fit_logreg((A - mu) / sd, y, l2=1.0), (B - mu) / sd),
    "40-NN": lambda A, y, B: knn(A, y.astype(float), B),
    "overfit trees": lambda A, y, B: Boost(rounds=200, lr=0.2, depth=6, min_leaf=3).fit(A, y.astype(float)).predict(B)}

def meta_features(preds): return np.column_stack([logit(preds[n]) for n in BASE])
# (a) base predictions on the rows the base models were trained on
insample = {n: f(Xtr, ytr, Xtr) for n, f in BASE.items()}
# (b) out-of-fold: every training row predicted by a model that never saw it
folds = np.arange(len(ytr)) % 5; oof = {n: np.zeros(len(ytr)) for n in BASE}
for k in range(5):
    for n, f in BASE.items(): oof[n][folds == k] = f(Xtr[folds != k], ytr[folds != k], Xtr[folds == k])
# base models refit on all training rows predict the test rows
test = {n: f(Xtr, ytr, Xte) for n, f in BASE.items()}

print(f"{'base model':<16}{'AUC on its own training rows':>30}{'out-of-fold AUC':>17}{'test AUC':>10}")
for n in BASE: print(f"{n:<16}{roc_auc(insample[n], ytr):>30.3f}{roc_auc(oof[n], ytr):>17.3f}{roc_auc(test[n], yte):>10.3f}")

print(f"\n{'meta-model (logistic on base outputs)':<40}{'weights (logistic, 40-NN, trees)':>34}{'test AUC':>10}")
for label, feats in (("trained on in-sample base predictions", insample), ("trained on out-of-fold predictions", oof)):
    F = meta_features(feats); m, s = F.mean(0), F.std(0)
    beta = fit_logreg((F - m) / s, ytr, l2=1.0)
    out = predict_proba(beta, (meta_features(test) - m) / s)
    print(f"{label:<40}{np.array2string(beta[1:], precision=2, suppress_small=True):>34}{roc_auc(out, yte):>10.3f}")
rk = lambda v: np.argsort(np.argsort(v)) / len(v)
print(f"{'plain average of the three ranks':<40}{'':>34}{roc_auc(sum(rk(v) for v in test.values()), yte):>10.3f}")
base model        AUC on its own training rows  out-of-fold AUC  test AUC
logistic                                 0.731            0.724     0.736
40-NN                                    0.802            0.765     0.769
overfit trees                            1.000            0.758     0.757

meta-model (logistic on base outputs)     weights (logistic, 40-NN, trees)  test AUC

The overfit trees predict their own training rows perfectly (AUC 1.000) but score only 0.758 out of fold and 0.757 on the test rows. The meta-model trained on the in-sample predictions sees that model as flawless, gives it a weight of 6.86 against about −0.1 for the others, and scores 0.750 on the test rows, worse than the nearest-neighbour model alone (0.769). Trained on out-of-fold predictions, the weights are balanced (0.08, 0.71 and 0.61) and the test AUC is 0.786, better than every base model and than the plain average of ranks (0.781). In this example the meta-model's edge over the average is small (0.005), which is typical: stacking earns more with many diverse models and a lot of data, and a weighted average captures most of the gain with much less risk. Lab 6 (Feature Pipelines & Stacking) has you build this yourself.

A few pitfalls recur. Time-ordered data: out-of-fold predictions from random folds use the future to predict the past; use time-ordered folds (chapter 18). Leaderboard probing: fitting blend weights on the public leaderboard score is the selection effect of the previous section in disguise. Too many layers: each extra level adds fitted parameters and training cost for diminishing return. Weak models: adding a model that is much worse than the others rarely helps. In a trial run of the first experiment with a plain logistic regression (AUC 0.732) in place of the neural network, the three-family average scored 0.793, slightly below the best single model's 0.795. And calibration: an average of probabilities from differently calibrated models is not calibrated (chapter 15); recalibrate the final blend if you use the probabilities for decisions.

From competition to product: what the winning solution costs

A competition ends at the submission. A product starts there. The ensembles that win can contain dozens of models, each with its own features, library versions and training run. In production every one of those is code to deploy, a dependency to patch, an input to monitor and a latency to pay: serving an ensemble of M models in parallel means the response waits for the slowest of them, so the tail-latency arithmetic of tail latency at scale applies directly, and the extra accuracy has to be worth the on-call burden of chapter 21. The Netflix Prize is the standard case: the winning blend of many predictors never ran in full in production, because the engineering cost outweighed the measured gain (the details and the primary sources are in Case 1). The XGBoost case (Case 5) shows the opposite end: a system whose engineering made the strong single model fast enough to use everywhere.

Competitions also reward things that real projects must refuse. Case 6 collects leaks that won competitions (row order, identifiers and timestamps that gave away the label): finding them is a legitimate competition skill and a disaster in production, because the leak does not exist at prediction time. The same instinct, pointed at your own pipeline, is what chapter 14 teaches.

A sensible translation into practice is a short list of what to keep:

  • Keep the validation discipline entirely. It costs nothing at serving time and prevents the failures that do cost.
  • Keep the habit of a strong, simple baseline and an honest comparison. The gain of a complicated system is measured against it.
  • Keep two or three diverse models, not fifty. Most of an ensemble's gain tends to come from the first few diverse members; in the experiment above, a three-model blend already captured a third of the distance to the ceiling.
  • Price the gain. A 0.003 improvement in AUC is worth a maintenance burden only if you can convert it into money or risk reduction at your scale, which is the subject of the next chapter.

Systems connection

Stacking is a small data-flow system with a state machine: base-model fits, out-of-fold predictions, meta-model fit, refits and scoring, each depending on the output of the last and each needing to be reproducible. The habits of reproducible experiments (pinned environments, seeds, logged runs) are what let a team rerun a blend that won six weeks ago, and the experiment-tracking tools in the toolbox exist for this. Serving the result is the design problem of Designing with ML & LLMs: feature stores, latency budgets and fallbacks.

State of play, October 2026

The recipe has been industrialised. AutoML systems such as AutoGluon (Erickson et al., 2020, "AutoGluon-Tabular: Robust and Accurate AutoML for Structured Data") automate multi-layer stacking with bagged base models, and the TabArena benchmark (2025) reports that post-hoc ensembling of tuned models gives the best performance for individual model families, that cross-model ensembles push the state of the art further, and that some deep models are over-represented in cross-model ensembles because of validation-set overfitting, which is the selection effect of this chapter showing up in a benchmark. Tabular foundation models (chapter 19) now appear as members of such ensembles. Public leaderboards, prize pools and platform rules change often; check the platform you plan to use.

Key takeaways

  • A public leaderboard is a noisy sample, and picking the best of many scores overstates it: with 200 public rows, the public leader's score was 8.2 points above its true accuracy and its private rank about 170th of 1,000.
  • Build local validation that mirrors the test split, fix folds and seeds, and treat the leaderboard as one more small fold. Real leaderboards overfit less than worst-case arguments suggest (Roelofs et al., 2019), but shake-ups still happen.
  • The winners' loop is the course in miniature: baseline and validation, data study and leak hunt, features, a boosted-tree backbone, diverse extra models, a late ensemble.
  • Averaging removes only the error the models do not share: variance σ2(ρ + (1 − ρ)/M). Three different families scored AUC 0.810, above the best member (0.795); three variants of one family scored 0.791, below theirs (0.796).
  • Stacking needs out-of-fold base predictions. Trained on in-sample predictions the meta-model gave an overfit model a weight of 6.86 and scored 0.750; trained out of fold it scored 0.786.
  • A winning ensemble is not a product. Price the serving cost, latency and maintenance against the gain, and keep a few diverse models, not dozens.

Check your understanding

Your cross-validation AUC improves from 0.842 to 0.846 with a new feature, but your public leaderboard score falls by 0.002. The public set is 400 rows. What do you do?

  1. Drop the feature, because the leaderboard is the real test.
  2. Keep the feature unless there is a reason to think local validation does not mirror the test split, since a 400-row public set is very noisy.
  3. Ignore both and decide by the one with the larger change.
  4. Submit twice with different seeds and take the better score.
A 400-row public set has a standard error of several percentage points on most metrics, so a 0.002 difference is noise. The local score uses far more rows and identical folds. Choosing by the better of two submissions is the selection effect again. If the two signals disagreed by a large margin, investigate the validation design.

You blend three gradient-boosted models that differ only in tree depth and get no improvement over the best one. Which change is most likely to help?

  1. Add more depths of the same algorithm.
  2. Increase the number of boosting rounds in each.
  3. Average probabilities instead of ranks.
  4. Add models with different inductive biases, such as a neural network or a nearest-neighbour model, that make different mistakes.
The three boosted models are highly correlated (about 0.9 or more in the experiment), so averaging removes little. More of the same adds more of the same mistakes. A different family lowers the error correlation and so the variance of the average. Rank versus probability averaging changes the scale, not the diversity.

A colleague fits a stacking meta-model on the base models' predictions for the same rows the base models were trained on, and the meta-model gives one base model nearly all the weight. What is the likely problem?

  1. That base model overfits its training rows, so its in-sample predictions look near perfect; the meta-model must be trained on out-of-fold predictions.
  2. That base model is simply the best; no problem.
  3. The meta-model has too few features.
  4. The base models should have been trained on less data.
In-sample predictions of a flexible model are optimistic, so a meta-model trained on them trusts that model far too much, and the stack performs worse on new rows (0.750 against 0.786 in the experiment). Out-of-fold predictions fix this. The number of features and the amount of base-model training data are not the issue.

The variance of the average of M models is σ2(ρ + (1 − ρ)/M). Ten models each have error variance 4 and correlation 0.9 between their errors. What is the variance of the average?

  1. 0.4
  2. 0.9
  3. 3.64
  4. 4.0
4 × (0.9 + 0.1/10) = 4 × 0.91 = 3.64. The naive "divide by M" answer of 0.4 would hold only for uncorrelated errors. At correlation 0.9 ten models barely beat one (3.64 against 4.0), which is why diversity matters more than the number of models.

A team proposes shipping a 40-model stack that beat the best single model by 0.002 AUC in cross-validation. What is the best first question?

  1. Can we make it 80 models?
  2. What is that 0.002 worth in money or risk at our volume, compared with the cost of serving, monitoring and maintaining 40 models?
  3. Which of the 40 models has the most parameters?
  4. Can we tune the meta-model on the public leaderboard?
A tiny gain is worth a large system only if it converts into value that exceeds the engineering and operational cost, as with the Netflix Prize blend that was never fully deployed. Making it larger or tuning on a leaderboard does not answer that question, and parameter counts are irrelevant to it.

You stack models on a daily demand series using random 5-fold out-of-fold predictions. Why is this a problem?

  1. Random folds let base models train on days after the days they predict, so the meta-model learns from predictions that use the future; use time-ordered folds.
  2. Five folds is too few for time series.
  3. Stacking cannot be used for time series at all.
  4. The meta-model must be a tree model for time series.
Out-of-fold predictions for a given day come from models trained on both earlier and later days, which is unavailable in forecasting, so the meta-model sees an unrealistically good set of base predictions. Time-ordered (forward-chaining) folds respect that. Stacking is fine for time series if built that way; the fold count and meta-model type are secondary.
