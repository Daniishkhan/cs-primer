What Learning Is: Generalisation

A model that scores perfectly on the examples it was shown has told you almost nothing. This chapter explains what we actually want from learning (good predictions on cases nobody has seen yet), why the gap between the two is the central problem of the field, and how training, validation and test sets let you measure that gap honestly.

Why this matters

Every later chapter in this course, and most of the failures you will meet in practice, come back to one question: will this work on data it has not seen? A team that cannot answer it ships a model that looked superb in a notebook and fails in the first week of production. After this chapter you will be able to say precisely what "overfitting" is, spot it from two numbers, split data so that your final score is an honest estimate, put an error bar on that score, and explain why no learning algorithm wins everywhere. These are the foundations under metrics, baselines, regularisation, cross-validation and leakage.

What we actually want

Start with the plainest possible picture. You have a table of examples. Each example has some inputs, called features (a customer's age, the number of orders last month), and one thing you wish you could predict from them, the label or target (did the customer cancel?). In scikit-learn notation the features form a matrix X of shape (n_samples, n_features) and the labels a vector y of length n_samples. Learning, in this course, means choosing a function from features to labels using the examples. The chosen function is the model; the act of choosing it is training or fitting.

To choose, you need a way to say how wrong a prediction is. A loss function L(ŷ, y) scores one prediction ŷ against the true label y: squared error for a number, "1 if wrong, else 0" for a class. Averaged over the training examples it gives the training error (also called empirical risk), and fitting is usually a search for the function that makes it small.

Here is the catch. The training error is not what you care about. You care about the error on future cases: next month's customers, tomorrow's transactions. Imagine the world as a machine that produces (features, label) pairs according to some unknown data distribution. Your training examples are a finite sample from it. The quantity you want is the average loss over everything the machine could produce, the generalisation error (also called expected loss or risk):

R(h) = E(x, y) [ L(h(x), y) ]     versus     R̂(h) = (1/n) ∑i L(h(xi), yi)

The left is the expectation over the whole distribution; the right is the average over the n examples you happened to get. You can compute the right-hand side. You can never compute the left, because you do not have the distribution. Everything in this chapter is about estimating the left side without fooling yourself. The difference between the two is the generalisation gap, and a model generalises well when that gap is small and the error itself is low.

One assumption hides in this picture, and it is worth stating because it breaks in real life. Statisticians call the examples i.i.d., "independent and identically distributed": each example is drawn separately from the same distribution, and future cases come from that distribution too. Everything below depends on it. When tomorrow's customers differ from last year's, or when one customer appears fifty times in your table, the assumption is false and your estimates will be too optimistic. Later sections, and chapters 3, 13 and 14, return to this.

Analogy

A student revises with a book of past exam papers. If the student memorises the answers to those exact papers, their score on those papers is perfect and tells you nothing about the real exam. If they learn the underlying method, they may score a little less on the past papers and far more on a new one. A set of unseen practice papers (marked once, not memorised) tells you which kind of student you have.

Where it breaks down: an exam board writes new questions independently of the students, but your data is often shaped by your own past decisions, and the world can change the syllabus without telling you.

Seeing it: fitting a curve to twenty points

The cleanest way to see generalisation is with a problem small enough to draw. The world here is a sine wave with noise: the true pattern is y = sin(2πx), but every observed y has Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.3 added. You are given 20 noisy points and asked to fit a polynomial. The one choice you control is the degree: degree 0 is a flat line, degree 1 a sloped line, degree 3 a cubic with two bends, and so on. Higher degree means more freedom to bend. We will measure the mean squared error (MSE), the average of (prediction minus label) squared, on the 20 training points and on 500 fresh points from the same world.

The noise has variance 0.32 = 0.09. No model, however clever, can predict noise, so 0.09 is the irreducible error: the floor under any achievable MSE on this problem.

"""Fit polynomials of increasing degree to 20 noisy points; compare error on seen and unseen data."""
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial import Polynomial

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
NOISE = 0.3

def sample(n):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
    y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, NOISE, n)
    return x, y

x_tr, y_tr = sample(20)
x_va, y_va = sample(500)

def mse(p, x, y):
    return float(np.mean((p(x) - y) ** 2))

print(f"noise floor (irreducible MSE) = {NOISE**2:.3f}")
print(f"{'degree':>6} {'train MSE':>10} {'valid MSE':>10}  gap")
for d in [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15]:
    p = Polynomial.fit(x_tr, y_tr, d)
    tr, va = mse(p, x_tr, y_tr), mse(p, x_va, y_va)
    print(f"{d:>6} {tr:>10.3f} {va:>10.3f}  {va - tr:+.3f}")

Read the table from the top. Degree 0 predicts one constant for everything and is poor on both sets. Degree 1 and 2 are better, and degree 3 is where the training error collapses to 0.053. The validation error drops too, to 0.116, near (but above) the floor of 0.09. So far both columns agree: more flexibility helped.

After degree 4 the columns part. Training error keeps shrinking, because a more flexible curve can always bend closer to the 20 points it is trained on; at degree 15 it is 0.016, which is below the noise floor, and that is impossible for a model that has learned the real pattern. It has learned the noise. Validation error meanwhile explodes: 2.2 at degree 9, 5,707 at degree 12. The curve threads through every training point and swings wildly between and beyond them (the data do not reach all the way to x = 0 or x = 1, so the polynomial is extrapolating at the edges, where high-degree polynomials are violent).

One more detail: at degree 1 the validation error (0.266) is lower than the training error (0.389), a negative gap. With only 20 training points, that particular sample happened to contain a few unlucky noisy points. A single small sample gives noisy answers about everything, including the gap. Underfitting, overfitting and the dial between them

The experiment shows the two ways to fail. Underfitting is when the model is too rigid to capture the real pattern: error is high on training data and on new data. Overfitting is when the model is flexible enough to fit the idiosyncrasies of the particular sample, including its noise: error is low on training data and much higher on new data. The knob that moves you between them is called model capacity (or complexity): how rich a family of functions the algorithm can choose from. Polynomial degree is one capacity knob. Others you will meet: the depth of a decision tree, the number of neighbours in k-nearest-neighbours (fewer is more flexible), the strength of a penalty on large weights.

You can diagnose which side you are on from two numbers, the training error and the validation error, read against a sensible reference level (a baseline, the noise floor if you can estimate it, or human performance):

Training errorValidation errorReadingTypical remedies
HighHigh, close to trainingUnderfitting (high bias)More flexible model, better features, less regularisation, train longer
LowMuch higherOverfitting (high variance)More data, simpler model, stronger regularisation, fewer or cleaner features, early stopping
LowLow, close togetherGood fit for this dataCheck the validation set is representative, then stop tuning
LowLow, but production is poorValidation does not match realityLook for leakage, shift, duplicates (chapter 14)

The words "bias" and "variance" in the table are the standard names for the two failure sources. Bias is error from a model class that cannot represent the truth (a line cannot be a sine wave). Variance is error from the model changing a lot when the training sample changes (the degree-12 curve would look completely different on a second sample of 20 points). Chapter 6 develops the decomposition and the tools for trading one against the other; for now, the practical point is that the right capacity depends on how much data you have. Degree 9 is catastrophic with 20 points and tolerable with 80 (try it in the widget above). More data lowers variance, which is why a flexible model that overfits a small sample may be the best choice on a large one.

Common misconception

"A model with 100% training accuracy is overfit, and one with lower training accuracy is safer." Neither follows. Overfitting is about the gap and the level of the validation error, not about training error alone. A flexible model can reach near-zero training error and still generalise well if there is enough data and structure, and a model with modest training error can still be badly overfit if you selected it from thousands of candidates. Always judge from error on data the model did not train on.

Three sets, three jobs

If training error is a biased judge of generalisation, we need data that played no part in fitting. The standard protocol splits the labelled data into three disjoint parts, each with one job.

Training set
Used to fit the model's parameters. The algorithm sees these labels and nothing else.
Validation set (also dev set)
Used to make choices: which algorithm, which hyperparameters (settings you choose rather than learn, such as the degree or the learning rate), which features, when to stop. You can look at it as often as you like.
Test set (also holdout)
Used once, at the end, to report the final estimate of generalisation error. It must not influence any decision.

Why two held-out sets rather than one? Because choosing is itself a kind of fitting. Every time you compare candidates on a set and keep the one that scores best, information from that set leaks into your choice, and the winner's score becomes optimistic. The validation set absorbs that optimism so the test set stays clean. Step through the workflow:

Step through

Spending labelled data: 1,000 rows, three jobs

  1. Start with everything you have labelled. Say 1,000 rows. It is tempting to fit on all of it, because more data usually helps. But then you would have nothing left to measure with.
  2. Lock the test set first. Before any exploration of modelling, set aside a fraction (here 20%) at random and do not look at its labels again. Doing this first matters: if you peek, even at summaries, the temptation to adapt grows.
  3. Split the rest into train and validation. Here 600 and 200. The training part teaches the model. The validation part is your steering wheel.
  4. Fit every candidate on the training set only. Different algorithms, different degrees, different feature sets. All the fitting uses only the 600 rows.
  5. Choose using the validation set. Score each candidate on the 200 validation rows and keep the best. You may repeat this loop many times; the validation set is there to be spent. Often you then refit the winning recipe on train plus validation.
  6. Touch the test set once. The chosen model is scored on the 200 locked rows. That number is your estimate of generalisation error, and it is only trustworthy because nothing was tuned against it. If it disappoints, you do not get to tune and re-test with the same rows.

How a validation set gets used up

"You may look at the validation set as often as you like" is true in a limited sense. Each look is a small act of fitting, and with enough looks the validation score stops being honest. This is selection bias, sometimes called the winner's curse: the best of many noisy scores is better than the typical one, even if every candidate is equally good.

The most extreme case is easy to simulate. Make the labels pure coin flips, so no model can possibly beat 50%. Create k "models" that are random guessers. Pick the one with the best accuracy on a validation set of 200 rows, then score the winner on a fresh test set of 200 rows. Repeat 300 times and average.

"""Pure noise, many candidate models: how optimistic is the best validation score?"""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
N_VAL = N_TEST = 200
TRIALS = 300

print(f"{'candidates':>10} {'best valid acc':>15} {'its test acc':>13}")
for k in [1, 10, 100, 1000]:
    best_val, its_test = [], []
    for _ in range(TRIALS):
        y_val = rng.integers(0, 2, N_VAL)                 # labels are coin flips: no signal at all
        y_test = rng.integers(0, 2, N_TEST)
        # each candidate "model" is a random guesser, so its predictions on val and test are independent
        val_acc = (rng.integers(0, 2, (k, N_VAL)) == y_val).mean(axis=1)
        test_acc = (rng.integers(0, 2, (k, N_TEST)) == y_test).mean(axis=1)
        winner = val_acc.argmax()
        best_val.append(val_acc[winner])
        its_test.append(test_acc[winner])
    print(f"{k:>10} {np.mean(best_val):>15.3f} {np.mean(its_test):>13.3f}")
candidates  best valid acc  its test acc
         1           0.505         0.501
        10           0.555         0.502
       100           0.588         0.499
      1000           0.614         0.492

With one candidate there is no selection and both numbers sit at 50%. As the number of candidates grows, the best validation accuracy climbs to 61.4% with 1,000 candidates, a "result" that would look promising on a slide, while the winner's test accuracy stays at 50%. Nothing was learned; the maximum of many noisy numbers was reported. Real tuning rarely involves 1,000 random guessers, but trying 200 hyperparameter settings, 30 feature sets and a few architectures against one small validation set is the same mechanism with smaller magnitude. Cawley and Talbot (2010) documented how this selection bias inflates reported performance in published work, and Dwork et al. (2015) gave the formal argument for why reusing a holdout adaptively erodes it, along with a way to limit the damage.

Three practical rules follow.

  1. Make the validation set big enough that its noise is small relative to differences you care about (the next section shows how big that is), or use cross-validation to reuse the data more efficiently.
  2. Treat small validation differences as noise. If two models differ by less than the error bar, prefer the simpler or cheaper one.
  3. Keep the test set for last and for once. If you must use it a second time (a failed launch review, say), say so in the report, and plan to collect a fresh test set rather than pretending the old one is clean.

Test sets wear out at scale too

The same erosion can happen across a whole research community that uses one benchmark for years. Recht et al. (2019), in "Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet?", built new test sets following the original collection process as closely as they could and found accuracy drops of 11 to 14 percentage points for the models they evaluated, with accuracy gains on the old test set still carrying over to the new one. They found little evidence that repeated use of the old test set was the cause, and point instead to models being sensitive to small shifts in the data distribution. The lesson for you: even an untouched test set only estimates performance on data like itself.

How big a test set, and how to split

A test-set accuracy is itself an estimate from a finite sample, so it carries an error bar. If the true accuracy is p and the test set has n independent rows, the measured accuracy has standard error √(p(1−p)/n). Roughly 95% of the time the measurement lands within 1.96 standard errors of the truth. The script below prints that formula next to a simulation of 20,000 imaginary test sets for each size.

"""How precise is a test-set accuracy? Binomial standard error, checked by simulation."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
TRUE_ACC = 0.90
print(f"{'test size':>9} {'std error':>10} {'95% range':>16} {'simulated sd':>13}")
for n in [100, 500, 1000, 10_000]:
    se = (TRUE_ACC * (1 - TRUE_ACC) / n) ** 0.5
    sims = rng.binomial(n, TRUE_ACC, 20_000) / n          # 20,000 imaginary test sets of size n
    print(f"{n:>9} {se:>10.4f} {TRUE_ACC - 1.96*se:>7.3f} to {TRUE_ACC + 1.96*se:.3f} {sims.std():>13.4f}")
test size  std error        95% range  simulated sd
      100     0.0300   0.841 to 0.959        0.0300
      500     0.0134   0.874 to 0.926        0.0133
     1000     0.0095   0.881 to 0.919        0.0095
    10000     0.0030   0.894 to 0.906        0.0030

The formula and the simulation agree to three decimals. A model that is truly 90% accurate will score anywhere from 84.1% to 95.9% on a test set of 100 rows, a range that makes any comparison between two models meaningless unless they differ by a lot. At 1,000 rows the range is about ±1.9 points; at 10,000 rows, ±0.6. The error shrinks as 1/√n: quadrupling the data only halves the error bar. Comparisons between two models on the same rows are tighter than this (the errors are correlated), which Statistics for Evals and Statistics for Experiments cover with paired tests and the bootstrap.

Common split sizes, such as 60/20/20 or 80/10/10, are conventions, not laws. The real constraint is the one above: the validation and test sets must each be large enough that their noise is below the differences you need to detect, and the training set large enough to learn from. With a million rows, 1% for each held-out set is plenty. With 500 rows, a single split is too noisy and you should prefer cross-validation for model selection while still holding out a small test set.

The split must match how the model will be used

Random splitting is correct only when new cases look like a random draw from the same pool as the old ones. Three common situations violate that.

  • Time. A model that predicts next month from history is deployed into the future. A random split lets the training data contain rows from after the validation rows, so the model gets to peek at the future. Split by time: train on the past, validate on the period after it. See chapter 13 and chapter 18.
  • Groups. If one patient contributes twenty scans, a random split puts some scans in training and others in validation, and the model can recognise the patient. Split by patient. The same goes for customers, devices, documents from one source.
  • Imbalance. If 1% of rows are positive, a random 200-row validation set holds about 2 positives and its scores are mostly noise. Use stratified splitting (keep the class proportions the same in every part) and look at the number of positives, not just the number of rows.

Each of these is a case where the i.i.d. assumption fails, and each is a source of the "too good to be true" results that chapter 14 teaches you to hunt.

Systems connection

The test set is a contract with the future, and software engineers know what a contract needs: isolation, one-time use and fidelity to production. A test set the team consults repeatedly behaves like a shared mutable global. The mismatch between a random split and a real deployment is the same hazard as testing against a staging environment that differs from production; Designing with ML & LLMs shows how training-serving skew and point-in-time correctness extend the idea to features. And a model that scores well offline still needs a staged rollout and live monitoring, as in Deployment Strategies: the test set is the first line of defence, not the last.

No free lunch: why there is no best learner

If generalisation means doing well on unseen cases, a natural question is whether some algorithm is simply best at it. The no free lunch theorem says no, in a precise sense. Wolpert (1996), in "The Lack of A Priori Distinctions Between Learning Algorithms", showed that if you average over all possible target functions, with each equally likely, every learning algorithm has the same expected accuracy on cases outside its training data. (Wolpert and Macready, 1997, proved the sibling result for optimisation algorithms in "No Free Lunch Theorems for Optimization".)

The reason is simple once you see a tiny case. Take every function from three bits to a label: there are 28 = 256 of them. Show a learner the correct label for 5 of the 8 possible inputs. Exactly 23 = 8 of the 256 functions agree with those five labels, and they differ on the other three inputs in every possible combination. For any prediction the learner makes on an unseen input, half of those eight functions say yes and half say no. The training data say nothing about the unseen inputs, because without further assumptions nothing connects them.

The script confirms it by brute force. It enumerates all 256 functions, trains four very different learners on the five seen inputs and measures accuracy on the three unseen ones, averaged over all 256 functions. It then repeats the experiment restricted to the 20 monotone functions (flipping an input bit from 0 to 1 never lowers the output), a world with structure.

"""A tiny no-free-lunch check: every boolean function of 3 bits, five seen inputs, three unseen."""
from itertools import product

inputs = list(product([0, 1], repeat=3))          # 8 possible inputs
seen, unseen = inputs[:5], inputs[5:]             # the learner sees 5 of them

def always_one(train):   return lambda x: 1
def majority(train):
    ones = sum(y for _, y in train)
    return lambda x: int(ones * 2 >= len(train))
def nearest(train):      # copy the label of the training input with the fewest differing bits
    return lambda x: min(train, key=lambda t: sum(a != b for a, b in zip(t[0], x)))[1]
def parity_guess(train): return lambda x: sum(x) % 2

def monotone(f):         # flipping any bit 0 -> 1 never decreases the output
    return all(f[a] <= f[b] for a in inputs for b in inputs if all(p <= q for p, q in zip(a, b)))

all_functions = [dict(zip(inputs, bits)) for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=8)]
mono_functions = [f for f in all_functions if monotone(f)]

def mean_unseen_accuracy(learner, functions):
    total = 0.0
    for f in functions:
        h = learner([(x, f[x]) for x in seen])
        total += sum(h(x) == f[x] for x in unseen) / len(unseen)
    return total / len(functions)

print(f"all functions: {len(all_functions)}   monotone functions: {len(mono_functions)}")
print(f"{'learner':<14}{'all functions':>15}{'monotone only':>15}")
for name, L in [("always one", always_one), ("majority", majority), ("nearest", nearest), ("parity guess", parity_guess)]:
    print(f"{name:<14}{mean_unseen_accuracy(L, all_functions):>15.3f}{mean_unseen_accuracy(L, mono_functions):>15.3f}")
all functions: 256   monotone functions: 20
learner         all functions  monotone only
always one              0.500          0.783
majority                0.500          0.467
nearest                 0.500          0.650
parity guess            0.500          0.517

Averaged over all functions, all four learners score exactly 0.500, including "always predict 1" and a deliberately silly parity guess. Restricted to a world with structure, they separate: "always one" reaches 0.783 because in a monotone world the unseen inputs (101, 110, 111) have many 1-bits and tend to be positive, while "majority" falls below chance at 0.467 and nearest-neighbour reaches 0.650. A learner is good for a world, not in general.

Here is what this does and does not tell you.

  • It does say that every learning algorithm rests on an assumption about the world, called its inductive bias: linear models assume the label changes smoothly and additively with the features, nearest neighbours assume that similar inputs have similar labels, decision trees assume that axis-aligned splits can carve out the pattern. Learning is only possible because we bet on such assumptions.
  • It does say that "which algorithm is best?" has no answer without saying which data. This is why we test on validation data rather than trusting a ranking from a paper.
  • It does not say that all algorithms are equally good on the problems you actually meet. Real data are not drawn uniformly from all functions; they have structure (smoothness, sparsity, repetition) that some inductive biases exploit. Gradient-boosted trees are a strong default for tabular data, not because of any theorem, but because their bias matches the structure of typical business tables (chapter 9; Grinsztajn et al., 2022, compare them with neural networks on 45 datasets and find trees ahead at medium sizes).
  • It does not say that learning is hopeless. It says to choose your bias on purpose, and to check it against held-out data.
Go deeper optional

Why the count works. Fix the 5 seen inputs and their labels. A function is consistent with them if it agrees on those 5 inputs. The other 3 inputs are unconstrained, so there are 23 = 8 consistent functions, and across them each unseen input takes label 0 in exactly 4 and label 1 in exactly 4. A deterministic learner outputs one prediction per unseen input, so it is right in exactly 4 of the 8 functions on each input, an average accuracy of 1/2 regardless of what it predicts. Averaging over the choice of seen labels as well does not change this.

Beyond zero-one data. The general theorem (Wolpert, 1996) covers any finite input and label space and a range of loss functions, with "off-training-set" error: error measured only on inputs not in the training set. If the test inputs may repeat training inputs, a memoriser gains something on those repeats, which is why the theorem is stated for off-training-set error.

Related result. Learning theory has a constructive counterpart: if you restrict the model class to something not too rich (finite, or of bounded capacity), then with enough i.i.d. data the training error is guaranteed to be close to the generalisation error. Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David's Understanding Machine Learning develops this carefully. The two views fit together: restricting the class is exactly what supplies the inductive bias that NFL says you cannot do without.

A modern wrinkle: double descent

The U-shaped validation curve above is the classical picture. Belkin et al. (2019), in "Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias-variance trade-off", showed that for some model families, as capacity grows past the point where the model can fit the training data exactly, test error can fall again, a pattern they call double descent. This is part of why very large neural networks can generalise despite fitting their training data perfectly. It does not remove the need for held-out evaluation, and it does not rescue a polynomial from the situation above: the lesson is that "flexible implies overfit" is a tendency, not a law. Whether a given flexible model generalises is something you measure, with the protocol in this chapter.

Key takeaways

  • We train on a sample but care about performance on future cases from the same world: the generalisation error. Training error is a biased, optimistic estimate of it.
  • Underfitting (high error everywhere) and overfitting (low training error, high validation error) are read from two numbers against a reference level. Capacity is the dial, and the right setting depends on how much data you have.
  • Use three sets: train to fit, validation to choose, test once to report. Every choice made against a set uses it up.
  • The best of many noisy scores is optimistic: with 1,000 random candidates the best validation accuracy was 61.4% on a task with no signal, and 50.0% on test.
  • A test-set score has an error bar of √(p(1−p)/n); 100 rows give about ±6 points at 90% accuracy, 10,000 rows about ±0.6.
  • The split must mimic deployment: by time, by group, stratified. Otherwise the i.i.d. assumption fails and the estimate flatters you.
  • No free lunch: every learner needs an inductive bias; none is best on all problems. Pick the bias that fits your data and verify on held-out data.

A model has training accuracy 99% and validation accuracy 71%. A colleague proposes adding more flexible features to push training accuracy to 100%. What is the best response?

  1. Agree: higher training accuracy always means a better model.
  2. Agree, but only if the test set is also used to check.
  3. Disagree: the gap points to overfitting, so look for more data, simpler models or regularisation instead.
  4. Disagree: validation accuracy should never be lower than training accuracy, so the split is broken.
A 28-point gap with a near-perfect training score is the signature of overfitting; more capacity widens it. Option 1 confuses training fit with generalisation. Option 2 would burn the test set for a choice that belongs to validation. Option 4 is wrong because validation error is normally higher than training error, and the opposite only occurs by chance in small samples, as with the degree-1 fit in the chapter.

You compare 500 hyperparameter settings on one validation set of 300 rows and report the best validation accuracy, 78%, as your expected performance. Why is that probably optimistic?

  1. Taking the best of many noisy scores selects partly for luck, so the winner's true accuracy is likely lower.
  2. Validation sets always give higher accuracy than test sets.
  3. Hyperparameters cannot be tuned on a validation set.
  4. 300 rows is too few to compute any accuracy.
This is selection bias: the maximum of many noisy estimates is biased upward, as the 1,000-random-guessers experiment showed (61.4% on validation, 50.0% on test). Option 2 is false in general. Option 3 is backwards: the validation set exists for tuning. Option 4 is wrong because accuracy can be computed on any size; it is just noisy.

Your test set has 400 rows and the model scores 90% on it. Roughly what is the 95% range for the true accuracy, assuming independent rows?

  1. 89.9% to 90.1%
  2. 89% to 91%
  3. 80% to 100%
  4. 87% to 93%
Standard error = √(0.9 × 0.1 / 400) = 0.015, so 1.96 standard errors is about 2.9 points: roughly 87% to 93%. Option 1 would need a test set of millions. Option 2 is less than half the correct width, a common result of forgetting the 1.96 factor and the square root. Option 3 is far too wide for 400 rows.

A churn model predicts which customers will cancel next month. Customer records are split randomly into train and validation, and some customers have a record for every month. What is the most likely consequence?

  1. Validation accuracy will be pessimistic because the groups are small.
  2. Validation accuracy will be optimistic because the same customer appears on both sides, and the future leaks into the past.
  3. Nothing, because random splits are always unbiased.
  4. Training accuracy will fall because duplicates confuse the optimiser.
Rows from the same customer, and from later months, are correlated with the ones in training, so validation rows are not independent fresh cases and the estimate flatters the model. Split by customer and by time. Option 3 holds only under the i.i.d. assumption, which this setup breaks. Option 1 has the direction of the error reversed.

The no-free-lunch theorem is best summarised as:

  1. No learning algorithm can ever generalise.
  2. Gradient boosting is provably the best algorithm for tabular data.
  3. Averaged over all possible target functions, every learner has the same off-training-set accuracy, so a learner is only good relative to assumptions about the data.
  4. Simple models always beat complex models.
NFL is an average over all functions equally weighted; real problems have structure, which is what inductive biases exploit. Option 1 overstates it: learning works when assumptions fit. Option 2 is an empirical regularity, not a theorem. Option 4 is a different claim (Occam-style) and not implied.

With 20 training points, a degree-9 polynomial has validation MSE 2.2 while a degree-3 polynomial has 0.12. With 2,000 training points from the same world, what do you expect for degree 9?

  1. Much closer to degree 3, because more data reduces the variance of a flexible model.
  2. Even worse, because more data gives a high-degree polynomial more to overfit.
  3. Identical, because degree alone determines error.
  4. Training error will exceed validation error.
The right capacity depends on the amount of data: with plentiful data the flexible model's fitted curve is pinned down everywhere and it behaves, as the widget shows when you raise the training points. Option 2 reverses the effect. Option 3 ignores data size. Option 4 would be an accident of one sample, not an expectation.
