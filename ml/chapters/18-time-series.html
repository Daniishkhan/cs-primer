Time Series Forecasting

Predicting the next weeks of a quantity that you have watched for years breaks the central assumption of earlier chapters, that rows are independent draws, and rewards a different set of habits: split the series into its parts, measure every method against baselines that are surprisingly hard to beat, build lag features that only look backwards, and test by rolling the forecast origin forward. This chapter teaches those habits with a series you can regenerate and a backtest you can run.

Why this matters

Demand, traffic, energy load, cash flow, hospital admissions, server capacity and sales are all forecasts, and decisions (how much to stock, how many staff, how much to provision) rest on them. Time series are also where the validation errors of chapter 13 and the leaks of chapter 14 are most expensive, because every feature has a date and every split must respect it. After this chapter you will be able to decompose a series, choose and compute honest baselines (and know when a complicated model has not beaten them), build lag-feature models for several horizons, run a rolling-origin backtest, and turn a point forecast into an interval and a stocking decision.

Running the examples

The scripts in this chapter import a small shared helper so each listing can stay short. Download lib.py, tslib.py and ts01.py into the same folder as the scripts you save from this page, then run them with python3 (NumPy is the only dependency). The outputs shown were produced exactly this way.

What makes time series different

A time series is a sequence of measurements of one quantity taken at regular times: daily sales of one product, hourly electricity demand, weekly hospital admissions. Forecasting means predicting its future values, up to some horizon (how far ahead: 14 days, 12 months). Everything in the earlier chapters assumed that rows could be shuffled without changing the problem. Here they cannot, for three reasons.

  • Order carries information. Today's value is related to yesterday's. This relationship, autocorrelation, is the most useful signal in the series and also the reason that a random train/test split is invalid: a test day sandwiched between training days is nearly interpolated.
  • The future is on one side only. At prediction time you hold the past and nothing else. Every feature must be computable from data dated before the forecast origin, the moment the forecast is made.
  • The world drifts. The relationship a model learned may change with prices, competitors, seasons and weather, so the model must be judged on how it does after the training period, which is the only period that resembles use.

A distinction worth making early: forecasting uses the series' own history (and perhaps known future inputs such as the calendar or a planned promotion), whereas an ordinary prediction problem uses other columns of the same row. In practice you mix both. The techniques are the same ones as before, applied under a stricter rule about time.

The anatomy of a series

Most business series are well described as a sum of four things. The trend is the slow underlying direction (growth, decline, a level shift). Seasonality is a pattern that repeats with a fixed period tied to the calendar: weekly (more footfall on Saturdays), yearly (more ice cream in July). A third piece, sometimes called cycles, is longer, irregular swings such as business cycles, which no calendar predicts; treat them as part of the level. What is left is the residual or noise. The additive form is yt = trendt + seasonalt + residualt. When the seasonal swings grow in proportion to the level (a business that doubles also doubles its Saturday peak), the multiplicative form multiplies the pieces instead; taking logarithms turns it back into an additive one.

The script below builds a three-year daily series with all these parts and an autocorrelated noise term (today's surprise carries half of itself into tomorrow, which real residuals typically do), then recovers the parts by ordinary least squares on calendar features: a straight-line trend, six weekday indicator columns and a yearly sine-cosine pair (the cyclic encoding from chapter 17). Because we know the truth we can check the recovery.

"""Anatomy of a series: trend + weekly + yearly seasonality + noise, recovered by least squares."""
import numpy as np

def make_series(seed=7, days=1096):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    t = np.arange(days)
    weekly = np.array([-14, -10, -6, -2, 8, 22, 2.0])             # effect by day of week (Mon..Sun)
    season = 25 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (t - 80) / 365.25)           # yearly cycle, peak in late June
    noise = np.zeros(days)
    for i in range(1, days):                                      # AR(1) noise: today's surprise carries over a little
        noise[i] = 0.5 * noise[i - 1] + rng.normal(0, 10)
    return 200 + 0.05 * t + weekly[t % 7] + season + noise, t

    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
    resid = y - X @ beta
    print(f"{len(y)} days; mean {y.mean():.1f}, sd {y.std():.1f}")

The fitted trend (0.052 a day against a true 0.050), weekday effects and yearly amplitude (24.9 against 25.0) match the truth closely, and 84.9% of the variance is explained by calendar structure alone. What is left, the residual, has standard deviation 11.1 and a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.52: the noise still remembers yesterday. That leftover memory is what lag features and smoothing methods exploit, and it is why a forecast made one day ahead is easier than one made two weeks ahead (a surprise today says a lot about tomorrow and little about day 14).

Real series add complications that this one hides: holidays that move (Easter), promotions, outliers from stockouts or outages, structural breaks (a new competitor, a pandemic), and several seasonal periods at once (daily and weekly in hourly data). Part of forecasting practice is making a calendar of known events available as inputs, since the future dates of Christmas and a planned sale are known in advance, and part is deciding what to do with a history that no longer represents the world.

Baselines that are hard to beat

Chapter 4 argued that every model must beat a trivial baseline. In forecasting, the baselines deserve more respect than anywhere else, because a few trivial rules are strong and many sophisticated models fail to beat them. The standard set, described in Hyndman and Athanasopoulos's open textbook Forecasting: Principles and Practice, is:

Mean
Forecast the average of all history. Right only if the series has no level shift.
Naive
Forecast the last observed value for every future step. The best possible forecast if the series is a random walk.
Seasonal naive
Forecast the value from one season ago: next Tuesday equals last Tuesday. Captures the seasonal pattern with no model at all.
Drift
The naive forecast plus the average change per step seen so far: a straight line through the first and last points.
Seasonal average
Average the last few same-weekday values, a seasonal naive with noise reduction.
Simple exponential smoothing
A weighted average of all past values with weights that decay geometrically: levelt = α · yt + (1 − α) · levelt−1. A larger α reacts faster and is noisier.
Holt-Winters
Exponential smoothing with a level, a trend and a seasonal profile, each updated by its own weight (Holt, 1957; Winters, 1960). The classic workhorse of demand forecasting.

Measuring error without fooling yourself

Forecast error is usually summarised by MAE (mean absolute error), RMSE (root mean squared error, which punishes large misses more) or a percentage error. Percentage errors such as MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) look scale-free but misbehave: a forecast of 5 for an actual of 1 is a 400% error, a series that touches zero makes MAPE infinite, and over-forecasts are penalised more heavily than under-forecasts. When you compare series of different sizes use the mean absolute scaled error (MASE) of Hyndman and Koehler (2006), "Another look at measures of forecast accuracy": the forecast's MAE divided by the MAE of the one-step seasonal naive forecast on the training data. A MASE of 1 means "as accurate as the in-sample seasonal naive"; below 1 is better. It is defined for any series and any horizon, and does not blow up at zero.

The next script compares the seven baselines over a rolling-origin backtest (explained fully two sections on): 41 forecast origins, a new one every 17 days, each forecasting the next 14 days from the data before it. It runs on two series. A is the retail-like series from above. B is a pure random walk, which has no seasonality and no trend to find.

"""Forecasting baselines on two series, scored with a rolling-origin backtest (14-day horizon, a new origin every 17 days)."""
import numpy as np
from tslib import *

y, t = make_series()
H, FIRST, STEP = 14, 400, 17
methods = {"mean of history": mean_all, "naive (last value)": naive, "drift": drift, "seasonal naive (7)": snaive,
           "avg of last 4 same weekdays": ma_season, "simple exp. smoothing": ses, "Holt-Winters additive": holt_winters}

def backtest(y, fn):
    errs = []
    for o in origins(len(y), FIRST, H, STEP):
        hist, act = y[:o], y[o:o + H]
        errs.append((mase(act, fn(hist, H), hist), np.mean(np.abs(act - fn(hist, H))), np.sqrt(np.mean((act - fn(hist, H)) ** 2))))
    return np.mean(errs, axis=0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
walk = 200 + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 5, len(y)))        # a series with no seasonality at all: a random walk
print(f"{len(origins(len(y), FIRST, H, STEP))} forecast origins, horizon {H} days")
for name, series in (("A. retail-like: trend + weekly + yearly + noise", y), ("B. random walk (no pattern to find)", walk)):
    print(f"\n{name}\n{'method':<30}{'MASE':>7}{'MAE':>8}{'RMSE':>8}")
    for m_name, fn in methods.items():
        mase_, mae, rmse = backtest(series, fn)
        print(f"{m_name:<30}{mase_:>7.2f}{mae:>8.1f}{rmse:>8.1f}")

The plain mean (MASE 2.14) is worst, since it ignores the trend and the seasons; the naive forecast, 1.40, ignores the weekly pattern. Seasonal naive, a rule that fits in one line, gets 1.10, and the best two methods, Holt-Winters at 0.91 and the average of the last four same weekdays at 0.96, are only 17% and 13% better than it. Averaging four weeks of Tuesdays beats copying one Tuesday because it cancels noise, an idea that returns in ensembles (chapter 20).

On the random walk the ranking flips. There is no pattern to exploit, so the strongest methods are those that do the least: simple exponential smoothing (1.00) and the naive forecast (1.05). Seasonal naive (1.14) and the seasonal average (1.48) hurt, because they copy and average noise that is not seasonal, and the mean (8.06) is hopeless. The lesson is that a model is only good relative to the right baseline for that series. A fancy method that cannot beat the naive forecast has found nothing.

Common misconception

"A more complex model will forecast better." In the large forecasting competitions the evidence has been mixed and instructive. In the M4 competition (100,000 series; Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos, 2020) most pure machine-learning methods did worse than standard statistical ones and combinations of simple methods did well, while the winner, Smyl's, was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and a recurrent network. In M5 (2022 results, Walmart sales, 42,840 series) boosted trees on lag features did best. Neither result says that complexity wins: what won was a method suited to the data, evaluated honestly. Start with the baselines, and let a model earn its place against them.

Lag features and machine-learning models

The baselines use only the series itself and a fixed recipe. A machine-learning model lets you add more information and learn how to combine it. The trick is to turn forecasting into the supervised problem of the earlier chapters: build a table where each row is one target day and the columns are things known at the forecast origin.

The usual columns are lag features (the value 1, 7, 14, 28 days before), rolling statistics (mean, standard deviation or minimum over the last 7 or 28 days), calendar features (day of week, month, holiday flag, as cyclic encodings where appropriate), and known future inputs (planned price, promotion, weather forecast). The rule from the aggregates section of chapter 17 applies in full: every lag and every rolling window must end before the origin, never at the target day. A 7-day rolling mean that includes the target day is the temporal leak of chapter 14, and in time series it is the most common way to build an excellent-looking model that does not work.

Which horizon? Recursive and direct forecasting

Suppose you want the next 14 days. There are two standard strategies. A recursive forecast trains one model to predict one day ahead, then feeds its own prediction back in as a lag to predict the next day, and so on. It needs one model, but errors compound and the model is trained for a task (one step ahead, with true recent values) that differs from its use (later steps, with its own guesses as inputs). A direct forecast trains the model for the horizon itself: for horizon h, each training row uses only information available h days before its target. One model can serve all horizons if h is a feature, and that is how the next script does it. In M5-style competitions this direct approach, with boosted trees and a careful calendar, did very well.

One fact about trees matters here. A regression tree predicts the average of the training targets in a leaf, so it can never forecast a value beyond the range it was trained on; it cannot extrapolate a trend. The remedy is to make the target something that stays in range: predict the difference from a recent level (the mean of the last 28 days) instead of the raw value, and add the level back afterwards. The script compares both on the same series. It runs 17 forecast origins from day 600 on. For each origin it trains a boosted-tree model on the previous 365 days' worth of (origin, horizon, target) triples, with features: the horizon, the target's weekday, the yearly sine and cosine, the last value, the last-7 and last-28-day means, and the value one or more whole weeks before the target that is already known. The booster is the small least-squares one from the earlier chapters.

"""Lag features + boosted trees for a 14-day forecast, trained directly for every horizon, versus the baselines."""
import numpy as np
from lib import Boost
from tslib import *

y, t = make_series()
H = 14
ORIGINS = list(range(600, 1096 - H + 1, 30))                 # 17 forecast origins

def features(y, o, d, h):
    """Features for target day d, made at origin o (uses y[:o] only; h = d - o + 1 is the horizon)."""
    known = y[:o]
    last7, last28 = known[-7:].mean(), known[-28:].mean()
    lag = known[d - 7 * ((d - o) // 7 + 1)]                    # same weekday, most recent week we know
    return [h, d % 7, np.sin(2 * np.pi * d / 365.25), np.cos(2 * np.pi * d / 365.25), known[-1], last7, last28, lag]

def build(y, o, lookback=365):
    """Training rows: pretend we stood at earlier origins o' < o and forecast the next H days; the target is known."""
    X, T, L = [], [], []
    for o2 in range(max(30, o - lookback), o - H + 1, 3):
        for h in range(1, H + 1):
            d = o2 + h - 1
            X.append(features(y, o2, d, h)); T.append(y[d]); L.append(y[o2 - 28:o2].mean())
    return np.array(X), np.array(T), np.array(L)

res = {k: [] for k in ("seasonal naive", "Holt-Winters", "boosted, raw target", "boosted, target minus last-28-day mean")}
for o in ORIGINS:
    hist, act = y[:o], y[o:o + H]
    X, T, L = build(y, o)
    Xt = np.array([features(y, o, o + h - 1, h) for h in range(1, H + 1)]); base = y[o - 28:o].mean()
    raw = Boost(rounds=100, lr=0.1, depth=4, min_leaf=20).fit(X, T).predict(Xt)
    dlv = base + Boost(rounds=100, lr=0.1, depth=4, min_leaf=20).fit(X, T - L).predict(Xt)
    for k, f in zip(res, (snaive(hist, H), holt_winters(hist, H), raw, dlv)):
        res[k].append(mase(act, f, hist))
print(f"{len(ORIGINS)} origins, horizon {H}, training rows per origin about {len(X)}")
print(f"{'method':<42}{'mean MASE':>10}{'worst origin':>14}")
for k, v in res.items():
    print(f"{k:<42}{np.mean(v):>10.2f}{np.max(v):>14.2f}")

The boosted model on the raw target scores a MASE of 1.13, no better than the seasonal naive (1.09): trained on days with lower levels, its leaves cannot represent the higher level that the later origins have reached. The same model on the target minus the recent level scores 0.89, ahead of Holt-Winters (0.95) and of the seasonal naive, and its worst origin is 1.30 against 1.85 for Holt-Winters. The gain over a well-tuned smoothing method is modest, which is the usual size of such gains on a single clean series. Where machine learning pays is when there are many related series and many external inputs, so that one model can share what it learns across series (the "global model" idea in the competition-winning solutions).

Analogy: driving by the rear-view mirror

Forecasting from lags is steering a car while looking only in the rear-view mirror. If the road is straight and you can see what it has been doing, you keep to the line: that is the trend and seasonality. Sharp bends that nothing in the mirror hints at will catch you out, and the further ahead you must steer without looking, the worse you do.

Where it breaks down: a driver cannot see the road ahead, but a forecaster often can see part of it. The calendar, planned promotions, price changes and a weather forecast are known in advance, and including them as features is the one way to look through the windscreen. Leaving them out throws information away; letting unplanned things into the future columns (an actual sales figure for the target day) is the leak.

Backtesting: rolling the origin forward

You cannot judge a forecaster on one forecast. The honest procedure is a rolling-origin backtest (also called time-series cross-validation or walk-forward validation): fix a horizon, pick a first origin, fit the method on everything before it, forecast the horizon, score the result against what happened, then move the origin forward and repeat. The scores, averaged, estimate how the method would have done had you used it through that period. It is chapter 13's time-ordered split, run many times so that the estimate is not at the mercy of one lucky month.

Several choices matter. Match the horizon to the decision. If stock is ordered two weeks ahead, the 14-day error is what counts, not the one-day error. Use enough origins and cover the seasons. One backtest year that happened to have no holiday disruption flatters every method; in the experiments above the worst single origin was 1.30 to 1.85 against averages near 1, so the average alone hides how bad a bad month can be. Report the worst origins too. Expanding or sliding window. An expanding window uses all history and suits stable series; a sliding window of fixed length forgets old regimes and suits drifting ones. Re-fit exactly as you would in production, including any encoding, scaling or hyperparameter selection, inside each origin; tuning hyperparameters on the same backtest you report is the selection bias that chapter 13 quantified. And for many series, aggregate errors across series with a scale-free metric such as MASE, or a business-weighted one.

From a forecast to a decision

A forecast of 120 units is a single number about an uncertain future. The decision that follows (how many units to stock) depends on the costs of being wrong in each direction, which are rarely equal. This is the cost-based thinking of chapter 2 and chapter 15 again: with a cost of Cunder per unit short and Cover per unit left over, the best amount to stock is the quantile of the demand distribution at Cunder / (Cunder + Cover) (the classical newsvendor result). If a missed sale costs 4 and a leftover costs 1, stock the 80th percentile. A mean forecast is the right input only when the costs are symmetric.

So you need prediction intervals or, more generally, quantile forecasts, and a loss that rewards good ones: the pinball loss at quantile q, which charges q × (shortfall) when the forecast was too low and (1 − q) × (excess) when it was too high. Its minimiser is the true q-quantile. There are many ways to build intervals; the simplest honest one is empirical: collect the forecast errors your method actually made at each horizon in earlier backtest origins, and add their quantiles to the current point forecast. The script builds 80% intervals for the Holt-Winters forecast in two ways, one that assumes errors have the one-step standard deviation at every horizon (a Gaussian interval), and one from the empirical backtest errors of 60 earlier origins, tests the coverage on 55 later origins, and then runs the stocking rule.

"""From a point forecast to a decision: prediction intervals, pinball loss and a stocking rule."""
import numpy as np
from tslib import *

y, t = make_series()
H = 14
fn = holt_winters
def one_step_resid_sd(hist):                                  # in-sample one-step errors of the same method
    errs = [hist[i] - fn(hist[:i], 1)[0] for i in range(len(hist) - 120, len(hist))]
    return np.std(errs)

def backtest_errors(upto, n_origins=60, step=5):              # forecast errors (by horizon) from origins before `upto`
    E = []
    for o in range(upto - H - (n_origins - 1) * step, upto - H + 1, step):
        E.append(y[o:o + H] - fn(y[:o], H))
    return np.array(E)                                        # shape (n_origins, H)

test_origins = range(700, 1096 - H + 1, 7)
cov_g, cov_e, wid_g, wid_e, pin = [], [], [], [], {"gaussian": [], "empirical": []}
def pinball(q, act, fc): d = act - fc; return np.mean(np.maximum(q * d, (q - 1) * d))
for o in test_origins:
    hist, act = y[:o], y[o:o + H]; pt = fn(hist, H)
    sd = one_step_resid_sd(hist)
    g_lo, g_hi = pt - 1.2816 * sd, pt + 1.2816 * sd           # 80% interval if errors had the one-step sd at every horizon
    E = backtest_errors(o)
    e_lo, e_hi = pt + np.quantile(E, 0.1, axis=0), pt + np.quantile(E, 0.9, axis=0)
    cov_g.append((act >= g_lo) & (act <= g_hi)); cov_e.append((act >= e_lo) & (act <= e_hi))
    wid_g.append((g_hi - g_lo).mean()); wid_e.append((e_hi - e_lo).mean())
    pin["gaussian"].append(pinball(0.1, act, g_lo) + pinball(0.9, act, g_hi))
    pin["empirical"].append(pinball(0.1, act, e_lo) + pinball(0.9, act, e_hi))
cg, ce = np.mean(cov_g, axis=0), np.mean(cov_e, axis=0)
print(f"{len(test_origins)} test origins; target coverage of the 80% interval is 0.80")
print(f"{'':<28}{'horizon 1':>10}{'horizon 7':>10}{'horizon 14':>11}{'all':>8}{'mean width':>12}{'pinball sum':>13}")
print(f"{'one-step Gaussian interval':<28}{cg[0]:>10.2f}{cg[6]:>10.2f}{cg[13]:>11.2f}{cg.mean():>8.2f}{np.mean(wid_g):>12.1f}{np.mean(pin['gaussian']):>13.2f}")
print(f"{'empirical backtest interval':<28}{ce[0]:>10.2f}{ce[6]:>10.2f}{ce[13]:>11.2f}{ce.mean():>8.2f}{np.mean(wid_e):>12.1f}{np.mean(pin['empirical']):>13.2f}")

# A stocking decision: each unit short costs 4, each unit left over costs 1, so stock the 0.8 quantile (critical ratio 4/(4+1)).
cost_pt, cost_q = [], []
for o in test_origins:
    hist, act = y[:o], y[o:o + H]; pt = fn(hist, H)
    E = backtest_errors(o); q80 = pt + np.quantile(E, 0.8, axis=0)
    cost = lambda stock: np.mean(4 * np.maximum(act - stock, 0) + 1 * np.maximum(stock - act, 0))
    cost_pt.append(cost(pt)); cost_q.append(cost(q80))
print(f"\nstocking cost per day: order the mean forecast {np.mean(cost_pt):.1f}   order the 0.8-quantile {np.mean(cost_q):.1f}")

The empirical interval is 19% wider (35.2 against 29.6 on average) and covers 77%, closer to the target; neither is perfect with 55 test origins and a method whose errors vary by weekday. The sum of pinball losses for the two bounds is nearly the same (5.16 and 5.23), a reminder that a proper scoring rule trades off width and coverage and that "closer to 80%" is not the same as "scores better". The decision result is clearer. Many series, intermittent demand and covariates

Three situations change the picture. Many related series (thousands of products in hundreds of stores) can be forecast one at a time with a statistical method, or all at once with a single global model whose features include the series' identity and its statistics. Global models borrow strength across series, which helps the short and noisy ones; they were the dominant approach in M5. Forecasts of related series often need to add up (store forecasts summing to the region's), which is the subject of hierarchical reconciliation in the textbook below. Intermittent demand, where most days have zero sales, breaks methods built for smooth series and percentage errors alike; specialised methods (Croston's method and its variants) and quantile forecasts handle it better. Covariates and censoring: sales are not demand. A product that sold out on Tuesday shows zero sales on Wednesday when demand was not zero, and a model trained on that history will learn to under-forecast. Record stock levels so you can flag or model censored days, and always record what promotion, price and weather were in force.

Systems connection

Capacity planning is forecasting with an on-call pager. Load testing and capacity planning uses the same ingredients (trend, daily and weekly seasonality, headroom for the tail) to decide how many machines to provision, and the over/under cost asymmetry above is the reason to provision for a high quantile and not for the mean. The storage side, ingesting and querying millions of timestamped points, is in search and specialised stores, and the queueing and peak-load arguments in queueing theory explain why the busiest hour sets the capacity.

State of play, October 2026

Two things have changed in the last few years. First, pre-trained time-series models (for example Chronos from Amazon, TimesFM from Google, Moirai from Salesforce) forecast a new series zero-shot, with no training on it, and recent versions also accept covariates. They are strong on public benchmarks such as GIFT-Eval (Aksu et al., 2024) and are a sensible additional baseline. Be careful reading the leaderboards: some entries were pre-trained on data that overlaps the benchmark's training splits, and at least one paper reports models that declare test-data leakage in their own configuration; check how a number was produced before relying on it. Second, boosted trees on lag features and well-tuned smoothing remain competitive, and benchmark results differ across datasets, horizons and metrics. The reliable practice has not changed: run the simple baselines, add a foundation model and a boosted model as challengers, and choose using a rolling-origin backtest on your own data.

Key takeaways

  • Time series break the independence assumption: order carries information, features must come from before the forecast origin, and validation must be forward in time.
  • Think of a series as trend, seasonality and a residual that still has memory. Calendar regression recovered a 0.050-a-day trend as 0.052 and explained 84.9% of the variance.
  • Baselines are strong. Seasonal naive (MASE 1.10) was about 20% behind the best method on a retail-like series; on a random walk the plain naive forecast and simple smoothing won and seasonal methods lost.
  • Use MASE (or another scale-free, zero-safe metric) rather than MAPE, and report the worst backtest origins as well as the mean.
  • Direct lag-feature models with boosted trees were competitive (0.89 against Holt-Winters 0.95), but trees cannot extrapolate: the same model on the raw target scored 1.13.
  • Use a rolling-origin backtest whose horizon matches the decision and refit everything inside each origin.
  • Decisions need quantiles, not means. Stocking the 0.8-quantile cut cost per day from 27.5 to 21.2 with the same model.

Check your understanding

A colleague validates a daily-sales model with random 5-fold cross-validation and reports an error half that of the backtest your team runs. Which explanation is most likely?

  1. Random folds use more training data, so the model is simply better.
  2. Random folds put test days between training days, so the model nearly interpolates autocorrelated neighbours, which is easier than forecasting the future.
  3. The backtest is wrong because it uses a rolling origin.
  4. Cross-validation always underestimates error.
With autocorrelated data, a test day's neighbours on both sides are in training, so the model is closer to filling a gap than predicting ahead. Rolling-origin evaluation reproduces the real task (the past only). More training data is not the issue, and cross-validation is optimistic here for a specific reason, not generally.

On a series that is a random walk, which of these forecasts is expected to do best?

  1. Seasonal naive, since it uses the most structure.
  2. The mean of all history.
  3. A four-week same-weekday average.
  4. The naive forecast (last value), or a lightly smoothed version of it.
For a random walk the last value is the best predictor of every future value, and smoothing it a little reduces noise in the level. Seasonal copying and averaging add noise from a pattern that does not exist, and the mean ignores where the walk currently is (MASE 8.06 in the experiment).

A boosted-tree model trained on raw sales performs well in backtests early in the data but its forecasts lag behind a growing series at later origins. What is the best fix?

  1. Predict the target minus a recent level (or a ratio to it) and add the level back, because trees cannot extrapolate beyond the values seen in training.
  2. Increase the number of trees until the model fits the growth.
  3. Shuffle the training rows so the trees see later values early.
  4. Drop the calendar features, which confuse the trees.
Leaf values are averages of training targets, so predictions stay inside the training range however many trees you add. Re-expressing the target relative to a recent level removes the trend from what the trees must learn. Shuffling breaks the time logic and does not widen the range; the calendar features are not the problem.

Missing a sale costs 9 and a leftover unit costs 1. The forecast distribution for tomorrow has a median of 100. Which stock level minimises expected cost?

  1. The mean, 100, since it is unbiased.
  2. The 50th percentile, because it minimises absolute error.
  3. The 90th percentile, since the critical ratio is 9 / (9 + 1) = 0.9.
  4. The 10th percentile, since leftovers are cheap.
The newsvendor rule stocks the quantile at Cunder / (Cunder + Cover) = 0.9. Because shortages cost nine times as much as surplus, you accept a high chance of leftovers. The median is right only for symmetric costs, and the 10th percentile gets the direction of the asymmetry backwards.

You add the feature "7-day rolling mean of sales" to a daily model, computed with a centred window (3 days before to 3 days after the target day), and the backtest error drops by a third. What should you conclude?

  1. The feature is excellent; keep it.
  2. The window includes the target day and three future days, so it leaks; recompute it from days before the origin and see what remains.
  3. The model needs more lag features.
  4. MASE is unsuitable for rolling features.
A centred window contains the quantity being predicted. The fall in error is the leak, not skill; chapter 14 measured the same effect (held-out R2 0.582 from the centred mean against 0.312 for the trailing one). The fix is a trailing window ending before the origin.

A product sold out on Tuesday and its recorded sales that day are zero. How should this affect training data for a demand forecast?

  1. Nothing: the zero is the truth about sales.
  2. Replace the zero with the mean of all sales.
  3. Drop the product entirely.
  4. Flag or model the day as censored, because observed sales understate demand and a model trained on it will under-forecast.
Sales equal demand only when stock is available. Treating stockout zeros as demand teaches the model that demand disappears, which then causes more stockouts. Flagging the day (or using a censored-demand method) preserves the information; replacing it with an arbitrary mean or dropping the product discards or distorts it.
