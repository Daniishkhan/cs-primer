Anomaly Detection

Some of the most valuable rows in a table are the ones that do not look like the others: a stolen card, a failing pump, a corrupted record. This chapter builds the standard detectors from first principles (z-scores, Mahalanobis distance, nearest-neighbour distances, local outlier factor and isolation forests), shows where each one fails, and ends with the part that decides whether a detector is useful: the arithmetic of rare events.

Why this matters

Anomaly detection is usually asked for when labels are scarce or missing: fraud has been seen only a few times, a new failure mode has never been seen, nobody has time to label a billion log lines. It is also where overconfident projects go to fail, because the quantity you care about is rare, so a detector that sounds accurate can still bury its users in false alarms. After this chapter you will be able to choose a detector by the kind of anomaly you expect, understand why the simplest rules fail in specific ways, see why isolation forests score points by how easily they are cut off from the rest, and convert a false-positive rate and a base rate into the number of alerts a human will actually have to read. If you do have labelled anomalies, the right tools are those of chapter 15; this chapter is for the case where you do not, or where tomorrow's anomaly will not look like yesterday's.

What is an anomaly?

An anomaly (or outlier) is a row that does not fit the pattern of the rest of the data. That definition depends on a model of "the rest", so an anomaly is always relative to an assumption about normal behaviour. Three kinds recur:

Point anomaly
A single row that is unusual on its own: a transaction for 40 times the customer's usual amount.
Contextual anomaly
A row that is unusual given its context but ordinary otherwise: a heating load that is normal in January and alarming in July, or a purchase of 200 pounds that is routine at a supermarket and odd at a bus stop. Time series (chapter 18) are full of these.
Collective anomaly
A group of rows that is unusual together though each member is fine: fifty small transfers in a minute, each unremarkable.

There are also three working set-ups, which differ in what data the model may see. In supervised detection you have labels for both normal and anomalous rows, and the problem is ordinary classification with severe class imbalance. In novelty detection (semi-supervised) you have a training set believed to be clean and ask whether new rows resemble it. In outlier detection (unsupervised) the data is unlabelled and may already contain anomalies, and you look for the strange rows inside it. This chapter mostly addresses the last two. Whatever the set-up, a detector returns a score for each row (bigger means stranger), and turning scores into decisions needs a threshold, which is a business decision and not a statistical one. We come back to that at the end.

Analogy: the new face at a village fete

In a village where everyone knows everyone, a stranger is spotted in seconds, not because anyone has a rule for strangers but because everyone has a strong picture of the faces that belong. A detector does the same: it builds a picture of normal (a mean and spread, a neighbourhood of similar rows, a typical depth in a tree) and measures how badly a new row fits it.

Where it breaks down: in a village, the stranger is probably just a visitor. In data, "does not fit" does not mean "wrong": a rich customer making a large legitimate purchase is as anomalous as a thief. Detectors find the unusual, and a person or a second model decides whether it is bad.

Statistical detectors

The z-score and its blind spot

The oldest detector measures how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean: z = (x − μ)/σ, flagging |z| above 3. For a normal distribution about 0.27 percent of values exceed 3, so a rule that flags 1 in 370 is sensible on clean, bell-shaped data. But the mean and the standard deviation are themselves computed from the data, and anomalies distort both. This is masking: enough outliers inflate σ so much that none of them looks extreme. The cure is robust statistics. The median absolute deviation (MAD), the median of |x − median|, is barely moved by outliers, and the modified z-score 0.6745 (x − median)/MAD (Iglewicz and Hoaglin, 1993) uses it. Tukey's fence flags values beyond Q3 + 1.5 × IQR (or below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR), built from quartiles for the same reason.

Many columns: Mahalanobis distance

Per-column rules fail in a second way, because they ignore how columns relate. Suppose two columns have correlation 0.9 (height and weight, or two sensors on the same machine). Most normal rows lie along a diagonal ridge. A row with a high value on one column and a low value on the other is odd in a way that neither marginal shows. The Mahalanobis distance measures distance in units of the cloud's own shape: d2 = (x − μ)T S−1 (x − μ), where S is the covariance matrix. It equals the ordinary squared distance after the data has been stretched and rotated to be round (A0 chapter 6 on matrices as transformations). If the clean data is roughly Gaussian in p dimensions, d2 follows a chi-square distribution with p degrees of freedom, which gives a principled threshold. For p = 2 the upper 0.1 percent point is −2 ln(0.001) = 13.8.

The script runs both ideas. Part 1: 270 good readings (mean 50, standard deviation 5) with 30 bad ones between 80 and 110, a 10 percent contamination. Part 2: 2,000 normal rows with correlation 0.9, plus three hand-placed odd points whose coordinates are individually modest.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)

# Part 1: one column, 10% of rows are bad readings. Does the z-score notice them?
good = rng.normal(50, 5, 270)
bad = rng.uniform(80, 110, 30)                       # 30 injected anomalies (10% of rows)
x = np.concatenate([good, bad])
z = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()
med = np.median(x); mad = np.median(np.abs(x - med))
mz = 0.6745 * (x - med) / mad                        # "modified z-score": median and MAD instead of mean and sd
q1, q3 = np.percentile(x, [25, 75]); fence = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)
print(f"mean {x.mean():.1f} and sd {x.std():.1f} (clean values: 50 and 5); median {med:.1f}, MAD {mad:.1f}")
print(f"found of 30 anomalies:  z > 3: {(z[270:] > 3).sum():2d}   modified z > 3.5: {(mz[270:] > 3.5).sum():2d}"
      f"   Tukey fence (Q3 + 1.5 IQR = {fence:.1f}): {(x[270:] > fence).sum():2d}")
print(f"false alarms among 270 good rows:  {(z[:270] > 3).sum()}  {(mz[:270] > 3.5).sum()}  {(x[:270] > fence).sum()}")

# Part 2: two correlated columns. Each value looks ordinary on its own; the pair does not.
cov = np.array([[1.0, 0.9], [0.9, 1.0]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], cov, 2000)
odd = np.array([[1.6, -1.6], [-1.7, 1.5], [1.5, -1.8]])  # each coordinate within about 1.8 sd
Xall = np.vstack([X, odd])
mu, S = X.mean(0), np.cov(X.T)
d2 = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Xall - mu, np.linalg.inv(S), Xall - mu)   # squared Mahalanobis distance
zmax = np.abs((Xall - mu) / X.std(0)).max(1)
thr = -2 * np.log(0.001)                             # chi-square, 2 degrees of freedom, upper 0.1 percent
print(f"\nthreshold on squared Mahalanobis distance: {thr:.1f}   (marginal rule: any |z| > 3)")
print(f"normal rows flagged: marginal {(zmax[:2000] > 3).sum()}, Mahalanobis {(d2[:2000] > thr).sum()}  (of 2000)")
for i, p in enumerate(odd):
    print(f"odd point {p}: max |z| = {zmax[2000 + i]:.1f} -> marginal flags: {zmax[2000 + i] > 3};"
          f"  Mahalanobis^2 = {d2[2000 + i]:.1f} -> flags: {d2[2000 + i] > thr}")

found of 30 anomalies:  z > 3: 11   modified z > 3.5: 30   Tukey fence (Q3 + 1.5 IQR = 65.9): 30

odd point [ 1.5 -1.8]: max |z| = 1.9 -> marginal flags: False;  Mahalanobis^2 = 51.4 -> flags: True

In part 1, the injected outliers inflate the standard deviation from 5 to 14.8 and the mean from 50 to 54.8. A plain z-score above 3 then catches only 11 of the 30 anomalies: the other 19 are masked by the damage they did. The median (51.1) and MAD (3.8) are almost untouched, so the modified z-score and Tukey's fence both catch all 30 with no false alarms among the 270 good rows. In part 2, none of the three odd points trips the marginal rule (largest |z| 1.7, 1.8 and 1.9), but each has a squared Mahalanobis distance of 48 to 51, far above 13.8. The marginal rule also flagged 6 of the 2,000 normal rows, against 1 for the Mahalanobis rule, since a rule on two columns separately cannot match the shape of the data. One caveat: the covariance matrix here was estimated from clean data. If outliers are present when you estimate it, they inflate S and mask themselves, exactly as in part 1; the robust version (the minimum covariance determinant estimator) exists for this reason. Both methods also assume one blob. A dataset with several clusters needs a method that does not.

Distance and density methods

Instead of assuming a distribution, ask: how far is this row from its neighbours? The simplest k-nearest-neighbour score is the distance to the row's k-th nearest neighbour (or the mean distance to the k nearest). Isolated rows get large scores. It needs no distributional assumption and handles several clusters, but has a blind spot when clusters differ in density. In a sparse cluster, ordinary points are far from their neighbours; in a dense cluster, a point that is merely 1 unit away from everything may be extraordinary. A single global distance cutoff cannot be right for both.

The local outlier factor (LOF) (Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander, 2000, "LOF: Identifying Density-Based Local Outliers") fixes this by comparing each point's density with the density of its neighbours. In four steps: take the k-distance of a point (distance to its k-th neighbour); define the reachability distance from p to a neighbour o as max(k-distance(o), d(p, o)), which smooths out tiny distances inside tight clumps; define the local reachability density of p as the inverse of its mean reachability distance to its neighbours; and score LOF(p) as the average of the neighbours' density divided by p's own density. A point in a region as dense as its neighbours gets a score near 1; a point much less dense than the neighbours it sits beside gets a score well above 1.

Isolation forests

All the methods so far model what normal looks like, then measure the distance from it. The isolation forest (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008, "Isolation Forest", ICDM; extended in a 2012 TKDD paper) turns the problem round: anomalies are few and different, so they should be easy to separate from everything else. Pick a random column, pick a random split value between that column's minimum and maximum in the current node, and cut. Repeat on each side. A point in an empty region of space will be alone after a few cuts; a point in the middle of a crowd will need many.

The number of cuts needed is the depth of the point in a random binary tree, its path length h(x). One tree is noisy, so the method builds many (100 is typical) and averages. Each tree uses a small random subsample (256 rows in the paper's recommendation), which helps in two ways: it is fast (the cost per tree depends on the subsample, not on the dataset size), and it reduces masking and swamping, since a small sample contains fewer anomalies and fewer clumps for them to hide in. The score converts the average depth into a number between 0 and 1: s(x) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(ψ), where ψ is the subsample size and c(ψ) = 2(ln(ψ − 1) + 0.5772) − 2(ψ − 1)/ψ is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree, used to normalise by the depth you would expect for a point that is not special. A score near 1 means the point was isolated much faster than average, near 0.5 means average, below 0.5 means harder to isolate than average. The depth limit of each tree is only about log2 ψ, because we only care about points that are isolated early.

All four on one dataset

The script implements all of this (LOF and the isolation forest in under 30 lines each) and scores the dataset in the figure: 600 normal points in two clusters, 16 far-away global anomalies and 15 local anomalies in a ring just outside the dense cluster. For each detector it reports the AUC (the chance that a randomly chosen anomaly scores higher than a randomly chosen normal row) separately for the two kinds of anomaly. An AUC of 0.5 is chance. The detectors are one global Mahalanobis distance, the kNN distance, LOF and the isolation forest.

import numpy as np

def make_data(rng):
    dense = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (400, 2))                       # tight cluster, centre (0, 0)
    sparse = rng.normal(0, 2.5, (200, 2)) + [9, 0]             # wide cluster, centre (9, 0)
    glob = np.c_[rng.uniform(-8, 22, 16), rng.choice([-1, 1], 16) * rng.uniform(7, 9, 16)]  # far-away points
    ang = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 15); rad = rng.uniform(1.0, 1.6, 15)
    local = np.c_[rad * np.cos(ang), rad * np.sin(ang)]         # a ring just outside the dense cluster
    X = np.vstack([dense, sparse, glob, local])
    kind = np.r_[np.zeros(600, int), np.ones(len(glob), int), np.full(15, 2)]
    return X, kind                                              # kind: 0 normal, 1 global, 2 local anomaly

def dist(A, B):
    return np.sqrt(np.maximum((A ** 2).sum(1)[:, None] + (B ** 2).sum(1)[None] - 2 * A @ B.T, 0))

def knn_score(X, k=10):                                          # distance to the k-th nearest neighbour
    D = dist(X, X); np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
    return np.sort(D, 1)[:, k - 1]

def lof(X, k=20):                                                # local outlier factor (Breunig et al., 2000)
    D = dist(X, X); np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
    nn = np.argsort(D, 1)[:, :k]; kd = np.sort(D, 1)[:, k - 1]   # neighbours and k-distance
    reach = np.maximum(D[np.arange(len(X))[:, None], nn], kd[nn])  # reachability distance to each neighbour
    lrd = 1.0 / reach.mean(1)                                    # local reachability density
    return lrd[nn].mean(1) / lrd                                 # neighbours' density relative to mine

def c(n):                                                        # average path length of an unsuccessful BST search
    return 0.0 if n <= 1 else 2 * (np.log(n - 1) + 0.5772156649) - 2 * (n - 1) / n

def itree_depth(x, S, rng, limit, depth=0):
    if depth >= limit or len(S) <= 1: return depth + c(len(S))
    j = rng.integers(S.shape[1]); lo, hi = S[:, j].min(), S[:, j].max()
    if lo == hi: return depth + c(len(S))
    t = rng.uniform(lo, hi)                                      # random feature, random split value
    side = S[:, j] < t
    return itree_depth(x, S[side] if x[j] < t else S[~side], rng, limit, depth + 1)

def iforest(X, rng, trees=100, psi=128):
    limit = int(np.ceil(np.log2(psi))); depth = np.zeros(len(X))
    for _ in range(trees):
        S = X[rng.choice(len(X), psi, replace=False)]            # each tree sees a small random subsample
        depth += [itree_depth(x, S, rng, limit) for x in X]
    return 2 ** (-(depth / trees) / c(psi))                      # near 1: isolated quickly; below 0.5: deep

def auc(score, pos):                                             # P(random anomaly outscores random normal)
    r = np.argsort(np.argsort(score)) + 1.0
    n1 = pos.sum(); n0 = len(pos) - n1
    return (r[pos].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
    X, kind = make_data(rng)
    print(f"{len(X)} points: {np.sum(kind == 0)} normal, {np.sum(kind == 1)} global anomalies, {np.sum(kind == 2)} local anomalies")
    d = np.sqrt(((X - X.mean(0)) @ np.linalg.inv(np.cov(X.T)) * (X - X.mean(0))).sum(1))
    scores = {"Mahalanobis (global)": d, "kNN distance (k=10)": knn_score(X), "LOF (k=20)": lof(X),
              "isolation forest": iforest(X, rng)}
    print(f"{'AUC':22s} {'global':>8s} {'local':>8s}")
    for name, s in scores.items():
        g = auc(s[kind != 2], kind[kind != 2] == 1); l = auc(s[kind != 1], kind[kind != 1] == 2)
        print(f"{name:22s} {g:8.3f} {l:8.3f}")
631 points: 600 normal, 16 global anomalies, 15 local anomalies
AUC                      global    local
Mahalanobis (global)      1.000    0.384
kNN distance (k=10)       1.000    0.678
LOF (k=20)                0.993    0.992
isolation forest          0.999    0.692

The global anomalies are easy for everyone: AUC 1.000 for the Mahalanobis and kNN distances, 0.999 for the isolation forest and 0.993 for LOF. The local anomalies separate the methods. The single-Gaussian Mahalanobis distance gets 0.384, worse than chance, because the ring lies near the middle of the data's overall mean and spread, so that model of normal counts them among the more ordinary points. The kNN distance reaches 0.678: the ring points have large distances to their 10th neighbour, but not larger than many ordinary points in the sparse cluster. The isolation forest gets 0.692: ring points are cut off fairly quickly, but so are the thin outer parts of the sparse cluster, which mixes them in with ordinary points (I did not test this explanation further). LOF reaches 0.992, because it asks the right question: a ring point's neighbours (dense-cluster points) are far denser than it is. The lesson is not that LOF is best. It is that the kind of anomaly you expect decides the detector. Had the anomalies been global only, a Mahalanobis rule or an isolation forest would have been the cheaper choice, and LOF costs an n × n distance table in this form.

The isolation forest has its own failure modes. Its cuts are axis-parallel, so anomalies that differ from normal only along a diagonal direction, like the off-ridge points of the correlated example, may be cut off little faster than ordinary points; scaling columns helps with units, and rotating or adding combined columns can help with diagonals. Columns that carry no information add random cuts that waste depth. With no labels to check against, anomaly scores from a column set you have not looked at are a hypothesis, not a finding.

Common misconception

"The detector flagged it, so it is a fraud, a fault or an error." A detector reports that a row is unusual under its model of normal. It does not say why, and the reason is often innocent: a new product launch, a holiday, a first-time customer, a sensor that was recalibrated. Equally, a row that is not flagged is not safe: a clever adversary imitates normal behaviour, and a failure mode that has become common is no longer an anomaly. Detection is the first stage of a pipeline whose next stage (a person, a rule, a supervised model trained on the confirmed cases) is where "bad" gets decided. The explanations of chapter 16 can help, for example by showing which columns made a row unusual.

Other methods, briefly

  • One-class SVM fits a boundary around the training data in a kernel feature space (chapter 7) and flags points outside it. It is a novelty detector, sensitive to the kernel width and to scaling, and slow beyond a few tens of thousands of rows.
  • Mixture models fit several Gaussians and flag rows with low likelihood, extending the Mahalanobis idea to several clusters.
  • Reconstruction error. Compress with PCA (chapter 11) or an autoencoder (A2) and flag rows that the compressed model cannot rebuild. Normal rows lie on the structure the model learnt; anomalies do not.
  • Supervised models on partial labels. Once reviewers confirm some alerts, those become labels; a classifier trained on them, with the care that imbalance requires, usually overtakes any unsupervised score. The unsupervised detector then serves to find candidates and new patterns.

Thresholds, base rates and review capacity

Evaluating a detector without labels is hard, and when you do have a few, the accuracy you compute can be misleading. Rare events turn small false-positive rates into overwhelming numbers of false alarms, a form of base rate neglect (compare the precision and recall of chapter 2). Take a day with 1,000,000 events of which 0.1 percent (1,000) are truly anomalous, and a detector that catches 90 percent of the anomalies. The script varies only the false-positive rate, the share of normal events wrongly flagged:

# Base rates: what a detector's false-positive rate means when anomalies are rare.
events, prevalence, tpr = 1_000_000, 0.001, 0.90
pos = events * prevalence; neg = events - pos
print(f"{events:,} events, {prevalence:.1%} anomalous ({pos:,.0f}), detector catches {tpr:.0%} of them")
print(f"{'false-positive rate':>20s} {'alerts':>9s} {'true alerts':>12s} {'precision':>10s}")
for fpr in (0.05, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001):
    tp, fp = tpr * pos, fpr * neg
    print(f"{fpr:>20.2%} {tp + fp:>9,.0f} {tp:>12,.0f} {tp / (tp + fp):>10.1%}")
cap = 500                                    # assume the million events arrive in one day
fpr = 0.01
print(f"\nreviewers can read {cap} alerts a day; at a {fpr:.0%} false-positive rate that day produces "
      f"{tpr * pos + fpr * neg:,.0f} alerts, {(tpr * pos + fpr * neg) / cap:.0f} times capacity")

At a 1 percent false-positive rate, which sounds excellent, the detector raises 10,890 alerts and only 900 are real: a precision of 8.3 percent, with reviewers who can read 500 alerts a day facing 22 times their capacity. Even a 0.1 percent false-positive rate (1 in 1,000 normal events) gives precision below half. A useful design therefore starts at the end of the pipeline. How many alerts can be reviewed? What does a missed anomaly cost compared with a wasted review? That sets a threshold, and often the right form is not "score above x" but "the top 500 scores per day", which keeps the workload stable when the score distribution drifts. This is the cost-based threshold reasoning of chapter 15, and the design problem is worked in Studio 2 (fraud detection).

Ways to evaluate when labels are scarce, from cheapest to most informative: (1) review a sample of the top scores and record the precision at the top: even 50 reviewed alerts tell you more than any internal metric; (2) inject synthetic anomalies of the kinds you fear (a copied record with one value changed, a replayed sequence) and measure recall on them, remembering that you only measure the anomalies you imagined; (3) backtest on known incidents: would the detector have scored last year's confirmed failures high, and how many false alerts would it have produced meanwhile; (4) watch the alert rate in production, since a sudden change in the share of flagged rows is itself a drift signal (chapter 21).

Systems connection

Anomaly scores are the engine behind many monitoring and alerting systems, and the failure mode is the same as the base-rate table: too many false positives and the on-call stops reading alerts. Alerting & On-Call argues for alerting on symptoms users feel and for keeping pages rare, which is the same discipline as setting a review budget for a detector; a model that raises an alert should earn its page. In security, anomaly detectors on authentication and network logs face adversaries who adapt, so see threat modelling before assuming that today's normal will stay normal.

Key takeaways

  • "Anomaly" is relative to a model of normal. Point, contextual and collective anomalies need different detectors, and a flagged row is unusual, not necessarily bad.
  • The z-score is fragile: outliers inflate the standard deviation (masking; 11 of 30 found in our run). Use the median and MAD or Tukey's fence, and the Mahalanobis distance when columns are correlated.
  • Distance-based scores handle clusters but a single cutoff fails when densities differ; LOF compares each point with its neighbours' density and caught the local anomalies (AUC 0.992 against 0.68 for kNN distance).
  • Isolation forests score by how few random cuts isolate a row (2.2 against 10.0 in our figure); they are fast and assumption-light, but axis-parallel and weak on diagonal or local anomalies.
  • Choose the detector by the kind of anomaly expected, and use contamination in the training data with care: outliers corrupt the very statistics used to find them.
  • Rare events make false positives dominant: at 0.1 percent prevalence and a 1 percent false-positive rate, precision is 8.3 percent. Set the threshold from review capacity and cost, evaluate on reviewed top-k alerts, and monitor the alert rate.

Check your understanding

A sensor feed has 10 percent of its values wildly wrong. You flag any value more than 3 standard deviations from the mean and catch only about a third of them. What explains the misses and what is the fix?

  1. The bad values inflate the standard deviation (masking); use the median and MAD, or Tukey's fence
  2. 3 standard deviations is too strict; use 2
  3. The bad values are not outliers, because there are too many of them
  4. The mean is biased downwards; use a trimmed standard deviation only
In the listing, 30 bad values among 300 raised the standard deviation from 5 to 14.8, so only 11 of 30 exceeded 3. The median and MAD barely moved and caught all 30. Lowering the cutoff to 2 would raise false alarms on a heavy-tailed column without addressing the cause, and having many bad values does not make them normal.

Two sensors on a pump are correlated at 0.9. A reading has each sensor within 2 standard deviations of its mean, but one is high and the other low. Why does Mahalanobis distance flag it when per-column z-scores do not?

  1. It uses a lower threshold than z-scores
  2. It averages the two z-scores, which exposes the difference
  3. It measures distance relative to the cloud's shape, so a point off the correlation ridge is far away even when each coordinate is ordinary
  4. It ignores the second sensor
Mahalanobis distance rescales space by the covariance matrix, so directions in which the data barely varies (across the ridge) count for much more than directions in which it varies a lot. Our odd points had max |z| of 1.7 to 1.9 and squared distances of 48 to 51 against a threshold of 13.8. Its threshold comes from a chi-square distribution, not from being lower, and it uses both sensors together.

A dataset has a very dense cluster and a very sparse one. Points just outside the dense cluster have a 10th-neighbour distance similar to ordinary points in the sparse cluster. Which detector is best placed to flag them, and why?

  1. A global Mahalanobis distance, because it uses all points
  2. A kNN distance score, because those points are far from their neighbours
  3. A z-score on each column, because it is simplest
  4. LOF, because it compares a point's density with that of its neighbours, so it sees that these points are much less dense than the cluster beside them
In the experiment LOF reached a local-anomaly AUC of 0.992, against 0.678 for kNN distance and 0.384 for Mahalanobis (the ring is near the overall centre of the data). The kNN distance cannot separate them because a single distance scale does not fit both clusters, and a z-score ignores local density entirely.

Why does an isolation forest build each tree on a small random subsample (for example 256 rows) instead of the whole dataset?

  1. To reduce memory only; accuracy is unaffected
  2. It makes training fast and reduces masking, since fewer anomalies and fewer dense clumps are present for anomalies to hide in
  3. Because path lengths cannot be computed on more than 256 rows
  4. Because the algorithm needs fewer rows than columns
Subsampling keeps each tree's cost independent of dataset size and, as the paper argues, reduces masking (an anomaly hidden among similar anomalies) and swamping (normal points close to anomalies). Option 1 misses the accuracy benefit. Path lengths can be computed on any size, and there is no such row-versus-column requirement.

A fraud team has a detector with a 1 percent false-positive rate and 90 percent recall on 1,000,000 daily events, 0.1 percent of which are fraud, with 500 alerts reviewable a day. Which action most improves the situation?

  1. Raise recall from 90 to 95 percent, since missing fraud is the main risk
  2. Review all 10,890 alerts by hiring more staff each time the model changes
  3. Cut the false-positive rate (or rank alerts and review only the top 500), because precision of 8.3 percent is dominated by the 9,990 false alarms
  4. Accept the detector, since 1 percent is a small error rate
With only 1,000 true cases, the 9,990 false alarms (1 percent of 999,000) outnumber the 900 true ones more than ten to one. Moving recall from 90 to 95 percent adds 50 true alerts to 10,890; cutting the false-positive rate to 0.1 percent lifts precision to about 47 percent. Ranking and reviewing the top 500 also bounds the workload. A 1 percent rate is small only against the wrong baseline, the 999,000 normal events.

You have no labels at all and want to know whether a new unsupervised detector is any good. Which step gives the most trustworthy evidence?

  1. Have domain experts review a random sample of the highest-scoring rows and record the proportion that are genuinely of interest
  2. Check that its scores have a bell-shaped distribution
  3. Compare its top-scored rows with another unsupervised detector and treat agreement as accuracy
  4. Report the proportion of rows it flags as the detection rate
Expert review of the top scores measures what you care about, precision where the alerts will be read. A bell-shaped score distribution says nothing about whether the high scores matter. Two detectors can agree on the same unrepresentative rows, and the share flagged is a property of the threshold, not of correctness.
