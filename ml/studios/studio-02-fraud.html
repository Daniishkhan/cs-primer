Design a Fraud Detection Model

About one card payment in a thousand is fraud. The labels arrive weeks late, the people on the other side change tactics when you catch them, and a small team can review only a sliver of the traffic. This studio designs the model around those three facts instead of around an accuracy number.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock working session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the data-science loop: clarify, estimate, choose metrics, understand the labels, build features, baseline, set the operating point against a limited queue, draw the system, plan for an adversary, roll out, and summarise aloud. A rubric at the end separates a competent answer from a senior one. The experiments run on a synthetic transaction log generated with a fixed seed (about 1.4 million rows over 91 days); the volumes, costs and delays in the brief are assumptions, labelled as such, so change them and see which conclusions survive.

📐 The brief

A payments company says: "We process about 10 million card transactions a month and lose roughly a million dollars a year to fraud. Build a model that scores every transaction as it is authorised, so fraud is stopped before the money moves, without declining good customers."

  • The decision must be made during authorisation: the model has a latency budget measured in tens of milliseconds, not minutes.
  • A team of ten fraud analysts reviews flagged transactions and can handle roughly 16,000 cases a month. Everything else is automatic.
  • Fraud is confirmed by cardholder reports and chargebacks, which arrive days to months after the transaction.
  • Fraudsters probe the system. When a pattern stops working they change it.
  • Builds on cross-validation, leakage and imbalance, thresholds and calibration; see also anomaly detection and the calibration lab.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes. The scripts use NumPy only.

  • The load is modest: about 4 transactions a second on average and a few tens at peak. Scoring is not a scale problem; it is a latency and freshness problem (see tail latency: a slow feature lookup, not the model, is what breaks the budget).
  • Review capacity is 0.16% of traffic, about 1.6 times the number of frauds. If every alert were fraud, analysts could cover everything. At 20% precision they reach about a third. Precision at the top of the list is what the business buys with analyst time.
  • A model trained today is blind to most of the last week. Only about a third of last week's fraud has been reported, so two thirds are sitting in the training table labelled as legitimate. A naive pipeline treats them as clean examples, which is the opposite of the truth for exactly the most recent, most informative attack.
  • Money does not reconcile yet. These assumptions imply $1.2 million a month, about $14 million a year, with no controls, while the brief says roughly a million a year is lost. Either existing controls already stop most fraud, or the assumed rate or amount is too high. Reconcile this with finance before promising value: if the residual loss is small, the model's worth lies in fewer false declines and less review labour, and that changes the metric.

3Metrics and the cost of mistakes

Which metrics will you report, and which one decides whether a model ships? What goes wrong if you optimise ROC AUC? Write the expected cost of an alert.

  • ROC AUC hides the problem. With 99.9% legitimate traffic, a model can have an AUC of 0.98 and still send analysts a list that is 90% false alarms, because even a 2% false-positive rate is 200 alerts per 10,000 legitimate transactions, compared with 10 frauds.
  • Expected cost of acting on a transaction with fraud probability p and amount a: decline cost = (1 − p) × (lost sale + friction); allow cost = p × (a + chargeback fee); review cost = analyst time + the residual risk. Act when the expected cost of acting is below the expected cost of allowing. That is the principled threshold (chapter 2, chapter 15).
  • Slice every metric: by merchant category, card-present or not, country, new versus old cards, and amount band. An average that is good can hide a segment that is bad; see statistics for experiments for the interval arithmetic with 140 frauds in a week.

4Labels: delayed, censored and selected

List every source of fraud labels and what is wrong with each. What is the label of a transaction from yesterday? Which training rows should the model be allowed to learn from?

Compare with a worked answer
SourceDelayWhat is wrong with it
Cardholder report or dispute (chargeback)Days to monthsLate, incomplete (some customers never notice), and sometimes wrong (a disputed purchase that was genuine, called friendly fraud)
Analyst decision on a reviewed caseHoursFast and fairly accurate, but only exists for the cases the previous model sent to the queue: a selection-biased sample
Step-up outcome (code entered or not)Seconds to minutesFail does not mean fraud; a good customer with no phone fails too
Declined transactionsNeverNo outcome: you will never know what a declined payment would have been (the counterfactual is missing)

  • Yesterday's transaction has no label yet, not a negative one. The table needs a column for label status (unknown, confirmed fraud, confirmed legitimate) and the date it was set. "Not reported" must not be encoded as 0 without a maturity rule.
  • Maturity cut-off: a row counts as legitimate only when its age exceeds the point by which, say, 95% of fraud is reported (30 days in the estimate above). Younger rows are either excluded from the negative class or given weights.
  • Selection bias is a second, subtler leak. Training on analyst-labelled cases teaches the new model to imitate the old model. Keep a small random sample (say 0.1% of traffic, reviewed regardless of score) as an unbiased test set and for calibration. It is expensive: at a 0.1% fraud rate, finding thirty frauds by random review means opening about 30,000 transactions, which is nearly two months of analyst capacity. Sample more densely from the middle of the ranking, where fraud is five percent instead of a tenth of a percent (stage 7), and reweight to correct for the sampling.
  • Declines remove data. If you decline everything above a threshold, you never observe its outcome, so the model cannot learn that it was right or wrong about those cases. A small percentage of high-score approvals ("let it through, with monitoring") buys the counterfactual at a measured cost; that is a business decision to put to the risk team, not a default.

5Data and features

Which features would you build, which are available in the authorisation window, and how do you guarantee that training and online serving compute them identically? Name two leaks.

Compare with a worked answer

  • Training-serving consistency is the central engineering risk. The velocity count "transactions in the last hour" must be computed at training time exactly as the online counter would have had it at that instant, from the event log, never from today's table. Use one definition and a point-in-time join (chapter 17); compare logged online features against recomputed offline values daily.
  • Leak 1: reputation features that use later labels. "Fraud rate of this merchant" computed over the whole dataset includes outcomes after the transaction. It must use only labels known at the time, and, because of reporting delay, labels that had arrived by then.
  • Leak 2: features written by the case-handling process. Account status, card-blocked flags or "reissued card" are set after fraud is found; they are consequences of the label (chapter 14).
  • Protected attributes and proxies: avoid features that encode protected characteristics, and audit decline and step-up rates by group (compare the COMPAS case on competing definitions of fair).
  • The new-card cold start: a new card has no history, so history features are missing exactly where fraud is concentrated. Make "missing" an explicit signal and give the model transaction-level features that need no history.

6Baselines, a first model and the label-delay experiment

What are the baselines? Then train on "today's table" three ways (oracle labels, naive labels, only matured rows) and test on the following week's true labels. Predict which wins and by how much.

Compare with a worked answer

The synthetic log has two attack types. Attack A (stolen cards) has large amounts, new devices and some distance from home, from day 0. Attack B (card testing) has tiny amounts at high velocity, and starts on day 45. We stand on day 56: attack B is eleven days old, so only a fraction of its labels exist. The model is a logistic regression (the A0 maths, chapter 5) on seven features; a boosted-tree model would be the next candidate (chapter 9). The review capacity K is 0.16% of the week's traffic.

First the shared generator, saved as s2_gen.py. It defines the transactions, the two attacks and the reporting delay, and a small logistic regression trained by gradient descent. The experiments import it.

"""Fraud: synthetic card transactions, two attack types, delayed labels. Shared by the experiments."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
DAYS, PER_DAY, B_START, EVADE_START = 91, 15_000, 45, 63   # attack B appears on day 45; attack B starts evading on day 63

def make():
    day = np.repeat(np.arange(DAYS), PER_DAY); n = len(day)
    kind = np.zeros(n, int)                                   # 0 legit, 1 attack A (stolen card), 2 attack B (card testing), 3 attack B after it learns to evade
    kind[rng.random(n) < 0.0008] = 1
    kind[(day >= B_START) & (rng.random(n) < 0.0005)] = 2
    amount = np.exp(rng.normal(3.4, 0.9, n))                  # legit: median about $30
    vel = rng.poisson(0.15, n).astype(float); new_dev = (rng.random(n) < 0.03).astype(float)
    far = (rng.random(n) < 0.03).astype(float); night = (rng.random(n) < 0.12).astype(float)
    risk = rng.beta(2, 8, n)
    a, b = kind == 1, kind == 2
    amount[a] = np.exp(rng.normal(4.5, 0.9, a.sum()));  vel[a] = rng.poisson(0.8, a.sum())
    new_dev[a] = rng.random(a.sum()) < 0.35; far[a] = rng.random(a.sum()) < 0.25
    night[a] = rng.random(a.sum()) < 0.30;   risk[a] = rng.beta(3.5, 4.5, a.sum())
    amount[b] = np.exp(rng.normal(1.6, 0.5, b.sum())); vel[b] = rng.poisson(2.5, b.sum())
    new_dev[b] = rng.random(b.sum()) < 0.45; far[b] = rng.random(b.sum()) < 0.08
    night[b] = rng.random(b.sum()) < 0.20;   risk[b] = rng.beta(2.5, 6, b.sum())
    ev = b & (rng.random(n) < np.clip((day - EVADE_START) / 28, 0, 1) * 0.9)         # card testers learning to stay under the velocity signal
    amount[ev] = np.exp(rng.normal(2.4, 0.6, ev.sum())); vel[ev] = rng.poisson(0.3, ev.sum())
    new_dev[ev] = rng.random(ev.sum()) < 0.20; far[ev] = rng.random(ev.sum()) < 0.08
    night[ev] = rng.random(ev.sum()) < 0.20;  risk[ev] = rng.beta(2.5, 6, ev.sum())
    kind[ev] = 3
    delay = rng.exponential(10, n)                            # days until the fraud is reported or charged back
    X = np.column_stack([np.log1p(amount), vel, new_dev, far, night, risk, ((amount < 15) & (vel >= 2)).astype(float)])
    return dict(day=day, kind=kind, y=(kind > 0).astype(int), amount=amount, X=X, delay=delay)

D = make()

def observed_label(today):
    """Label a model can see on `today`: fraud counts only once its report has arrived; the rest looks legitimate."""
    return (D["y"] == 1) & (D["day"] + D["delay"] <= today)

def fit(X, y, w=None, lr=1.0, steps=150, l2=1e-4):
    mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0) + 1e-9; Z = (X - mu) / sd
    w = np.ones(len(y)) if w is None else w
    th, b = np.zeros(Z.shape[1]), np.log(np.average(y, weights=w) / (1 - np.average(y, weights=w)))
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(Z @ th + b))); g = w * (p - y)
        th -= lr * (Z.T @ g / w.sum() + l2 * th); b -= lr * g.sum() / w.sum()
    return lambda X2: ((X2 - mu) / sd) @ th + b

Then the experiment.

  • Training on naive labels barely hurts the overall number, because attack A fraud is old and fully labelled, and the model sees enough of attack B (about 60% of B is caught at K) from the reports that have already arrived. 7The review queue and the operating point

You can review K cases a week. Which K, and in what order? Compare ranking by probability against ranking by expected loss (probability times amount). Is the model's score a probability?

Compare with a worked answer

The next script scores the test week with the "naive labels" model and sends the top K cases to review, for several K, ranking two ways. It charges $4 per review (five minutes of analyst time, an assumption) and counts as benefit the fraud amount that review stops. It then checks whether the score behaves like a probability.

"""Fraud: a limited review queue. Rank by probability, or by expected loss = probability x amount?"""
import numpy as np
from s2_gen import D, fit, observed_label
TODAY = 56
tr = D["day"] < TODAY; te = (D["day"] >= TODAY) & (D["day"] < TODAY + 7)
f = fit(D["X"][tr], observed_label(TODAY)[tr].astype(float))
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-f(D["X"][te]))); y = D["y"][te]; amt = D["amount"][te]
print(f"test week {te.sum():,} transactions, {y.sum()} fraud worth ${amt[y == 1].sum():,.0f}; mean score {p.mean():.4f} vs fraud rate {y.mean():.4f}")

REVIEW_COST = 4.0                                  # $ of analyst time per case (5 minutes)
def run(order, k):
    top = order[:k]
    return y[top].sum(), (amt * y)[top].sum(), (amt * y)[top].sum() - REVIEW_COST * k

by_p = np.argsort(-p, kind="stable"); by_loss = np.argsort(-(p * amt), kind="stable")
print(f"\n{'K':>5} | {'rank by p: caught':>17} {'$ stopped':>10} {'net $':>8} | {'rank by p x amount: caught':>26} {'$ stopped':>10} {'net $':>8}")
for k in (50, 100, 168, 300, 600, 1500):
    a = run(by_p, k); b = run(by_loss, k)
    print(f"{k:5d} | {a[0]:17d} {a[1]:10,.0f} {a[2]:8,.0f} | {b[0]:26d} {b[1]:10,.0f} {b[2]:8,.0f}")

# is the score a probability? compare the mean score with the observed fraud rate in slices of the ranking
print("\nreliability of the score (ranked by p):")
for lo, hi in ((0, 168), (168, 1000), (1000, 10_000), (10_000, len(p))):

  • Ranking by probability fills the queue with card testing. The model is most sure about the small, high-velocity attempts (attack B), which are cheap. At K = 168 it stops $1,142 of fraud, against $6,535 for ranking by probability times amount. The first ranking catches more frauds (37 against 23) but the second catches the expensive ones, and its net benefit is about twelve times higher. The dollar figures rest on a handful of large transactions, so treat them as an illustration of direction.
  • But expected-loss ranking neglects card testing, which comes first. Testers probe a stolen card with small purchases and then spend big. A pure dollar ranking ignores the precursor signal. In practice a hybrid is used: block or step up a card on testing behaviour (a rule or a card-level score), and rank the review queue by expected loss.
  • The score is not a probability. In the top 168 the mean score is 0.018 while 22% are fraud; in the next 800 it is 0.004 against 5%. The ordering is useful and the scale is wrong by an order of magnitude, because the model is a weak, regularised linear model trained on 0.13% positives. Before multiplying a score by an amount to get dollars, calibrate it on a mature, recent, representative window (isotonic regression or Platt scaling, as in the calibration lab). The ranking by p × amount above is only right if the order of p × amount is not distorted by the miscalibration; verify rather than assume.
  • The curve of net benefit is flat near its peak and falls when K grows, because each added case is less likely to be fraud. Plot net benefit against K and pick the point where the marginal case costs more than it saves, or the capacity, whichever is smaller.
  • A three-zone policy beats a single threshold: auto-decline above a high-confidence score where precision is very high; step-up or queue in the middle band; approve below. The middle band can be sized to the analyst capacity, and its width adjusts daily to the day's volume so the queue never overflows.

8Architecture

Draw the real-time path, the review loop and the label loop. Where do features come from at request time, what happens when the scoring service is slow or down, and where do the labels return?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Latency budget (an assumption): about 50 ms for the whole check: 5 to 10 ms for rules and model, 10 to 20 ms for the feature-store read. Fetch all features in one batched call, set a hard timeout, and keep the 99th percentile in the SLO (SLIs and SLOs).
  • Failure policy is a business rule written down: if scoring times out, fall back to the rule set, and if that fails, allow with a lower limit for known-good cards. Failing closed (declining everything) turns an outage into lost revenue; failing open (approving everything) invites fraudsters to cause the outage. See overload and load shedding.
  • One feature definition feeds the online store (from the stream) and the offline training set (from the same log), which is the point of a feature store. Streams and exactly-once concerns are in streams and change data capture and logs and exactly-once: a double-counted event doubles a velocity feature.
  • Every decision is logged with the model version, the features seen, the score and the action, so a decline can be explained and the next training set can be rebuilt as of any date.

Systems connection

The review queue is a bounded queue with a human service rate. Queueing theory applies: if flagged cases arrive faster than analysts clear them, the backlog grows without limit and cases are reviewed days late, when the money is gone. A threshold that adapts to the day's backlog is load shedding for people.

9An adversary who adapts

After launch, the card testers learn that high velocity gets them blocked and start spreading their attempts. Predict what happens to precision over the next four weeks with a frozen model, and with weekly retraining on the labels that exist by then.

Compare with a worked answer

From day 63 an increasing share of attack B (up to 90% by day 91) switches to an evasive profile: lower velocity, slightly larger amounts, fewer new devices. This is the same pipeline as the previous experiments; nothing about the model is broken. Three strategies are compared: a model frozen at day 56, a naive weekly retrain, and a weekly retrain that gives zero weight to rows from the last 14 days with no report yet.

"""Fraud: an adversary who adapts. A frozen model against two ways of retraining weekly on labels that arrive late."""
import numpy as np
from s2_gen import D, fit, observed_label

def train(start, drop_recent_unlabelled):
    tr = D["day"] < start; seen = observed_label(start)
    w = np.ones(tr.sum())
    if drop_recent_unlabelled:                       # rows of the last 14 days with no report yet are unknown, not legitimate
        w[(D["day"][tr] >= start - 14) & ~seen[tr]] = 0.0
    return fit(D["X"][tr], seen[tr].astype(float), w)

frozen = train(56, False)
print("precision@K and share of the week's fraud caught; K = 0.16% of the week's transactions")

    K = int(te.sum() * 0.0016); y, kind = D["y"][te], D["kind"][te]
    evade = (kind == 3).sum() / max(1, ((kind == 2) | (kind == 3)).sum())

  • Everyone decays. Precision at K falls from 22% to about 11% for the frozen model as the share of evading fraud goes from 0 to 77%. This is concept drift driven by an adversary (compare the flu-trends case on drift): the relationship between features and label is changed by people who respond to the model.
  • Retraining on delayed labels does not rescue you. The naive retrain is no better than the frozen model, and in the last week it is worse, because the newest evasive fraud is unreported and so is taught to the model as legitimate traffic. Dropping unconfirmed rows helps a little. The week-to-week differences are within noise (140 frauds a week), so read the pattern, not the digits.
  • What does help is shortening the loop, not the retraining cadence. Analyst-confirmed labels arrive in hours; sample the middle of the ranking for them (the 5% band in stage 7), so the queue discovers evasive cases the model scores low; engineer features that the adversary cannot cheaply change (card age, device history, cross-card links rather than per-card velocity); and keep fast, hand-written rules for newly seen patterns.
  • Monitor the review queue's precision daily. It is the earliest label-free-ish signal: analysts confirm within hours, and a sudden fall means the model has stopped finding what analysts are paid to find. Also watch the score distribution and feature drift (chapter 21).
  • Do not publish the decision boundary. Reason codes given to customers or merchants should be coarse. A decline message that says "velocity above 3 per hour" is a manual for the tester. This is a threat-modelling question (abuse and bots, threat modelling).

Common misconception

"Retrain more often and the model will keep up." Retraining only helps when the new data contains the new pattern and labels for it. With a ten-day mean reporting delay, a week of evasive fraud is mostly unlabelled when you retrain. Frequency without faster, less biased labels gives a model that is fresh and wrong.

10Validation, rollout and monitoring

How do you prove the new model is better before it declines real customers? Describe the evaluation, the shadow period, the staged launch and what you monitor afterwards.

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Offline, by time: rolling-origin evaluation over at least eight weeks, training on matured data and testing on the next week with matured labels (chapter 13); report precision and recall at K, dollars stopped, calibration, and the newest-attack segment.
  2. Shadow mode (four weeks): the candidate scores live traffic and its decisions are logged, not acted on. Compare against the champion on the same transactions once labels mature. This also catches training-serving skew, because you compare online features with offline recomputation.
  3. Challenger on a slice: route 5% of traffic, chosen by hashing the card id so a card sees one model only, to the challenger for the review queue and step-up. Watch the review queue's precision, step-up pass rate and complaint rate daily.
  4. Ramp to 100% while keeping a 1% to 2% holdout that always runs the previous model: the only clean comparison left when labels are late.
  5. Rollback is a configuration flag, tested before launch. The rules layer stays as the safety net.

11Trade-offs to defend

List four decisions where a reasonable engineer would choose differently, and say what would change your mind.

Compare with a worked answer
  • Boosted trees against a linear model. Trees capture interactions (small amount and high velocity and new device) without hand-built features and usually win on tabular data; a linear model is simpler to calibrate, explain and debug. Start with the linear model as a baseline; adopt boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost) when they beat it on the rolling evaluation by more than the week-to-week noise, then calibrate.
  • Supervised model against anomaly detection. Anomaly detectors (chapter 12) need no labels and can notice a brand-new attack, but flag every unusual good customer too (a holiday purchase in a new country). Use them as extra features and as a discovery tool for the sampled review band, not as the main decision.
  • Decline against step-up. Step-up recovers good customers at the price of friction and abandonment; declines are cheaper per case and permanent for the sale. Put the boundary where the expected cost curves cross, and revisit it when the step-up pass rate changes.
  • Freshness against stability. Daily retraining tracks attacks but risks fitting noisy, immature labels; a weekly release with a validated champion is easier to audit. A hybrid is a stable model plus a fast layer of rules and a small online-updated component.
  • Explainability against evasion. Customers and regulators need reasons; fraudsters read them. Keep detailed reasons internal (chapter 16) and give customers coarse ones.

Key takeaways

  • Start from the decision and the review capacity. The metric that ships a fraud model is precision and dollars stopped at the depth analysts can handle.
  • A label is a status with a date. Unreported recent fraud is unknown; treating it as legitimate, or discarding all recent data, both lose the newest attack.
  • Rank the queue by expected loss, but keep a rule or card-level score for card testing, which precedes the large losses.
  • A good ranking can be a bad probability. Calibrate on a mature window before turning scores into dollars.
  • Features must be computed the same way online and offline, from an event log, as of the transaction time.
  • Adversaries change the data. Retraining on late labels does not fix it; faster labels, sampled exploration, evasion-resistant features and queue-precision monitoring do.
  • Roll out in shadow, then a hashed slice, and keep a permanent holdout, because declines hide the outcomes you most need.
