Design Demand Forecasting for a Retailer

Millions of product-store pairs, promotions that change demand, shelves that sell out and hide the demand that went unmet, and a purchasing decision that turns every forecast into money. This studio designs the forecast backwards from the order it feeds.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock working session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the data-science loop: clarify, define what is forecast, estimate, look at the data, choose metrics, baseline and model, repair the labels, turn the forecast into an order, draw the system, handle awkward series, run it, and summarise aloud. A rubric at the end separates a competent answer from a senior one. The experiments run on a synthetic retailer of 3,000 product-store series over 156 weeks, generated with a fixed seed so that true demand is known and every claim can be checked against it; the volumes and prices for the real retailer are labelled assumptions.

📐 The brief

A retail operations director says: "We sell tens of thousands of products across hundreds of stores. Purchasing orders weekly and the buyers are drowning. Give us a demand forecast per product per store, so we stop running out of what sells and stop throwing away what does not."

  • Suppliers need orders four weeks ahead for most goods; fresh goods have a shorter lead time and a shelf life.
  • Promotions are planned by the commercial team weeks in advance and change demand a lot. Prices, store openings and holidays are known ahead.
  • The stock system records sales, deliveries and stock on hand. Sales are recorded only when an item is on the shelf.
  • Today buyers use last year's sales and judgement. Nobody keeps a record of forecast accuracy.
  • Builds on time series forecasting and feature engineering; it uses time-based validation and previews model to value.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes. The scripts use NumPy only.

2What exactly is forecast?

Define the unit (row), the target, the horizon and the information available at forecast time. Why does the horizon equal more than the lead time?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Unit: one product in one store in one week (or day, for fresh goods). Weekly is enough for ambient goods ordered weekly; daily data is aggregated up for them and used directly for fresh goods.
  • Target: units of demand, not units sold. Demand is never observed; it is estimated from sales plus an adjustment for stock-outs (stage 7).
  • Horizon: the order placed today covers the time from delivery until the next delivery can arrive. With a 4-week lead time and weekly ordering, an order placed now must cover demand in weeks 4 and 5 and carry safety stock for the forecast error accumulated over lead time plus review period. So the forecast must be good about 4 to 5 weeks out, not just next week.
  • Origin and the information set: for a target week w, the features may use anything known at the origin w − 4: sales up to that week, and the promotion calendar, prices and holidays for week w. Nothing else. A promotion flag for week w is allowed because it is planned; sales in week w − 1 is not, because it is not yet known when the order is placed (leakage).
  • Two outputs: a central forecast for planning and a distribution (quantiles, or a mean and dispersion) for ordering. Forecast the same thing for every horizon with the same row definition, so that evaluation is by lead time.
  • Pooled against per-series: thousands of short, noisy series are better modelled together, with series-level features, than one model each: a pooled model borrows strength from similar products. This is the approach that dominated the retail forecasting competitions (see the dated note at the end of stage 6).

  • The scale is millions, not billions. 2.4 million active series and 31 million series-weeks per run: a batch job, not a serving problem. It must score about 1,400 series-weeks a second to finish in six hours, which a vectorised model on a few machines can do easily (compare performance engineering).
  • The training table is the heavy part: 250 million rows and about 40 GB of features. You do not train on all of it every week; sample the slow series, train on the last two years, and keep the feature pipeline incremental.
  • The money is in availability and waste, not decimal places of accuracy. Half a point of fill rate is worth about $84,000 of margin a week and a quarter off the waste about $432,000 a week, under these assumptions. This is why the success metric is the cost of the order, and why a small bias matters more than a small error.
  • Most series are slow sellers. If 60% sell under two units a week, they are most of the rows and a small part of the revenue. Spend the modelling effort accordingly (stage 10).

4The data: sales are not demand

What goes wrong when you treat the sales table as demand? Where in the table does it go wrong most, and what does it do to your estimate of a promotion's effect?

Compare with a worked answer

The simulation keeps true demand (hidden in real life) next to recorded sales. Demand follows a negative binomial distribution around a mean that combines a level, a yearly cycle, a small trend and a promotion uplift. Supply failures randomly cap what can be sold, and happen more in promotion weeks. First the generator, saved as s3_gen.py.

"""Demand forecasting: 3,000 synthetic SKU-store series, 156 weeks. True demand is hidden; sales are censored by stock-outs."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
S, W = 3_000, 156
level = np.exp(rng.normal(1.6, 1.1, S))                                    # median ~5 units a week, long right tail
amp = rng.uniform(0.0, 0.45, S); phase = rng.uniform(0, 52, S); trend = rng.normal(0, 0.0015, S)
t = np.arange(W)[None, :]
season = 1 + amp[:, None] * np.cos(2 * np.pi * (t - phase[:, None]) / 52)
promo = np.zeros((S, W), bool)
starts = rng.random((S, W)) < 0.06                                          # a promo starts in 6% of weeks and lasts 1 or 2 weeks
for lag in (0, 1):
    promo[:, lag:] |= starts[:, :W - lag] & (rng.random((S, W - lag)) < (1.0 if lag == 0 else 0.5))
uplift = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(1.7), 0.2, S))
mu = level[:, None] * season * np.exp(trend[:, None] * t) * np.where(promo, uplift[:, None], 1.0)
R = 5.0                                                                     # negative binomial dispersion: variance = mu + mu^2 / R
demand = rng.negative_binomial(R, R / (R + mu))
p_out = np.where(promo, 0.25, 0.08)                                         # supply failures are likelier when a promotion sells well
capped = rng.random((S, W)) < p_out
cap = np.floor(mu * rng.uniform(0.3, 0.9, (S, W)))
sales = np.where(capped, np.minimum(demand, cap), demand).astype(float)
stockout = capped & (demand > cap)                                          # the inventory system records this flag
"""Demand forecasting: what the data looks like, and why sales are not demand."""
from s3_gen import *
print(f"{S:,} series x {W} weeks = {S*W:,} series-weeks; mean weekly demand {demand.mean():.2f} units, median series level {np.median(level):.1f}")
print(f"series averaging under 1 unit a week: {(demand.mean(1) < 1).mean():.0%}; zero-sales weeks overall: {(sales == 0).mean():.1%}")
print(f"promotion weeks: {promo.mean():.1%} of series-weeks, carrying {demand[promo].sum()/demand.sum():.1%} of demand")
print(f"stock-out weeks: {stockout.mean():.1%} of series-weeks overall, {stockout[promo].mean():.1%} in promotion weeks, {stockout[~promo].mean():.1%} otherwise")
lost = demand.sum() - sales.sum()
print(f"units demanded {demand.sum():,.0f}, units sold {sales.sum():,.0f}: sales understate demand by {lost/demand.sum():.1%}")
print(f"... but by {1 - sales[promo].sum()/demand[promo].sum():.1%} in promotion weeks, where the promotion effect is estimated")
est_uplift = sales[promo].mean() / sales[~promo].mean(); true_uplift = demand[promo].mean() / demand[~promo].mean()
print(f"apparent promotion uplift in sales {est_uplift:.2f}x, in true demand {true_uplift:.2f}x")
3,000 series x 156 weeks = 468,000 series-weeks; mean weekly demand 9.89 units, median series level 5.1
series averaging under 1 unit a week: 6%; zero-sales weeks overall: 11.0%
promotion weeks: 8.9% of series-weeks, carrying 14.6% of demand

  • Sales understate demand by 5% overall, and 12% in promotion weeks. A successful promotion is exactly when stock runs out, so the data hides the best weeks the most. Censoring is not random noise: it is correlated with the thing you want to learn.
  • A model trained on sales underestimates the promotion effect (1.62 times in sales against the true 1.76) and learns that popular weeks are less popular than they are. Each cycle of order, stock-out and under-order reinforces the last. This is a self-reinforcing loop, as in the model-risk case: the system's own decisions shape the data it trains on.
  • Zeros mean two different things: no customer wanted it, or the shelf was empty. A stock-out flag from the inventory system (on-hand quantity zero for part of the week) separates them. If the flag is unavailable, infer likely stock-outs from runs of zeros in products that normally sell.
  • Audit the table first (chapter 3): sales by week with the stock-out share; items with sales after a closure date; negative sales from returns; price or promotion fields that were edited after the event; duplicated store ids after a refit; and the weeks around a store's opening, where history is not representative.

5Metrics

Which accuracy metric do you report, which do you refuse to, and at which level of aggregation? What is the metric of record for the purchasing decision?

  • WAPE (weighted absolute percentage error: total absolute error divided by total demand) weights each series by its volume, so it reads as "how many units are we wrong by, as a share of units". It is stable when some series have zero demand. Report it with bias (total forecast minus total demand, over total demand), because a model can have a small WAPE and a systematic lean that costs money.
  • MAPE misleads here. The per-series MAPE says slow sellers are forecast better (0.51 against 0.63), while WAPE says they are twice as bad (0.89 against 0.41). MAPE divides by each week's demand, so it skips the 37% of slow-seller weeks with zero demand and is dominated by small denominators. Never average per-series percentage errors across a catalogue with intermittent items.
  • Accuracy depends hugely on the level. The same forecasts have a WAPE of 0.46 for one product in one store in one week, 0.28 for a four-week delivery cycle, 0.15 for the quarter and 0.03 for the national weekly total. A forecast that is "97% accurate" in the boardroom is 46% off at the shelf. State the level for every number; evaluate at the level of the decision.
  • The noise floor is not zero. The oracle forecast, using the true expected demand, still has a WAPE of 0.42 in stage 6, because demand is random. Compare your model with the oracle in simulation, and with a naive baseline in practice; accuracy beyond the floor does not exist.
  • The metric of record is the decision's cost. Pinball (quantile) loss scores a forecast of the q-th quantile: under-forecasts are charged q per unit and over-forecasts (1 − q) per unit, which is the newsvendor cost with ratio q (stage 8). Report it at the quantiles the replenishment uses, plus the realised cost: lost sales, waste and holding.

6Baselines, a model and the backtest

Which baselines do you build first, how do you backtest, and what do you expect from a pooled model with promotion features? Which baseline do you predict is hardest to beat?

Compare with a worked answer

Backtest by rolling the origin (chapter 18): four consecutive 13-week test blocks; for each block, train only on target weeks before it and forecast four weeks ahead. Baselines: the naive forecast (sales four weeks ago), a four-week average, and last year's value for the same week. The model is a pooled ridge regression on ratios to the series' recent level, with last-year seasonality, planned promotion flags (this week and last year), recent promotion share and annual cycle terms; sample weights equal the series level, so large series count more. The listing includes the oracle row, which forecasts the true mean and shows the noise floor.

"""Demand forecasting: baselines and a pooled regression, scored by rolling-origin backtest at a 4-week lead."""
import io, contextlib, numpy as np
with contextlib.redirect_stdout(io.StringIO()):
    from s3_gen import *
H = 4                                                                        # forecast made 4 weeks before the target week

def features(w, y=sales):
    """Everything known at origin o = w - H, for every series, as columns of ratios to the series' recent level."""
    o = w - H
    lvl = y[:, o - 12:o + 1].mean(1) + 0.5
    recent = y[:, o - 3:o + 1].mean(1) / lvl
    ly = y[:, w - 53:w - 50].mean(1)
    ly_lvl = y[:, o - 52 - 12:o - 52 + 1].mean(1) + 0.5
    seas = ly / ly_lvl
    ang = 2 * np.pi * w / 52
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(S), recent, seas, promo[:, w], promo[:, w - 52], promo[:, o - 3:o + 1].mean(1),
                         np.full(S, np.sin(ang)), np.full(S, np.cos(ang))])
    return X, lvl

def fit_ridge(weeks, y, lam=1.0, keep=None):
    A = np.zeros((8, 8)); b = np.zeros(8)
    for w in weeks:
        X, lvl = features(w, y); t = y[:, w] / lvl
        wt = lvl if keep is None else lvl * keep[:, w]            # keep: 0 drops a series-week from training
        A += (X * wt[:, None]).T @ X; b += (X * wt[:, None]).T @ t
    return np.linalg.solve(A + lam * np.eye(8), b)

def wape(truth, f): return np.abs(truth - f).sum() / truth.sum()
def bias(truth, f): return (f - truth).sum() / truth.sum()

blocks = [(104, 117), (117, 130), (130, 143), (143, 156)]
names = ["naive (sales 4 weeks ago)", "4-week average", "seasonal naive (last year)", "pooled regression", "oracle: the true expected demand"]
res = {n: [] for n in names}; seg = {n: {"promo": [0, 0], "plain": [0, 0]} for n in names}
for a, b in blocks:

  • The four-week average is the hard baseline (WAPE 0.50), better than the naive forecast (0.62) and last year's value (0.61). Averaging removes noise; last year's single week adds its own noise. Any model must beat the average, not the naive.
  • The pooled regression gets 0.457 against an oracle floor of 0.422, so it closes a little over half of the gap between the baseline (0.50) and what is possible in this data. The gain over the average comes mostly from the promotion flag (promo weeks: 0.44 against 0.53) and the seasonal index.
  • Every model is biased low by 4 to 7%, and the oracle is not. Bias shows up in a metric nobody looks at while error looks fine: the next stage explains it.
  • Stability across blocks is the point of rolling origins. The four quarters agree to within 0.006 of WAPE; with real data, a block-to-block spread much wider than that is itself a finding (a holiday, a new region, a data change).
  • The next model is a gradient-boosted tree model on the same table (chapter 9), which finds interactions such as "this product, in promotion, in summer" without hand-built terms, then possibly a stack (feature pipelines and stacking). It earns its place only by beating this regression in the same backtest.

State of play, October 2026

Retail forecasting has a public benchmark in the M5 competition (Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos, 2022), which asked for forecasts of 42,840 hierarchical Walmart sales series; I did not fetch the paper, and a secondary summary I read says the top entries were pure machine-learning methods dominated by gradient-boosted trees such as LightGBM, which beat the statistical benchmarks. Since 2024, pretrained time-series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai and others) offer zero-shot forecasts with no training. Whether they beat a tuned boosted model on a retailer's own data with promotions and stock-outs is an empirical question, and published comparisons are mixed. Treat them as a baseline to include in the backtest, not as a replacement for the data pipeline. This will change; check the current benchmarks.

7Stock-outs and promotions: repair the target

Train the same model on three versions of the sales table: as recorded, with stock-out weeks dropped from the targets, and with stock-out weeks repaired. What happens to the headline error and to the bias? Which matters for ordering?

Compare with a worked answer

The script reuses the backtest functions from the previous listing (saved as s3_models.py) and scores every variant against true demand, which only a simulation can do. "Repair" replaces each stock-out week by the mean of the previous eight weeks that were not stock-outs; "drop" also removes stock-out weeks as training targets. The oracle trains on true demand.

"""Demand forecasting: train on sales as recorded, or repair the stock-out weeks first. Scored against true demand."""
import io, contextlib, numpy as np
with contextlib.redirect_stdout(io.StringIO()):
    import s3_models as m
from s3_gen import *

def repair(y, flag, window=8):
    """Replace each stock-out week by the mean of the series' last `window` weeks that were not stock-outs."""
    out = y.copy()
    for w in range(window, y.shape[1]):
        good = ~flag[:, w - window:w]
        cnt = np.maximum(good.sum(1), 1)
        est = (out[:, w - window:w] * good).sum(1) / cnt
        out[:, w] = np.where(flag[:, w], np.maximum(est, y[:, w]), y[:, w])
    return out

8From forecast to order

Given a forecast, how many units do you order? Compare "order the mean forecast" with ordering a quantile chosen by the cost of being short or long. How does the right quantile differ between a perishable and a staple?

Compare with a worked answer

This is the newsvendor problem. Ordering one more unit gains the unit's margin cu (the cost of underage) if demand reaches it and costs co (the cost of overage) if it does not. The expected profit is maximised by ordering the quantile of the demand distribution equal to the critical ratio cu / (cu + co). A perishable item with no salvage value has a low ratio (order less than the mean); a staple that can be sold later at nearly cost has a high one (order more).

The script estimates the dispersion of the forecast errors on one quarter, forms a negative binomial distribution around each forecast, and orders four weeks ahead for the final quarter, using the repaired-data model. It scores both policies against true demand with price $10 and cost $6.

"""Demand forecasting: from a forecast to an order. Mean forecast versus the newsvendor quantile of the forecast distribution."""
import io, contextlib, numpy as np

y = c.repair(sales, stockout); keep = ~stockout
def forecasts(a, b):
    beta = m.fit_ridge(range(70, a), y, keep=keep)
    return np.array([np.maximum(m.features(w, y)[1] * (m.features(w, y)[0] @ beta), 0) for w in range(a, b)]), demand[:, a:b].T

# estimate the dispersion R of the forecast errors on an earlier quarter: Var(d - f) = f + f^2 / R
f0, d0 = forecasts(117, 130)
R_hat = (f0 ** 2).sum() / (((d0 - f0) ** 2).sum() - f0.sum())
print(f"estimated dispersion R = {R_hat:.1f} (the generator used R = 5.0, but forecast errors also include model error)")

def nb_quantile(mean, R, q, kmax=1200):
    """Smallest k with P(D <= k) >= q for a negative binomial with the given mean, vectorised over cells."""
    mean = np.maximum(mean, 1e-9); pk = (R / (R + mean)) ** R; cdf = pk.copy()
    out = np.zeros(mean.shape); done = cdf >= q
    for k in range(kmax):
        pk = pk * (k + R) / (k + 1) * mean / (R + mean); cdf = cdf + pk
        newly = (cdf >= q) & ~done; out[newly] = k + 1; done |= newly
        if done.all(): break
    return out

f, d = forecasts(143, 156)                                  # the final quarter, ordered 4 weeks ahead
price, cost = 10.0, 6.0
print(f"\n{f.size:,} SKU-store-week orders; demand units {d.sum():,.0f}; unit cost ${cost:.0f}, price ${price:.0f}")
print(f"{'product class':28s} {'critical ratio':>14s} {'policy':>22s} {'profit/wk':>10s} {'fill rate':>9s} {'left over':>9s}")
for label, salvage in (("perishable (salvage $0)", 0.0), ("staple (salvage $5.50)", 5.5)):
    cu, co = price - cost, cost - salvage
    cr = cu / (cu + co)
    for pol, q in (("order the mean forecast", np.round(f)), (f"order the {cr:.0%} quantile", nb_quantile(f, R_hat, cr))):
        sold = np.minimum(q, d); profit = price * sold - cost * q + salvage * np.maximum(q - d, 0)
        print(f"{label:28s} {cr:14.2f} {pol:>22s} {profit.sum() / 13:10,.0f} {sold.sum() / d.sum():9.1%} {np.maximum(q - d, 0).sum() / q.sum():9.1%}")

  • It moves in opposite directions for the two products. The perishable order is cut below the mean (fill rate down to 64%, waste down to 14% of units); the staple order is raised well above it (fill rate up to 94%, leftover rising to 44% of units, which costs little because they are resold at $5.50).   • Ordering the mean is one decision for both products, so both get a 76% fill rate. A blanket service-level target would be just as blind to cost: the staple reaches 94% at its profit-maximising 89th percentile, while chasing that figure for the perishable would add waste the economics do not justify.
  • The distribution matters as much as the mean. The dispersion estimate (about 3.9, compared with 5.0 for demand alone) includes model error. Check calibration of the quantiles by backtest: an 89% quantile should cover demand about 89% of the time, per segment, and slow and promoted items usually need their own dispersion (chapter 15 on calibration).
  • Real constraints bend the formula: minimum order quantities, pack sizes, shelf life, shelf capacity, budgets and the interaction across stores sharing a warehouse. Solve these in a replenishment optimiser that takes the quantile forecast as input; do not push them into the forecasting model.

9Architecture

Draw the weekly system from raw feeds to orders and back. Where is stock-out repair, where do planner overrides enter, and what watches the forecasts after launch?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Schedule and freshness: the run starts after the Sunday stock count and must finish before the supplier cut-off. Treat a late or partial run as an incident; the fallback is last week's forecast with a flag, not an empty order (see SLIs and SLOs for freshness objectives).
  • Everything is versioned: data snapshot, feature code, model and parameters, so an order can be explained and a past week reproduced (reproducible experiments).
  • Hierarchical consistency: store-level forecasts should add up to the regional and national plans finance uses. Forecast at the bottom level and sum, or forecast at several levels and reconcile so that parts add to wholes; test that the reconciled numbers do not hurt the bottom-level accuracy.
  • Interface for buyers: the forecast, its interval, the last year, the promotion effect assumed, and the reason for large changes. An override box with a required reason code turns arguments into data.

Systems connection

The weekly run is a batch pipeline with a deadline, so the usual reliability questions apply: idempotent steps that can be rerun, a record of which inputs each forecast used, and a late-data policy. The streams-and-batch trade-offs are in batch processing and streams and change data capture; the shape of ML systems in general is in Designing with ML & LLMs.

10Awkward series: new, slow and promoted

What do you do for a product launched last week, for a slow seller that sells one unit a month, and for the first week of a new store?

Compare with a worked answer
  • New products (cold start): there is no history, so borrow it. Forecast from attributes (category, brand, price, pack size, promotion plan) using the pooled model with series-level features, or from the first weeks of sales of the most similar existing products (nearest neighbours in attribute space, chapter 7). Use a wide interval, update quickly as the first sales arrive, and mark these forecasts so that buyers know they are guesses.
  • Intermittent demand (mostly zeros): a mean forecast of 0.4 units a week is correct and is never the demand in any week. Absolute-error accuracy rewards forecasting zero (the median), so WAPE on these series looks bad whatever you do (0.89 in stage 5). Use methods designed for them: Croston-style estimators that separate the chance of a sale from its size, or count distributions, and decide the stock by service level on a distribution, since the order is "keep one on the shelf", not a number from a curve. Cluster the ordering for them across stores through a warehouse.
  • New stores: use the store's attributes and the first weeks' ratio to similar stores' opening profiles; ignore the opening weeks when learning seasonal patterns, since they show the opening, not the season.
  • Fast sellers in promotion: the largest absolute errors sit here. Give them the most modelling effort: promotion mechanic, depth, display, competitor pricing if known, cannibalisation and the dip afterwards; keep the planner involved.
  • Substitution: when a product is out, customers buy another. Observed sales of the substitute are inflated; the sum over the category is more stable than the parts. Model at both levels and check the category total.
  • Outliers and events: a store closed for a flood, a one-off bulk order or a viral spike are not seasonal patterns. Flag and exclude them from training, and keep a calendar of events so that they are explained rather than learned.

Key takeaways

  • The forecast serves an order. Work backwards from the decision to the horizon, the level and the loss function.
  • Sales are censored demand, and censoring is worst in the weeks that matter most. Repair the target and track bias, which the usual error metrics barely see.
  • Report WAPE with bias, state the aggregation level, and avoid per-series percentage errors on intermittent items.
  • The four-week average is a strong baseline; a pooled model with planned promotion features beats it, and the gap to the noise floor shows how much is left.
  • Order the quantile set by the cost of being short against long, not the mean. The same forecast earned 10% to 16% more profit this way.
  • Cold-start, slow and promoted series need their own methods and their own metrics.
  • Run silently, pilot against matched controls, judge by realised cost, and log every override.
