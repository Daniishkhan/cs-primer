Frame a Churn Prediction Problem

A manager says "reduce churn". Turn that sentence into a target a model can learn, a prediction window that matches when someone can still act, a metric that reflects what a contact costs, a baseline to beat and an action worth taking. The model is the easy part of this studio; the framing is the work.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock working session of 45 to 75 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the data-science loop from the start-here chapter: clarify, define the target, choose metrics, estimate, audit the data, baseline, validate, decide the action, test it, build it, run it, and summarise it aloud. A rubric at the end separates a competent answer from a senior one. Every number in the worked answers is either computed by a script on this page (a synthetic dataset generated with a fixed seed, so you can rerun it) or labelled as an assumption you should challenge.

📐 The brief

A product lead at a subscription business says: "Churn is eating us. Build a model that predicts who is going to cancel, so the retention team can do something about it."

  • The business is a consumer subscription (think a streaming, fitness or software plan) with about 400,000 paying subscribers on three price tiers, and a monthly churn rate the finance team quotes as roughly 4%.
  • A retention team of a few people can contact a limited number of customers each month, by email, in-app message or a call, and can offer a discount or a pause.
  • There is a billing database, an event log of logins and usage, and a support-ticket system. Nobody has ever labelled a "churner".
  • Builds on framing problems and choosing metrics, exploratory data analysis and baselines first. It previews leakage and model to value.
  • You have 45 to 75 minutes. Nothing here needs more than NumPy.

2Define the target and the windows

Define the label exactly: who is a row, which date do the features describe, what is the prediction window, and what is the lead time? Draw the timeline for one customer and mark where a feature can and cannot come from.

Compare with a worked answer

A snapshot is the state of one customer on one date. A row in the training table is one customer at one snapshot date T. The label asks: did this customer cancel during a fixed window after T? The prediction window is that window; the lead time is the gap between T and the first day a cancellation counts, which is the time the retention team has to act.

  • Row: customer id and snapshot date. Each customer appears once per snapshot, so a customer who stays for a year contributes up to 52 weekly rows or 12 monthly ones. That has consequences for validation (stage 7).
  • Label: 1 if the customer's subscription ends (cancelled, or non-renewed after payment retries) between day 8 and day 37 after T, else 0. Choose the window from the billing cycle, not from convenience: a 30-day window matches monthly plans, and a quarterly plan needs a different one.
  • Censored rows: a snapshot taken less than 37 days before today has an unfinished window. Its label is unknown, not 0. Drop it from training, or you teach the model that recent customers never churn (see leakage and, for the time-series version, the forecasting chapter).
  • Who is eligible: active paying subscribers with at least some history. A free-trial user and a customer already in cancellation notice are different problems, with different features and actions.
  • Alternative framings to name and reject: a time-to-event model (survival analysis) predicts when, and suits planning; a regression on remaining months is noisy and rarely used to decide a contact. A single binary label over a fixed window is the simplest framing that matches a weekly decision, so start there.

3Choose the metrics

Name one model metric, one decision metric and one business metric. For each, say what it would hide. Why is accuracy useless here?

Compare with a worked answer

With a 4% churn rate, a model that predicts "nobody will churn" is 96% accurate. Accuracy rewards ignoring the minority. Use metrics that look at the ranking and at the list the team will actually act on (chapter 2).

LevelMetricWhat it tells youWhat it hides
ModelAverage precision (area under the precision-recall curve) on the next monthHow well the whole ranking puts churners first, with a baseline equal to the churn rate (about 0.04)Quality outside the top of the list, where nobody will be contacted
DecisionPrecision and recall of the top k, where k is the contact capacity; the lift is precision divided by the base rateWhat the retention team will see on Monday: how many of 12,000 contacts were going to leaveWhether contacting them changed anything; a certain churner may be beyond saving
BusinessNet margin kept per month, from a randomised test with a held-out control groupWhether the whole programme pays for itselfIt is slow (weeks), noisy, and says nothing about why
  • Report a calibrated probability too (chapter 15) if finance wants an expected number of cancellations next month; ranking metrics do not check that the numbers mean what they say.
  • ROC AUC looks flattering when positives are rare: false positives are divided by a huge number of negatives. Prefer precision-recall views for a 4% problem.
  • A guardrail metric: complaint rate and unsubscribe rate among contacted people. A retention campaign that annoys customers into leaving has a hidden cost.
  • Say the order: choose the business metric first, derive the decision metric from capacity and cost, and only then pick the model metric that tracks it. Optimising average precision tells you nothing if the top 3% is what the business uses.

4Estimate

How many cancellations a month, what share of them can a 12,000-contact budget possibly reach, and what precision would different models give at that depth? How big must a test of the offer be?

Compare with a worked answer

Back-of-the-envelope numbers come first, because they tell you how good the model must be before you build it. The sample-size formula for two proportions is the one in Statistics for Experiments.

"""Churn: sizing and the sample size of the retention test. Every input is an assumption."""
from math import sqrt, ceil
from statistics import NormalDist

subs, monthly_churn = 400_000, 0.04
churners = subs * monthly_churn
print(f"{subs:,} subscribers x {monthly_churn:.0%} a month = {churners:,.0f} cancellations a month")

budget_contacts = 12_000            # what the retention team can contact each month
print(f"contact budget {budget_contacts:,} = top {budget_contacts/subs:.1%} of the base")
print(f"random contacts reach {budget_contacts*monthly_churn:,.0f} future churners "
      f"({budget_contacts*monthly_churn/churners:.1%} of them)")
for cap in (0.10, 0.20, 0.30):
    print(f"a model that finds {cap:.0%} of churners in its top 3%: precision {cap*churners/budget_contacts:.1%}, lift {cap*churners/budget_contacts/monthly_churn:.1f}x")

# A/B test: does the offer cut churn among contacted people? two-proportion sample size
def n_per_arm(p0, p1, alpha=0.05, power=0.8):
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf
    pbar = (p0 + p1) / 2
    num = (z(1 - alpha/2) * sqrt(2 * pbar * (1 - pbar)) + z(power) * sqrt(p0*(1-p0) + p1*(1-p1))) ** 2
    return ceil(num / (p0 - p1) ** 2)

print()
for p0, p1 in ((0.30, 0.24), (0.30, 0.27), (0.10, 0.08), (0.04, 0.032)):
    n = n_per_arm(p0, p1)
    print(f"churn {p0:.0%} -> {p1:.1%}: {n:,} per arm ({2*n:,} total)")

  • The budget is the binding constraint, not the model. 12,000 contacts is 3% of the base while 16,000 customers leave each month. Even a perfect ranking could reach at most 12,000 of them. A random list reaches 480.
  • The depth of the list sets the required precision. A model that puts 20% of next month's churners into its top 3% has a precision of 26.7% there, 6.7 times the base rate. That is what "good" looks like: not 90% anything.
  • Testing the offer is expensive when churn is rare. Among the top-ranked group, where perhaps 10% would leave, cutting churn by a fifth (10% to 8%) needs about 3,200 customers per arm. Run the test on the contacted group, not on everyone: the whole base would need 8,500 per arm for a 4% to 3.2% change.
  • Consequence: a test with a control group costs a few thousand contacts that you withhold, which is why a retention team resists it. The expected value of knowing the true save rate (stage 8) is larger than the cost of the withheld contacts.

Three tempting leaks (chapter 14):

  • Visited the cancel page / opened the cancellation flow. It is the strongest feature in the data and it happens during the prediction window, after T. The model scores a customer who has already started leaving. The script in stage 6 shows what it does to a score.
  • "Account status" or "days since last payment" read from today's table. A cancelled account has no recent payments by definition. Snapshot the table daily, or reconstruct history from events.
  • Win-back or retention-offer flags. A customer who received an offer was already flagged by an earlier rule or model. The feature encodes the old targeting policy, not the customer's behaviour.

EDA first (chapter 3): check the churn rate by month, by plan and by tenure; look for duplicate customers after plan changes; compare missingness in the event log for old and new customers (a logging change in March can look like a behaviour change); and confirm that the train and test months look alike on every feature.

6Baselines and a first model

Before any fancy model, which baselines will you compute and what numbers do you expect? Then fit the simplest real model. How much better than the "fewest logins" rule should it be, and what does a suspiciously good result look like?

Compare with a worked answer

The ladder from baselines first: random scores, the majority class, a one-feature rule an analyst could write in SQL, then a linear model. The script builds a synthetic base of 30,000 customers observed in three monthly snapshots (generated with a fixed seed; the real mechanism is a logistic hazard on a hidden engagement level, so the observed features are noisy proxies). It trains on months 0 and 1 and tests on month 2, then adds a leaky feature.

"""Churn: a synthetic subscription base, three monthly snapshots, a time split, baselines and one leak."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 30_000                                         # customers; each appears in 3 monthly snapshots

tenure0 = rng.gamma(2.0, 9.0, N)                   # months subscribed at snapshot 0
price = rng.choice([8, 12, 20], N, p=[.5, .35, .15])
engage = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                       # latent engagement, not observed

def snapshot(m):
    """Features observed on day 0 of month m, and the label: cancelled during days 8-37."""
    ten = tenure0 + m
    logins = rng.poisson(np.exp(1.6 + 0.7 * engage - 0.1 * m * (engage < 0)))        # logins, last 14 days
    tickets = rng.poisson(0.25 + 0.2 * (engage < -0.5))                              # support tickets, last 30 days
    failed = (rng.random(N) < 0.04 + 0.03 * (price == 20)).astype(float)             # payment failed last cycle
    z = -3.3 - 0.9 * engage - 0.025 * ten + 0.5 * failed + 0.25 * tickets + 0.03 * (price - 12)
    y = (rng.random(N) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))).astype(int)
    cancel_page = ((y == 1) & (rng.random(N) < 0.6)) | (rng.random(N) < 0.01)       # visited cancel page: AFTER the cut-off
    X = np.column_stack([ten, logins, tickets, failed, price])
    return X, y, cancel_page.astype(float)

snaps = [snapshot(m) for m in range(3)]
Xtr = np.vstack([snaps[0][0], snaps[1][0]]); ytr = np.concatenate([snaps[0][1], snaps[1][1]])
Xte, yte = snaps[2][0], snaps[2][1]
print(f"train rows {len(ytr):,} (months 0-1), test rows {len(yte):,} (month 2); churn rate train {ytr.mean():.2%}, test {yte.mean():.2%}")

def avg_precision(y, s):
    o = np.argsort(-s, kind="stable"); y = y[o]
    tp = np.cumsum(y); prec = tp / np.arange(1, len(y) + 1)
    return float((prec * y).sum() / y.sum())

def top_k(y, s, frac):
    k = int(len(y) * frac); o = np.argsort(-s, kind="stable")[:k]
    return y[o].mean(), y[o].sum() / y.sum()

def report(name, y, s):
    p, r = top_k(y, s, 0.03)
    print(f"{name:34s} AP {avg_precision(y, s):.3f}   top 3%: precision {p:.1%} recall {r:.1%} lift {p / y.mean():.1f}x")

report("random scores", yte, rng.random(len(yte)))
print(f"{'majority class (predict no churn)':34s} accuracy {1 - yte.mean():.2%}, recall 0%")
report("rule: fewest logins", yte, -Xte[:, 1] + rng.random(len(yte)) * 1e-3)

def fit_logreg(X, y, lr=0.3, steps=600, l2=1e-3):
    mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0); Z = (X - mu) / sd
    w, b = np.zeros(Z.shape[1]), np.log(y.mean() / (1 - y.mean()))
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(Z @ w + b)))
        w -= lr * (Z.T @ (p - y) / len(y) + l2 * w); b -= lr * (p - y).mean()
    return lambda X2: ((X2 - mu) / sd) @ w + b, w

model, w = fit_logreg(Xtr, ytr)
report("logistic regression (5 features)", yte, model(Xte))
print("standardised weights [tenure, logins, tickets, failed, price]:", np.round(w, 2))

# the leak: add 'visited the cancel page', recorded after the prediction date
Xl_tr = np.column_stack([Xtr, np.concatenate([snaps[0][2], snaps[1][2]])]); Xl_te = np.column_stack([Xte, snaps[2][2]])
leaky, _ = fit_logreg(Xl_tr, ytr)
report("same model + cancel_page_visits", yte, leaky(Xl_te))
train rows 60,000 (months 0-1), test rows 30,000 (month 2); churn rate train 3.77%, test 3.66%
random scores                      AP 0.037   top 3%: precision 3.2% recall 2.6% lift 0.9x
majority class (predict no churn)  accuracy 96.34%, recall 0%
rule: fewest logins                AP 0.070   top 3%: precision 8.9% recall 7.3% lift 2.4x
logistic regression (5 features)   AP 0.085   top 3%: precision 12.3% recall 10.1% lift 3.4x
standardised weights [tenure, logins, tickets, failed, price]: [-0.31 -0.83  0.19  0.11  0.12]
same model + cancel_page_visits    AP 0.580   top 3%: precision 72.7% recall 59.6% lift 19.9x
  • Accuracy is 96.3% for a model that finds nobody. Never report it alone for a rare event.
  • The one-line rule is already 2.4 times better than random at the top 3%. Any model must beat that, not beat random. In this data it does, but by a modest margin: a lift of 3.4 and an average precision of 0.085, against a base rate of 0.037. That is what a real churn model often looks like: churn is hard to predict, because many cancellations follow events the data never records.
  • The weights tell a story (chapter 5): fewer logins, shorter tenure and recent support tickets raise the score. They describe association, not what to do about it.
  • The leak turns average precision from 0.085 to 0.580 and the top-3% precision to 73%. A jump of that size on one added column is a symptom, not a success. The right reaction is to ask "when was this recorded?" before celebrating.
  • Next models: gradient-boosted trees (chapter 9) usually add a few points of average precision on tabular data like this; they earn a place only if they beat the logistic model by more than the noise across folds.

7Validation plan

How do you split the data so the offline score predicts next quarter's result? What goes wrong if you shuffle the rows?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Split by time, not at random (chapter 13). The model will be used on future snapshots, so train on early months and test on a later one, as the script above does. Roll the origin forward for three or four tests to see the spread.
  • Customers repeat across snapshots. A shuffled split puts the same person in train and test, and the model learns that customer's habits. If you must mix months, keep each customer on one side with a grouped split. In the script, train and test share customers across months, which is realistic for deployment (you score the same people next month) but means you should not read the test score as performance on new customers.
  • Respect label maturity. A snapshot is usable only once its window closes. Today's test month must end at least 37 days ago.
  • Judge on the decision metric. Report precision at 3% and lift with a confidence interval across rolling tests, because with about 1,100 churners in a month of 30,000 customers, a single top-3% precision of 12.3% has a standard error of roughly one percentage point.
  • Do not tune on the test month. Choose features and hyperparameters on earlier folds; touch the final month once.
  • Seasonality: a December snapshot behaves differently from a March one (gifts, resolutions, price changes). Include at least a full year of history before claiming a stable score.

8From a score to an action

Given a ranked list, how many people do you contact? Write the profit as a function of how many you contact, name the assumption that the answer is most sensitive to, and say what happens if it is wrong.

Compare with a worked answer

Contacting a customer costs a message plus, for those who take it, a discount. If the customer was going to leave, the contact saves them with some probability. If they were not going to leave, the contact is wasted and may even prompt them to review their bill. Expected profit of contacting the top k customers is: (churners among them × save rate × value of a save) − (stayers among them × harm per nudge × value) − (k × cost per contact). The script uses the test month from stage 6 and varies the save rate, which is the quantity nobody has measured.

"""Churn: from a ranking to an action. Value of contacting the top k, under stated assumptions."""
import io, contextlib, numpy as np
with contextlib.redirect_stdout(io.StringIO()):
    import s1_data as d
score = d.model(d.Xte); y = d.yte
order = np.argsort(-score, kind="stable"); ys = y[order]
n = len(y)

value_save = 240.0                       # $ margin kept if a would-be churner stays (about $12 x 20 months)
contact_cost, offer_cost, take_up = 1.0, 6.0, 0.30    # $ per contact; discount paid by those who accept; share who accept
sleeping = 0.002                         # extra churn caused by contacting someone who would have stayed
unit = contact_cost + take_up * offer_cost

def profit(k, save_rate):
    tp = ys[:k].sum(); fp = k - tp
    return tp * save_rate * value_save - fp * sleeping * value_save - k * unit

print(f"{n:,} customers, {y.sum():,} churners; cost per contact ${unit:.2f}; value of a save ${value_save:.0f}")
print(f"break-even precision at 20% save rate: {unit / (0.20 * value_save):.1%}  (at 5%: {unit / (0.05 * value_save):.1%})\n")
print(f"{'contacted':>9} {'share':>6} {'precision':>9} | profit $ at save rate: {'5%':>7} {'10%':>7} {'20%':>7}")
for k in (150, 300, 600, 900, 1500, 3000):
    print(f"{k:9,d} {k/n:6.1%} {ys[:k].mean():9.1%} |{'':22}{profit(k, .05):7,.0f} {profit(k, .10):7,.0f} {profit(k, .20):7,.0f}")
best = max(range(50, n, 50), key=lambda k: profit(k, 0.20))
print(f"\nbest k at 20% save rate: {best:,} contacts ({best/n:.1%}), profit ${profit(best, .20):,.0f}")
rk = y[np.random.default_rng(1).permutation(n)][:best]
rp = rk.sum() * .2 * value_save - (best - rk.sum()) * sleeping * value_save - best * unit
print(f"random {best:,} contacts: precision {rk.mean():.1%}, profit ${rp:,.0f}")

  • The break-even precision is cost divided by (save rate × value). At a 20% save rate it is 5.8%, so contacts keep paying until the next customer on the list has less than a 5.8% chance of leaving. At a 5% save rate it is 23.3%, higher than anything this model offers, so every list is a loss.
  • The same model is a profit centre or a cost centre depending on the save rate. At 20% the best list is 4,300 contacts out of 30,000 for about $5,600 a month; at 5% the best list is empty. Scaled to the real base (400,000 customers), the 3% budget is worth about $32,000 a month at a 20% save rate and loses about $21,000 at 5%.
  • Model accuracy is a second-order effect. Compare random targeting, which loses $6,250 at the same list size even at a 20% save rate, with the model's gain: the ranking matters, but the offer's effectiveness decides the sign.
  • Capacity truncates the list. If the team can only handle 900 contacts, the best list is 900 whatever the optimum says. Say both numbers, and show how much value is left on the table.
  • The hidden assumption is that the highest churn probability is the best target. It is not necessarily: the customers who most need saving are those whose behaviour an offer can change. That is uplift (next stage), and it is why the save rate is a quantity to measure, not to assume.

9Test whether the action works

Design the experiment that measures the save rate. Who is randomised, what is the control, how long does it run, and what would make you stop the programme?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Pick the population by the model, then randomise within it. Take the top 12,000 on a scoring Monday. Assign, at random, 80% to the offer and 20% to a holdout (a control group that receives nothing). Because assignment is random, the two groups differ only by the offer, and the difference in churn is the effect.
  2. Measure the outcome the business cares about: cancellations in the 30-day window, and revenue after 90 days (a discount may delay churn rather than prevent it).
  3. Size it from stage 4: about 3,200 per arm to detect 10% falling to 8%. With 20% of 12,000 held out (2,400), a single week is under-powered; pool four Mondays or hold out 30%.
  4. Check the guardrails: unsubscribes, complaints and refund requests among the contacted group.
  5. Decide in advance: if the lower end of the 95% confidence interval for the save rate is below the break-even (5.8% at the assumed economics), stop contacting the marginal customers and keep only the head of the list.
  • Persuadable versus sure-thing versus lost cause versus sleeping dog. Customers split into those the offer saves, those who stay anyway, those who leave anyway, and those the contact pushes out. A churn-propensity model ranks "will leave" and cannot separate the first from the third. An uplift model estimates the difference between the outcome with and without the action, and needs the randomised data this test produces.
  • Keep the holdout running after launch (a few percent, permanently). It is the only way to notice that the offer stopped working, that the model decayed, or that a discount trained customers to wait for one.
  • Without randomisation, the comparison is biased. Comparing contacted and non-contacted customers after the fact confounds the offer with the model's selection: you contacted the likelier leavers, so the offer looks harmful. See statistics for experiments.

10Architecture of the weekly pipeline

Sketch the batch pipeline from raw events to a contact list and back to the next training set. Where does the point-in-time rule live, and where do the contact decisions get logged?

Compare with a worked answer

Weekly scoring does not need real-time serving. A scheduled batch job (see batch processing and, for the general shape of ML systems, Designing with ML & LLMs) is simpler and easier to audit.

  • One code path for training and scoring features. The snapshot builder produces the same table for history and for today. Two implementations drift apart, and the model sees different features online than offline (training-serving skew, covered in chapter 21).
  • Everything is versioned: the data snapshot id, the code commit, the model file and its metrics, so a past list can be reproduced (see reproducible experiments).
  • Cost and latency are small: a weekly job over 400,000 rows and a few dozen features runs in minutes on one machine. The effort is in the data pipeline, not the compute.
  • Interface: a ranked list with the top three contributing signals per customer in plain language, so the agent on a call knows what to ask about. Treat explanations as hints (chapter 16), not as reasons a customer would endorse.

Key takeaways

  • Framing is most of the work: one row is a customer on a date, the label is an event in a window that starts after a lead time, and features may use only what was known on that date.
  • For a rare event, report ranking and top-k metrics against the base rate. Accuracy rewards predicting nobody.
  • The contact budget sets the depth of the list and the precision you need; do the arithmetic before you build.
  • Baselines are the benchmark, not random. A suspiciously large jump from one feature is a leak until proved otherwise.
  • Validate on later months, only on matured labels, and remember that the same customers appear repeatedly.
  • Profit depends on how much the action changes behaviour. Measure that with a randomised holdout before polishing the model, and keep the holdout.
  • Predicting who will leave is not the same as finding who can be saved; uplift modelling targets the difference an action makes.
