Lab 1: Linear & Logistic Regression from Scratch

Chapters 5 and 6 fitted lines and probabilities with a few calls to NumPy. That is the right way to work, and the wrong way to learn what those calls do. In this lab you rebuild the whole family in plain Python lists: least squares by the normal equations, gradient descent, ridge, the lasso, the sigmoid, log-loss and a penalised logistic regression. Every function is checked against a hand-worked value, a finite-difference gradient or an optimality condition, so "it ran" is never the standard: it has to be right, and when it is not, the check says which formula you slipped on.

Why this lab matters

Linear and logistic models are the baseline every other model in this course has to beat, and in credit scoring, click prediction and clinical risk they are often the model that ships. They are also the cheapest place to learn the machinery everything later reuses: a loss, its gradient, a step size, a penalty, and the habit of testing a gradient against finite differences before trusting it. Almost every bug in this territory is a missing factor or a flipped sign: a forgotten 2 in the squared-error gradient, y - p where p - y belongs, a penalty applied to the intercept, a standardisation fitted on the wrong rows. None of them crashes. They change the answer a little, and a model that is a little wrong is the hardest kind to notice. Writing each step yourself, against checks that know the right numbers, is how you stop making those mistakes in library code too.

Standard library only, deterministic, a few seconds

Nothing here needs NumPy, scikit-learn or the network. Every dataset comes from a seeded random.Random, so every run prints the same numbers, and a failure names its seed so you can replay it: python3 check.py solution 4 3 runs milestone 4 on dataset seed 3 only. The whole check takes about four seconds. Python 3.9 or newer. The conventions are those of the chapters: X is a list of rows of shape (n_samples, n_features), y a list of length n_samples, weights are stored as theta = [b, w1, ..., wd] with the intercept first, ridge minimises a sum of squares plus alpha times the squared weights, and the lasso minimises a mean of squares plus alpha times the absolute weights, exactly as chapter 6 defines them.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each using the one before:

  1. The closed form: list-based matrix helpers, a Gaussian-elimination solver and fit_ols, which solves the normal equations of chapter 5, plus mse and R2.
  2. Gradient descent: standardisation, the analytic gradient of the squared error, a generic descent loop, and the experiment that shows why the units of your features set the step size.
  3. Ridge and cross-validation: an L2 penalty with a closed-form solution, k-fold validation, and choosing alpha from the training data alone.
  4. The lasso: the soft-threshold function and coordinate descent, checked against the lasso's own optimality conditions, and the point where all weights become exactly zero.
  5. Log-loss: an overflow-proof sigmoid, binary cross-entropy with clipping, and the logistic gradient (1/n) AT(p - y), checked by finite differences.
  6. Logistic regression: a fitted, optionally penalised classifier; what perfect separation does to unpenalised weights, and how to read the result as odds ratios.

Milestone 1: the closed form, from nothing

Chapter 5 derived the normal equations: put a column of ones in front of X to get the design matrix A, and the weights that minimise the squared error satisfy (ATA) theta = ATy. NumPy hides the linear algebra in np.linalg.solve; here you write it. Take the three points from chapter 5, (1, 2), (2, 3) and (3, 5). Then A has rows (1, 1), (1, 2), (1, 3); ATA is [[3, 6], [6, 14]] and ATy is [10, 23]. Two equations, 3b + 6w = 10 and 6b + 14w = 23: subtract twice the first from the second to get 2w = 3, so the slope is 1.5 and the intercept is (10 − 9) / 3 = 1/3. Your code must reproduce that, and then 150 noisy flats, with the same four lines of logic.

The solver is the one part with real craft. Gaussian elimination reduces the system to triangular form by subtracting multiples of one row from the rows below it, then back-substitutes from the last unknown upwards. The craft is the choice of pivot, the number you divide by at each step. If it is zero the method breaks (for [[0, 1], [1, 0]] the first pivot is zero and the rows must be swapped); if it is merely tiny, rounding errors are magnified enormously. Partial pivoting picks, among the rows still available, the one with the largest entry in the current column, which is what every library does. And a pivot that is negligible next to the rest of the matrix means the matrix is singular: its columns are not independent, and there is no unique answer. Two identical columns do exactly this, and solve should say so with a ValueError instead of returning garbage.

That last case is the lab's first piece of foreshadowing. In milestone 3 the same failure appears with a measure of how close you are to it, and the penalty of ridge regression is what repairs it.

def dot(a, b):
    """Dot product of two equal-length lists."""
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))

def transpose(M):
    """Transpose of a matrix given as a list of rows."""
    return [list(col) for col in zip(*M)]

def matmul(A, B):
    """Matrix product A @ B for lists of rows: (m, k) @ (k, n) -> (m, n)."""
    Bt = transpose(B)
    return [[dot(row, col) for col in Bt] for row in A]

def solve(A, b):
    """Solve A x = b for square A by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting.

    Raises ValueError("singular matrix") if a pivot is negligible next to the
    largest entry of A (a duplicated or constant-multiple column does this).
    Neither A nor b is modified.
    """
    n = len(A)
    M = [list(map(float, A[i])) + [float(b[i])] for i in range(n)]   # augmented matrix
    scale = max(abs(v) for row in A for v in row) or 1.0
    for c in range(n):
        p = max(range(c, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][c]))              # the row with the biggest pivot
        if abs(M[p][c]) < 1e-12 * scale:
            raise ValueError("singular matrix")
        M[c], M[p] = M[p], M[c]
        for r in range(c + 1, n):
            f = M[r][c] / M[c][c]
            if f:
                M[r] = [a - f * k for a, k in zip(M[r], M[c])]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for r in range(n - 1, -1, -1):                                    # back-substitution
        x[r] = (M[r][n] - sum(M[r][j] * x[j] for j in range(r + 1, n))) / M[r][r]
    return x

def add_intercept(X):
    """Prepend a column of ones to every row: the design matrix A."""
    return [[1.0] + list(row) for row in X]

def fit_ols(X, y):
    """Ordinary least squares by the normal equations (A^T A) theta = A^T y.

    Returns theta = [b, w1, ..., wd]. Raises ValueError if A^T A is singular.
    """
    A = add_intercept(X)
    At = transpose(A)
    return solve(matmul(At, A), [dot(col, y) for col in At])

def predict_linear(X, theta):
    """Predictions theta[0] + sum_j theta[j+1] * x[j] for every row."""
    return [theta[0] + dot(theta[1:], row) for row in X]

def mse(y, p):
    """Mean squared error."""
    return sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(y, p)) / len(y)

def r2(y, p):
    """Coefficient of determination: 1 - SS_res / SS_tot (1.0 is perfect, 0.0 is 'predict the mean')."""
    ybar = sum(y) / len(y)
    ss_res = sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(y, p))
    ss_tot = sum((a - ybar) ** 2 for a in y)
    return 1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot

Milestone 2: gradient descent, and why feature scale matters

The normal equations exist for linear regression and almost nowhere else, so the tool worth owning is gradient descent: start anywhere, compute the gradient of the loss, step the other way, repeat. For the mean squared error the gradient with respect to theta is (2/n) AT(A theta − y): for each column of A, multiply it by the residuals and average. Chapter 5 reads it in words. You will write it in three lines, and then check it the way professionals do: against numeric_gradient, which nudges each weight by ±10−6 and measures how the loss moved. If your formula and the finite differences disagree by more than rounding, one of them is wrong, and it is never the finite differences.

The descent loop itself is generic: it takes a loss function, a gradient function, a starting point, a step size and a number of steps. You will reuse it unchanged for logistic regression in milestone 6. One convention matters because the check and the figure depend on it: history[t] is the loss at the start of step t, so history[0] is the loss of the starting point and the list has exactly steps entries.

Then the experiment of chapter 5. The rent data has features on very different scales: area up to 120, distance up to 15, age up to 80. The loss surface is a long narrow canyon, and a step size small enough not to bounce off the walls crawls along the floor. Standardising each column (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) turns the canyon into a round bowl. The two functions you write for this, column_stats and standardise, are the ones that milestones 3, 4 and 6 call, so there is one rule to keep: the statistics come from the training rows and are applied unchanged to anything else. Recomputing them on test data is the preprocessing leak of chapter 14.

The figure is the lab's own data, produced by your gd_linear: 2,000 steps each, the same rows, the same code. A step size of 0.1 is perfectly safe after standardising and fatal before it, and 0.0001 is the best a raw-feature run can do. Raising the raw step size to 0.001 makes the loss multiply by about 190 every step (the check prints the first few values). If you want to see this move under your fingers, chapter 5's gradient-descent explorer draws the same canyon.

def column_stats(X):
    """Per-column (means, standard deviations), the standard deviation being the population one
    (divide by n). A constant column gets sd = 1.0 so that dividing by it is harmless."""
    n = len(X)
    mu = [sum(col) / n for col in zip(*X)]
    sd = [math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in col) / n) or 1.0 for col, m in zip(zip(*X), mu)]
    return mu, sd

def standardise(X, mu, sd):
    """(x - mu) / sd, column by column. mu and sd come from the TRAINING rows only."""
    return [[(v - m) / s for v, m, s in zip(row, mu, sd)] for row in X]

def mse_gradient(X, y, theta):
    """Gradient of the mean squared error with respect to theta = [b, w...]:  (2/n) A^T (A theta - y)."""
    n = len(y)
    resid = [p - t for p, t in zip(predict_linear(X, theta), y)]
    g = [2.0 * sum(resid) / n]
    for col in zip(*X):
        g.append(2.0 * dot(col, resid) / n)
    return g

def gradient_descent(loss_fn, grad_fn, theta0, lr, steps):
    """Plain batch gradient descent. Returns (theta, history).

    history[t] is loss_fn(theta) at the START of step t (so history[0] is the loss of theta0)
    and has exactly `steps` entries. Each step is theta <- theta - lr * grad_fn(theta).
    """
    theta, history = list(theta0), []
    for _ in range(steps):
        history.append(loss_fn(theta))
        g = grad_fn(theta)
        theta = [t - lr * gj for t, gj in zip(theta, g)]
    return theta, history

def gd_linear(X, y, lr, steps):
    """Fit a linear model by gradient descent on the MSE, starting from all zeros.

    Returns (theta, history) with theta = [b, w...] in the units of the X you pass in.
    """
    def loss(th):
        return mse(y, predict_linear(X, th))

    def grad(th):
        return mse_gradient(X, y, th)

    return gradient_descent(loss, grad, [0.0] * (len(X[0]) + 1), lr, steps)

Milestone 3: ridge regression and choosing alpha by cross-validation

Chapter 6's lesson is that a flexible model fits the noise along with the signal, and that the cure is to pay for complexity. Ridge regression adds the sum of the squared weights, scaled by alpha, to the squared error. Its great virtue for this lab is that it still has a formula: the normal equations gain a constant on the diagonal, w = (ZTZ + alpha I)−1 ZT(y − mean y), where Z is the standardised feature matrix. The extra diagonal makes the matrix invertible even when columns are duplicated, which closes the loop with milestone 1.

Two details that every implementation gets wrong at least once. First, standardise before penalising: the penalty adds up squared weights, and a weight measured per square metre is a different size from the same weight per square foot. Without standardising the penalty would favour some features for no reason except their units, and the check proves you did it by scaling a column by 1,000 and asking for identical predictions. Second, never penalise the intercept: it only sets the average level of the predictions. Centring y and standardising the columns makes the intercept equal to mean(y) automatically, which is why the given StdLinear container stores y_mean separately.

Hand check, one feature: with the points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5) the standardised column is (−1.2247, 0, 1.2247), ZTZ = 3 and ZT(y − mean y) = 3.6742, so the weight is 3.6742 / (3 + alpha): 1.2247 at alpha = 0, which is ordinary least squares, and 0.6124 at alpha = 3. As alpha grows the weight goes to zero and the model predicts the mean for everyone.

Then the question chapter 6 left to a library call: which alpha? The training loss always prefers alpha = 0, so the choice has to be made on rows the fit did not see. k-fold cross-validation splits the training rows into k groups, fits on k − 1 of them, scores on the held-out one, and averages the k scores; select_alpha runs that for each candidate and keeps the best. Here fold membership is i % k, so the check can predict exactly which rows each fit should see, with a spy model that records them.

The scenario is chapter 6's: a degree-12 polynomial, 13 parameters, fitted to 18 noisy points of a sine wave. Unpenalised, the training RMSE is below the noise floor of 0.30 and the test RMSE is in the tens, because the weights are large and nearly cancel on the training points and explode between them. The cross-validated alpha brings the test RMSE to between 0.39 and 0.46 on the three datasets the check tries.

def ridge_fit(X, y, alpha):
    """Ridge regression: minimise sum (y - yhat)^2 + alpha * sum w_j^2 on standardised features.

    Standardise X with column_stats, centre y, and solve (Z^T Z + alpha I) w = Z^T (y - mean(y)).
    The intercept is never penalised: it is the mean of y once the columns are centred.
    Returns a StdLinear. alpha = 0 is ordinary least squares.
    """
    mu, sd = column_stats(X)
    Z = standardise(X, mu, sd)
    ybar = sum(y) / len(y)
    yc = [v - ybar for v in y]
    G = matmul(transpose(Z), Z)
    for j in range(len(G)):
        G[j][j] += alpha
    w = solve(G, [dot(col, yc) for col in zip(*Z)])
    return StdLinear(mu, sd, ybar, w)

def kfold_rmse(fit, X, y, k=5):
    """Mean validation RMSE over k folds. Row i belongs to fold i % k.

    fit(X_train, y_train) must return an object with .predict(X). Each fold is scored on
    the rows it did not train on; the result is the average of the k fold RMSEs.
    """
    scores = []
    for f in range(k):
        tr = [i for i in range(len(y)) if i % k != f]
        va = [i for i in range(len(y)) if i % k == f]
        model = fit([X[i] for i in tr], [y[i] for i in tr])
        scores.append(rmse([y[i] for i in va], model.predict([X[i] for i in va])))
    return sum(scores) / k

def select_alpha(X, y, alphas, k=5):
    """The alpha with the lowest k-fold CV RMSE for ridge_fit, as (alpha, cv_rmse).
    Ties go to the first alpha in the list."""
    best = None
    for a in alphas:
        score = kfold_rmse(lambda Xt, yt, a=a: ridge_fit(Xt, yt, a), X, y, k)
        if best is None or score < best[1]:
            best = (a, score)
    return best

Milestone 4: the lasso, by coordinate descent

Replace the squared weights by their absolute values and you have the lasso: minimise (1/2n) ∑(y − yhat)2 + alpha ∑|wj|. The absolute value has a corner at zero, so there is no gradient there and no closed form, but the problem has a beautiful structure: if you hold every weight but one fixed, the best value of that one is a one-line formula. Coordinate descent cycles through the features applying it, over and over, until nothing moves.

Here is the formula. Standardise the columns so each has mean square one. Let r be the current residual, and for feature j define rhoj = (1/n) zj · r + wj. This is the weight feature j would take if it were the only one allowed to change and there were no penalty (the term (1/n) zj · r is how much it is currently asked to move, and wj is where it already is). The penalty then pulls that proposal towards zero by alpha and stops at zero. That pull is the soft-threshold function, S(rho, alpha) = sign(rho) × max(|rho| − alpha, 0), and the new weight is S(rhoj, alpha).

The flat yellow band is why the lasso selects features: any feature whose own best weight is smaller than alpha in absolute value is set to exactly zero, not merely shrunk. The same function tells you the smallest alpha that zeroes every feature: alpha_max is the largest |(1/n) zj · (y − mean y)|. Above it, the model is the mean; below it, the most useful feature switches on first.

How do you test an iterative optimiser without another copy of it? With the problem's own optimality conditions, which for the lasso are two sentences. For a feature with a non-zero weight, its correlation with the residual, (1/n) zj · r, must equal alpha times the sign of the weight (the penalty's pull and the data's push balance exactly). For a feature held at zero, that correlation must be no larger than alpha in absolute value (the push is too weak to overcome the penalty). The check computes both from your output and the raw data, for every feature, at five values of alpha. If you stop sweeping too early, or forget the + wj in rho, these fail with a message naming the feature.

The scenario: 60 rows, 20 features, five real (weights 4, −3, 2, −2, 1.5) and fifteen noise columns, noise sd 3. At alpha = 0 the lasso is ordinary least squares and uses all 20 features. At alpha = 0.3 it keeps the five real features and a handful of strays (9 to 12 of 20), and its error on fresh data is lower than least squares on all three datasets the check tries. Ridge on the same data never produces an exact zero; the check asserts that too.

def soft_threshold(r, t):
    """S(r, t) = sign(r) * max(|r| - t, 0): shrink r towards zero by t, and stop at zero."""
    if r > t:
        return r - t
    if r < -t:
        return r + t
    return 0.0

def alpha_max(X, y):
    """The smallest alpha at which the lasso solution is all zeros: max_j |(1/n) z_j . (y - mean y)|
    for standardised columns z_j."""
    mu, sd = column_stats(X)
    Z = standardise(X, mu, sd)
    n = len(y)
    ybar = sum(y) / n
    yc = [v - ybar for v in y]
    return max(abs(dot(col, yc)) / n for col in zip(*Z))

def lasso_fit(X, y, alpha, max_sweeps=1000, tol=1e-9):
    """Lasso: minimise (1/2n) sum (y - yhat)^2 + alpha * sum |w_j| on standardised features.

    Coordinate descent: cycle through the features; for feature j compute
    rho_j = (1/n) z_j . r + w_j, where r is the current residual (so rho_j is the best weight for j
    on its own, given the others), and set w_j = soft_threshold(rho_j, alpha). Stop when no weight
    moved by more than tol in a whole sweep, or after max_sweeps. Returns a StdLinear.
    """
    mu, sd = column_stats(X)
    Z = standardise(X, mu, sd)
    n, d = len(y), len(X[0])
    ybar = sum(y) / n
    r = [v - ybar for v in y]                       # residual with all weights at zero
    cols = [list(c) for c in zip(*Z)]
    w = [0.0] * d
    for _ in range(max_sweeps):
        biggest = 0.0
        for j in range(d):
            zj = cols[j]
            rho = dot(zj, r) / n + w[j]             # mean(z_j^2) = 1, so this is the one-feature optimum
            new = soft_threshold(rho, alpha)
            delta = new - w[j]
            if delta:
                r = [ri - delta * zi for ri, zi in zip(r, zj)]
                w[j] = new
                biggest = max(biggest, abs(delta))
        if biggest < tol:
            break
    return StdLinear(mu, sd, ybar, w)

Milestone 5: the sigmoid, log-loss and the logistic gradient

Now probabilities. Logistic regression keeps the weighted sum z = b + w · x and passes it through the sigmoid, p = 1 / (1 + e−z), which squeezes any real number into (0, 1). It is scored by log-loss (binary cross-entropy): for each row the cost is −ln of the probability the model gave to what actually happened, and the loss is the average. Three pieces, each with a trap that has bitten real code.

  • The sigmoid overflows. math.exp(1000) raises OverflowError, so the textbook formula crashes on a confident model. For negative z use the algebraically equal ez / (1 + ez), whose exponential is tiny instead of huge. The check calls sigmoid(-1000) and sigmoid(1000).
  • Log-loss is infinite at 0 and 1. A model that says exactly 0 for something that happens pays −ln 0. Clip every probability into [10−12, 1 − 10−12], so a confident mistake costs about 27.6 and the training run goes on.
  • The gradient is p − y, not y − p. Chapter 5's derivation: the sigmoid's derivative p(1 − p) cancels the denominators of the log-loss, leaving (1/n) AT(p − y), the same "features times prediction error" shape as least squares. With the penalty (l2/2) ∑ wj2 added to the loss, the gradient gains l2 × wj for every weight except the intercept.

The gradient check from milestone 2 earns its keep here. Get the sign wrong and gradient descent still runs: it climbs the loss, the weights run off, and an afternoon is lost to a model that "doesn't converge". def sigmoid(z):
    """1 / (1 + e^-z), without ever overflowing: sigmoid(-1000) is 0.0 and sigmoid(1000) is 1.0."""
    if z >= 0:
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-z))
    e = math.exp(z)
    return e / (1.0 + e)

def log_loss(y, p, eps=1e-12):
    """Mean binary cross-entropy -[y ln p + (1 - y) ln(1 - p)], with p clipped into [eps, 1 - eps]."""
    total = 0.0
    for t, q in zip(y, p):
        q = min(max(q, eps), 1.0 - eps)
        total -= t * math.log(q) + (1.0 - t) * math.log(1.0 - q)
    return total / len(y)

def predict_proba(X, theta):
    """P(y = 1 | x) = sigmoid(theta[0] + theta[1:] . x) for every row."""
    return [sigmoid(theta[0] + dot(theta[1:], row)) for row in X]

def logistic_gradient(X, y, theta, l2=0.0):
    """Gradient of  mean log-loss + (l2 / 2) * sum_{j >= 1} theta_j^2  with respect to theta.

    The log-loss part is (1/n) A^T (p - y); the penalty adds l2 * theta_j to every entry except the
    intercept (entry 0), which is never penalised.
    """
    n = len(y)
    err = [p - t for p, t in zip(predict_proba(X, theta), y)]
    g = [sum(err) / n]
    for col, wj in zip(zip(*X), theta[1:]):
        g.append(dot(col, err) / n + l2 * wj)
    return g

def odds_ratio(w):
    """The factor by which the odds multiply when a feature rises by one unit: e^w."""
    return math.exp(w)

Milestone 6: fitting logistic regression, with and without a penalty

The last milestone is short because the earlier ones did the work: standardise the training rows, start from zeros and hand the loss and its gradient to the gradient_descent you wrote in milestone 2. The loss is the mean log-loss plus the penalty (l2/2) ∑ wj2, with the intercept free. The returned StdLogistic remembers the training means and standard deviations, so predict_proba on raw rows does the standardising itself, and to_original() converts the weights back to the original units (the check verifies both routes agree, which catches a population-versus-sample standard deviation mismatch).

Three things to verify, and the check does all of them. Convergence. At the optimum the gradient is zero, so after 400 steps at step size 0.5 every entry of the unpenalised gradient should be below 0.002. The intercept's balance. The zero gradient of the intercept says mean(p) = mean(y): the average predicted churn probability equals the churn rate of the training rows, a free calibration check. Beating the base rate. On chapter 5's churn data the test log-loss must be clearly below that of a model which says the base rate to everyone, and the signs must make sense: tenure negative, spend and tickets positive.

Then perfect separation, the situation chapter 5 warned about. If a line splits the two classes exactly, the log-loss can always be lowered a little more by making the weights bigger, which sharpens the boundary, so the best unpenalised weights are at infinity. Gradient descent shows it plainly: it never converges, it just stops when you do. A tiny penalty restores a finite optimum.

Finally, reading the answer. A logistic weight on a standardised feature is a log-odds change per standard deviation, and odds_ratio(w) = ew is the factor by which the odds multiply. The check prints them for the churn data (an odds ratio of about 0.3 per standard deviation of tenure and about 1.9 per standard deviation of tickets). Resist reading 1.9 as "the probability nearly doubles": try it on the churn model in the first stretch exercise below, where the same odds ratio moves the probability by 1.7 points from a base of 2% and by 15 points from a base of 50%.

A stability note

Plain gradient descent on this loss is stable while lr × (l2 + 0.25) stays below about 2, because the log-loss curvature on standardised features is at most about a quarter and the penalty adds l2 to it. With lr = 0.5, an l2 of 10 diverges, which is a good thing to see once. The check uses a smaller step size for its heaviest penalty.

def fit_logistic(X, y, l2=0.0, lr=0.5, steps=400):
    """Logistic regression by gradient descent on standardised features, from all-zero weights.

    Minimises mean log-loss + (l2 / 2) * sum w_j^2 (intercept free). Standardise with column_stats
    (training rows only), then use gradient_descent with logistic_gradient. Returns a StdLogistic.
    Plain gradient descent is stable while lr * (l2 + 0.25) stays below about 2, so a heavy penalty needs a smaller lr.
    """
    mu, sd = column_stats(X)
    Z = standardise(X, mu, sd)

    def loss(th):
        return log_loss(y, predict_proba(Z, th)) + 0.5 * l2 * sum(w * w for w in th[1:])

    def grad(th):
        return logistic_gradient(Z, y, th, l2)

    theta, _ = gradient_descent(loss, grad, [0.0] * (len(X[0]) + 1), lr, steps)
    return StdLogistic(mu, sd, theta)

Systems connection

The standardiser you built has an operational life: its means and standard deviations are part of the model, and a serving system that computes them again on live traffic, or applies yesterday's statistics to a changed feature, quietly changes every prediction. That is the training-serving skew that Designing with ML & LLMs treats as a design problem, and the preprocessing leak of chapter 14 is its offline twin. The same chapter explains why a model this small is often the right one to deploy: evaluating it is a few dozen multiplications, which is what lets it sit in a request path with a tight latency budget.

What you learned

  • Least squares is one linear solve, (ATA) theta = ATy, and its defining property is that the residuals are orthogonal to every column. A pivot near zero means the columns are dependent and there is no unique answer.
  • Gradient descent needs a gradient you have tested against finite differences, and a step size that depends on the units of the features: standardise with training statistics only, then one step size suits every weight.
  • Ridge keeps every weight small and has a formula; the lasso sets weights exactly to zero through the soft-threshold, and is verified by its optimality conditions. Choose alpha by cross-validation on training rows.
  • Logistic regression is the same loop with a different loss: the sigmoid must not overflow, log-loss must be clipped, and the gradient is (1/n) AT(p - y). On separable data the unpenalised optimum is at infinity, and a small L2 penalty fixes it.
  • Exponentiated logistic weights are odds ratios, which multiply odds, not probabilities.

Check your understanding

Gradient descent on raw rent features converges slowly at step size 0.0001 and diverges at 0.001. Which change fixes both problems?

  1. Use step size 0.0005, halfway between the two
  2. Run for 20,000 steps at 0.0001
  3. Standardise the features with training-row statistics, then use a step size such as 0.1
  4. Standardise using statistics computed on the test rows, so they match the data being predicted
The stable step size is set by the steepest direction of the loss surface, which depends on the units of the features; with area up to 120 and distance up to 15 the surface is a long narrow canyon. Standardising makes it round, so a single larger step size works for every weight. Simply running longer (option 2) eventually helps but wastes thousands of steps, and 0.0005 is probably past the stability limit. Option 4 is the preprocessing leak: statistics must come from the training rows only.

You call lasso_fit(X, y, alpha) with alpha slightly larger than alpha_max(X, y). What do you get?

  1. The ordinary least squares solution, because the penalty is too weak to matter
  2. All weights exactly zero, so every prediction equals the mean of y
  3. Only the single most correlated feature, with a shrunken weight
  4. A solution with small but non-zero weights on every feature
alpha_max is the largest correlation between a standardised feature and the centred target. Above it, every feature's own best weight rho is smaller than alpha in absolute value, so the soft-threshold sets all of them to exactly zero. Just below it, the strongest feature switches on (option 3 describes that, but for alpha slightly smaller). Option 4 describes ridge, which never produces exact zeros.

A colleague implements the logistic gradient as (1/n) AT(y - p) and runs gradient descent with the usual update theta - lr * gradient. What happens?

  1. Nothing visibly different: the sign only changes the direction of the update by a constant
  2. It converges to the same weights, but twice as slowly
  3. It converges to the weights of the opposite class
  4. The log-loss rises with every step, so the weights run away from the optimum
The update subtracts the gradient to go downhill. With the sign flipped, the "gradient" points downhill instead, so subtracting it moves uphill and the loss grows. A finite-difference check catches this immediately, which is why milestone 5 has one. Option 3 sounds plausible but there is no such thing as the weights of the opposite class here: the same formula would just minimise the wrong thing.

Unpenalised logistic regression is trained on data that a straight line separates perfectly, for 400 steps and then for 2,000. How do the weights compare?

  1. The 2,000-step weights are larger, and would keep growing: a sharper boundary always lowers the log-loss a little
  2. They are identical, because the loss reached its minimum before 400 steps
  3. The 2,000-step weights are smaller, because later steps correct earlier overshoot
  4. They oscillate, because the step size is too large for separable data
On separable data every correctly classified point gains from a more confident prediction, so the log-loss has no minimum: its infimum is zero, approached only as the weights go to infinity. Gradient descent therefore keeps growing the weights (6.2 at 400 steps and 9.6 at 2,000 in the lab's data). An L2 penalty creates a finite optimum, which the check confirms is reached by 400 steps.

A logistic model reports an odds ratio of 1.9 per standard deviation of tickets. A customer currently has a 50% chance of churning. About what is the chance after one more standard deviation of tickets, other features fixed?

  1. About 95%, because 50% × 1.9
  2. About 69%, because the probability rises by 19 points
  3. About 66%, because odds of 1 become odds of 1.9, which is a probability of 1.9 / 2.9
  4. About 52%, because the effect is small near 50%
The odds multiply by 1.9: from 1 to 1.9, and a probability is odds / (1 + odds) = 1.9 / 2.9 = 0.655. The effect on a probability is largest in the middle of the sigmoid (about 15 points here) and shrinks near 0 and 1. Options 1 and 2 treat the odds ratio as a multiplier or increment on the probability; option 4 has the shape backwards.
