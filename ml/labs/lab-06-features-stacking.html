Lab 6: Feature Pipelines & Stacking

Chapter 17 introduced target encoding, which turns a 300-level postcode into one informative number, and chapter 20 introduced stacking, which lets a small model learn how to combine several big ones. Both learn a statistic from labelled rows and hand it to a later model, and both produce spectacular validation scores when a row's own label sneaks into its own input. In this lab you build the safe versions: a smoothed target encoding, the out-of-fold encoding that keeps each row's label away from its own feature, a whole pipeline that survives cross-validation, out-of-fold predictions from any model, and a stacked ensemble that really does beat its parts. A checker flips labels, spies on what each model was trained on, and shows you the leaky twin of every honest answer, with its inflated score.

Why this lab matters

Two of the most useful ideas in applied machine learning are also two of the easiest ways to fool yourself. A target encoding of a postcode, a merchant, a product or a user ID is often the strongest feature in a lender's, insurer's or retailer's table, and the naive version contains the label it is supposed to predict. A stacked ensemble is how competition winners squeeze out the last points, and the naive version trusts a model's predictions on the very rows it memorised. Neither bug raises an error. Both make the validation score higher, which is why they survive code review, and both vanish in production, where a new postcode has no label and a new row was not in the training set.

This lab is the sequel to Lab 4, which built a harness that detects leaks. Here you build the pipelines that avoid them, and the checks use the same instincts: a perturbation test (flip a label, see what moves), spy models that record what they were shown, and a leaky twin to compare against. You also meet the cost side of ensembles, which the Netflix Prize case made famous: every extra base model is another fit, another dependency and, at serving time, another latency to wait for.

Pure Python, seeded, about ten seconds

Python 3.9 or newer and the standard library only: no NumPy, no scikit-learn. Every dataset comes from a seeded random.Random, so the numbers on this page are the numbers you will see (see reproducible experiments). The biggest jobs are the nearest-neighbour model (it uses math.dist, which runs in C) and the unbraked tree, and the whole check takes under ten seconds. A failed property check names its seed, and python3 check.py solution 3 2 replays milestone 3 on seed 2 alone. Everything a base model learns, it learns inside the function you hand it, so a spy can see exactly which rows it was trained on.

What you'll build

Five milestones, each adding a group of functions and a group of checks:

  1. Smoothed target encoding: a table that maps each postcode to a shrunk average of the label, with the overall rate as the fallback for levels never seen.
  2. Out-of-fold encoding: encode each fold from the other folds only, so no row's encoding contains its own label. Verified by flipping labels and watching which encodings move.
  3. A leak-safe pipeline inside cross-validation: encode, fit and score within each training part, and compare the honest AUC with the leaky one on noise postcodes and on real ones.
  4. Out-of-fold predictions: for any model, one prediction per row from a model that never saw that row, verified by spy models.
  5. A stacked ensemble: a meta-model on the logits of the base models' out-of-fold predictions, beating its best part, and collapsing when it is trained on in-sample predictions.

Milestone 1: smoothed target encoding

Target encoding (also called mean encoding or likelihood encoding; Micci-Barreca, 2001) replaces a category by the average of the target within it: the default rate of customers in postcode "pc017". It compresses a column with hundreds of levels into one number a model can use, which one-hot encoding cannot do without hundreds of columns. The danger is in the arithmetic. A level with four training rows and one positive encodes as 0.25, and that 0.25 includes the positive row's own label. This milestone builds the table; the next one removes the leak that the table creates.

First, smoothing. A level with three rows should not be trusted as much as one with three thousand, so blend the level's mean with the overall rate, weighted by how many rows support it:

encoded = (n × level mean + m × prior) / (n + m)

Here n is the number of rows in the level, the prior is the overall mean of the label, and m is the smoothing weight, which you can read as "the number of imaginary average rows added to every level". With m = 20 a level of three rows sits 20/23 = 87% of the way to the prior, and a level of 3,000 rows sits 0.7% of the way. This is empirical Bayes shrinkage, the same instinct as the ridge penalty of chapter 6. Since n × level mean is just the number of positives in the level, the formula needs only two counts per level.

Take cats = a, a, a, b with labels 1, 1, 0, 0. The prior is 0.5. With m = 20, level a has 3 rows and 2 positives: (2 + 10) / 23 = 0.5217. Level b has 1 row and no positives: (0 + 10) / 21 = 0.4762. With m = 0 you get the raw means, 0.667 and 0. And a level the table has never seen, such as a postcode that first appears in production, gets the prior: it is the honest answer to "what do we know about this level?".

Solution spoiler 
def smoothed_means(cats, y, m):
    """Learn a target encoding. Returns (table, prior).

    prior is the overall mean of y. For each level seen in cats, with n rows and level mean mu:

        table[level] = (n * mu + m * prior) / (n + m)

    m is the smoothing weight: "the number of imaginary average rows added to every level". m = 0 gives the
    raw level mean; a level with few rows is pulled towards the prior, a level with many hardly moves.
    """
    prior = sum(y) / len(y)
    count, total = {}, {}
    for c, t in zip(cats, y):
        count[c] = count.get(c, 0) + 1
        total[c] = total.get(c, 0) + t
    table = {c: (total[c] + m * prior) / (count[c] + m) for c in count}     # n * mu is the sum of the labels
    return table, prior


def encode(table, prior, cats):
    """Look each level up in the table. A level the table has never seen gets the prior."""
    return [table.get(c, prior) for c in cats]

Milestone 2: out-of-fold encoding

Smoothing reduces how much label leaks into a row's encoding; it does not remove it. Look at a postcode with exactly one training row. With m = 20 and a prior of 0.3, the encoding is (1 + 6) / 21 = 0.333 if the row is positive and 6 / 21 = 0.286 if it is negative. The two values differ, and the difference is the row's own label. A model that sees 0.333 learns, from the training rows, that high means positive, which is true of every training row with a singleton postcode by construction. For a new row in production the column carries no such information.

The cure is to never compute a row's encoding from a set that contains that row. Split the training rows into k folds. For each fold in turn, learn the table from the other k − 1 folds, and use it to encode the held-out fold. Every training row then carries an encoding built without its own label, which is how the column will look for new rows. (For validation and production rows you use a table learned from all the training rows; milestone 3.) A level that appears only in the held-out fold gets the prior of the other folds, which is also exactly what a brand-new postcode will receive.

Here is the hand case the checker uses: cats a, a, b, b, a, b, labels 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, three folds {0, 1}, {2, 3}, {4, 5}, and m = 0. Fold 0 (rows 0 and 1, both a) is encoded from rows 2 to 5, where a appears once with label 0, so both rows get 0.0. Fold 1 (b, b) is encoded from rows 0, 1, 4, 5, where b appears once with label 0, so 0.0. Fold 2 (a then b) is encoded from rows 0 to 3, where a is 1 of 2 and b is 2 of 2, so 0.5 and 1.0. The result is [0, 0, 0, 0, 0.5, 1.0].

How do you test that a function has no leak, without reading it? Change one label and ask which outputs moved. If the label of row i is flipped, the encodings of the rows in i's own fold must be bit-for-bit unchanged, because they were built from the other folds. The encodings of other folds are allowed to change (they were built partly from fold i), and the checker insists that at least some do, so a function that returns a constant cannot pass. It flips twelve random labels and compares every row each time.

Finally, the scenario. On 1,500 applications whose postcodes are pure noise, the checker measures how well the encoding alone predicts the label, as an AUC, in two ways:

The in-sample encoding scores 0.75 on postcodes that carry no information at all; it is simply memorising labels. The out-of-fold one scores 0.48, below a coin toss, and that is not a bug. When a noise postcode has a fixed number of positives in total, a positive row leaves the other rows with one fewer, so its out-of-fold encoding is slightly lower than a negative row's. The bias is small and points the honest way (against the label), and it means you can recognise a leak-free noise feature by an AUC at or just under one half. With real postcode effects the honest encoding finds the signal (0.66) and still scores far below the leaky one (0.84).

Solution spoiler 
def oof_encode(cats, y, folds, m):
    """Encode the training rows without any row's own label (or any label of its fold) reaching it.

    For each fold: learn smoothed_means from the rows NOT in the fold, then encode the fold's rows with it
    (levels unseen outside the fold get that table's prior). Returns one value per row, in row order.
    """
    out = [0.0] * len(cats)
    for fold in folds:
        held = set(fold)
        rest = [i for i in range(len(cats)) if i not in held]
        table, prior = smoothed_means([cats[i] for i in rest], [y[i] for i in rest], m)
        for i, v in zip(fold, encode(table, prior, [cats[i] for i in fold])):
            out[i] = v
    return out

Milestone 3: a leak-safe pipeline inside cross-validation

A single encoding is a piece of a pipeline, and a pipeline has to be validated as a whole. The rule of chapter 13 and chapter 14: every learned step lives inside the fold. For target encoding that has three parts.

  1. Split first. In cross-validation, an outer fold's rows are the validation rows, and the rest are the training part. Nothing about the validation labels is used again until you score.
  2. Inside the training part, encode the training categories out of fold (milestone 2), so the model that learns from them sees honest encodings.
  3. Encode the validation rows with a table learned from all the training part (milestone 1). There is no label of theirs to hold out, so no further trick is needed. Append the encoding to the numeric columns, fit, predict, and take the fold's AUC.

The checker builds the leaky alternative for you, with your own milestone 1 functions: learn one table from all rows, encode every row with it, and then cross-validate. It looks sensible, every individual step is correct, and it is wrong, because each validation row's encoding was built partly from that row's own label. Here is how far the two disagree, on 1,500 applications:

With noise postcodes the honest pipeline learns nothing from the column and scores what the numeric columns score alone (0.704), as it must. The leaky pipeline reports 0.791: nearly nine points of AUC that do not exist. With real postcode effects the honest pipeline gains three points over the numeric columns (0.707 against 0.680) and the leaky one claims 0.829. A team that tuned the smoothing weight against the leaky number would pick the setting that leaks most, since less smoothing means more label in the feature.

Two more properties are tested. A prediction for a test row must not depend on which other rows are being scored: the checker scores the first 40 test rows alone and among 160 others and compares. If something uses statistics of the test set (a mean, a standardisation, a table), the numbers change and the pipeline has quietly looked at data it should not have. And a postcode never seen in training must produce a finite probability, not a crash (the prior does the work).

def pipeline_predict(Xtr, cats_tr, ytr, Xte, cats_te, k=5, m=10.0, seed=0):
    """Train the whole pipeline on the training rows and score the test rows. Returns one probability per test row.

    1. Encode the training categories out of fold (k folds from kfold_folds(len(ytr), k, seed), smoothing m).
    2. Learn the table on ALL the training rows and encode the test categories with it.
    3. Append the encoding to the numeric columns as one extra column; fit_logreg on the training rows; predict.

    Nothing about the test rows' labels is available here, and the test rows play no part in any step.
    """
    folds = kfold_folds(len(ytr), k, seed)
    enc_tr = oof_encode(cats_tr, ytr, folds, m)
    table, prior = smoothed_means(cats_tr, ytr, m)
    enc_te = encode(table, prior, cats_te)
    A = [list(r) + [e] for r, e in zip(Xtr, enc_tr)]
    B = [list(r) + [e] for r, e in zip(Xte, enc_te)]
    return fit_logreg(A, ytr)(B)


def cv_auc(X, cats, y, folds, k=5, m=10.0, seed=0):
    """Honest cross-validated AUC of the pipeline: for each outer fold, run pipeline_predict on the other rows,
    score the fold, and average the fold AUCs."""
    aucs = []
    for fold in folds:
        held = set(fold)
        tr = [i for i in range(len(y)) if i not in held]
        scores = pipeline_predict([X[i] for i in tr], [cats[i] for i in tr], [y[i] for i in tr],
                                  [X[i] for i in fold], [cats[i] for i in fold], k, m, seed)
        aucs.append(roc_auc(scores, [y[i] for i in fold]))
    return sum(aucs) / len(aucs)

Milestone 4: out-of-fold predictions

Target encoding learns a statistic from labels and hands it to a model. The second half of the lab does the same with a whole model's output. Chapter 20's blending and stacking section gives the reason: to learn how to combine base models, a meta-model needs to see their predictions on rows they have not been trained on, because that is what their predictions on new rows will look like. The predictions a model makes on its own training rows are the best it will ever produce.

The procedure is the one you just built, applied to predictions instead of encodings. Split the training rows into k folds. For each fold, fit the model on the other k − 1 folds and predict the held-out fold. After k rounds every training row has a prediction from a model that never saw it. Those predictions form one column of the meta-model's training matrix.

How different are the two kinds of prediction? Here are the AUCs of the four base models on 900 training rows, scored on the rows they were fitted to and out of fold:

The logistic regression barely changes: with ten columns and 900 rows it cannot memorise. The unbraked tree goes from 0.59 out of fold to a perfect 1.000 on its own rows, which is the signature of memorisation. A meta-model that is shown the red dots will conclude the tree is flawless and hand it nearly all the weight. This is the same failure as the in-sample target encoding, one level up.

Ordering and duplicates

Out-of-fold predictions only mimic new rows if the folds do. With time-ordered data, random folds let a model see the future of the rows it predicts, so use time-ordered folds (chapter 18). With repeated customers, patients or scans, rows of one entity must stay in one fold (the grouped folds of chapter 13); otherwise a model predicts a row from its own twin. The third experiment near the end of this page measures the second case: duplicate every row and the memoriser's out-of-fold AUC jumps from 0.62 to 0.91.

Solution spoiler 
def oof_predictions(fit_predict, X, y, folds):
    """Predict every row with a model that never saw it.

    For each fold: call fit_predict(X_train, y_train, X_fold) with the rows OUTSIDE the fold as training data
    and the fold's rows as the rows to predict. Returns one score per row, in row order.
    """
    out = [0.0] * len(y)
    for fold in folds:
        held = set(fold)
        tr = [i for i in range(len(y)) if i not in held]
        preds = fit_predict([X[i] for i in tr], [y[i] for i in tr], [X[i] for i in fold])
        for i, s in zip(fold, preds):
            out[i] = s
    return out

Milestone 5: a stacked ensemble that beats its parts

Stacking (Wolpert, 1992, "Stacked generalization"; the regression version is Breiman, 1996, "Stacked regressions") trains a meta-model whose inputs are the base models' predictions and whose target is the true label. Averaging gives every model equal weight and fits nothing; stacking learns the weights, and with a flexible meta-model it can learn that one model is better in one range of scores. The price is one more thing to overfit, which is why the meta-model is almost always small. Here it is a logistic regression on the logits of the base predictions: the logit scale makes a prediction of 0.9 and 0.99 different in the way they are different, and the given logit clips at 0.02 and 0.98 so a base model that says exactly 0 or 1 cannot become an infinite input.

The recipe, in the order the lab does it:

For the training rows, each base model contributes its out-of-fold prediction (milestone 4), which gives an n × 4 matrix; the meta-model is fitted on it with the true labels (fit_stacker). For new rows, each base model is refitted on all the training rows and predicts them; the meta-model combines those predictions. So the base models are fitted k + 1 times each, here six. The checker proves it with a spy: one call per fold on the complement of the fold, and one call on all 300 rows to predict 37 new ones. With honest=False the lab keeps the classic mistake available, so you can measure it: the base models are fitted once on all the rows and predict those same rows, and the meta-model trains on those inflated predictions.

An honest caveat about the data

The gain here is larger than the half-point that stacking earned in chapter 20's example, because this dataset was designed for it: three kinds of structure, each captured by a different model, and noise columns that distract the others. On real tabular data, a weighted average of a few diverse models often captures most of the gain with much less risk; the competition habits in chapter 20 are about diversity, not about the number of layers. Try the ablation in the experiments below to see which model earns its place.

def fit_stacker(P, y):
    """The meta-model. P is a list of rows, one per case, holding the base models' predictions for that case.

    Replace every prediction by its logit (the given logit clips at 0.02 and 0.98), then fit_logreg on those
    columns. Returns (predict, weights): predict(P_new) gives one probability per row, and weights are the
    meta-model's coefficients on the standardised logit columns, one per base model.
    """
    model = fit_logreg([[logit(v) for v in row] for row in P], y)

    def predict(P_new):
        return model([[logit(v) for v in row] for row in P_new])

    return predict, list(model.weights)


def stack_fit_predict(base_models, Xtr, ytr, Xnew, folds, honest=True):
    """A stacked ensemble. base_models is a list of fit_predict functions. Returns (scores, weights).

    honest=True  (the recipe): build the meta-model's training matrix from OUT-OF-FOLD predictions, one column
                 per base model (oof_predictions with `folds`).
    honest=False (the mistake, kept to measure it): use each base model's predictions on its OWN training rows.

    Either way: fit_stacker on that matrix and ytr; refit every base model on ALL the training rows to predict
    Xnew; give those predictions to the meta-model. `weights` is what fit_stacker returned.
    """
    if honest:
        cols = [oof_predictions(fp, Xtr, ytr, folds) for fp in base_models]
    else:
        cols = [fp(Xtr, ytr, Xtr) for fp in base_models]
    predict, weights = fit_stacker([list(r) for r in zip(*cols)], ytr)
    new_cols = [fp(Xtr, ytr, Xnew) for fp in base_models]
    return predict([list(r) for r in zip(*new_cols)]), weights

Going further

  • Average the fold models instead of refitting. At prediction time, average the k fold models' predictions instead of refitting on all rows. It saves one fit and gives a small ensemble for free. Compare the test AUC and say what the meta-model now sees that it was not trained on.
  • A plain rank average. Replace the meta-model by the mean of the base models' ranks and compare with the stack on this dataset. When would you prefer it?
  • Non-negative weights. Constrain the meta-weights to be at least zero (a coordinate-descent or projected-gradient loop on the same logistic loss). Breiman's stacked regressions did, and it makes the weights read as shares.
  • Other encodings. Add a count (frequency) encoding and a rare-level bucket ("other" for levels with fewer than 5 training rows) to the pipeline. Which helps on noise postcodes, and why can a frequency encoding help even though it never reads the label?
  • Nested validation for the knobs. The smoothing weight m is a hyperparameter chosen by looking at validation scores. Choose it inside an inner loop and report the outer score, and compare with choosing it on the same folds you report.
  • Time-ordered folds. Build a dataset in which the postcode effect drifts over time and use a forward-chaining split for both the encoding and the stack (see chapter 18). How do the honest numbers change?
  • Ensemble latency. Time predict for each base model on 1,500 rows. If the four were called in parallel and the answer had to wait for the slowest, what is the added tail latency, and is 0.03 AUC worth it?

Systems connection

The encoding table is state that must travel. At serving time the model needs the postcode table learned from all the training rows, versioned with the model, and a defined answer for a postcode it has never seen. Computing features one way offline and another online is the classic train-serve skew, and the reason feature stores exist (designing with ML). A stack multiplies this: four base models mean four artefacts, four sets of dependencies and, if they are called in parallel and the answer waits for the slowest, the fan-out arithmetic of tail latency at scale. The Netflix Prize winners' blend of many predictors never ran in full in production for exactly this reason (Case 1).

What you learned

  • A smoothed target encoding is (n × level mean + m × prior) / (n + m): few rows means close to the prior, many rows means close to the level mean, and an unseen level gets the prior.
  • Smoothing shrinks the leak but does not remove it; a singleton level still encodes its own label. Out-of-fold encoding does remove it: each fold is encoded from the others only. A flipped-label test proves it without reading the code.
  • A whole pipeline must be validated as a whole: every learned step, encoding included, lives inside the fold. Encoding all rows first made a column of pure noise look like nine points of AUC.
  • Out-of-fold predictions are what a model's predictions on new rows look like. In-sample predictions are not: an unbraked tree scored 1.000 on its own rows and 0.59 out of fold.
  • Stacking trains a small meta-model on the logits of out-of-fold predictions and refits every base model on all the training rows for new data, which is k + 1 fits each. Honest, it beat its best part; in-sample, it trusted the memoriser and lost.
  • The folds themselves must mimic production: time-ordered when time matters, grouped when entities repeat. Every model you add is a cost, and an ablation tells you which ones pay for themselves.

Check your understanding

A postcode has 4 training rows, 3 of them positive. The overall rate is 0.20 and the smoothing weight is m = 16. What is its smoothed encoding?

  1. 0.75, the raw mean of the level
  2. 0.31
  3. 0.20, the prior, because 16 is larger than 4
  4. 0.48, the average of the raw mean and the prior
(3 + 16 × 0.20) / (4 + 16) = 6.2 / 20 = 0.31. The level moves 16/20 = 80% of the way from its raw mean 0.75 towards the prior 0.20, not all the way: m = 16 outweighs 4 rows but does not erase them. Averaging the raw mean and the prior (0.475) would give the level and the prior equal say, which is m = n.

With m = 20 and a prior of 0.3, a postcode with a single training row is encoded as 0.333 if that row is positive and 0.286 if negative. Why is smoothing alone not enough to make this safe?

  1. The values are too close to the prior for a model to notice
  2. Smoothing makes the encoding depend on how many rows the level has
  3. It is safe, because every value is close to the prior
  4. The encoding still moves with the row's own label, so a model can learn "high means positive" from training rows, where that holds by construction
What matters is not the size of the difference but that it exists and points the right way for every singleton level in the training set. A flexible model will find and use it, then fail on new rows whose encodings carry no label. Smoothing reduces how much leaks; only keeping the row's label out of its own encoding (out-of-fold) removes the leak.

You flip the label of one row in fold 3 and recompute the out-of-fold encodings. Which encodings may change?

  1. Only the flipped row's own encoding
  2. Every row in fold 3 and no others
  3. Rows outside fold 3, and no row inside fold 3
  4. Any row in any fold, since all encodings use the same labels
Fold 3 is encoded from folds 1, 2, 4 and 5, none of which contains the flipped label, so nothing in fold 3 can change. The other four folds are each encoded from tables that include fold 3, so rows there (the same postcode directly, any row through the prior) may change. If the flipped row's own encoding moved, its label leaked into its feature.

You stack 3 base models with 5-fold out-of-fold predictions. How many times are the base models fitted in total, not counting the meta-model?

  1. 18
  2. 15
  3. 3
  4. 8
Each base model is fitted 5 times to produce its out-of-fold column and once more on all the training rows to predict new data: 6 fits, and 3 × 6 = 18. Fifteen forgets the refit; 3 counts models, not fits; 8 adds 3 to 5 instead of multiplying.

A stacked model's meta-weights are 0.08, 0.1 and 3.1 for a logistic regression, a kNN and a deep unpruned tree, and on new data the stack scores worse than the logistic regression alone. What is the most likely cause?

  1. The meta-model needs more layers
  2. The tree is the strongest model, and the weights are right; the test set is unlucky
  3. The logits were clipped
  4. The meta-model was trained on the base models' predictions for their own training rows, so the tree looked perfect
A memorising tree predicts its own training rows almost perfectly, so on in-sample predictions it appears to be the only model worth listening to. On new rows it is mediocre. The fix is out-of-fold predictions for the meta-model's training matrix, not another layer. Clipping at 0.02 and 0.98 is a protection against infinite logits and does not create weights like these.

You forecast daily demand and build a stack on rows ordered by day, using random 5-fold out-of-fold predictions. What is the problem?

  1. None: out-of-fold predictions are always safe
  2. Models are trained on later days to predict earlier ones, so the out-of-fold predictions are better than any deployed model could produce
  3. Random folds are too small
  4. The meta-model cannot be a logistic regression on time series
In production the model knows the past and predicts the future. A random fold trains on days that come after the days it predicts, so the out-of-fold predictions use information a deployed model never has, and the meta-model learns to trust them too much. Use forward-chaining (time-ordered) folds, as in chapter 18. The same applies to the target encoding of a feature that drifts over time.
