Lab 4: The Leakage Hunt

Chapter 13 showed how to make a validation score match the deployment, and chapter 14 sorted the ways that information crosses the wall between training and validation into five families. Knowing the families does not find the leak in the dataset on your desk. In this lab you build the tool that does: a validation harness that takes a dataset and the way a team validated it, runs a battery of detectors, and reports which leak it found with the numbers that prove it. It has to find four planted leaks, one of them in preprocessing, and, just as important, stay silent on a clean dataset with a strong and perfectly legitimate feature.

Why this lab matters

A leaked model is the best-looking model you will ever train. Its validation score is high, stable and reproducible, because validation carries the same leak as training, so nothing in the usual dashboard warns you. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) found leakage errors in 17 fields of machine-learning-based science, so it is not a beginner's mistake. The defence is a habit, not a trick: every surprising result is a bug report until a harness has failed to break it. This lab turns the habit into code. You will write a leak scan and a timeline audit, a test that catches preprocessing leakage without knowing which step is guilty, grouped folds that expose duplicate and same-entity contamination, and a perturbation test that finds the future hidden inside a rolling feature.

The harness also teaches the other half of detection, which is restraint. Every detector has a threshold, every threshold has false alarms, and a harness that cries wolf on clean data gets switched off. The final milestone grades you on both: all four leaks found, nothing flagged on the control.

Five datasets, seeded, about six seconds

Python 3.9 or newer and the standard library only. make_case(kind, seed) builds five small datasets (a churn table, 100 patients with 300 measurements, 150 patients with repeated scans, a daily sales series and a loan table); each comes from a seeded random.Random, so the numbers on this page are the numbers you will see. The models are tiny: a logistic regression fitted by gradient descent and a 1-nearest-neighbour memoriser, both given. A failed property check names its seed, and python3 check.py solution 6 1 replays milestone 6 on the second version of each dataset only. The checker uses spy models that record exactly which rows and columns each fold trained on, so a leak in your own harness is caught the same way your harness catches one in the data.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each adding a detector, ending with all of them wired together:

  1. The measuring tools: ROC AUC with ties, k-fold splits and a cross-validation loop in which every learned step lives inside fit.
  2. The column scan and the timeline audit: score every column alone against the target, and check the feature register for columns written after the prediction time. Catches target leakage and collection artefacts.
  3. Preprocessing inside the fold: feature selection done the wrong way and the right way, and a shuffled-label test that exposes the wrong way without being told where to look.
  4. Twins across the wall: grouped folds, the share of validation rows with a twin in training, and the gap between ordinary and grouped scores.
  5. The future inside a feature: forward-chaining folds and a perturbation test that finds features that read day t or later.
  6. The whole hunt: run everything on every case and report each leak with its evidence, and nothing else.

Milestone 1: the measuring tools

A harness is only as honest as its measuring tools, so start with those. The metric is the ROC AUC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) of chapter 14: the probability that a randomly chosen positive row gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative one, with a tie counting one half. 0.5 is chance, 1.0 a perfect ranking and 0.0 a perfectly inverted one. You compute it from ranks: sort the scores, give each row its rank (tied scores share the average of their ranks), sum the ranks of the positives, and subtract the smallest sum a positive group could have. For scores 0.1, 0.4, 0.35, 0.8 and labels 0, 0, 1, 1, three of the four positive-negative pairs are ordered correctly, so the AUC is 0.75. The checker also compares your function with a brute-force count over every pair, on forty random lists with many ties.

Next, k-fold cross-validation (chapter 13): split the rows into k folds, and for each fold train on the other k − 1 and score on the held-out one. The contract gives the exact recipe so that a checker can reproduce it: shuffle the indices with random.Random(seed) and deal them round-robin. A split is a pair (train_idx, val_idx).

The key design decision is what a model is: a function fit(X_train, y_train) that returns a function predict(X). Everything that learns from data happens inside fit: the standardisation, the feature choice, the weights. Because cross_val_auc calls fit on training rows only, any step you put inside it is automatically leak-free. Any step you apply to the whole table before calling it is not. That one sentence is chapter 14's whole defence against preprocessing leakage, and milestone 3 breaks it on purpose.

Your task

Write roc_auc(scores, y) (raise ValueError if y has one class), kfold_splits(n, k, seed) and cross_val_auc(fit, X, y, splits), which returns the mean of the fold AUCs.

python3 check.py shows this milestone as ✔. It checks the textbook AUC (0.75), perfect, inverted and fully tied scores, a partial tie (0.5, 0.5, 0.9 with labels 0, 1, 1 gives 0.75), the single-class error, and forty seeded lists against the pair count. For the folds it checks that each fold's validation set is exactly shuffled[i::k], that every row is validated once, and that sizes for 10 rows in 3 folds are 4, 3, 3. A spy model proves that fit receives only each fold's training rows, and logistic regression on the clean loan data must reach an AUC of about 0.72.

Hints try without first
  • roc_auc: sort indices by score, then walk runs of equal scores. A run occupying sorted positions i to j (0-based) has average rank (i + j)/2 + 1.
  • AUC from ranks is (sum of the positives' ranks − n1(n1 + 1)/2) / (n1 n0).
  • kfold_splits: perm = list(range(n)); random.Random(seed).shuffle(perm), then fold i is sorted(perm[i::k]). The training rows of the fold are every other index, sorted.
  • cross_val_auc: predict = fit(Xtr, ytr) then predict(Xva); do not reuse a predictor across folds.

def roc_auc(scores, y):
    """ROC AUC: the probability that a random positive row has a higher score than a random negative one,
    counting ties as one half. Computed from ranks (tied scores share the average of their ranks).

    Raises ValueError if y does not contain both classes.
    """
    n1 = sum(1 for t in y if t == 1)
    n0 = len(y) - n1
    if n1 == 0 or n0 == 0:
        raise ValueError("roc_auc needs both classes in y")
    order = sorted(range(len(scores)), key=lambda i: scores[i])
    ranks = [0.0] * len(scores)
    i = 0
    while i < len(order):
        j = i
        while j + 1 < len(order) and scores[order[j + 1]] == scores[order[i]]:
            j += 1
        for t in range(i, j + 1):
            ranks[order[t]] = (i + j) / 2 + 1          # 1-based average rank of the tie group
        i = j + 1
    return (sum(r for r, t in zip(ranks, y) if t == 1) - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)


def kfold_splits(n, k, seed):
    """Plain k-fold: shuffle the row indices with random.Random(seed), then deal them round-robin into k folds
    (fold i gets shuffled[i::k]). Returns [(train_idx, val_idx), ...], both sorted ascending."""
    perm = list(range(n))
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(perm)
    folds = [sorted(perm[i::k]) for i in range(k)]
    out = []
    for i in range(k):
        held = set(folds[i])
        out.append((sorted(j for j in range(n) if j not in held), folds[i]))
    return out


def cross_val_auc(fit, X, y, splits):
    """Mean validation AUC over the folds. For each (train_idx, val_idx): fit on the training rows only, score the
    validation rows, compute the fold's AUC. `fit` is a model as defined in the header (it learns inside)."""
    aucs = []
    for tr, va in splits:
        predict = fit([X[i] for i in tr], [y[i] for i in tr])
        aucs.append(roc_auc(predict([X[i] for i in va]), [y[i] for i in va]))
    return sum(aucs) / len(aucs)

Milestone 2: the column scan and the timeline audit

Target leakage is a feature that is a consequence or a proxy of the label: a column recorded after the outcome, or the label under another name. The first line of defence in chapter 14 is cheap and blunt. Score every column on its own by how well it ranks the target, and look for anything that is far from 0.5 in either direction. Honest features of human or market behaviour are rarely close to perfect, so a single column with an AUC of 0.96 deserves an explanation before it deserves credit.

The second detector needs no data at all. The feature register is a table with one row per column saying where it comes from and, crucially, when its value is written relative to the prediction time T, the moment the model will be asked to score a row. In this lab it is the list lag_days: −1 means known the day before T and 9 means written nine days after it. Any column with a positive lag is a leak by definition, whatever its AUC. The audit and the scan catch different cases. A column that is late but only weakly informative slips under any AUC threshold and the register still finds it; a column whose lag was recorded wrongly is found only by the scan. Both belong in the harness, and the checker constructs a case for each.

def column_auc_scan(X, y, names):
    """Score every column alone by how well it ranks the target.

    Returns a list of (name, auc, distance) in column order, where auc = roc_auc(column, y) and
    distance = abs(auc - 0.5): how far from useless, in either direction.
    """
    out = []
    for c, name in enumerate(names):
        a = roc_auc([row[c] for row in X], y)
        out.append((name, a, abs(a - 0.5)))
    return out


def suspects(scan, flag_at=0.30):
    """Names of the columns whose distance is at least flag_at, in column order."""
    return [name for name, _, d in scan if d >= flag_at]


def audit_register(names, lag_days):
    """The feature register's timeline check: names of the columns written AFTER the prediction time
    (lag_days > 0), in column order."""
    return [n for n, lag in zip(names, lag_days) if lag > 0]

Milestone 3: preprocessing inside the fold, and the shuffled-label test

The second family is the sneakiest because there is no bad column to find. The mistake is in the order of operations: a step that learns from data (choosing features, imputing, scaling, encoding, balancing) is a small model with its own parameters, and if it is fitted on all the rows before the split, the validation rows have helped choose the training features. For unsupervised steps like scaling the effect is usually small; for steps that use the labels it can be enormous. The classic demonstration, from section 7.10.2 of Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman's The Elements of Statistical Learning, is the one in this milestone: pick the best features by their correlation with the labels, then cross-validate. Here are 20 fresh datasets of pure noise (100 rows, 300 columns, coin-flip labels, so the honest AUC is 0.5), keeping the 10 best columns:

"""Selection on all rows versus inside the fold: 20 fresh datasets of pure noise (100 rows, 300 columns)."""
import random

wrong, right = [], []
for s in range(20):
    rng = random.Random(1000 + s)
    X = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(300)] for _ in range(100)]
    y = [rng.randrange(2) for _ in range(100)]
    sp = kfold_splits(100, 5, s)
    wrong.append(cv_with_selection(logreg_model, X, y, sp, 10, inside=False))
    right.append(cv_with_selection(logreg_model, X, y, sp, 10, inside=True))
print("labels are coin flips, so the honest AUC is 0.50")
print(f"select on all rows, then 5-fold CV: mean {sum(wrong) / 20:.3f}  (range {min(wrong):.2f} to {max(wrong):.2f})")
print(f"select inside each fold:            mean {sum(right) / 20:.3f}  (range {min(right):.2f} to {max(right):.2f})")

Selecting on all rows first yields a cross-validated AUC of 0.81 on data with nothing to learn, up to 0.88. Selecting inside each fold gives 0.46, which is chance (the small shortfall below 0.5 is a known quirk of cross-validating pure noise; the range, 0.34 to 0.57, is what it is). With 300 candidates some columns correlate with the labels by luck, and picking the best ten using all the labels picks exactly the lucky ones, validation rows included.

You will write both versions. select_top_k ranks columns by the absolute Pearson correlation with the labels. cv_with_selection takes the flag inside: when false it selects once from every row and then cross-validates (the leak), and when true it selects inside each fold from that fold's training rows only (the fix).

The harness cannot assume it knows which step is guilty, though. Real pipelines have many steps and one of them leaks. The detector here is the shuffled-label test: take the team's whole validation procedure, feed it labels that have been randomly permuted, and look at the score. Shuffled labels carry no signal, so an honest procedure scores about 0.5. A procedure that scores well on labels that mean nothing is fitting to the labels of its own validation rows. In the table of the chapter's experiment the leaky pipeline scores 0.836 on shuffled labels and the in-fold one 0.495. The test needs no knowledge of the pipeline's internals, only the ability to call it with different labels, which is why shuffle_test takes the team's validation function cv_fn(X, y, splits) as an argument.

def select_top_k(X, y, k):
    """The k columns whose Pearson correlation with y has the largest absolute value.

    Returns the column indices sorted ascending. Ties in |correlation| go to the lower index; a constant
    column has correlation 0.
    """
    n, p = len(X), len(X[0])
    my = sum(y) / n
    sy = math.sqrt(sum((t - my) ** 2 for t in y))
    scores = []
    for c in range(p):
        col = [r[c] for r in X]
        mc = sum(col) / n
        sc = math.sqrt(sum((v - mc) ** 2 for v in col))
        scores.append(abs(sum((v - mc) * (t - my) for v, t in zip(col, y)) / (sc * sy)) if sc > 0 and sy > 0 else 0.0)
    best = sorted(range(p), key=lambda c: (-scores[c], c))[:k]
    return sorted(best)


def cv_with_selection(fit, X, y, splits, k, inside):
    """Mean validation AUC when the model only sees the k best columns (by select_top_k).

    inside=False: choose the columns ONCE, using every row (the wrong way round), then cross-validate on
    those columns. inside=True: for each fold choose the columns using that fold's training rows only,
    then fit and score on those columns.
    """
    if not inside:
        cols = select_top_k(X, y, k)
        Xs = [[r[c] for c in cols] for r in X]
        return cross_val_auc(fit, Xs, y, splits)
    aucs = []
    for tr, va in splits:
        Xtr, ytr = [X[i] for i in tr], [y[i] for i in tr]
        cols = select_top_k(Xtr, ytr, k)
        predict = fit([[r[c] for c in cols] for r in Xtr], ytr)
        aucs.append(roc_auc(predict([[X[i][c] for c in cols] for i in va]), [y[i] for i in va]))
    return sum(aucs) / len(aucs)


def shuffle_test(cv_fn, X, y, splits, n_shuffles=6, seed=0):
    """Run the team's whole validation, cv_fn(X, y_shuffled, splits), on randomly permuted labels and return
    the mean AUC over n_shuffles shuffles.

    One random.Random(seed) does all the shuffling: for each shuffle copy y and call rng.shuffle on the copy.
    Shuffled labels carry no signal, so an honest procedure scores about 0.5. Do not modify y.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    total = 0.0
    for _ in range(n_shuffles):
        yy = list(y)
        rng.shuffle(yy)
        total += cv_fn(X, yy, splits)
    return total / n_shuffles

Milestone 4: twins across the wall

Train-test contamination needs no cleverness from the model, only a good memory. The same record, or something close to it, sits on both sides of the split, and the model is scored by recall. Duplicates come from data entry, from joins that multiply rows, and, in the case here, from entities that appear many times: a patient with several scans, a customer with several orders. Scans of one patient look alike and share a label, so a random split puts a patient's scans in training and validation both.

The dataset is 150 patients with one to four scans each (379 scans). The label belongs to the patient, the features are a patient-specific fingerprint plus a little noise, and there is nothing to learn that generalises to a new patient. A 1-nearest-neighbour model, which copies the label of the closest training scan, cannot do better than chance on someone it has never seen, but it can look up a twin.

"""Ordinary folds versus patient folds, for a 1-nearest-neighbour model that can only memorise."""

With ordinary folds 86% of validation scans have a sibling in training and the AUC is 0.912. With folds that keep each patient together there are no twins and the AUC falls to 0.491. Both numbers come from the same data and the same model; only the split differs. The deployment will meet new patients, so the second number is the honest one, and the gap between them is the evidence of contamination.

group_kfold builds folds in which no group is divided. The contract is greedy and deterministic: shuffle the distinct groups (in order of first appearance) with random.Random(seed), sort them by size descending with a stable sort, and hand each group to the fold that currently holds the fewest rows. Largest-first keeps the folds balanced to within the size of the largest group. twin_share measures contamination directly: the fraction of validation rows (over all folds) whose group also occurs in the same fold's training rows. contamination_gap combines them for a case, using the case's own model.

The decision rule in the final hunt needs both a large twin share and a real score gap. A dataset in which every row is its own group has a twin share of zero and no gap, as in the loan table. A dataset with repeated entities where the model does not exploit them would have a high share but no gap, and no reason to alarm anyone.

Your task

Write group_kfold(groups, k, seed), twin_share(splits, groups) and contamination_gap(case, k=5, seed=0), which returns (random_auc, grouped_auc, share) where the share is that of the ordinary folds.

The check works a small case by hand (groups a, a, a, b, b, c in two folds: validation sets [0, 1, 2] and [3, 4, 5]) and runs three seeded layouts of 120 groups of 1 to 5 rows, requiring that no group straddles a fold, every row is validated once, fold sizes differ by at most the largest group, and the same seed gives the same folds. twin_share is checked on three hand-built cases (1.0, 0.0 and 1/3). On the twins case the share must exceed 0.6, the ordinary AUC 0.85 and the grouped AUC fall between 0.40 and 0.60; on the clean case the share must be 0 with no gap.

def group_kfold(groups, k, seed):
    """k folds in which no group is ever split between training and validation.

    Take the distinct groups in order of first appearance, shuffle that list with random.Random(seed), sort it by
    group size descending (a stable sort, so ties keep the shuffled order), then give each group in turn to the
    fold that currently holds the fewest rows (the lowest fold number on a tie).
    Returns [(train_idx, val_idx), ...] with both lists sorted ascending.
    """
    order = list(dict.fromkeys(groups))
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(order)
    size = {g: 0 for g in order}
    for g in groups:
        size[g] += 1
    order.sort(key=lambda g: -size[g])
    fold_of, load = {}, [0] * k
    for g in order:
        f = min(range(k), key=lambda i: (load[i], i))
        fold_of[g] = f
        load[f] += size[g]
    out = []
    for f in range(k):
        out.append(([i for i, g in enumerate(groups) if fold_of[g] != f], [i for i, g in enumerate(groups) if fold_of[g] == f]))
    return out


def twin_share(splits, groups):
    """The fraction of validation rows (counted over all folds) whose group also appears in that fold's training rows."""
    twins = total = 0
    for tr, va in splits:
        seen = {groups[i] for i in tr}
        twins += sum(1 for i in va if groups[i] in seen)
        total += len(va)
    return twins / total


def contamination_gap(case, k=5, seed=0):
    """Compare ordinary folds with group folds for the case's own model.

    Returns (random_auc, grouped_auc, share) where random_auc = cross_val_auc on kfold_splits(n, k, seed),
    grouped_auc = cross_val_auc on group_kfold(case.groups, k, seed), and share = twin_share of the ordinary folds.
    """
    n = len(case.y)
    rand = kfold_splits(n, k, seed)
    grp = group_kfold(case.groups, k, seed)
    return (cross_val_auc(case.fit, case.X, case.y, rand), cross_val_auc(case.fit, case.X, case.y, grp),
            twin_share(rand, case.groups))

Milestone 5: the future inside a feature

Temporal leakage hides in feature construction. A rolling average, a cumulative sum or a normalisation by the global maximum can quietly include rows from after the prediction time. The usual culprit is a window centred on the day being predicted: a seven-day mean centred on day t averages days t − 3 to t + 3, so it contains the day itself and three days of its future. The sales case in this lab predicts whether a day's sales will be above the mean of the previous 28 days, from three features: yesterday's value, a trailing 7-day mean (days t − 7 to t − 1) and a centred 7-day mean.

The feature register is no help here. The team logged all three features as known the day before (lag −1), because each was "just a rolling mean". The register reports what people believe, and this belief is wrong. The scan misses it too, since the centred mean on its own is a mild predictor. What catches it is a test of the code, which is the chapter's own advice: change the value of the target at day t and check that the feature for day t does not move.

The perturbation test does exactly that, for every feature function and a spread of days. For each day t, add a large number to every value of the series from day t onwards, recompute the feature, and compare its value at day t with the original. A feature that is legitimately known before day t cannot depend on anything at or after t, so it cannot move. Adding the bump to all later days, not just day t, also catches features that peek only into the future, such as tomorrow's value. It needs no model, no labels and no knowledge of how the feature was written; it only needs the feature as a function.

Time-ordered validation (time_splits, written here too) does not fix the leak, because the feature already contains the future of every training and validation day alike; it only stops other kinds of temporal leakage, such as training on days that come after the validation days.

def time_splits(n, k):
    """Forward-chaining folds for time-ordered rows. With b = n // (k + 1), fold i (i = 1..k) trains on rows
    [0, i*b) and validates on rows [i*b, (i+1)*b); the last fold validates on everything from k*b to n.
    Returns [(train_idx, val_idx), ...]; every validation row is later than every training row."""
    b = n // (k + 1)
    out = []
    for i in range(1, k + 1):
        end = (i + 1) * b if i < k else n
        out.append((list(range(0, i * b)), list(range(i * b, end))))
    return out


def perturbation_test(feature_fn, series, positions, bump=1000.0):
    """Find the days on which a feature peeks at day t or later.

    For each t in positions: copy the series, add `bump` to every value from index t onwards, recompute
    feature_fn on the copy and compare the feature's value AT DAY t with the original. A feature that is
    legitimately known before day t cannot move. Return the sorted list of positions where it moved
    (by more than 1e-9). Never modify `series`.
    """
    base = feature_fn(series)
    moved = []
    for t in positions:
        bumped = [v + bump if i >= t else v for i, v in enumerate(series)]
        if abs(feature_fn(bumped)[t] - base[t]) > 1e-9:
            moved.append(t)
    return sorted(moved)


def temporal_leaks(case, step=37):
    """Names of the case's features (in case.feature_fns order) that fail the perturbation test at the days
    range(10, len(series), step). A case without a series has none."""
    if case.series is None:
        return []
    positions = list(range(10, len(case.series), step))
    return [name for name, fn in case.feature_fns.items() if perturbation_test(fn, case.series, positions)]

Milestone 6: the whole hunt

Now wire the detectors together. hunt(case) runs every detector on a case, whatever its history, and returns a dictionary from leak kind to a short evidence string. The harness does not know which case it is looking at, which is the point: it applies the same four tests everywhere.

The thresholds are judgement calls, and each is set by the spread of the statistic on honest data. A shuffled-label AUC from five-fold validation on a few hundred rows has a standard deviation of a few hundredths, so 0.60 sits several standard deviations above 0.5 and well below the leak's 0.84. The twin thresholds guard against two different false alarms: repeated entities that the model cannot exploit (high share, no gap) and noisy score differences between fold layouts (gap, no share). The scan's 0.30 sits between the strongest honest column in the clean case (distance 0.195) and the weakest leak (0.46).

def hunt(case, seed=0):
    """Run every detector on a case and return {kind: evidence} for each leak found (an empty dict if none).

    kind "target": column_auc_scan flags a column at flag_at 0.30, or audit_register finds a column written after T.
    kind "preprocessing": shuffle_test of case.cv_fn on kfold_splits(n, 5, seed) averages above 0.60.
    kind "contamination": contamination_gap gives share >= 0.05 AND random_auc - grouped_auc >= 0.10.
    kind "temporal": temporal_leaks finds at least one feature (add the forward-chaining AUC to the evidence).
    The evidence is a short string with the numbers that convinced you. Do not modify the case.
    """
    found = {}
    n = len(case.y)
    splits = kfold_splits(n, 5, seed)
    flagged = suspects(column_auc_scan(case.X, case.y, case.names))
    late = audit_register(case.names, case.lag_days)
    if flagged or late:
        found["target"] = f"scan flags {flagged or 'nothing'}; register says written after T: {late or 'nothing'}"
    shuffled = shuffle_test(case.cv_fn, case.X, case.y, splits, 6, seed)
    if shuffled > 0.60:
        found["preprocessing"] = f"the team's validation scores {shuffled:.3f} on shuffled labels (honest: 0.5)"
    rand, grp, share = contamination_gap(case, 5, seed)
    if share >= 0.05 and rand - grp >= 0.10:
        found["contamination"] = f"{share:.0%} of validation rows have a twin in training; AUC {rand:.3f} random folds, {grp:.3f} grouped folds"
    bad = temporal_leaks(case)
    if bad:
        fc = cross_val_auc(case.fit, case.X, case.y, time_splits(n, 4))
        found["temporal"] = f"features {bad} change when day t or later is perturbed (forward-chaining AUC with them: {fc:.3f})"
    return found

Look under the hood

  • The shuffled-label test is a poor man's permutation test. A proper one repeats the shuffle hundreds of times and reports the share of shuffled scores at least as large as the real one (a p-value). Six shuffles is enough to see a gap of 0.3 and not enough to measure small leaks. The cost grows with the number of shuffles times the cost of the whole pipeline, which is why teams run it nightly on a sample.
  • What the harness cannot see. It only knows what the case tells it. A leak through a column whose lag was recorded wrongly and whose AUC is modest, a duplicate with no group id, or a label that was defined from the features will pass. Chapter 14's adversarial validation (train a classifier to tell your training rows from your test rows) is another detector, aimed at distribution shift and contamination you cannot name; it is a natural sixth milestone.
  • Leaks and benchmarks. Contamination is the same bug at larger scale when test questions appear in a model's training text. Contamination, saturation and decay applies the twin test to language-model benchmarks.
  • Where this sits in scikit-learn. The equivalents are below. The lab's fit returning predict corresponds to a Pipeline; the point of a pipeline is that every learned step is refitted inside each fold.
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif

Going further

  • Adversarial validation. Label training rows 0 and test rows 1, train a classifier to tell them apart and report its AUC and its most important column. Add it as a detector for shift and for collection artefacts that the single-column scan misses.
  • Near-duplicate detection. Hash rounded feature vectors, or compare rows by distance, to find twins when no group id exists. Compare the detected twin share with the true one in the twins case.
  • A p-value for the shuffle test. Run 100 shuffles, count how many scores reach the real one, and report a permutation p-value. How many shuffles does it take to separate a mild leak from chance?
  • Target encoding. Chapter 17's target encoding is a preprocessing step that uses the labels. Implement it the wrong way and the right way and apply the shuffled-label test; Lab 6 builds the leak-safe version.
  • Leaks in the loop. Selecting among many models on one validation set and reporting its score is a leak in the evaluation loop. Simulate 50 random models on noise and report the best validation AUC, then re-score it on fresh data.
  • The competition view. Read Leaks That Won Competitions and add a detector for each kind of leak it describes that this harness cannot yet see.

Systems connection

The same bug appears in production as train/serve skew: the training pipeline reads a column as it stands today in the warehouse, while the serving path computes it from what is known now. Feature stores exist largely to give every feature one definition and point-in-time correctness (designing with ML and LLMs). The timeline audit of milestone 2 is lineage: knowing when each column was written needs the table lineage and data-quality checks of data platforms. A leakage harness belongs in the pipeline like a unit test, run on every training job, with a failing check blocking the model from promotion, the same discipline as the canary gate in deployment strategies.

What you learned

  • Every learned step belongs inside fit, so it is refitted on each fold's training rows. Anything fitted on the whole table first is a small model that has seen the validation labels.
  • A column scan (AUC far from 0.5 in either direction) and a feature register (columns written after the prediction time) catch target leakage and collection artefacts. They fail in different ways, so use both, and pick a threshold from the spread of honest columns.
  • The shuffled-label test needs no knowledge of the pipeline: an honest procedure scores about 0.5 on labels that mean nothing, and the preprocessing leak scored 0.84.
  • When entities repeat, an ordinary split puts twins on both sides. Compare ordinary and grouped folds: 86% twins and an AUC of 0.91 against 0.49 is contamination.
  • A feature known before day t cannot move when day t or later is changed. The perturbation test checks that with no model and no labels, and it caught the centred window that the register and the scan missed.
  • A harness is judged on restraint as well as recall: all four leaks found and nothing flagged on the clean case.

Check your understanding

A team's pipeline scores a 5-fold AUC of 0.84 when you run it with the labels randomly permuted. What does that tell you?

  1. The model is very powerful and the validation set is easy
  2. Something in the pipeline fits to the labels of its own validation rows, such as selection done before the split
  3. The shuffle did not work, because a permutation cannot change AUC
  4. The classes are imbalanced, so the AUC is inflated
Permuted labels carry no signal, so an honest procedure scores about 0.5. A high score means information about the labels is reaching the model through something other than the training rows, which is what preprocessing fitted on all rows does. Class imbalance does not move AUC away from 0.5 on random labels, and a more powerful model cannot find a signal that is not there.

The column scan flags export_row_number with an AUC of 0.00 against the label. What is the right response?

  1. Keep it, since an AUC of 0.00 means the column is useless
  2. Keep it but multiply it by −1 to make the AUC 1.00
  3. Standardise it so the model does not over-weight it
  4. Drop it and find out how the export ordered the rows, because the ordering encodes the label
An AUC of 0 is a perfectly inverted ranking, as informative as an AUC of 1: the scan reports the distance from 0.5 for that reason. The column carries the label through how the data was collected, and it will not exist in the same form in production. Reversing or scaling it only makes the shortcut easier to use.

With ordinary folds a memorising model scores 0.91 and with patient folds 0.49. 86% of validation scans have a twin in training. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The model learned to recognise individual patients and has learned nothing that transfers to new patients
  2. The patient folds are too small to give a reliable score
  3. Grouped folds are pessimistic by design, so the truth is about 0.70
  4. The model is overfitted, so a stronger regulariser would raise the grouped score
The model memorises: a twin in training is a lookup. If the deployment meets new patients, the grouped score is the honest one and it is chance. Grouped folds are not pessimistic when the deployment really contains unseen patients, and regularisation cannot add a signal that is absent; the labels are patient-level coin flips.

Which of these features fails the perturbation test (it changes at day t when days t and later are changed)?

  1. The mean of days t − 7 to t − 1
  2. The value on day t − 1
  3. The mean of days t − 2 to t
  4. The running mean of all days up to t − 1
Only the third window includes day t itself, so a bump at day t moves it. The other three end at day t − 1 and cannot change. The third one looks innocent because it is a trailing window, but it ends one day too late, which is the usual form of the bug.

Why can the feature register audit not replace the perturbation test for temporal leakage?

  1. The register is slower to run on large tables
  2. The register records what people believe about when a feature is written, and a feature built with the wrong window will be logged as fine
  3. The perturbation test also detects target leakage in columns
  4. The register only works for categorical features
The register is a claim, and a centred rolling mean logged as "known yesterday" is a wrong claim. The perturbation test checks the code that produced the feature and so does not rely on the claim. Speed is not the issue, and the register works for any column.

On 20 datasets of pure noise, selecting features inside each fold gives a mean AUC of 0.46, with a range of 0.34 to 0.57. How should you read that?

  1. The model has learned a real but inverted signal
  2. The pipeline has a leak that lowers the score
  3. The selection step is broken and should be removed
  4. Consistent with chance: 100-row datasets are noisy, and nowhere near the 0.81 of the leaky version
With 100 rows each fold has 20 validation rows, so single scores scatter widely around 0.5, and cross-validating pure noise is known to come out a little under 0.5. The relevant comparison is with 0.81 from selecting on all rows. A leak raises scores, it does not lower them, and nothing here is evidence of an inverted signal.
