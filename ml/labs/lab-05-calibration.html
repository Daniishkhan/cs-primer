Lab 5: Thresholds & Calibration

Chapter 15 gave a screening model two separate jobs: put the right cases first, and say how likely each one is. The first is easy to measure and the second is easy to get wrong, and the cost-based threshold only works when both are right. In this lab you build the instruments (a precision-recall curve, a reliability table, proper scores), the three ways of choosing a threshold (from costs, from a recall promise, from a review capacity), the two standard repairs (Platt scaling and isotonic regression) and the odds corrections for undersampling and label shift. Then you run one fraud-desk decision end to end on scores that come out of a rebalanced, overfitted model. A checker compares every function with hand-worked values, independent oracles and the generator's hidden true probabilities, and says which mistake you probably made.

Why this lab matters

The threshold rule of chapter 2 is one line of algebra: flag a case when its probability exceeds cFP / (cFP + cFN). The trouble is hidden in the word probability. A boosted model, an overfitted regression and a model trained on a rebalanced sample all produce numbers that rank cases well and mean nothing, and nothing in the AUC will tell you. Chapter 15 argued this with a table; here you feel it. You will see two sets of scores with the same ranking, the same AUC and the same average precision, whose decisions differ by 30% in cost, and you will build the tools that find the difference and close it.

The same skills turn up wherever a score becomes an action: credit limits and loan approvals, fraud queues, triage in a clinic, content moderation, spam, and the confidence a retrieval or agent system attaches to its own answers (grounding and abstention in the evaluation course). The data here has a property no real data has, and it is what makes the checker exact: every case carries a hidden true probability, so the expected cost of a decision can be computed without sampling noise. On real data you only have labels, which is why chapter 15 insists on many validation positives (try the first experiment near the end of this page).

Pure Python, seeded, about two seconds

Python 3.9 or newer and the standard library only: no NumPy, no scikit-learn. Every dataset comes from a seeded random.Random, so the numbers on this page are the numbers you will see (see reproducible experiments). The largest dataset has 40,000 cases, which is small enough for lists and large enough that a 3% event gives a thousand positives. A failed property check names its seed, and python3 check.py solution 4 2 replays milestone 4 on seed 2 alone. Your scores are compared with the true probabilities that the generator keeps secret, so a "close enough" in this lab means close to the best decision that exists, not close to a noisy sample.

What you'll build

Six milestones, each adding a group of functions and a group of checks:

  1. The precision-recall curve: one point per distinct score (tied cases enter together), and average precision as the area under the step curve.
  2. Thresholds: the cost rule, the average cost of any threshold, the best threshold by a single sweep, and the thresholds that deliver a recall promise or fill a review capacity.
  3. Reliability: equal-width bins, the Brier score, log loss and expected calibration error, and what each does and does not see.
  4. Platt scaling: a two-parameter logistic fit on the logit of the score, by Newton's method, checked against the two equations that define the optimum.
  5. Isotonic regression: the best non-decreasing step function by pool-adjacent-violators, checked against the min-max formula, and a comparison of the two repairs as the calibration set grows.
  6. Odds corrections and the whole decision: undo undersampling, shift a prior, then recalibrate on held-out cases and apply the cost rule, scored against the truth.

The data: a screening problem with a known truth

make_cases(n, seed, stretch, shift) draws n cases. Each has a hidden log-odds z = −4.5 + 1.6 × N(0, 1), so its true probability of being positive is p = σ(z) and its label is drawn with exactly that probability. About 3% of cases are positive. The model under test reports s = σ(stretch × z + shift). With stretch = 1, shift = 0 the scores are the true probabilities (an honest model). Any positive stretch keeps the ranking of p, so the AUC and the average precision do not move; only the meaning of the numbers does.

SettingWhat the model's scores do
stretch = 1, shift = 0Honest: s equals the true probability.
stretch > 1Exaggerated log-odds, as in an overfitted model. Because the log-odds of a rare event are mostly very negative, doubling them pushes most scores down, towards 0.
stretch < 1Shrunk log-odds: an underconfident model.
shift ≠ 0A bias: every log-odds is moved by the same amount.

The costs are constants in the file: C_FP = 5 (an analyst spends ten minutes on a harmless case) and C_FN = 100 (the loss a miss lets through), the pair that gave a threshold of 0.0476 in chapters 2 and 15. Every function takes the costs as arguments, so you can change them.

Milestone 1: the precision-recall curve

A classifier's score becomes a decision through a threshold t: flag every case with score ≥ t. Each threshold gives two numbers (chapter 15). Precision is the share of flagged cases that are positive, and recall is the share of all positives that were flagged. Lowering the threshold flags more cases: recall can only rise, precision usually falls. The precision-recall curve is that trade-off drawn for every threshold at once, and for a rare positive class it is the picture that matters, because the mountain of easy negatives that flatters accuracy and ROC curves does not enter precision.

Take four cases with scores 0.9, 0.8, 0.7 and 0.6 and labels 1, 0, 1, 0. Walking the threshold down gives one point per distinct score:

ThresholdFlaggedTrue positivesPrecisionRecall
0.9111.0000.5
0.8210.5000.5
0.7320.6671.0
0.6420.5001.0

Average precision (AP) summarises the curve as one number. Read it as precision, averaged over the positives as each is found: every time recall rises from Rk−1 to Rk you collect the precision at that point, weighted by the size of the step.

AP = ∑k (Rk − Rk−1) × Pk, with R0 = 0

For the table: 0.5 × 1.000 + 0 × 0.5 + 0.5 × 0.667 + 0 × 0.5 = 0.833. A perfect ranking scores 1.0, and random scores give about the base rate, because every threshold then has precision close to the share of positives. Note what this definition does not do: it does not draw straight lines between points. scikit-learn's average_precision_score uses the same step definition, and its documentation warns that the trapezoid rule on the curve uses linear interpolation and can be too optimistic.

The one subtlety is ties. A threshold is a statement about scores, so cases with equal scores are flagged together or not at all. If three cases share the score 0.5, there is no threshold that flags one of them. A loop that adds one case at a time and records a point after each manufactures operating points that cannot exist, and the check has a tie example to catch it: scores 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.9 with labels 1, 0, 0, 1 have two points, (precision 1.0, recall 0.5) and (precision 0.5, recall 1.0), and AP = 0.75.

def precision_recall_points(scores, y):
    """One (threshold, precision, recall) per DISTINCT score, from the highest threshold down.

    The point for threshold t describes the rule "flag every case with score >= t": precision is the share
    of flagged cases that are positive, recall the share of positives that are flagged. Cases with equal
    scores enter together, since no threshold can separate them. Raises ValueError if y has no positives.

        precision_recall_points([0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6], [1, 0, 1, 0])
        -> [(0.9, 1.0, 0.5), (0.8, 0.5, 0.5), (0.7, 0.667, 1.0), (0.6, 0.5, 1.0)]   (rounded)
    """
    n_pos = sum(y)
    if n_pos == 0:
        raise ValueError("precision_recall_points needs at least one positive in y")
    order = sorted(range(len(scores)), key=lambda i: -scores[i])
    points = []
    tp = flagged = 0
    i = 0
    while i < len(order):
        t = scores[order[i]]
        while i < len(order) and scores[order[i]] == t:    # take the whole tie group
            tp += y[order[i]]
            flagged += 1
            i += 1
        points.append((t, tp / flagged, tp / n_pos))
    return points


def average_precision(scores, y):
    """Average precision: the sum over curve points of (recall step) x (precision at that point).

        AP = sum_k (R_k - R_{k-1}) * P_k,   with R_0 = 0

    A step function under the precision-recall curve, with no interpolation between points. A perfect
    ranking scores 1.0; a random one scores about the base rate of positives.
    """
    ap, prev_recall = 0.0, 0.0
    for _, precision, recall in precision_recall_points(scores, y):
        ap += (recall - prev_recall) * precision
        prev_recall = recall
    return ap

Milestone 2: thresholds from costs, recall and capacity

A point on the curve is only useful once you choose one. Chapter 15 gives three routes, and they are the same decision seen from different sides.

From costs. Suppose the model's number p really is the probability that a case is positive. Flagging it costs (1 − p) × cFP in expectation (it is harmless with probability 1 − p, and then you wasted a review). Leaving it alone costs p × cFN. Flag when the first is smaller:

(1 − p) cFP < p cFN  ⇔  p > cFP / (cFP + cFN)

With costs of 5 and 100 that is 5/105 = 0.0476: review anything with more than a 4.8% chance of being a positive. Note how far this is from 0.5. A miss that costs twenty times a false alarm justifies flagging cases that are 95% likely to be harmless.

From a recall promise. "We catch at least 80% of fraud" fixes recall and lets precision fall where it must: choose the highest threshold whose recall reaches the target. From a capacity. "Two analysts can review 400 cases a day" fixes the number flagged: the threshold is the 400th-largest score. If several cases tie at that score a capacity cannot split them, so more than 400 are flagged.

To compare thresholds on data you need the average cost of a rule, which is a sum over cases: a false alarm costs cFP, a miss cFN, a correct decision nothing, divided by the number of cases. And to find the best threshold on a set of labelled cases you can use the same sweep as milestone 1. As the threshold drops past each distinct score, one group of cases changes from "not flagged" to "flagged", so the running counts of true and false positives give the cost of every candidate in one pass. Here is the hand example the checker uses, with cFP = 5 and cFN = 10 on scores 0.9, 0.2, 0.6, 0.1 and labels 1, 0, 0, 1:

For a model that stretches the log-odds the same ranking has its best threshold at a completely different place, so the formula threshold lands on the steep part of the curve. The formula was never wrong; the numbers it was fed were.

def cost_threshold(c_fp, c_fn):
    """The probability above which flagging is cheaper than not flagging: c_fp / (c_fp + c_fn).

    Flagging a case with true probability p costs (1 - p) * c_fp in expectation; leaving it costs p * c_fn.
    Flag when the first is smaller, which is when p > c_fp / (c_fp + c_fn).
    """
    return c_fp / (c_fp + c_fn)


def expected_cost(scores, y, threshold, c_fp, c_fn):
    """Average cost per case of the rule "flag when score >= threshold"."""
    cost = 0.0
    for s, t in zip(scores, y):
        if s >= threshold:
            if t == 0:
                cost += c_fp
        elif t == 1:
            cost += c_fn
    return cost / len(scores)


def best_threshold(scores, y, c_fp, c_fn):
    """The threshold with the lowest average cost on these cases, and that cost: (threshold, cost).

    Candidates are every distinct score, plus float("inf") for "flag nothing". Among equal costs return the
    HIGHEST threshold (fewest flags). Must run in O(n log n): sort once and sweep, do not call expected_cost
    for every candidate.
    """
    n = len(scores)
    n_pos = sum(y)
    best_t, best_cost = float("inf"), n_pos * c_fn / n
    order = sorted(range(n), key=lambda i: -scores[i])
    tp = fp = 0
    i = 0
    while i < n:
        t = scores[order[i]]
        while i < n and scores[order[i]] == t:
            if y[order[i]] == 1:
                tp += 1
            else:
                fp += 1
            i += 1
        cost = (fp * c_fp + (n_pos - tp) * c_fn) / n
        if cost < best_cost:               # strict: an equal cost at a lower threshold flags more, so keep the higher
            best_t, best_cost = t, cost
    return best_t, best_cost


def threshold_for_recall(scores, y, target):
    """The HIGHEST threshold whose recall is at least `target` (a promise like "catch 90% of fraud").

    Raises ValueError if target is not in (0, 1].
    """
    if not 0.0 < target <= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("target recall must be in (0, 1]")
    for t, _, recall in precision_recall_points(scores, y):
        if recall >= target - 1e-12:
            return t
    raise ValueError("unreachable: the lowest threshold has recall 1")


def threshold_for_capacity(scores, k):
    """The threshold that flags the k highest-scoring cases: the k-th largest score.

    If cases tie at that score, all of them are flagged, so more than k may be (a capacity cannot split a tie).
    """
    if not 1 <= k <= len(scores):
        raise ValueError("k must be between 1 and the number of cases")
    return sorted(scores, reverse=True)[k - 1]

Milestone 3: proper scores and the reliability diagram

A model is calibrated if, among the cases it gives probability p, a fraction p turn out positive. It is a property of the numbers, separate from ranking: any monotone distortion of the scores keeps the AUC and the curve of milestone 1 exactly as they were, and breaks calibration. Three tools measure it.

The reliability diagram (or calibration curve) sorts the cases into bins of predicted probability and, for each bin, compares the mean prediction with the observed fraction of positives. Points on the diagonal are calibrated. reliability_table returns the numbers behind it. With equal-width bins, case i goes into bin min(int(p * n_bins), n_bins - 1); the min is there so that a probability of exactly 1.0 joins the last bin instead of creating an eleventh.

The Brier score is the mean squared difference between the probability and the 0/1 outcome, and log loss (the cross-entropy of maximum likelihood) is the mean of −ln of the probability given to what happened:

Brier = (1/n) ∑ (pi − yi)2     log loss = −(1/n) ∑ [yi ln pi + (1 − yi) ln(1 − pi)]

Both are proper: their expected value is lowest exactly when the model reports the true probabilities, so improving either without changing the ranking means improving calibration. Log loss punishes a confident miss ferociously: a case that is positive but scored 0 would cost infinity, so the lab clips probabilities into [10−12, 1 − 10−12] and a certain-and-wrong forecast costs about 27.6. A forecast of the base rate b for every case has Brier b(1 − b), a useful yardstick.

Expected calibration error (ECE) averages the bin gaps, weighted by how many cases each bin holds:

ECE = ∑bins (nb / n) × | mean predictedb − observed rateb |

The weighting is the part people forget: an unweighted mean of gaps lets a bin with two cases count as much as a bin with twenty thousand, and the checker's hand table catches it. ECE has two weaknesses that the data of this lab shows clearly. A forecast of the base rate for everyone has an ECE of zero and no skill at all, so report it next to a ranking metric and a proper score, never alone. And with a rare class the choice of bins matters enormously. Here 97% of the stretched model's scores are below 0.05, so ten equal-width bins put nearly every case in the first one:

That is why the figure uses quantile bins on a log scale, and why reliability_table takes n_bins as an argument. The second experiment near the end of this page shows ECE changing with the number of bins; the "going further" list suggests equal-count bins.

def brier_score(p, y):
    """Mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the 0/1 outcome (lower is better)."""
    return sum((a - t) ** 2 for a, t in zip(p, y)) / len(p)


def log_loss(p, y, eps=1e-12):
    """Mean of -log(probability given to what happened). Probabilities are clipped into [eps, 1 - eps]."""
    total = 0.0
    for a, t in zip(p, y):
        a = min(max(a, eps), 1.0 - eps)
        total -= math.log(a) if t == 1 else math.log(1.0 - a)
    return total / len(p)


def reliability_table(p, y, n_bins=10):
    """The numbers behind a reliability diagram, using equal-width bins of predicted probability.

    Case i goes into bin min(int(p[i] * n_bins), n_bins - 1) (so p = 1.0 joins the last bin). Returns a list
    of (bin_index, count, mean_predicted, observed_rate) for the NON-EMPTY bins, in bin order.
    """
    count = [0] * n_bins
    sum_p = [0.0] * n_bins
    sum_y = [0] * n_bins
    for a, t in zip(p, y):
        b = min(int(a * n_bins), n_bins - 1)
        count[b] += 1
        sum_p[b] += a
        sum_y[b] += t
    return [(b, count[b], sum_p[b] / count[b], sum_y[b] / count[b]) for b in range(n_bins) if count[b]]


def ece(p, y, n_bins=10):
    """Expected calibration error: the bin-size-weighted average of |mean predicted - observed rate|."""
    n = len(p)
    return sum(c / n * abs(mp - rate) for _, c, mp, rate in reliability_table(p, y, n_bins))

Milestone 4: Platt scaling

A model that is badly calibrated but ranks well is cheap to repair, if you have a small set of cases it has not seen. Platt scaling (Platt, 1999, "Probabilistic outputs for support vector machines and comparisons to regularized likelihood methods") fits a logistic regression with one input, the model's own score on the logit scale, and two parameters:

P(y = 1 | s) = σ(a · logit(s) + b),   logit(s) = ln(s / (1 − s))

The slope a stretches or shrinks the log-odds and the intercept b moves them. These are exactly the two distortions the generator applies (stretch and shift), which is why Platt scaling can fix them perfectly here: if the model reports σ(2z + 0.5), the honest map is a = 1/2 and b = −0.25. On real models the sigmoid shape is an assumption, and the chapter lists where it fails. With a > 0 the map is strictly increasing, so the ranking is untouched: the AUC after Platt scaling is the AUC before it. (A negative a would mean the model is anti-informative.)

The fit is ordinary maximum likelihood, and with two parameters Newton's method is exact enough and short. With z = logit(s) and p = σ(az + b) for each case, the gradient of the log loss (summed over cases) is

g = ( ∑ (p − y) z , ∑ (p − y) )    and the Hessian is   H = [ ∑ w z2 , ∑ w z ; ∑ w z , ∑ w ] with w = p(1 − p)

Each iteration solves a 2 × 2 system, (a, b) ← (a, b) − H−1g, and converges in about seven steps. The optimum satisfies two equations that the checker uses as an oracle: the average residual (p − y) is zero, and so is the average of (p − y) × z. In words: the calibrated probabilities add up to the number of positives, and the residuals are uncorrelated with the score.

Where to start Newton's method

The obvious start is the identity map (a = 1, b = 0). On rare-event data it is a trap: if the scores are tiny and the base rate is 3%, every p is near 0, every weight w is nearly zero, the Hessian is nearly singular, and the first step is enormous. The reference solution starts from the best constant model instead (a = 0, b = logit of the base rate), and halves a step that does not lower the log loss. Try the other start, without the safeguard, and watch the slope reach 1010.

One more detail from the hand case. If the data has two score levels, 0.2 (positives 1 in 4) and 0.8 (3 in 4), the two-parameter fit can reproduce both observed rates exactly, so the slope is determined by two logits: a = ln 3 / ln 4 = 0.7925 and b = 0. The checker uses it to test the fit to six decimals before it moves on to random data.

def fit_platt(scores, y, iters=100):
    """Fit P(y = 1 | score) = sigmoid(a * logit(score) + b) by maximum likelihood. Returns (a, b).

    Newton's method (iteratively reweighted least squares) on the two parameters. With z = logit(score) and
    p = sigmoid(a z + b), the gradient is (sum (p - y) z, sum (p - y)) and the Hessian is
    [[sum w z^2, sum w z], [sum w z, sum w]] with w = p (1 - p). Start from a = 0 and b = logit(base rate),
    the best constant model: a start at (1, 0) can sit where every w is tiny and the first step is enormous.
    Halve a step that does not lower the log loss. Stop when the step is below 1e-10.
    """
    z = [logit(s) for s in scores]
    n = len(z)

    def loss(a, b):
        return sum(math.log1p(math.exp(-abs(a * zi + b))) + max(a * zi + b, 0.0) - t * (a * zi + b)
                   for zi, t in zip(z, y)) / n

    a, b = 0.0, logit(sum(y) / n)
    for _ in range(iters):
        ga = gb = haa = hab = hbb = 0.0
        for zi, t in zip(z, y):
            p = sigmoid(a * zi + b)
            e = p - t
            w = max(p * (1.0 - p), 1e-12)
            ga += e * zi
            gb += e
            haa += w * zi * zi
            hab += w * zi
            hbb += w
        det = haa * hbb - hab * hab
        da = (hbb * ga - hab * gb) / det
        db = (haa * gb - hab * ga) / det
        step, base = 1.0, loss(a, b)
        while step > 1e-6 and loss(a - step * da, b - step * db) > base + 1e-15:
            step /= 2.0
        a, b = a - step * da, b - step * db
        if abs(step * da) < 1e-10 and abs(step * db) < 1e-10:
            break
    return a, b


def apply_platt(params, scores):
    """Calibrated probabilities sigmoid(a * logit(score) + b) for each score."""
    a, b = params
    return [sigmoid(a * logit(s) + b) for s in scores]

Milestone 5: isotonic regression

Platt scaling assumes the distortion is a sigmoid. When it is not (a model that is right at the top and wrong at the bottom, say), you want a map that assumes nothing except that a higher score should never mean a lower probability. Isotonic regression finds the best non-decreasing function through the (score, label) pairs, best in the sense of least squared error. The solution is a step function, and the classic way to find it is the pool-adjacent-violators (PAV) algorithm.

Sort the cases by score. Push them onto a stack one group at a time, each as its own block whose value is its mean label. Whenever the top block's value is not above the block beneath it, those two blocks violate the ordering, so merge them into one block holding their combined mean. Repeat until the stack is increasing. Merging can create a new violation one level down, which is why it is a loop. Step through the example the checker uses: labels 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0 at scores 1 to 6.

Step through

Pool adjacent violators on six points

  1. Push the first point. Scores sorted, labels 0 1 0 1 1 0. The first block is the point at score 1, mean 0.
  2. Push the second. Mean 1 is above 0: the stack is increasing, nothing to do.
  3. Push the third: a violation. Its label is 0, so the new block has mean 0, below the block under it (mean 1). A non-decreasing function cannot go down.
  4. Pool the violators. Merge the two blocks into one with mean (1 + 0) / 2 = 0.5, covering scores 2 and 3. The stack is increasing again.
  5. Push the fourth. Mean 1 above 0.5: fine.
  6. Push the fifth: a tie of means. Mean 1 against 1. The lab pools blocks whose means are not strictly increasing (previous mean >= top mean), so this counts as a violation.
  7. Pool them. One block with mean 1 covering scores 4 and 5.
  8. Push the sixth: a violation. Label 0, mean 0, below the block under it (mean 1).
  9. Pool, and the stack settles. The merged block covers scores 4 to 6 with mean 2/3. The block under it has mean 0.5, which is lower, so the merging stops. The answer: 0, 0.5, 0.667 with edges at scores 1, 3 and 6.

Two details are decisions the lab makes for you, and the checker tests both. First, equal scores are grouped before anything else: no function of the score can give two cases with the same score different values, so tied cases form one point carrying the sum of their labels and their count. Second, equal means are pooled (the test is "previous mean ≥ top mean"). That makes both the block edges and the block values strictly increasing, which gives every input exactly one answer; with labels 0, 0, 1, 1 you get two blocks, not four.

The fitted model is two lists: xs, the largest score in each block, and vals, the block means. To predict a new score, find the first block whose upper edge is at least the score (bisect_left on xs), and use the last block for scores above everything seen. Between two edges the value is therefore that of the block on the right; scikit-learn's IsotonicRegression interpolates linearly between blocks instead, which is a different (also reasonable) convention.

PAV has a closed form that makes a good independent oracle. For labels y1, ..., yn sorted by score, the fitted value at position i is

maxj ≤ i   mink ≥ i   mean(yj, ..., yk)

The checker computes that O(n3) formula on forty random small lists and compares. It also checks the properties that make isotonic regression a calibrator: block values lie in [0, 1], the fitted values add up to the number of positives (pooling preserves totals), and no non-decreasing function does better on squared error, so the fit must beat Platt scaling on the very data it was fitted to.

The last check is the one that explains why Platt scaling exists. Isotonic regression is far more flexible, and flexibility needs data. The checker fits both on 400, 1,500 and 20,000 calibration cases from a model with stretched log-odds, and scores them on 30,000 new cases. Here is the real output from the reference solution:

def fit_isotonic(scores, y):
    """The best non-decreasing step function through the (score, label) pairs, by least squares.

    Returns (xs, vals): block i covers the scores greater than xs[i-1] and up to xs[i], and its value is
    vals[i], the mean label in the block. xs is strictly increasing and vals strictly increasing.

    Pool adjacent violators: sort by score, put equal scores together first (no function can separate
    them), then push groups one at a time onto a stack of blocks; while the top block's mean is not above the
    one beneath it, merge the two.
    """
    pairs = sorted(zip(scores, y))
    groups = []                                     # [x, sum of labels, count], one per distinct score
    for s, t in pairs:
        if groups and groups[-1][0] == s:
            groups[-1][1] += t
            groups[-1][2] += 1
        else:
            groups.append([s, t, 1])
    blocks = []                                     # [x_max, sum, count]
    for g in groups:
        blocks.append(g)
        while len(blocks) > 1 and blocks[-2][1] / blocks[-2][2] >= blocks[-1][1] / blocks[-1][2]:
            top = blocks.pop()
            blocks[-1] = [top[0], blocks[-1][1] + top[1], blocks[-1][2] + top[2]]
    return [b[0] for b in blocks], [b[1] / b[2] for b in blocks]


def apply_isotonic(model, scores):
    """Value of the block containing each score: the first block whose upper edge xs[i] is >= the score.

    Scores above every edge get the last block's value.
    """
    xs, vals = model
    return [vals[min(bisect_left(xs, s), len(xs) - 1)] for s in scores]

Milestone 6: odds corrections and the whole decision

Two more repairs are one line of algebra each, and both come from the same observation: probabilities change in a predictable way when the class balance of the data changes, because odds scale. A positive-to-negative odds ratio of 1 : 4 is the same as p = 0.2; if positives become twice as common relative to negatives, the odds become 2 : 4 and p = 1/3.

Undersampling. To train faster, a team keeps all the positives and only a fraction β of the negatives. In the sample, the odds of a positive are 1/β times the real odds, so the model's probability ps is inflated. Solving for the real probability (Dal Pozzolo and colleagues, 2015, "Calibrating Probability with Undersampling for Unbalanced Classification"):

ps / (1 − ps) = [p / (1 − p)] / β  ⇒  p = β ps / (β ps − ps + 1)

Check it on a number: β = 0.1 and a sampled-world probability of 0.5 gives 0.05 / 0.55 = 0.0909. Label shift. If the model was trained when 1% of cases were positive and the rate is now 3%, the way positives look has not changed, but there are more of them; multiply the odds by the ratio of new to old prior odds (Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker, 2002). A model score of 0.5 (odds 1) becomes odds (0.03/0.97) / (0.01/0.99) = 3.06, a probability of 0.754. The two are the same idea. Undersampling is a prior shift from the sampled rate to the true rate, and the checker verifies that your two functions agree.

Corrections like these fix one known cause. They cannot fix the others: an overfitted model is also stretched, and no formula that knows only β sees that. Hence the whole pipeline. calibrated_flags is what a team would run in production: recalibrate on held-out cases, apply the cost rule, return flags. The scenario in the checker is deliberately realistic. A model with stretched log-odds is trained on a sample with 2% of the negatives kept, so its scores are inflated and stretched. def correct_undersampling(ps, beta):
    """Undo training on a sample that kept a fraction beta of the negatives (and all positives).

    p = beta * ps / (beta * ps - ps + 1)    (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2015; chapter 15)
    """
    return beta * ps / (beta * ps - ps + 1.0)


def shift_prior(p, train_rate, new_rate):
    """Rescale a probability when the positive rate moves from train_rate to new_rate (label shift).

    Multiply the odds p / (1 - p) by [new_rate / (1 - new_rate)] / [train_rate / (1 - train_rate)].
    """
    odds = p / (1.0 - p)
    odds *= (new_rate / (1.0 - new_rate)) / (train_rate / (1.0 - train_rate))
    return odds / (1.0 + odds)


def calibrated_flags(cal_scores, cal_y, test_scores, c_fp, c_fn, method):
    """The whole pipeline: recalibrate on held-out data, then apply the cost rule. Returns 0/1 flags.

    method is "none" (use test_scores as they are), "platt" or "isotonic": fit on (cal_scores, cal_y), apply to
    test_scores. Flag a test case when its probability is at least cost_threshold(c_fp, c_fn).
    """
    if method == "none":
        probs = list(test_scores)
    elif method == "platt":
        probs = apply_platt(fit_platt(cal_scores, cal_y), test_scores)
    elif method == "isotonic":
        probs = apply_isotonic(fit_isotonic(cal_scores, cal_y), test_scores)
    else:
        raise ValueError(f"unknown method {method!r}")
    t = cost_threshold(c_fp, c_fn)
    return [1 if p >= t else 0 for p in probs]

Systems connection

A threshold is a dial between two queues, and the rest of the system decides how much each costs. A review team has a throughput (capacity is the same decision as a threshold, milestone 2), and a flood of alerts is an operations problem before it is a modelling one: alerting and on-call makes the same point about pagers. In designing with ML the score is a service output with a contract; if that contract says "probability", someone downstream will multiply it by a cost, and the calibrated map of this lab is a piece of the model that must ship, version and be monitored with it. And the fraud design in Studio 2 asks you to size the review queue; the numbers in this lab are what you would use to defend the answer.

What you learned

  • A precision-recall point belongs to a threshold, and cases with equal scores enter together. Average precision is the step-wise sum of recall increments times precision, and random scores give about the base rate.
  • The cost rule p > cFP / (cFP + cFN) is optimal for honest probabilities and expensive for anything else. A recall promise and a review capacity are the same decision from other directions, and a recall promise is only as sharp as the number of validation positives.
  • Brier score and log loss are proper scores; ECE is the weighted bin gap and has a floor that depends on bins. Report calibration next to ranking, never alone, and with rare positives look at quantile bins on a log scale.
  • Platt scaling is a two-parameter logistic fit on the logit, found by Newton's method from the best constant model; the optimum is the point where residuals sum to zero and are uncorrelated with the score. It never changes the ranking.
  • Isotonic regression is the least-squares non-decreasing step function (pool adjacent violators). It fixes any monotone distortion and needs thousands of cases; with few it overfits and Platt wins.
  • Odds scale under undersampling and label shift, so both are fixed by a one-line formula. Anything unknown (overfitting, drift) needs a recalibrator fitted on held-out cases. Calibrate on rows the model did not train on.

A false alarm costs 2 and a miss costs 48. With honest probabilities, you should flag a case when its probability is at least

  1. 2 / 48 = 0.0417, the ratio of the two costs
  2. 0.5, because probabilities above one half are positives
  3. 2 / (2 + 48) = 0.04
  4. 48 / (2 + 48) = 0.96, because misses are the costly mistake
Flagging costs (1 − p) × 2 in expectation and leaving the case alone costs p × 48. They are equal when 2 − 2p = 48p, so p = 2/50 = 0.04. The ratio of the costs, 0.0417, is the odds-style slip (it would be right for the odds p/(1 − p), not for p). 0.5 is the answer only when the two costs are equal, and 0.96 reverses the roles of the costs.

Models A and B rank every case identically, so their AUC and average precision are the same. A's probabilities are honest; B's log-odds are doubled. Both flag cases above the formula threshold 0.0476. What happens?

  1. B has a higher cost per case, because its numbers no longer mean probabilities and the same threshold selects different cases
  2. They flag exactly the same cases, so the costs are equal, because the ranking is the same
  3. B has a lower cost per case, because its predictions are more confident
  4. The costs are equal but B has lower precision
The same ranking means the same cases are flagged only if the threshold is applied at the same position in the ranking. A fixed threshold on the score is applied at different positions when the scores are distorted. In the lab's scenario the stretched model costs 2.25 per case at the formula threshold against 1.72 for the honest one. Being more confident does not make a model more right; that is what calibration measures.

A system predicts the base rate (3%) for every case. Which statement is true?

  1. Its ECE is large, because most cases are negative and it never predicts anything high
  2. Its Brier score is 0, because it never contradicts the base rate
  3. Its average precision is 1.0, because all scores are tied
  4. Its ECE is about 0 and its Brier score is b(1 − b) = 0.029, so it is calibrated and has no skill
The only bin holds every case, with mean prediction 0.03 and observed rate 0.03, so ECE is zero. The Brier score is b(1 − b), about 0.029. With every score tied there is one precision-recall point, whose precision is the base rate, so AP is 0.03, not 1. This is why ECE must be reported alongside a ranking metric.

A model trained on a sample that kept 5% of the negatives (β = 0.05) outputs 0.5 for a case. What is the corrected probability?

  1. 0.5, because a probability is a probability
  2. About 0.048
  3. 0.025, which is 0.05 × 0.5
  4. 0.95, because the negatives were thinned out
p = β ps / (β ps − ps + 1) = 0.025 / (0.025 − 0.5 + 1) = 0.025 / 0.525 = 0.0476. Equivalently the odds of 1 are divided by 1/β = 20. The product 0.025 forgets the denominator; 0.5 ignores the correction; 0.95 goes the wrong way.

You have 300 held-out cases, only 9 of them positive, to repair a model's probabilities. Which repair, and why?

  1. Isotonic regression, because it makes no assumption about the shape of the distortion
  2. Neither: with 9 positives no calibration is possible
  3. Platt scaling, because two parameters can be estimated from 9 positives and a step function cannot
  4. Retrain the model on the 300 cases
Isotonic regression would build a handful of blocks out of 9 positives, with the lowest block likely to hold a value of exactly 0 (the lab's 400-case row shows the penalty in log loss). Two parameters are a modest demand even on 9 positives, though the estimates will be noisy and worth bootstrapping. Retraining on the calibration cases would throw away the model's training data and leave nothing unseen to calibrate on.

Pool adjacent violators runs on scores 1, 2, 3 with labels 1, 0, 0. What is the fitted function?

  1. 1, 0, 0, which is already the best fit to the labels
  2. 0.5, 0.5, 0, after merging the first two cases
  3. 1, 0.5, 0.5, after merging the last two cases
  4. 1/3 for all three cases, one block
Pushing the second case (mean 0) beneath a block of mean 1 is a violation, so they merge to 0.5. The third case (mean 0) is now below the block of mean 0.5, another violation, so they merge to (1 + 0 + 0)/3. The labels fall as the score rises, which no non-decreasing function can follow; the best it can do is the constant, the mean. Stopping after the first merge leaves the stack still decreasing.
