A Recruiting Model That Learned Bias

According to a 2018 Reuters report, Amazon built a resume-screening model on ten years of past applications, found it penalised women, edited out the offending terms, and then disbanded the project because the fix was not trusted. The case asks why removing words from a model does not remove the bias in its labels.

What I could and could not read

The only account of this system is journalism. I read Jeffrey Dastin's Reuters report, "Amazon scraps secret AI recruiting tool that showed bias against women" (10 October 2018), in full, through an Internet Archive copy captured in January 2019 (Reuters' own page required a login). It rests on "five people familiar with the effort" who spoke on condition of anonymity. Amazon has published no technical description of the system that I could find, and I could not inspect its data or models. I also read parts of Upturn's report Help Wanted: An Examination of Hiring Algorithms, Equity, and Bias (Bogen and Rieke, December 2018), which cites the Reuters story, and New York City's page on Local Law 144. The EU AI Act timing mentioned in the dated box comes from search-result summaries only; I did not read the legal texts. Reuters' account says what the models penalised but not exactly what outcome they were trained to predict. Where I discuss mechanism, I say which parts are my reasoning and which are Reuters'.

The pattern in one sentence

A model trained to reproduce past human decisions learns the pattern of those decisions, including their bias, and deleting the words that expose the pattern does not delete the pattern.

Context

In 2014, Reuters reports, a team at Amazon began building programs to review job applicants' resumes "with the aim of mechanizing the search for top talent". The tool gave candidates scores from one to five stars, "much like shoppers rate products on Amazon". One source is quoted: "Everyone wanted this holy grail ... where I'm going to give you 100 resumes, it will spit out the top five, and we'll hire those." The effort sat in Amazon's Edinburgh engineering hub, grew to around a dozen people, and was set up at a moment when Amazon's headcount, Reuters says, had more than tripled since June 2015 to 575,700.

By 2015, Reuters reports, the company realised the system "was not rating candidates for software developer jobs and other technical posts in a gender-neutral way". The article's explanation of why: "Amazon's computer models were trained to vet applicants by observing patterns in resumes submitted to the company over a 10-year period. Most came from men, a reflection of male dominance across the tech industry." The project was disbanded by the start of 2017.

Label
The outcome a supervised model is trained to predict. When the label is "was hired" or "was rated highly by a person", it records a human decision, with whatever biases that decision carried. See framing problems.
Proxy
A feature that is not the protected attribute (here gender) but is correlated with it, so a model can use it to recover the same information.
Selection rate
The share of a group that gets the favourable outcome, here the share shortlisted. Comparing selection rates between groups is the simplest audit.
Disparate impact
A rule or model that treats everyone the same on its face but results in a much lower selection rate for a protected group. It can be unlawful in some jurisdictions even without intent.

What the system did, according to Reuters

Dastin reports the details that matter for a machine-learning reader.

  • Scale. "The group created 500 computer models focused on specific job functions and locations. They taught each to recognize some 50,000 terms that showed up on past candidates' resumes." That is a very wide sparse feature space: tens of thousands of word indicators per model.
  • What the models ignored and preferred. The algorithms "learned to assign little significance to skills that were common across IT applicants, such as the ability to write various computer codes". Instead, "the technology favored candidates who described themselves using verbs more commonly found on male engineers' resumes, such as 'executed' and 'captured,' one person said."
  • The penalised terms. "In effect, Amazon's system taught itself that male candidates were preferable. It penalized resumes that included the word 'women's,' as in 'women's chess club captain.' And it downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges, according to people familiar with the matter." The colleges were not named.
  • The attempted fix. "Amazon edited the programs to make them neutral to these particular terms. But that was no guarantee that the machines would not devise other ways of sorting candidates that could prove discriminatory, the people said."
  • A second problem. "Gender bias was not the only issue. Problems with the data that underpinned the models' judgments meant that unqualified candidates were often recommended for all manner of jobs, the people said. With the technology returning results almost at random, Amazon shut down the project, they said."
  • How it was used. "Amazon's recruiters looked at the recommendations generated by the tool when searching for new hires, but never relied solely on those rankings." Amazon declined to comment on the technology's challenges, but said the tool "was never used by Amazon recruiters to evaluate candidates"; Reuters notes the company "did not dispute that recruiters looked at the recommendations".
  • Afterwards. Amazon kept a "much-watered down version" for chores such as culling duplicate candidate profiles, and a new Edinburgh team was formed to try automated screening again with a focus on diversity (both per sources).

The mechanism: why a model learns bias from labels

Reuters does not say what target the models were trained on; its description is of "patterns in resumes submitted to the company" over ten years. In the usual set-up for this kind of tool the label is something like "was this person hired, or advanced", which is a record of earlier human decisions. I treat that as the likely situation and label it as my reasoning. Under that assumption, three things follow.

  1. The model is graded on agreement with the past. If recruiters in the past marked down women, a model that reproduces their decisions has scored well. In the language of framing and metrics, the metric rewards the bias because the label contains it.
  2. Protected information does not need to be a column. A resume has tens of thousands of words, clubs, schools and verb choices. Any of them that differ between groups is a path to the same information. Reuters' examples are exactly of this kind: a word, two colleges, and a style of verbs.
  3. Deleting features is whack-a-mole. Neutralising "women's" removes one path. If the gap in the labels remains, the optimiser will move the weight to the next-best correlate. Reuters' sources said so at the time. Upturn's report puts the general point in a heading: "Predictive hiring tools can reflect institutional and systemic biases, and removing sensitive characteristics is not a solution", because predictions based on past hiring decisions "can both reveal and reproduce patterns of inequity ... even when tools explicitly ignore race, gender, age, and other protected attributes".

A toy you can run

The script is mine, on invented data; it is not Amazon's system. It makes 20,000 applicants, half of them women, with the same distribution of true ability. Past hiring decisions mark women down by a fixed amount (0.8 standard deviations of ability) and add noise. The features are a noisy skill score plus three proxies: a "women's club" indicator, an "all-women's college" indicator and a continuous "style" score (a stand-in for verb choice) that is on average lower for women. Gender is never a feature. A logistic regression is trained on the biased decisions for 14,000 applicants, and the top 30 per cent of scores on the other 6,000 form the shortlist. Model A uses all features. Model B drops the two obvious word features, as Amazon did. Model C drops every feature that is correlated with gender. Model D keeps A's features but is trained on a different label: an outcome that depends on ability only, standing in for something like later job performance measured independently of the original hiring decision.

"""Toy: a resume screen trained on biased past decisions (seed 5). Synthetic data and my own sketch,
not Amazon's system. Gender is never a feature; 'proxy' words correlate with it."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
N = 20000
woman = rng.random(N) < 0.5
skill = rng.normal(0, 1, N)                              # true job-relevant ability, same distribution for both groups
# past human decisions: skill matters, but women were marked down by 0.8 (the biased label)
hired = (skill - 0.8*woman + rng.normal(0, 0.7, N)) > 0.5
# resume features (no gender column): skill signals plus proxies for gender
f_skill = skill + rng.normal(0, 0.5, N)
w_club  = (woman & (rng.random(N) < .30)).astype(float)               # "women's chess club captain"
w_coll  = (woman & (rng.random(N) < .12)).astype(float)               # an all-women's college
style   = (-0.9*woman + rng.normal(0, 1, N))                           # verbs / phrasing correlated with gender
def fit(X, y, steps=400, lr=0.5, l2=1e-3):                              # logistic regression, full-batch gradient descent
    X = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))]; w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps): w -= lr * (X.T @ (1/(1+np.exp(-X@w)) - y) / len(y) + l2*w)
    return w
def score(w, X): return 1/(1+np.exp(-(np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))] @ w)))
tr, te = np.arange(N) < 14000, np.arange(N) >= 14000
def report(label, X):
    w = fit(X[tr], hired[tr]); p = score(w, X[te]); top = p >= np.quantile(p, 0.7)          # shortlist the top 30%
    wm, mn = woman[te], ~woman[te]; sel_w, sel_m = top[wm].mean(), top[mn].mean()
    hi = skill[te] > 1.0                                                                      # equally strong candidates
    print("%-34s shortlist rate women %.3f men %.3f | ratio %.2f | strong candidates: women %.2f men %.2f" % (
        label, sel_w, sel_m, sel_w/sel_m, top[wm & hi].mean(), top[mn & hi].mean()))
    return w
print("past hiring rate: women %.3f, men %.3f (same skill distribution)\n" % (hired[woman].mean(), hired[~woman].mean()))
report("A: skill + all resume words",       np.c_[f_skill, w_club, w_coll, style])
report("B: drop the two obvious words",     np.c_[f_skill, style])
report("C: drop every gender-linked feature", np.c_[f_skill])
# D: same model A, but trained on a label that is not the biased decision (e.g. later job performance, same for both groups)
perf = (skill + rng.normal(0, 0.7, N)) > 0.5
wD = fit(np.c_[f_skill, w_club, w_coll, style][tr], perf[tr]); p = score(wD, np.c_[f_skill, w_club, w_coll, style][te]); top = p >= np.quantile(p, .7)
print("%-34s shortlist rate women %.3f men %.3f | ratio %.2f" % ("D: A's features, outcome-based label", top[woman[te]].mean(), top[~woman[te]].mean(), top[woman[te]].mean()/top[~woman[te]].mean()))
past hiring rate: women 0.139, men 0.341 (same skill distribution)

A: skill + all resume words        shortlist rate women 0.247 men 0.353 | ratio 0.70 | strong candidates: women 0.85 men 0.95
B: drop the two obvious words      shortlist rate women 0.270 men 0.330 | ratio 0.82 | strong candidates: women 0.88 men 0.93
C: drop every gender-linked feature shortlist rate women 0.299 men 0.301 | ratio 0.99 | strong candidates: women 0.92 men 0.92
D: A's features, outcome-based label shortlist rate women 0.302 men 0.298 | ratio 1.01

Reading the output. In the past data, women were hired at 13.9 per cent and men at 34.1 per cent although ability was identical. Model A shortlists women at 70 per cent of the men's rate; among strong candidates (ability above one standard deviation) 85 per cent of the women and 95 per cent of the men make the shortlist. Dropping the two obvious words (B) lifts the ratio to 0.82 but leaves a gap, because the style score still carries gender; note that a ratio of 0.82 would look acceptable on a crude threshold while strong women are still shortlisted less often (88 per cent against 93 per cent). Only when every gender-linked feature is gone (C) does the gap close, and that works here only because I built the data and know which features are proxies, and because skill is measured cleanly. Real resumes have no such labels. D shows the structural fix: change the label, and the gap closes (1.01) even though the proxy features are still present, since they no longer predict the target.

Try it yourself

Change the 0.8 in the hired line to 0.0 and rerun: with unbiased labels, the proxies stop mattering and A, B and C become similar. Then change it to 1.6 and see how far the ratios fall. Finally, make the "style" score also depend on skill (add 0.5*skill to it) and see why dropping it (C) now has a cost in the quality of the shortlist as well.

State of play, October 2026

Rules for automated hiring tools have moved since 2018. New York City's Local Law 144 (page read on nyc.gov) bars employers and employment agencies from using an automated employment decision tool unless it has had a bias audit within a year of use, with results made public, and required notices have been given; the city's consumer-protection department began enforcing it on 5 July 2023. In the EU, search summaries I saw (not the legal text) list recruitment systems among the AI Act's high-risk categories and report that Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the "Digital Omnibus on AI"), published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026, moved the main obligations for those systems to 2 December 2027 (see governance and regulation). Check the current texts before relying on any date.

Common misconception

"Just remove gender and the model cannot discriminate." This is the idea the case is about. Removing the column removes the easiest path, not the information; the model can rebuild it from correlated features, and when the label itself is biased it has every reason to do so. The audit that catches it compares outcomes by group (selection rates), not the list of features used. There is also the opposite error: concluding that a model is fair because group outcomes match, when its errors may differ by group. That argument is the subject of the COMPAS case.

What the critics and later work said

  • The company's account differs in emphasis. Amazon said the tool "was never used by Amazon recruiters to evaluate candidates"; Reuters' sources said recruiters looked at its recommendations but did not rely on them alone. Both can be true. I cannot adjudicate it from the sources.
  • The failure was caught internally. By Reuters' account the bias was found in 2015 and the project ended before the tool was used as the sole basis of decisions. That is the review process working, late. The wider lesson, which Upturn emphasises, is that candidates often cannot know a tool was used, and so cannot challenge it.
  • Interpretability would have found the first symptom. A linear model over word indicators would show a negative weight on "women's" immediately, and permutation importance finds the same on any model: see interpreting models. But the explanation shows what the model uses, not whether the label is fair, and it will not find the proxies nobody thought to look for.

Systems connection

Bias findings are incidents and should be handled like them: detection, containment (the tool was recruiters' input, not the decision-maker), and a blameless review (postmortems). The data that fed the tool is personal data about applicants, with its own retention and purpose limits (data protection and privacy), and governance frameworks and audit duties for such systems are covered in governance frameworks and regulation.

Key takeaways

  • Reuters (2018), from anonymous sources: 500 models, about 50,000 terms each, trained on ten years of mostly male resumes, penalised "women's" and two women's colleges, and favoured verbs like "executed".
  • Neutralising named terms was judged no guarantee; the results were nearly random and the team was disbanded by early 2017.
  • A model trained on past decisions reproduces them; gender need not be a feature.
  • In the toy, dropping the obvious words left a selection ratio of 0.82; changing the label closed the gap.
  • Audit selection rates by group, question the label, and do not equate "gender-blind" with "fair".

Check your understanding

Amazon edited its programs to be neutral to the term "women's" and the two women's colleges. Why did the engineers still doubt the fix, according to Reuters?

  1. The edit made the model slower
  2. The machines might devise other ways of sorting candidates that could prove discriminatory
  3. The colleges' names were not known
  4. The edit violated company policy
The sources said it was no guarantee the machines would not find other discriminatory routes. The colleges' names being unknown is a detail of the reporting, not the reason.

In the toy, model D keeps all the proxy features but shortlists women and men at almost the same rate. What changed?

  1. The proxies were removed
  2. The model was made more complex
  3. More training rows were used
  4. The label was changed to an outcome that does not carry the biased past decisions
The proxies are still present, but they no longer predict the target, so the model gives them no weight. That is the structural fix: the label, not the features. Removing proxies is the approach tested in models B and C.

Model B drops the two obvious word features and its women-to-men selection ratio is 0.82. Which conclusion is best?

  1. Bias is reduced but not removed: other features still carry gender, and strong women are still shortlisted less often
  2. The model is now fair, since 0.82 is close to 1
  3. Gender must still be a feature
  4. The result proves that proxies cannot be found
In the toy the style score is a proxy, and among strong candidates women are shortlisted at 88 per cent against 93 per cent. Gender is never a feature (option 2), and the proxy was in fact findable, because I built the data.

A colleague says: "our screening model is gender-blind because gender isn't in the data." What is the best response?

  1. Agree: a model cannot use what it never sees
  2. Agree, but add gender to the data for monitoring only
  3. Check outcomes by group and examine the label, since other features can stand in for gender
  4. Replace logistic regression with a deep network
The check is on outcomes, not inputs. Option 1 is the misconception. Option 2's monitoring is sensible, but it does not answer the claim, and a different model family does not remove a bias in the label.
