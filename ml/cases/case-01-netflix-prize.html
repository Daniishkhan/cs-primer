The Netflix Prize

In 2009 a merged team of three groups won $1,000,000 for beating Netflix's recommender by 10 per cent, using a blend of hundreds of models. Three years later Netflix said the accuracy gains did not seem to justify the engineering effort. The case is about the distance between the number a competition measures and the thing a product needs.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read Yehuda Koren's paper "The BellKor Solution to the Netflix Grand Prize" (August 2009), which describes his part of the winning team's solution and gives the dataset layout and many RMSE values. I read Netflix's own 2012 post, "Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 Stars (Part 1)" by Xavier Amatriain and Justin Basilico, through an Internet Archive copy (the live page returned HTTP 403). I read the Wikipedia article on the Netflix Prize for dates, prize scores and participation counts; that is a secondary source, so I label those figures "as summarised on Wikipedia". I also read The Next Web's April 2012 coverage of the post. I could not fetch the Narayanan and Shmatikov paper and cite it only through Wikipedia. The Netflix post says little about the final ensemble beyond the passage I quote, so anything about how it would have been engineered is my reasoning, labelled as such.

The pattern in one sentence

A model can win on the number a competition measures and still not be worth running, because the production system pays for every extra model in engineering, serving and upkeep, and the real objective may have moved on.

Context

In October 2006 Netflix, then mostly a DVD-by-mail rental service, offered $1,000,000 to anyone who could beat its own recommender, Cinematch, by 10 per cent. The measure was RMSE (root mean squared error: the square root of the average squared gap between predicted and actual star ratings; see framing problems and choosing metrics) on ratings that were hidden from competitors. Koren's paper describes the data: more than 100 million dated ratings from 1 to 5 stars by 480,189 anonymous customers of 17,770 movies, collected between the end of 1999 and the end of 2005. About 99 per cent of the possible user-by-movie cells are empty, because a customer rates only a small portion of the catalogue.

The split mattered as much as the prize. For each user, the last nine ratings (or fewer, for light raters) were held out; the rest was training data. The hold-out was split three ways into Probe, Quiz and Test. Competitors received the Probe labels, submitted predictions for the Quiz and Test cells together, and saw only the Quiz RMSE on a public leaderboard. The winner was whoever scored best on the Test half, a number Netflix never disclosed. Koren notes that this precluded gaming the contest by learning about the Quiz set through repeated submissions. This is the hold-out discipline of cross-validation done right applied to a public contest, and the hold-out is time-ordered rather than random for the same reason: the model must predict new ratings from older ones, as it would in service.

The leaderboard was a measuring stick that thousands of people tried to climb. As summarised on Wikipedia, the contest drew more than 20,000 teams from over 150 countries by June 2007, and the final tally was 44,014 submissions from 5,169 teams.

RMSE
Root mean squared error. For ratings it is in stars; Cinematch scored 0.9514 on the Quiz set (Netflix's post quotes 0.9525, which Wikipedia gives as the Test-set figure), meaning typical errors of a bit under one star.
Baseline predictor
A prediction that uses only the user's and movie's average tendencies, not their interaction. Koren's example: the overall mean is 3.7 stars, Titanic is rated 0.5 above average and a critical user 0.3 below, so the baseline for that pair is 3.7 - 0.3 + 0.5 = 3.9.
Matrix factorisation
Describing each user and each movie by a short list of numbers (factors) so that their dot product approximates the rating. See SVD and low rank in A0.
Blending (stacking)
Training a second model that takes the predictions of many first-stage models as its inputs. Koren notes that "stacked generalization" is the more accurate term. See how competitions are won.
Progress Prize
A $50,000 yearly prize for the best result so far, which required publishing the method. It is why so much of the work is documented.

The mechanism: what the winning solution was made of

The winning team, BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, combined three groups: BellKor, BigChaos and Pragmatic Theory. Koren's 2009 paper describes the BellKor part; the team's final blend includes all the predictors from its 2008 Progress Prize report plus the newer ones he describes. It is a catalogue of how a modern competition solution is assembled, and the pieces are worth knowing individually.

Baselines first. Koren starts with the humble user and movie biases and makes them time-aware: a movie's popularity drifts, a user's rating scale drifts, and a user's ratings on a single day cluster around a mood. The paper says a baseline of this kind, with a per-user scaling, reaches an RMSE of 0.9555 and "can explain almost as much of the data variability as the commercial Netflix Cinematch recommender system, whose published RMSE on the same Quiz set is 0.9514". Adding a feature he calls frequency (how many ratings the user gave that day) lowers it to 0.9278, better than Cinematch with no personalisation at all. This is the lesson of baselines first, at competition scale.

Koren's explanation of why the frequency feature helps is revealing. He hypothesises that high-frequency days mostly reflect which movies get rated in bulk (people tend to rate in bulk the ones they remember as favourites or as bad examples), a "biased selection of movies to be rated", not a change in taste. That is a collection artefact in the sense of the leakage chapter: it predicts the held-out ratings because of how the data was gathered, which is fair game in a contest. He adds that the feature's benefit mostly disappears once interaction terms are in the model.

Then the personal models. On top of the baselines sit matrix-factorisation models (the paper lists variants with 20 to 2,000 factors, with Quiz RMSEs from 0.8914 down to 0.8762), neighbourhood models (item-to-item similarity on the residuals of the baseline, 0.8870 to 0.8887) and Restricted Boltzmann Machines (a neural-network type, 0.8928 to 0.8952 for the variants listed). No single factor, neighbourhood or RBM model in the paper's lists beats 0.8762.

Then the blend. The final stage is a gradient-boosted decision-tree blender (GBDT; see random forests and gradient boosting) that takes the other models' predictions as inputs, together with a few extra features: how many movies the user rated, how many users rated the movie, the day and the frequency. Koren reports three blend sets: 454 predictors from the whole team, a 75-predictor subset chosen by forward selection, and 24 BellKor predictors. The GBDT on the 454 predictors reaches 0.8603 and on the 75-predictor subset 0.8606, with 200 trees. He also explains why blending was where the last stretch came from: late in the contest, "individual predictors, even if novel and accurate, are unlikely to make a difference to the blend", so with 30 days left the team looked for better blending instead.

Using these reported values, the best single factor model closes about four fifths of the distance between Cinematch (0.9514) and the final Quiz score (0.8554): (0.9514 - 0.8762) / (0.9514 - 0.8554) is 0.78. The remaining fifth took hundreds of models. That is my arithmetic on the reported numbers, and it understates the blend's role a little, because the best single model is itself the product of the team's best ideas.

What happened

As summarised on Wikipedia, the 2007 Progress Prize went to a team that reached 0.8712 (an 8.43 per cent improvement), the 2008 one to BellKor and BigChaos at 0.8616, and the Grand Prize threshold was 0.8572 on the Test half (0.8563 on the Quiz half). The finish was a photograph: BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos scored 0.8567 on the Test half, a 10.06 per cent improvement, and a rival team called The Ensemble scored 0.8553 on the Quiz half. Wikipedia reports that The Ensemble matched BellKor's result and that BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos won because it submitted 20 minutes earlier. The prize was awarded on 21 September 2009. Koren calls the choice of a 10 per cent target "some touch of luck": a small deviation either way would have made the contest too easy or impossibly hard.

Then came the part that is the subject of this case. In April 2012 Netflix published "Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 Stars". The authors recall that Netflix had chosen RMSE as "a proxy question that was easier to evaluate and quantify" than the real goal of better recommendations. They describe the 2007 Progress Prize: the winning team reported more than 2,000 hours of work to reach the "final combination of 107 algorithms" and gave Netflix the source code. Netflix looked at the two best underlying algorithms, matrix factorisation (which the community called SVD) and Restricted Boltzmann Machines, which scored 0.8914 and 0.8990 RMSE alone and 0.88 as a linear blend. There were limits to overcome, such as being "built to handle 100 million ratings, instead of the more than 5 billion that we have", and not being built to adapt as members add ratings; then "we put the two algorithms into production, where they are still used as part of our recommendation engine" (as of 2012).

On the Grand Prize ensemble the post is blunt. It calls the work "a truly impressive compilation and culmination of years of work, blending hundreds of predictive models to finally cross the finish line", and continues: "We evaluated some of the new methods offline but the additional accuracy gains that we measured did not seem to justify the engineering effort needed to bring them into a production environment. Also, our focus on improving Netflix personalization had shifted to the next level by then." The reason for the shift, the authors write, is that Netflix launched instant streaming in 2007, one year into the contest. For DVDs the task was filling a queue for the mail, with no feedback during viewing; for streaming, members look for something to watch now, and "we can observe viewing statistics such as whether a video was watched fully or only partially". The post says 75 per cent of what members watched came from some sort of recommendation, and describes a homepage of rows of videos ranked and arranged for a household, not a predicted star.

Read the reasons together. The first is cost: even the 2007 algorithms needed work to scale from 100 million ratings to more than 5 billion and to update as members rated more titles, and the Grand Prize blend contained, in Koren's account, 454 predictors (Netflix says "hundreds of predictive models"). Koren himself notes that producing Probe-set results for the blend meant fully re-training models with the Probe set excluded, which needs extra computational resources. The second is that the problem changed: predicting the star a customer would give a DVD is a different task from choosing what to show a streaming customer who is already in the app, where the signals are plays and partial views and the output is a ranking. The third (my inference, not a Netflix statement) is that the contest data stopped at the end of 2005 and the product kept moving.

Common misconception

"Netflix wasted a million dollars" or "Netflix never used anything from the Prize." Netflix's own post says it put two of the Progress Prize algorithms into production and that they were still in use in 2012. What it did not deploy was the Grand Prize ensemble as a whole. Koren, writing as a participant in 2009, also argues the contest produced a public dataset, a spike in related publications and better algorithms, and the Progress Prize rules required publication of methods. Those benefits are his view, not an audit, and I did not verify what else Netflix gained internally. The fair summary is narrower: the leaderboard objective was not the production objective, and the last increments of accuracy were not worth their cost.

The privacy epilogue

As summarised on Wikipedia, researchers Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov showed in 2007 that individual users in the "anonymous" data could be identified by matching their ratings against public IMDb ratings. In December 2009 four Netflix users filed a class action, and in March 2010 Netflix cancelled the planned second contest, citing the lawsuit and Federal Trade Commission privacy concerns. Releasing row-level data for a competition has a cost that no leaderboard shows; see data protection and privacy.

What the critics and later work said

  • Winning at the metric is not shipping. The competitions chapter makes the same point: a competition solution is judged on one fixed score, a product on accuracy, latency, cost and the ability to change it. Netflix's authors made that trade-off explicit.
  • Offline error is a proxy. RMSE on held-out stars is not engagement or retention. Whether a better RMSE helps users has to be tested online, as in from model to value. I found no published A/B test of the winning blend in my sources.
  • Small gains are real but bounded. Koren's own conclusion is that "an accurate treatment of main effects is probably at least as significant as coming up with modeling breakthroughs". The simple parts earned most of the improvement.

Try it yourself: how much does a blend add, and what does it cost?

The script below is my own toy, not Netflix's data. It generates a synthetic ratings matrix (400 users by 300 movies, about 12 per cent filled, seed 7) from biases plus four hidden factors plus noise, then trains six models: the global mean, user and item biases, three matrix-factorisation models by stochastic gradient descent (2, 4 and 8 factors, fitted on the baseline's residual) and an item-to-item neighbourhood model. It then blends them with a ridge-regularised linear stacker whose weights are fitted on a validation block, and scores everything on a third block it never touched. The count of SGD updates is a rough stand-in for the training bill.

"""Toy: why blending many rating models helps a little, and what it costs.
Synthetic ratings (seed 7); my own sketch, not Netflix's data or code."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
U, M, K = 400, 300, 4
P = rng.normal(0, 0.55, (U, K)); Q = rng.normal(0, 0.55, (M, K))
bu = rng.normal(0, 0.35, U); bi = rng.normal(0, 0.45, M)
full = 3.6 + bu[:, None] + bi[None, :] + P @ Q.T
mask = rng.random((U, M)) < 0.12                      # about 12% of cells rated
R = np.clip(np.round(full + rng.normal(0, 0.6, (U, M))), 1, 5)
idx = np.argwhere(mask); rng.shuffle(idx)
n = len(idx); tr, va, te = idx[:int(.7*n)], idx[int(.7*n):int(.85*n)], idx[int(.85*n):]
def rmse(pred, ix): return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((pred[ix[:,0], ix[:,1]] - R[ix[:,0], ix[:,1]])**2)))

mu = R[tr[:,0], tr[:,1]].mean()
G = np.full((U, M), mu)                                # model 0: global mean

def baseline(lam=10, it=10):                           # user + item biases (Koren's "baseline predictors")
    b_u, b_i = np.zeros(U), np.zeros(M)
    for _ in range(it):
        for i in range(M):
            r = tr[tr[:,1]==i]; b_i[i] = (R[r[:,0], i] - mu - b_u[r[:,0]]).sum() / (lam + len(r))
        for u in range(U):
            r = tr[tr[:,0]==u]; b_u[u] = (R[u, r[:,1]] - mu - b_i[r[:,1]]).sum() / (lam + len(r))
    return b_u, b_i
b_u, b_i = baseline(); B = mu + b_u[:, None] + b_i[None, :]

def mf(k, lam, epochs=40, lr=0.02, seed=0):            # matrix factorisation by SGD on residual of the baseline
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); p = r.normal(0, .1, (U, k)); q = r.normal(0, .1, (M, k))
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for j in r.permutation(len(tr)):
            u, i = tr[j]; e = R[u, i] - B[u, i] - p[u] @ q[i]
            pu = p[u].copy(); p[u] += lr * (e * q[i] - lam * p[u]); q[i] += lr * (e * pu - lam * q[i])
    return B + p @ q.T
F2 = mf(2, 0.05, seed=1); F4 = mf(4, 0.05, seed=2); F8 = mf(8, 0.10, seed=3)

def knn(kn=20):                                        # item-item neighbourhood on baseline residuals
    Z = np.zeros((U, M)); Z[tr[:,0], tr[:,1]] = R[tr[:,0], tr[:,1]] - B[tr[:,0], tr[:,1]]
    nrm = np.linalg.norm(Z, axis=0) + 1e-9; S = (Z.T @ Z) / np.outer(nrm, nrm); np.fill_diagonal(S, 0)
    out = B.copy()
    for i in range(M):
        nb = np.argsort(-np.abs(S[i]))[:kn]; w = S[i, nb]
        out[:, i] += (Z[:, nb] @ w) / (np.abs(w).sum() + 1e-9)
    return out
KN = knn()

models = {"global mean": G, "biases (baseline)": B, "MF k=2": F2, "MF k=4": F4, "MF k=8": F8, "item kNN": KN}
print("%-20s %9s %9s" % ("model", "val RMSE", "test RMSE"))
for name, m in models.items(): print("%-20s %9.4f %9.4f" % (name, rmse(m, va), rmse(m, te)))

def blend(names, ridge=1e-3):                          # linear stacking: weights fit on the validation block
    A = np.stack([models[k][va[:,0], va[:,1]] for k in names], 1); y = R[va[:,0], va[:,1]]
    w = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + ridge*np.eye(len(names)), A.T @ y)
    out = sum(wi * models[k] for wi, k in zip(w, names)); return out, w
print()
base_te = rmse(B, te)
for names in (["biases (baseline)"], ["biases (baseline)", "MF k=4"],
              ["biases (baseline)", "MF k=2", "MF k=4", "MF k=8"],
              ["biases (baseline)", "MF k=2", "MF k=4", "MF k=8", "item kNN"]):
    out, w = blend(names); t = rmse(out, te)
    print("blend of %d model(s): test RMSE %.4f  (%.2f%% better than biases alone)" % (len(names), t, 100*(base_te - t)/base_te))
print("\nSGD updates to train the 3 MF models: %d (3 x 40 epochs x %d ratings), plus kNN and the blend fit" % (3*40*len(tr), len(tr)))
print("ratings: train %d, val %d, test %d, density %.1f%%" % (len(tr), len(va), len(te), 100*mask.mean()))
model                 val RMSE test RMSE
global mean             0.9597    0.9905
biases (baseline)       0.8591    0.8904
MF k=2                  0.8640    0.9024
MF k=4                  0.8663    0.8705
MF k=8                  0.8454    0.8651
item kNN                0.8548    0.8859

blend of 1 model(s): test RMSE 0.8911  (-0.08% better than biases alone)
blend of 2 model(s): test RMSE 0.8591  (3.52% better than biases alone)
blend of 4 model(s): test RMSE 0.8455  (5.04% better than biases alone)
blend of 5 model(s): test RMSE 0.8455  (5.04% better than biases alone)

SGD updates to train the 3 MF models: 1199160 (3 x 40 epochs x 9993 ratings), plus kNN and the blend fit
ratings: train 9993, val 2141, test 2142, density 11.9%

Reading the output: the best single model (8 factors) reaches 0.8651 on test, 2.8 per cent better than biases alone at 0.8904. The best blend, 0.8455, is 5.0 per cent better than biases alone and about 2.3 per cent better than the best single model, for about 1.2 million gradient updates in total and several more models to keep running. The first blend row (a "blend" of one model) shows the stacker adds nothing by itself, and the fifth model changes nothing at four decimals: returns diminish. The point is the ratio, not the numbers; the toy is far smaller and cleaner than the real data.

Try it yourself

Raise the noise standard deviation in the script from 0.6 to 1.2 and see how much room is left for any model to improve. Then time each model with time.perf_counter() and decide what accuracy gain per second of compute you would pay for. Finally, write down what you would need to measure online to decide whether a 2 per cent RMSE gain is worth shipping to a team that retrains weekly, using the model-to-value chapter.

Systems connection

An ensemble multiplies operational surface. Each of the 454 predictors would need its training pipeline, its feature inputs, a place to run and a way to detect that it has gone wrong. That is the model-serving and feature-store cost described in Designing with ML & LLMs, and the reason reliability engineers ask for fewer moving parts (deployment strategies). The second Netflix reason, a product whose signals change, is the monitoring problem of the last chapter.

Key takeaways

  • Reported numbers: Cinematch 0.9514, best single factor model 0.8762, GBDT blend of 454 predictors 0.8603, Grand Prize 0.8554 on the Quiz set and 0.8567 on the Test set (the last two as summarised on Wikipedia).
  • Time-aware biases beat the incumbent before any personalisation; the simple parts carried most of the gain.
  • Blending bought the last stretch at the price of hundreds of models.
  • Netflix's 2012 post says it deployed two Progress Prize algorithms but judged the Grand Prize ensemble's extra gains not worth the engineering effort, because its focus had shifted with streaming.
  • Ask what the production objective is before optimising a leaderboard metric, and price each added model.

Check your understanding

The Netflix Prize winner was decided on a Test half whose score was never published, while competitors saw the Quiz half. Why?

  1. To keep the winner's score confidential for legal reasons
  2. Because the Test half was easier
  3. So repeated submissions could not be used to learn about the scored cells and game the contest
  4. Because RMSE cannot be computed on a public set
Koren says withholding the Test score precluded gaming the contest by learning about the Quiz set through repeated submissions. The legal and difficulty explanations have no support in the sources; RMSE works on any set.

In Koren's paper, the single best factor model scored 0.8762 and the GBDT blend of 454 predictors 0.8603. What is the best summary of what the blend added?

  1. Nothing: a good single model is all you need
  2. A real but smaller gain than the single model gave over the baselines, at the cost of many more models
  3. Most of the improvement over Cinematch
  4. A guarantee of better online engagement
The best single model already closes about four fifths of the gap to the final score; the blend adds the rest. It says nothing about online engagement, which the contest never measured.

Which statement best matches what Netflix's authors wrote in 2012?

  1. Nothing from the contest was ever used
  2. The final ensemble's extra gains did not seem to justify the engineering effort; two earlier algorithms were put into production
  3. The winning algorithm contained a bug that made it unusable
  4. Matrix factorisation had been disproved
The post says the two Progress Prize algorithms (matrix factorisation and RBM) went into production and are still used, while the Grand Prize ensemble's extra gains did not justify the effort. "Nothing was used" is the tempting overstatement.

In the toy, a "blend of one model" shows no gain, and a fifth model changes nothing at four decimals. What does that show?

  1. The stacker is broken
  2. More models always help
  3. Blending only works on real data
  4. A blend helps when its inputs make different errors; adding models that overlap with existing ones adds little
With one input the stacker can only rescale it. Gains come from diversity; a neighbourhood model close to the biases-plus-factors predictions has little new to contribute. "More models always help" is the tempting error: returns diminish.
