COMPAS and Competing Definitions of Fair

In 2016 a news investigation reported that a widely used criminal risk score wrongly flagged Black defendants as high risk almost twice as often as White defendants. The score's maker replied that the score meant the same thing for everyone: a given score predicted the same chance of reoffending in both groups. Within months, statisticians showed that both statements could be true at once, and that, when base rates differ, no score can satisfy both and also have equal error rates. The case is about why that is arithmetic, not a disagreement about facts.

What I could and could not read

I read, through a tool that summarises long pages: ProPublica's article "Machine Bias" (Angwin, Larson, Mattu and Kirchner, 2016), its methodology article ("How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm", Larson et al.), its later "technical response" to the maker's critique, and the papers by Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (arXiv 1609.05807), Corbett-Davies and colleagues (arXiv 1701.08230, abstract only) and Dressel and Farid (Science Advances, 2018). I read the full text (extracted from the PDF) of Alexandra Chouldechova's "Fair prediction with disparate impact" (arXiv 1610.07524) and of the rejoinder by Anthony Flores, Kristin Bechtel and Christopher Lowenkamp, "False Positives, False Negatives, and False Analyses" (Federal Probation, September 2016), which argues the case for the score. I did not read the maker's own report (Northpointe, now equivant; the copy I tried to fetch returned a "page not found"), so its arguments reach me only through the rejoinder, Kleinberg et al. and ProPublica's reply. The COMPAS model itself is proprietary, and no source I read describes its internals; I say nothing about them. I did not check whether or how COMPAS is used today, so there is no "state of play" claim here. The simulation is my own, with invented distributions, and does not reproduce the Broward data.

The pattern in one sentence

When two groups have different base rates, a risk score cannot in general be calibrated within each group and also give them equal false positive and false negative rates, so "is it fair?" becomes a question about which error a decision-maker is willing to share unequally, not a question a metric can answer.

Context: a score used in decisions about people

COMPAS is a commercial risk assessment tool. For each defendant it produces a score from 1 to 10 that estimates the risk of reoffending. ProPublica's methodology note groups the scores into low (1 to 4), medium (5 to 7) and high (8 to 10). Courts and corrections staff can use such scores in decisions about bail, supervision and sentencing, which is why error rates matter: a false positive is a person labelled risky who did not go on to reoffend, and a false negative is a person labelled safe who did.

ProPublica obtained scores for people arrested in Broward County, Florida, in 2013 and 2014, and checked who was charged with a new crime in the next two years. The article reports a sample of "more than 7,000" people. The methodology article says it obtained scores for 18,610 people; after removing scores that were not from pretrial assessment, 11,757 remained, and the error-rate tables use 7,214 defendants. Recidivism here means a new arrest within two years, not a proven offence. That choice will matter later.

Base rate (prevalence)
The share of a group who have the outcome, here the share who are rearrested within two years.
Calibration within groups
Among people in a group who get score s, the fraction with the outcome is about the same for every group. The score "means the same" for everyone.
Predictive parity
The positive predictive value (PPV), the share of people flagged high risk who do reoffend, is equal across groups.
False positive rate (FPR)
Among people who did not reoffend, the share flagged high risk.
False negative rate (FNR)
Among people who did reoffend, the share flagged low risk.
Error-rate balance
Equal FPR and equal FNR across groups.

The accusation: unequal errors

ProPublica's central finding, as the methodology note tabulates it, concerns the people who were labelled "higher risk" (medium or high):

General recidivism, two yearsAllBlackWhite
False positive rate32.35%44.85%23.45%
False negative rate37.40%27.99%47.72%

In plain terms: among defendants who did not go on to be rearrested, about 45 per cent of Black defendants had been labelled higher risk, against about 23 per cent of White defendants. Among defendants who were rearrested, about 48 per cent of White defendants had been labelled lower risk, against 28 per cent of Black defendants. The article states that the score was "particularly likely to falsely flag black defendants as future criminals", at almost twice the rate of White defendants, and that White defendants were mislabelled as low risk more often. It also reports that, of those deemed likely to reoffend, 61 per cent were arrested again within two years, and that only 20 per cent of those predicted to commit violent crimes did so. ProPublica later stated that if only the "High" label counted, the disparity in false positives was larger: black defendants who did not reoffend were three times as likely as White defendants to be labelled high risk.

These error rates are facts about the data. Nobody in the debate disputed the arithmetic, and Chouldechova's analysis uses the same figures. The dispute was about what they mean.

The defence: the score means the same thing for everyone

The rejoinder by Flores, Bechtel and Lowenkamp argues that ProPublica's report "was based on faulty statistics and data analysis". Its analysis looks at a different question: does a given score predict the same likelihood of rearrest for both groups? Using the same data, it reports a general-rearrest base rate of 47 per cent for everyone, 39 per cent for White defendants and 52 per cent for Black defendants. The rate of rearrest within each risk category was close across the two groups:

The authors read this as showing no predictive bias: a high score means about 73 to 75 per cent rearrest for both groups, and the score separates rearrested from not-rearrested people about equally well in each (AUC 0.69 and 0.70, with "no significant difference"). They write that racial differences in failure rates reflect the conduct of defendants and of the criminal justice system rather than bias in the assessment. That is a claim about the cause of the base-rate difference, and it is notable that the same paper also says the justice system might be biased against poor minorities through factors such as policing patterns, prosecutorial behaviour and judicial biases. I take it as their argument that a risk tool built on objective data can still help reduce bias compared with the status quo; whether it does is not something the papers I read test.

Chouldechova independently checked the score against the same data. She plots the observed rearrest rate at each score for both groups and finds that COMPAS "appears to adhere well" to the calibration condition she calls test fairness; she cites Flores et al. as verifying this by logistic regression. On the face of it, both sides now have solid evidence.

The mathematics: why both were right

Chouldechova gives a short identity. Let p be a group's base rate (prevalence), let PPV be the share of the high-risk label that reoffends, and let FPR and FNR be the false positive and false negative rates for the high-risk label. Then (her Equation 2.4):

FPR = p/(1 − p) × (1 − PPV)/PPV × (1 − FNR)

It is just the definitions of the four quantities rearranged (Bayes' rule from A0). Now suppose two groups have the same PPV, because the score is calibrated, but different base rates p. The right-hand side then differs between the groups unless something else moves. So the FPR and FNR cannot both be equal. In her words: "Since the COMPAS RPI approximately satisfies test fairness, we know that some level of imbalance in the error rates must exist." She notes the recidivism rate among Black defendants in the data is 51 per cent against 39 per cent for White defendants, and attributes the same observation to independent concurrent work by Kleinberg et al.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan state it as a theorem. They formalise three conditions: (A) calibration within groups; (B) balance for the negative class, meaning negative-class members receive the same average score in both groups; and (C) balance for the positive class, the same for positive-class members. Their Theorem 1.1 says that if a risk assignment satisfies all three, "the instance must either allow for perfect prediction" or the groups must have equal base rates. They add that approximately satisfying all three requires data close to one of those special cases. In their framing, ProPublica's critique targets conditions B and C, while the counter-analyses showing the tool was "equally well calibrated" target A.

A simulation you can run

The script below builds two groups of 400,000 people each. Each person has a true risk p drawn from a Beta distribution (mean 0.39 for group A, 0.52 for group B, chosen by me to resemble the base rates reported above, not fitted), the outcome is drawn with probability p, and the "score" is the decile of p. A score built this way is calibrated by construction, because the outcome really is drawn with that probability. The script then checks calibration, computes error rates with one threshold, checks Chouldechova's identity, tries a higher threshold for group B, computes Kleinberg's balance conditions and, last, repeats the exercise for two groups with the same distribution.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
N = 400_000
groups = {"A": (2.0, 3.1), "B": (2.6, 2.4)}          # Beta(a, b) distributions of true risk
data = {}
for name, (a, b) in groups.items():
    p = rng.beta(a, b, N)
    y = (rng.random(N) < p).astype(int)               # outcome drawn with probability p
    data[name] = (np.ceil(p * 10).clip(1, 10).astype(int), y)   # score = decile of p, 1..10

print("1. Calibration: observed rate by score (the score means the same in both groups)")
print("   score   " + "  ".join(f"{d:5d}" for d in range(1, 11)))
for g, (s, y) in data.items():
    print(f"   group {g}  " + "  ".join(f"{y[s == d].mean():5.2f}" for d in range(1, 11)))

def rates(s, y, cut):
    hi = s >= cut
    return dict(prev=y.mean(), ppv=y[hi].mean(), fpr=hi[y == 0].mean(), fnr=(~hi)[y == 1].mean())

print("\n2. One threshold (score >= 5 is 'high risk') for everyone")
for g, (s, y) in data.items():
    r = rates(s, y, 5); print(f"   group {g}: base rate {r['prev']:.3f}  PPV {r['ppv']:.3f}  FPR {r['fpr']:.3f}  FNR {r['fnr']:.3f}")
    chk = r['prev']/(1-r['prev']) * (1-r['ppv'])/r['ppv'] * (1-r['fnr'])
    print(f"            identity: p/(1-p) * (1-PPV)/PPV * (1-FNR) = {chk:.3f}  (FPR {r['fpr']:.3f})")

print("\n3. Equalise FPR by giving group B a higher threshold")
sA, yA = data["A"]; rA = rates(sA, yA, 5)
for cut in (5, 6, 7):
    sB, yB = data["B"]; r = rates(sB, yB, cut)
    print(f"   B cut {cut}: FPR {r['fpr']:.3f} (A: {rA['fpr']:.3f})  FNR {r['fnr']:.3f} (A: {rA['fnr']:.3f})  PPV {r['ppv']:.3f} (A: {rA['ppv']:.3f})")

print("\n4. Kleinberg et al.'s balance conditions: average score (1 to 10) by outcome")
for g, (s, y) in data.items():
    print(f"   group {g}: non-reoffenders {s[y == 0].mean():.2f}   reoffenders {s[y == 1].mean():.2f}")

print("\n5. Same score distribution in both groups (equal base rates)")
same = {}
for g in "AB":
    p = rng.beta(2.3, 2.8, N); y = (rng.random(N) < p).astype(int)
    same[g] = (np.ceil(p * 10).clip(1, 10).astype(int), y)
for g, (s, y) in same.items():
    r = rates(s, y, 5); print(f"   group {g}: base rate {r['prev']:.3f}  PPV {r['ppv']:.3f}  FPR {r['fpr']:.3f}  FNR {r['fnr']:.3f}")

What the output shows, step by step.

  1. The score means the same thing in both groups. At every score from 1 to 10 the observed outcome rate differs between the groups by at most 0.01. A score of 5 corresponds to about 45 per cent in both.
  2. Yet the errors differ sharply. With one threshold (5 or higher is "high risk"), group B's false positive rate is 0.548 against 0.326 for A, and its false negative rate is 0.159 against 0.333. The base rates are 0.520 and 0.393. The identity reproduces each FPR to three decimals.
  3. The PPV is not exactly equal either. At this coarse threshold it is 0.624 for B and 0.571 for A, because the mix of scores inside the "high" band differs between the groups, even though every individual score is calibrated. Chouldechova's PPV condition is the coarse-band version of calibration, and in real data it holds only approximately: the table from Flores et al. above shows 73 against 75 per cent in the high band, but 29 against 35 in the low band.
  4. Equalising the errors costs something else. Raising group B's threshold to 6 brings its FPR to 0.363 and FNR to 0.299, close to A's 0.326 and 0.333, but its PPV rises to 0.677, and a person in group B with score 5 (a 45 per cent risk) is treated as "low" while a person in group A with the same score and the same risk is treated as "high". Equal errors, bought with unequal treatment of identical scores.
  5. Balance conditions fail too. The average score among non-reoffenders is 3.78 in A and 4.83 in B; among reoffenders it is 5.41 and 6.50. Conditions B and C of Kleinberg et al. are violated, as the theorem predicts.
  6. With equal base rates, the conflict disappears. Section 5 gives two groups with the same score distribution (base rates 0.450 and 0.451): PPV, FPR and FNR agree to three decimals. The tension is created by the base-rate difference, not by the scoring method.

Common misconception

"One of the two sides must have been wrong, so the score is either fair or biased." The maths shows that a calibrated score used with different base rates will have unequal error rates, so both of the following can be true at once: "a score of 8 means the same risk for everyone" and "innocent people in one group are flagged more often". Which matters more is a judgement about the decision: what a false positive costs (for example, detention or a longer sentence for someone who would not have reoffended), what a false negative costs, and who bears each. A metric cannot settle that.

What critics and later work said

  • The score may add little. Dressel and Farid (Science Advances, 2018) used 7,214 Broward defendants and a 1,000-defendant subset. They report that COMPAS, which uses 137 features, had 65.2 per cent accuracy there, while pooled online volunteers who saw seven features per defendant reached 67.0 per cent (not statistically different), and a logistic regression with only two features, age and number of prior convictions, reached 66.8 per cent. A nonlinear model did not do better. So the accuracy ceiling looks low and simple baselines match it; this is the argument of chapter 4. They also report that error rates by race were similar in pattern for the human volunteers (false positive rates of 37.1 per cent for Black and 27.2 per cent for White defendants) as for COMPAS (40.4 and 25.4 per cent on their subset).
  • The label is an arrest. Dressel and Farid note that recidivism is measured as rearrest, an imperfect proxy that can reflect different arrest rates across groups. If policing intensity differs by group, the base rates and the labels are both affected. A calibrated predictor of rearrest is calibrated to the measurement, not to underlying behaviour. This is the biased-labels problem of case study 4.
  • Fairness constraints change the optimum. Corbett-Davies and colleagues (arXiv 1701.08230) frame fairness as constrained optimisation. According to their abstract, for several definitions the optimal constrained algorithm uses different thresholds by race, the unconstrained optimum uses one threshold, and using Broward data they show the safety cost of the constraints can be large. I read only the abstract, so I cannot say how large.
  • ProPublica's own reply. In its technical response, ProPublica says it did account for base rates using a logistic regression, and, as the page puts it, frames the core question as "Are higher scores distributed unequally between black and white defendants?". It answers that they are. That is the error-balance view stated openly.

Try it yourself

In fair_scores.py, change group B's Beta parameters to (2.0, 3.1) so both groups match and rerun: what happens to section 2? Then widen the gap instead (for example B as (3.5, 2.0)) and watch how the FPR gap and the FNR gap grow together. Finally, choose a cost, such as "a false positive is three times as bad as a false negative", and find which single threshold per group minimises total cost. Does the cost-minimising choice equalise any of the error rates? What does your answer say about choosing a definition of fair before choosing a threshold?

Key takeaways

  • ProPublica measured P(score | outcome): among people who did not reoffend, 44.85% of Black and 23.45% of White defendants were labelled higher risk. The maker's side measured P(outcome | score): similar rearrest rates at each score in both groups.
  • Both were arithmetically right. With different base rates, a calibrated score cannot also have equal false positive and false negative rates, except in the special cases of perfect prediction or equal base rates (Chouldechova; Kleinberg et al.).
  • My simulation shows it: identical calibration curves, FPR 0.326 against 0.548, and the identity holding to three decimals.
  • Equalising errors with group thresholds treats equal scores unequally. Choosing between these is a values decision, tied to the costs of each error and to what the label measures.
  • The deeper questions are about the label (rearrest) and the value of the score over a simple baseline, not only about the choice of metric.

Check your understanding

Within each risk category, the share of defendants rearrested was similar for Black and White defendants, yet the false positive rate differed. How can both hold?

  1. The data must contain errors
  2. The false positive rate is not affected by the score
  3. One of the two analyses used the wrong sample
  4. The two statements condition on different things (the score versus the outcome), and with different base rates the identity linking them forces the error rates apart
Chouldechova's identity shows the FPR depends on the base rate, the PPV and the FNR. Equal PPV with unequal base rates means unequal FPR or FNR. The sources agree on the numbers; they differ on which to call fair.

In the simulation, group B's threshold is raised from 5 to 6, bringing its error rates close to group A's. What is the price?

  1. The score becomes uncalibrated
  2. People with the same score and the same risk are now treated differently depending on their group
  3. The overall base rate changes
  4. The AUC falls to 0.5
The score itself is unchanged, so its calibration is unchanged. The decision rule now uses a different cutoff by group, so a score of 5 is "high" for one group and "low" for the other. A threshold does not change base rates or AUC.

Which situation lets a score be calibrated within groups and have equal error rates across them?

  1. Whenever the score is a logistic regression
  2. Whenever the groups are the same size
  3. When the groups have equal base rates, or when prediction is perfect
  4. Never, under any conditions
Kleinberg et al.'s Theorem 1.1 allows exactly those two special cases (and says approximate versions need data close to them). Model type and group size do not appear in the result. Section 5 of the script shows the equal-base-rate case.

Dressel and Farid found that a two-feature logistic regression matched COMPAS's accuracy on their data. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. On that data the prediction task has a low accuracy ceiling that simple models reach, which makes the trade-offs about errors matter more, not less
  2. COMPAS is unfair
  3. Age and prior convictions are the only causes of reoffending
  4. The fairness conflict disappears for simple models
The result is about accuracy. It does not show unfairness, causes, or the removal of the base-rate conflict, which applies to any score on groups with different base rates.
