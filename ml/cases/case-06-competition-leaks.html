Leaks That Won Competitions

A data-mining competition is a measurement: it asks which method predicts best, and answers with a leaderboard. Several well-known contests measured something else. A patient number, a hidden trace left by removing the answer, future prices, or a public copy of the "anonymous" data let entrants score highly without solving the problem the organisers cared about. The case follows four of them through the 2011 paper that named the pattern, and asks what organisers can do.

For the 2018 Santander contest I found only a secondary source, one participant's public repository. I could not fetch the KDD Cup 2008 winners' report (Rosset et al.), the competitions' forums, or any Kaggle statement, so what I say about the 2008 and 2010 contests is what Kaufman and colleagues report about them. Their paper also, in one place, calls the breast-cancer contest "INFORMS 2008" and elsewhere "KDD-Cup 2008"; the pneumonia contest was INFORMS 2008, and I follow the paper's main description. The pattern in one sentence

A leak is a route from the answer into the inputs that the real task would never offer, and a leaderboard rewards whoever finds it, so any trace of the target left by how the data was collected, numbered, cleaned, split or released is part of the problem you are setting.

Context: what counts as "legitimate" information

You met leakage in chapter 14 as a bug in your own pipeline. This case is about leakage in someone else's dataset. Kaufman, Rosset and Perlich define it as "the introduction of information about the data mining target, which should not be legitimately available to mine from" (abstract). The key word is legitimately. They point out that every problem sets its own rules for what a legitimate solution may use, and that leakage is information that breaks those rules. The usual rule for prediction is a no-time-machine rule: anything the model uses must have been available at the moment the prediction is needed.

They also note that the leaks are, in practice, not planted. The introduction of illegitimate information "is unintentional, and facilitated by the data collection, aggregation and preparation process. It is usually subtle and indirect, making it very hard to detect and eliminate." In their words, contests such as KDD-Cup 2008 and the INFORMS 2010 Data Mining Challenge were, as they put it, organised judiciously by capable individuals and still "suffered from severe leakage". Keep that in mind: this is not a story about careless organisers or dishonest entrants.

Leakage
Information about the target that would not be available when the model is used, present in the features or the training examples.
Legitimate
Allowed by the problem's rules as a basis for predicting the target. Each problem defines this for itself.
Giveaway attribute
A feature that predicts the target only because it is produced downstream of it, such as an account number assigned after an account is opened.
External leakage
Leakage through a data source outside what the organiser supplied, such as a public website that holds the test answers.
De-anonymisation
Matching records in a dataset with scrubbed identities back to named individuals or sources, using other data.
Learn-predict separation
Kaufman et al.'s method of building training examples so that, for each target, only legitimate inputs are included, for instance by cutting at a point in time.

Four contests, as the papers describe them

KDD Cup 2008: the patient number

This contest asked entrants to detect cancer from mammography data. Kaufman et al. report, summarising the winners' account, that the "Patient ID" feature, ignored by most competitors, "has tremendous and unexpected predictive power". The winners hypothesised that several clinical studies, institutions or equipment sources were merged to make the dataset, and that some of those sources collected patients "with prior knowledge of the patient's condition". The leak then arose because patient IDs were assigned consecutively for each source: "the merge was done without obfuscating the source". An ID such as 4,017 meant "from the source that mostly sent patients already suspected of cancer", whatever the image showed (the number 4,017 is my illustration, not from the data).

The paper adds a point that matters for fixes. Removing the ID is not enough, because the source leaves other fingerprints. If scanners at the high-cancer site produce slightly different grey levels, a model without the ID "could pick up this leaking signal from the remaining data", and the winners show evidence of this in their report (the paper's wording; I did not read that report).

INFORMS 2008: the hole where the answer used to be

The same year, a contest on pneumonia diagnosis from hospital records embedded the target as a special value in one or more features. The organisers removed those values, "however it was possible to identify traces of such removal": for instance, a record in which all the condition codes were missing, which is what a record looks like when the code that gave the diagnosis was deleted. Hiding the answer is not the same as removing every sign that the answer was there.

INFORMS 2010: the future in the test set

Entrants had to predict stock price movements over a one-hour horizon at five-minute intervals, from intraday trading data, sector and economic data, expert predictions and indices. The surprising result, per Kaufman et al., was that "about 30 participating groups achieved more than 0.9 AUC, with the best model surpassing 0.99 AUC". If real, those models would have made their operators very wealthy. They were not real: although the target stock's identity was hidden and the target was missing from the test file, the test set contained explanatory variables from the future, including highly related stocks in the same sector, and public sources such as Yahoo or Google Finance could identify "at least good candidates" for the target stock and hence reveal the test values. The organisers kept two rankings, one allowing future information and one forbidding it, and "in the end they had to admit that verifying future information was not used was impossible".

IJCNN 2011 Social Network Challenge: the graph was public

This Kaggle-run contest gave entrants a graph with user identities scrubbed: per Narayanan et al., 1,133,547 nodes and 7,237,983 edges, with 8,960 test edges of which half were true edges withheld from the training set and half were false. The task was link prediction: which of the test pairs are real connections? The winning team realised the graph had been crawled from Flickr, made their own crawl, matched nodes, and so were able to de-anonymise 64.7% of the test edge-set. They then combined that with a random forest on standard link-prediction features, trained partly on the de-anonymised test data, and reached a winning test AUC of 0.981. Their abstract says they were able to "effectively game the competition". A footnote records that, once ahead, they asked the organisers how to proceed and offered to withdraw; the organisers found their method acceptable and noted its novelty. The authors argue that while appropriate rules can discourage this, "technical measures to detect gaming are unlikely to be foolproof".

A later example, from a secondary source: Santander, 2018

The Santander Value Prediction Challenge on Kaggle (19 June to 21 August 2018, with a $60,000 prize, according to one participant's public repository) used an anonymised table. According to that participant, people found how the data had been encrypted and recovered a time-series structure, so that "lag data" became a strong predictor, and "top scoring methods must include data leakage information, otherwise the score is too low to compete". The same repository records the author's own results: a best model without the leak scored 1.37273 on the private leaderboard (rank 2,206), and a leak model scored 0.53032 (rank 28), lower being better. These are one person's records of their entries, not Kaggle's statement. I did not read the competition's forum or any statement by the organisers.

Reproducing the mechanism

You can see why such leaks are both powerful and brittle in a short simulation. It imitates KDD Cup 2008 in miniature: 20,000 synthetic patients from four sources of 5,000, numbered consecutively source by source. The sources differ in how many patients are positive (3, 4, 25 and 40 per cent), and each source's scanner adds a different offset to a "grey level" feature (0.0, 0.1, 0.9, 1.1). There are also eight genuine features, with a weak real signal. The model is logistic regression written in NumPy; the ID enters as the training-set positive rate of the patient's ID range (40 ranges). Everything is seeded.

It then scores four feature sets two ways: on a random half of the same data (what a competition test set looks like) and on 4,000 patients from a new hospital with new IDs and a 10 per cent positive rate. Finally it repeats the whole experiment after the organiser shuffles the IDs before release.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
prev = [0.03, 0.04, 0.25, 0.40]            # positive rate of each of 4 data sources
grey = [0.0, 0.1, 0.9, 1.1]                # each source's scanner offset (a hidden trace)

def make(sizes, prevs, greys, start_id=0, shuffle_ids=False):
    ys, X, ids = [], [], []
    for s, (n, p, gv) in enumerate(zip(sizes, prevs, greys)):
        y = (rng.random(n) < p).astype(float)
        f = rng.normal(size=(n, 8)) + 0.25 * y[:, None]          # weak real signal
        gl = gv + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)                           # scanner grey level
        ys.append(y); X.append(np.c_[f, gl]); ids.append(np.arange(n) + start_id); start_id += n
    y, X, ids = np.concatenate(ys), np.vstack(X), np.concatenate(ids)
    if shuffle_ids: ids = rng.permutation(ids)
    return y, X, ids

def auc(y, s):
    r = np.argsort(np.argsort(s)) + 1.0
    n1 = y.sum(); return (r[y == 1].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * (len(y) - n1))

def logreg(X, y, steps=400, lr=0.5):
    mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0) + 1e-9; Z = np.c_[(X - mu) / sd, np.ones(len(X))]
    w = np.zeros(Z.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps): w -= lr * Z.T @ (1/(1+np.exp(-Z @ w)) - y) / len(y)
    return lambda A: np.c_[(A - mu) / sd, np.ones(len(A))] @ w

def id_rate(ids_tr, y_tr, bins=40, total=20_000):                # "target rate of this ID range"
    edges = np.linspace(0, total, bins + 1); b = np.clip(np.digitize(ids_tr, edges) - 1, 0, bins - 1)
    rate = np.array([y_tr[b == k].mean() if (b == k).any() else y_tr.mean() for k in range(bins)])
    return lambda ids: rate[np.clip(np.digitize(ids, edges) - 1, 0, bins - 1)]

def run(name, y, X, ids, y2, X2, ids2):
    tr = rng.random(len(y)) < 0.5; te = ~tr                      # a competition-style random split
    rows = {}
    for label, cols, use_id in [("8 real features only", slice(0, 8), False),
                                ("+ ID range", slice(0, 8), True),
                                ("ID removed, grey level kept", slice(0, 9), False),
                                ("everything", slice(0, 9), True)]:
        f = id_rate(ids[tr], y[tr]) if use_id else None
        feat = lambda A, I: np.c_[A[:, cols], f(I)] if use_id else A[:, cols]
        model = logreg(feat(X[tr], ids[tr]), y[tr])
        rows[label] = (auc(y[te], model(feat(X[te], ids[te]))), auc(y2, model(feat(X2, ids2))))
    print(name)
    for k, (a, b) in rows.items(): print(f"  {k:30s} competition test AUC {a:.3f}   new hospital AUC {b:.3f}")

sizes = [5000] * 4
y2, X2, ids2 = make([4000], [0.10], [0.5], start_id=20_000)       # a new source, new IDs
run("IDs assigned source by source (consecutive):", *make(sizes, prev, grey), y2, X2, ids2)
run("IDs shuffled before release:", *make(sizes, prev, grey, shuffle_ids=True), y2, X2, ids2)
IDs assigned source by source (consecutive):
  8 real features only           competition test AUC 0.692   new hospital AUC 0.692
  + ID range                     competition test AUC 0.835   new hospital AUC 0.694
  ID removed, grey level kept    competition test AUC 0.775   new hospital AUC 0.643
  everything                     competition test AUC 0.836   new hospital AUC 0.691
IDs shuffled before release:
  8 real features only           competition test AUC 0.697   new hospital AUC 0.692
  + ID range                     competition test AUC 0.691   new hospital AUC 0.692
  ID removed, grey level kept    competition test AUC 0.770   new hospital AUC 0.641
  everything                     competition test AUC 0.768   new hospital AUC 0.641

Read the table in four steps. (1) With only the eight genuine features, the model scores 0.692 AUC both on the competition-style split and at the new hospital: that is its honest skill. (2) Adding the ID range lifts the competition AUC to 0.835, a gain of 0.143 from a number with no clinical meaning, and at the new hospital the benefit disappears (0.694): the new IDs carry no information about the new source. (3) Removing the ID is not enough. The grey level, a by-product of the scanner, still reveals the source: competition AUC 0.775, and at the new hospital the model is worse than honest (0.643), because the grey level it learned does not mean the same thing there. This is the case Kaufman et al. warn about. (4) In the second block the organiser has shuffled the IDs before release. The ID feature now adds nothing (0.691 against 0.697), but the grey-level trace remains and still inflates the score (0.770). Shuffling an obvious identifier does not remove the underlying source signal.

Common misconception

"The ID was the leak, so deleting it fixes the dataset." The leak is the correlation between how the row was collected and the label. The ID is the most visible carrier of that correlation. Kaufman et al. say it directly: "usually, where there is one leaking feature, there are more", and "removing the 'obvious' leaks that are detected may exacerbate the effect of undetected ones". A fix is to break the correlation itself, by validating on groups the model has not seen (grouped cross-validation), or by testing on a genuinely new source.

What organisers and researchers recommended

The papers I read give concrete advice, and it is worth separating what they say from what happened afterwards (which I could not verify).

  • Timestamp everything and cut by time. Kaufman et al. propose learn-predict separation: build training examples so that, for each target, only legitimate inputs are included. In the common no-time-machine case, legitimacy tags are timestamps and the separation is a cut in time. They warn that the way databases are usually organised, with records updated in place and a single timestamp per whole record, "is often the source of all evil that allows leakage".
  • Predict the real future. They suggest that organisers can ask entrants to predict targets that do not yet exist ("live prediction"), since nobody can look up the future. For the social-network contest they say the organisers could have asked entrants to predict new edges a month ahead, rather than synthetically removing edges from an existing network, which left traces and an online original to find.
  • Separate "find new data" from "predict with this data". Organisers want both to stop cheating and to welcome clever new sources; the paper says these goals conflict, and suggests one challenge for each.
  • Detect by exploring the data and distrusting great results. They recommend exploratory data analysis, noting that a patient ID being strongly correlated with the target is surprising "if we expect ids to be given with little or no knowledge of the patient's diagnosis", and treating performance that contradicts prior knowledge, such as the INFORMS 2010 AUCs against evidence on market efficiency, as a prompt to check for leaks.
  • Sometimes live with it. They note that for some problems a model trained on leaky data is still better than random in deployment, but that in other cases "an attempt to fix leakage only makes it worse".
  • Rules alone are not enough. Narayanan et al. argue that forbidding outside data and requiring source-code submission "may not eliminate the problem", and that releasing social-network data resilient to de-anonymisation "remains an open question". They note that differential privacy is "essentially the only mathematically sound methodology" for resisting it.

The Santander example suggests the problem did not go away after 2011, although I only have one participant's record of it. That is consistent with the papers' view that leakage is a property of the whole data-making process.

State of play: leakage outside competitions

The same families of leak appear in published science. Kapoor and Narayanan (Patterns, September 2023) surveyed papers that identified leakage and found it in 17 fields, affecting at least 294 papers. Their taxonomy has eight types, from textbook errors (no clean train-test separation, preprocessing or feature selection on the full dataset, duplicates), through illegitimate features such as a proxy for the outcome, to a test set not drawn from the distribution of interest (temporal leakage, non-independent samples such as the same patient in both sets, sampling bias). In their civil-war-prediction case study, errors were found in four of twelve papers, the same four that claimed complex models beat logistic regression; after correction, the AUC gap in one reanalysis fell from 0.14 to 0.01. This is a 2023 source; I did not check later work. They propose "model info sheets", a 21-question template to justify claims about leakage.

Key takeaways

  • A leak is information about the target that the real task would not offer. The papers describe leaks created by consecutive IDs, traces of removed answers, future data and a public copy of the data.
  • Leaks are usually unintentional and arise in data preparation, even when competent people organise the contest.
  • Removing the obvious carrier of a leak (an ID) does not remove the signal behind it; my simulation shows a grey-level trace still inflating the score.
  • An inflated score collapses on a genuinely new source: 0.835 became 0.694 in my simulation.
  • Recommended defences: time-based cuts, predicting the real future, separate tasks for finding data and predicting, exploratory analysis, suspicion of results that contradict prior knowledge, and validation by group.

Check your understanding

In the KDD Cup 2008 breast-cancer data, why might deleting the Patient ID column not make a model leak-free?

  1. Because IDs are needed to join the tables
  2. Because the model can reconstruct the ID from the row number
  3. Because the data source also leaves other traces, such as scanner characteristics, that identify the source and so correlate with the label
  4. Because deleting a column always reduces accuracy
The leak is the link between the collection source and the label. Kaufman et al. suggest equipment differences could carry the same information. The other options are not claims in the paper, and the simulation's grey-level feature shows the effect.

In INFORMS 2010, about 30 groups exceeded 0.9 AUC at predicting one-hour stock movements. What does Kaufman et al.'s guidance say to do with a result like that?

  1. Treat performance that contradicts prior evidence, here about market efficiency, as a reason to check the most informative features for leakage
  2. Accept it, because many teams agreed
  3. Increase the test set size
  4. Switch from AUC to accuracy
The paper says a substantial divergence from expected performance "merits testing the most informative observations the model is based on more closely for legitimacy". Agreement among many teams who all saw the same data proves nothing, and neither a larger test set drawn the same way nor a different metric removes the leak.

In the simulation, adding the ID range raised the competition AUC from 0.692 to 0.835 but left the new-hospital AUC at about 0.69. What explains this?

  1. The new hospital has too few patients
  2. Logistic regression cannot use IDs
  3. The ID feature was removed at test time
  4. The ID encodes which source a patient came from; in the competition split the same sources appear in train and test, but a new hospital's IDs carry no information about its patients
The gain was real on the random split because train and test shared sources. It cannot transfer to a new source. The model did use the ID range in both cases; the information simply was not there at the new hospital.

Narayanan et al. won the IJCNN 2011 Social Network Challenge by matching the anonymised graph to a crawl of Flickr. Which statement do they make about countermeasures?

  1. Banning source-code submission would have prevented it
  2. Rules can discourage de-anonymisation, but technical detection is unlikely to be foolproof, and forbidding outside data and requiring source code may not eliminate the problem
  3. Hiding the node degrees would have been enough
  4. The organisers disqualified them
That is their argued position in the paper. Their footnote records that the organisers found the method acceptable, so the team was not disqualified, and the paper says nothing to suggest that hiding degrees or banning code would suffice.
