Zillow Offers

Zillow's home-buying business needed to forecast prices three to six months ahead to within a few per cent. When prices turned and the backlog grew, the company wrote down hundreds of millions of dollars and wound the business down. The case is about what happens when a model's honest uncertainty is larger than the margin it is asked to protect.

What I could and could not read

I read Zillow Group's own words: the 2 November 2021 earnings release for the third quarter (Form 8-K exhibit on the SEC site), the Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 September 2021, the Form 10-K for 2021 (read in chunks; the large majority of it, but not every page), and CEO Rich Barton's same-day note to shareholders and employees, "Here is what we told shareholders and employees about our decision to wind down Zillow Offers". The Q3 shareholder letter PDF and the earnings-call transcript were not accessible to me, so I use the release and Barton's note instead. The 10-K and 10-Q pages were fetched through a tool that summarises long documents, so I quote only short passages and give figures that the tool reported as located in the text. The mid-December 2021 inventory update comes from a trade-press article (RealTrends), a secondary source. I did not find or read any description of Zillow's pricing model itself, so nothing below says how it worked internally; the toy simulation is my own and does not reproduce it.

The pattern in one sentence

When a business's profit per unit is smaller than the plausible error of the model that prices each unit, the model's accuracy stops being a technical metric and becomes the company's balance sheet, so it has to be sized, stress-tested and capped like any other risk.

Context

Zillow is best known as a listings site and for the Zestimate, its automated home-value estimate. Around 2018 it started Zillow Offers, an iBuying business: it made cash offers for homes, bought the ones sellers accepted, renovated them and resold them. (An iBuyer is a company that buys homes directly using an algorithmic offer, instead of acting as an agent.) The Form 10-Q describes the business as one that "buys and sells homes directly in dozens of markets across the country". Barton's note says the decision to start was taken "three and a half years ago", and that the aim was "to become a market maker, not a market risk-taker".

The key sentence in that note is about the forecast. The plan, Barton wrote, "was underpinned by the need to forecast the price of homes accurately, 3 to 6 months into the future", and the company "used historical data and countless simulations to test this belief". It set unit-economics targets that "required us to stay within +/- 200 basis points of breakeven". A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so the target was to land within plus or minus 2 per cent of the break-even price on each home, on average. On a $390,000 home, 2 per cent is about $7,800, an amount that a few months of price movement can easily exceed. A pricing model for this business is, in effect, a model that must predict the future with a tolerance of a few per cent.

iBuyer
A company that buys homes itself, using an automated valuation to set the offer, and resells them for a margin.
Unit economics
Profit or loss on one unit of the business, here one home, after purchase, holding, renovation, selling costs and resale price.
Model risk
Loss that arises from a model being wrong, being used outside the conditions it was built for, or being trusted too much. Banks formalise it; most ML teams do not.
Net realisable value
The price an asset is expected to sell for, minus the costs of selling it. Inventory worth less than it cost must be written down to this value.
Write-down
An accounting charge that reduces the recorded value of an asset to what it is now thought to be worth.

The mechanism: how a forecast becomes a loss

Consider one home with a true market value today of $400,000. The company's model estimates it at $410,000 (2.5 per cent high) and offers a few per cent below that. If the seller accepts, the company now owns a house whose resale price in three to six months depends on two things the model did not control: the market's direction in that window and the company's own holding time. The per-home margin is therefore the offer discount, minus selling and holding costs, plus or minus the market move, plus or minus the forecast error. With a break-even tolerance of 2 per cent, an error of 5 per cent on a home is a loss of more than twice the whole tolerance. This is an instance of the cost-of-errors framing from the start of the course: the useful measure is not average error but how the error distribution lines up against the margin.

Two features make the problem harder than ordinary forecasting, and both show up in Zillow's own words.

  • The errors are correlated across homes. If the market turns, every home in a city is overpaid together. Barton's phrase is that the error rate was "far more volatile than we ever expected possible" and made the company "look far more like leveraged housing traders than the market makers we set out to be". Independent errors would average out across thousands of homes; a shared market move does not.
  • Capacity turns forecast error into holding time. Barton cites "significant capacity and demand planning challenges, exacerbated by an admittedly difficult labor and supply chain environment", causing "a meaningful backup in our processing of homes". The earnings release says Homes segment revenue came in at about $1.19 billion against a prior outlook of about $1.45 billion, mainly because resale capacity constraints pushed some closings into the fourth quarter. More days on the balance sheet means more exposure to the market, which Barton says would lower return on equity.

There is also a selection effect that a forecast score will not show, and it is worth stating plainly because it is general. The company only owns the homes whose sellers accepted its offers. If sellers know their own home better than the model does, they accept the offers that are too high more often than the offers that are too low. Among homes bought, the model is therefore biased upwards even if it is unbiased among homes offered on. The 10-K describes the write-downs as resulting from "unintentionally purchasing homes at higher prices than our current estimates of future selling prices". I did not see Zillow attribute its losses to this selection effect, and the simulation below is my illustration, not a finding about Zillow.

What happened

The facts below come from Zillow's filings and Barton's note.

  • Growth. The Form 10-Q shows inventory of $3,758,207 thousand at 30 September 2021, up from $491,293 thousand at 31 December 2020. Zillow Offers revenue in the third quarter was about $1.17 billion, up from about $186 million a year earlier (earnings release). For the full year the 10-K reports 15,436 homes sold at an average price of $387.6 thousand, against 5,337 in 2020, and Zillow Offers revenue of $6.0 billion, up $4.3 billion.
  • The decision. On 2 November 2021 Zillow announced it would wind down Zillow Offers and cut about 25 per cent of its workforce over the following quarters. The 10-K says the company made the determination that day and that the reductions would take effect primarily in the first half of 2022.
  • The charges. The release reports an inventory write-down of $304.4 million in the third quarter, because Zillow bought homes in that quarter at prices above its then-current estimates of future selling prices, and expected a further $240 million to $265 million of losses, mainly on homes it expected to buy in the fourth quarter. The 10-K reports a total 2021 inventory write-down of $407.9 million, of which $211.0 million related to homes still in inventory at year end.
  • The tail. A RealTrends article dated 3 December 2021, reporting a company update, says Zillow held about 9,800 purchased homes and was contractually committed to buy about 8,200 more (roughly 18,000 in all) and expected to resell at 5 to 7 per cent below purchase price. That is a secondary source reporting the company's figures.

Barton gave several reasons, not one. Home-price forecasting was "far more volatile than we planned for"; it was "exacerbated by unpredictable black swan-type events"; scale would require "too much equity capital" and create "too much volatility in our earnings and balance sheet"; capacity problems were hurting; and the business had converted "only about 10% of the serious sellers who ask for a Zillow Offer". He also considered an alternative and rejected it: "We could blame this outsized volatility on exogenous, black swan events, tweak our models based on what we have learned, and press on. But based on our experience to date, it would be naive to assume unpredictable price forecasting and disruption events will not happen in the future." That sentence is the model-risk lesson in the company's own words: the question was not whether the next version of the model would be better, but whether the business could survive the next surprise.

A toy you can run

The script below is my own simulation, with assumptions I chose and none of Zillow's data. It creates 100,000 homes with lognormal values (median $350,000). A model estimates each value with a multiplicative error of a given size; the company offers 7 per cent below its estimate; a seller, who knows the value to within 3 per cent, accepts if the offer is at least 8 per cent under what they think the home is worth. The purchase is resold at the true value, adjusted by a market move, less a 5 per cent selling and holding cost (8 per cent in scenario D, for slower resale). The same random numbers are reused in every scenario, so only the stated parameter changes.

"""Toy: an instant home-buying business under forecast error and a turning market (seed 11).
All numbers are my assumptions, not Zillow's. Prices in thousands of dollars."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
N = 100_000
value = rng.lognormal(np.log(350), 0.35, N)                  # true market value today
def run(label, model_sd, market_drift, drift_sd, margin=0.07, cost=0.05, seller_sd=0.03):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(12)                          # same random draws in every scenario
    est = value * np.exp(rng.normal(0, model_sd, N))        # our model's estimate
    offer = est * (1 - margin)                               # we offer a discount to our estimate
    seller_view = value * np.exp(rng.normal(0, seller_sd, N))  # the seller knows their home better than we do
    accept = offer >= seller_view * (1 - 0.08)               # sellers take up to 8% below what they think it is worth, for speed
    drift = rng.normal(market_drift, drift_sd, N)            # change in the market between purchase and resale
    resale = value * (1 + drift) * (1 - cost)                # sale price net of selling costs and holding costs
    pnl = (resale - offer)[accept]
    print("%-34s buys %4.1f%% | mean P&L/home %+6.1fk (%+5.1f%%) | losing %4.1f%% | model over-estimate on bought homes %+5.1f%%" % (
        label, 100*accept.mean(), pnl.mean(), 100*pnl.mean()/offer[accept].mean(), 100*(pnl < 0).mean(),
        100*((est[accept]/value[accept]) - 1).mean()))
run("A: accurate model (sd 3%), flat",     0.03, 0.00, 0.02)
run("B: noisier model (sd 8%), flat",      0.08, 0.00, 0.02)
run("C: noisier model, market -3%",        0.08, -0.03, 0.02)
run("D: C plus slower resale (cost 8%)", 0.08, -0.03, 0.02, cost=0.08)
run("E: accurate model, market -3%", 0.03, -0.03, 0.02)
A: accurate model (sd 3%), flat    buys 60.0% | mean P&L/home   +2.5k ( +0.7%) | losing 40.5% | model over-estimate on bought homes  +1.4%
B: noisier model (sd 8%), flat     buys 55.0% | mean P&L/home  -12.4k ( -3.4%) | losing 69.2% | model over-estimate on bought homes  +5.7%
C: noisier model, market -3%       buys 55.0% | mean P&L/home  -23.0k ( -6.3%) | losing 87.0% | model over-estimate on bought homes  +5.7%
D: C plus slower resale (cost 8%)  buys 55.0% | mean P&L/home  -33.8k ( -9.2%) | losing 96.9% | model over-estimate on bought homes  +5.7%
E: accurate model, market -3%      buys 60.0% | mean P&L/home   -8.1k ( -2.3%) | losing 75.7% | model over-estimate on bought homes  +1.4%

Reading the output. With an accurate model (3 per cent noise) and a flat market the toy business makes about 0.7 per cent per home, and still loses on about 40 per cent of homes. With an 8 per cent noise model it loses 3.4 per cent per home, mostly because of the selection effect: among homes bought, the model over-estimated value by 5.7 per cent on average, although its errors average zero over all homes. A 3 per cent market fall costs another 3 points whatever the model does. Slower resale costs 3 more. The business has no margin to absorb any of them, which is the situation Barton's 200 basis point target implies. The numbers are produced by my assumptions (margin, seller knowledge, costs), so treat them as a demonstration of mechanism, not an estimate of Zillow's economics.

Common misconception

"The model was just bad." The sources I read do not support that, and they do not refute it either: none gives an accuracy figure for Zillow's valuations. What they do say is that the forecast needed a tolerance of a few per cent over three to six months, that realised errors were much more volatile than planned, and that the company judged the business too risky at that volatility. A model can be competent and the business still unsound, if the margin is thinner than the model's honest uncertainty.

What critics and later work said

  • Test the assumption behind the model, not only the model. Barton's note says the belief that prices could be forecast 3 to 6 months ahead was tested with "historical data and countless simulations". Historical data from a calm market is a weak test of a crash. The time-series chapter on backtesting by rolling the origin, and the time-based folds in cross-validation, show how to ask the question "how wrong would we have been in each past regime?". Stress scenarios go further, by simulating markets that never occurred.
  • Monitor the decision, not only the prediction. The model-to-value chapter separates offline error from business outcome and calls for monitoring the second. Here the leading signals (rising holding times, the processing backlog Barton mentions) were operational, not statistical.
  • Size exposure to uncertainty. The speed of growth in the filings (inventory up about 7.7 times in nine months, my arithmetic) meant that any systematic error was multiplied by a much larger book. In engineering terms, limit the blast radius: see blast radius.
  • Be fair to the people involved. Zillow announced a loss on a bet it had explicitly sized and tracked publicly against a stated target, and wound the business down rather than scaling a position it judged unsafe. That candour is why the case can be studied.

Systems connection

Barton's capacity problem is a queueing problem: when the arrival rate of homes approaches the processing rate, the time in the system grows sharply, and here time in the system is exposure to price risk. See queueing theory for engineers and load testing and capacity planning. The design-level view of putting a model inside a product is in Designing with ML & LLMs.

Key takeaways

  • The business required forecasting home prices 3 to 6 months ahead to within a few per cent; per Barton, the observed error was far more volatile than modelled.
  • Reported outcomes: a $304.4 million Q3 2021 write-down, $407.9 million for the year, about 25 per cent of staff affected.
  • Errors that share a cause (the market) do not average out; holding time turns capacity problems into price risk.
  • The set of deals you accept is selected by your counterparties, so the model's error among bought homes can exceed its average error.
  • Treat a model that sets prices as a source of financial risk: stress-test it, cap exposure and monitor the operations around it.

Check your understanding

Zillow's stated target was to stay within plus or minus 200 basis points of breakeven per home. What does that imply for the pricing model?

  1. It needs an average error of exactly zero across all homes
  2. Its forecast uncertainty over 3 to 6 months must be small compared with a margin of a few per cent
  3. It only needs to rank homes correctly, not price them
  4. It must beat the Zestimate on every home
A tolerance of 2 per cent means a typical error of 5 per cent erases the margin. A zero average error is not enough, because large errors on individual homes still lose money, and Barton's note does not mention beating the Zestimate on every home.

In the toy, the model has zero average error over all homes but over-estimates by 5.7 per cent on homes actually bought. Why?

  1. The random seed is unlucky
  2. Lognormal values are skewed
  3. The model is retrained after each purchase
  4. Sellers who know the home accept offers that are too high more often than offers that are too low
The accepted set is selected by the counterparties, a selection effect (a winner's curse). The seed and the skew do not explain it, as the same effect appears when the market is flat.

  1. It would be naive to assume unpredictable price forecasting and disruption events will not happen in the future

  3. The Zestimate was inaccurate
  4. Competitors had better models
Barton says the company could have blamed black swans and tweaked its models, but judged that surprises would recur and the business could not carry the volatility. The other claims appear nowhere in the sources I read.
