Google Flu Trends

In 2009 Google reported that counts of search queries could estimate flu activity a week or more ahead of the CDC. The model then missed the 2009 pandemic and, from 2011 to 2013, ran too high for 100 of 108 weeks. The case reads the original paper and its critics to ask what a model built from millions of candidate features can and cannot promise.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the original paper, Ginsberg et al., "Detecting influenza epidemics using search engine query data" (Nature, 2009), from the journal's HTML page; Lazer, Kennedy, King and Vespignani, "The Parable of Google Flu: Traps in Big Data Analysis" (Science, 14 March 2014), as a full PDF; Olson et al., "Reassessing Google Flu Trends Data for Detection of Seasonal and Pandemic Influenza" (PLoS Computational Biology, October 2013); and Google's own post "The Next Chapter for Flu Trends" (20 August 2015). I did not read the Nature news report of February 2013 (Butler) and know it only as Lazer et al. describe it. The list of 45 queries was never published, so no one outside Google, including me, can check which queries the model used; that is part of the story. All numbers below carry the name of the source they come from. The simulation near the end is my own and uses invented data.

The pattern in one sentence

Searching millions of candidate features for the few that track a short target series will find ones that match by coincidence or by season, and a model built on a platform you do not control can break when the platform changes, so validate through the hardest conditions and keep a simple baseline in the loop.

Context

Every winter, doctors in the United States report the share of visits that are for influenza-like illness (ILI: fever with cough or sore throat). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) compiles these reports into weekly national and regional figures, typically with a reporting lag of one to two weeks, Ginsberg et al. note. A week or two matters in an epidemic. In 2008 a small team at Google, working with the CDC, asked whether the words people type into a search box could give the number sooner. Google's 2015 post says the engineers "first started working on Flu Trends in 2008" and wanted to explore how real-world phenomena "could be modeled using patterns in search queries".

Their paper appeared in Nature in February 2009 and became a standard example of what large data could do. Its abstract claims that because the relative frequency of certain queries is highly correlated with the percentage of physician visits for influenza-like symptoms, the method "can accurately estimate the current level of weekly influenza activity in each region of the United States, with a reporting lag of about one day". This case follows that claim over the next six years.

ILI
Influenza-like illness, the share of doctor visits with flu-like symptoms. It is what the CDC measures and what Google Flu Trends (GFT) tried to predict.
Nowcasting
Estimating the present value of a quantity that is only reported with a delay, from faster indirect signals.
Overfitting
Fitting the quirks of the training sample so closely that the pattern does not carry to new data; see What Learning Is.
Algorithm dynamics
Lazer et al.'s term for changes that engineers make to a service, and that users make in how they use it, which alter the data a model was trained on.
Lagged model
A forecast that uses only the past values of the target itself, here the CDC figure from two weeks earlier, often with seasonal terms.

The mechanism: how GFT was built

The recipe, as the paper describes it, is a feature-selection pipeline. Google aggregated its search logs from 2003 to 2008 into weekly counts for the 50 million most common queries in the United States, kept separately for each of nine US regions. Each count was normalised by the total number of queries that week, so a query's value is a relative frequency. For every one of the 50 million queries the team fitted a one-variable model of the CDC's ILI percentage in each region, ranked queries by how well they fitted, and then asked how many of the top-ranked queries to add together. Cross-validation said the best number was the 45 highest-scoring queries, summed into a single "ILI-related query fraction". A final linear regression, shared by all nine regions, turned that fraction into an estimate. The fit used 128 training weeks from each of nine regions (1,152 points). The model was then checked on 42 further weeks per region, 18 March 2007 to 11 May 2008, which the paper says were excluded from all earlier steps. On those weeks the paper reports a mean correlation of 0.97 with the CDC data (minimum 0.92, maximum 0.99); on the fitting period the mean was 0.90.

Two details in the paper foreshadow what followed. First, the authors report that other top-ranked queries, not in the model, included topics like "high school basketball", "which tend to coincide with influenza season". Second, the method needs no knowledge of influenza: it finds whatever tracks the target. The paper presents that as a strength ("no previous knowledge about influenza"). It is also the weakness. A query that tracks the winter will track flu in a typical winter for the wrong reason. This is the situation exploratory analysis warns about: a strong correlation between two series that share a seasonal cycle says little about a causal link.

The 0.97 on the validation weeks, too, deserves a second look. The validation window was a single flu season. Seasons are the unit that matters (there is one outcome per winter), and a model that has to be right about the size of a peak, the timing of the peak and the behaviour off-season needs to be tested on more than one of each. This is the argument of the cross-validation chapter for validation that matches how the model will be used.

What went wrong

Spring 2009: the pandemic that was not there

In April 2009 a new H1N1 influenza virus began to spread. Olson et al. compared GFT with CDC and New York City surveillance and found that the original model "missed the spring 2009 pandemic wave at all levels", giving incidence estimates 30 to 40 times lower than ILI surveillance. Lazer et al. put the cause plainly: the early version was "part flu detector, part winter detector", and when flu arrived out of season the winter half had nothing to say. They add that the developers' practice of removing queries such as high-school basketball by hand "failed when GFT completely missed the nonseasonal 2009 influenza A-H1N1 pandemic". Google revised the model in 2009 using weeks of pandemic data and looser criteria for selecting queries; Lazer et al. say the revised model "has run ever since", with a few changes announced in October 2013.

2011 to 2013: the slow overestimate

The revised model had the opposite problem. Lazer et al. report that it missed high for 100 of the 108 weeks from 21 August 2011 to 1 September 2013, overshot the actual level in 2011-12 by more than 50 per cent, and in the 2012-13 season gave a reading more than double the CDC's. Olson et al. give the 2012-13 intensity as 268 per cent of ILI surveillance nationally (208 per cent regionally, 296 per cent in New York City) and note that the revised model's overestimate was 2 to 3 times. The overestimate reached the news in February 2013, but as Lazer et al. point out, it had been building for much longer.

Two further findings are about how the error behaved. First, Lazer et al. show it was not random: last week's error predicts this week's (temporal autocorrelation), and its direction varies with the time of year (seasonality). Errors with structure like that are information a model could have used. Second, Olson et al. caution against reading the correlation as a measure of quality: the revised model still had high correlations with surveillance (R2 of at least 0.86 at all levels) while missing peak magnitudes by a factor of two or more, because correlation measures whether two curves move together, not whether their sizes agree.

Why it happened

Lazer et al. offer two explanations; Olson et al. add a third angle. Taken together, they map onto three standard failure modes.

Too many candidates, too few points

Lazer et al.: GFT's method "was to find the best matches among 50 million search terms to fit 1152 data points", and the odds of finding terms that "match the propensity of the flu but are structurally unrelated, and so do not predict the future, were quite high". They call this "big data hubris": the assumption that big data substitute for, rather than supplement, traditional measurement.

The input changed under the model

Lazer et al. call it "algorithm dynamics": Google changed its own search service. It reported adding suggested related searches in June 2011 and, in February 2012, returning possible diagnoses for searches on symptoms like fever and cough. Searches for flu treatments and for telling a cold from flu "track closely with GFT's errors". They stress this is a likely, not proven, explanation. The media panic of 2012-13 cannot alone explain more than two years of high estimates.

A target that is itself a proxy

GFT estimated the CDC's ILI measure, which counts flu-like illness (including other viruses), not confirmed influenza. Olson et al. argue the model lacked the age, location and clinical detail to explain its failures, and that GFT's search terms were undisclosed, which made diagnosis hard.

Notice that the three are different problems with different cures. The first is a selection problem, cured by more data, regularisation and validation across seasons. The second is drift, covered in from model to value, which only monitoring and recalibration cure. The third is about what the label means, the question of framing. Better modelling of one does not fix another.

What the critics and later work said

  • A simple baseline was hard to beat. Lazer et al. show that using the CDC's own data, two weeks old, with seasonal terms, outperformed GFT. They report mean absolute errors in the out-of-sample period of 0.486 for GFT, 0.311 for lagged CDC data and 0.232 for the combination. They note that a 2010 study had already found GFT "not much better than a fairly simple projection forward using already available" CDC data. The lesson of baselines first was available early.
  • Combining beat replacing. The same authors argue that GFT held real signal ("greater value can be obtained by combining GFT with other near-real-time health data") and that by combining it with lagged CDC data and recalibrating dynamically, it "could have largely healed itself". The signal was not the failure; treating it as a replacement for surveillance was.
  • Transparency. The supporting materials, Lazer et al. write, "did not meet emerging community standards", the 45 search terms were never documented, and the sample terms released do not appear strongly related to GFT or CDC data. Without the terms, nobody outside could do the error analysis the problem needed.
  • The end. On 20 August 2015, Google's research blog said that instead of maintaining its own website, it would provide flu and dengue signal data directly to partners including Columbia University's Mailman School of Public Health, Boston Children's Hospital/Harvard and the CDC's Influenza Division, and would keep historical estimates available. The post gives no reason for the change beyond that framing. Olson et al. and later commentary suggest the accuracy problems; I found no Google statement saying so.

A toy you can run

This simulation is my own, with invented data, written to reproduce the structure of the failure, not GFT's numbers. There are seven years of weekly "flu" values (a winter cycle plus noise, and an off-season outbreak in week 190 to 201). There are 3,000 candidate "queries": 2,990 are either noise or winter-seasonal with no connection to flu, and 10 genuinely follow flu. As in the paper, the model ranks all candidates by correlation with flu over the first three years (156 weeks), averages the top 45, and fits a line. In week 260 a "search change" doubles the volume of the real queries. The script also scores a lagged baseline (the flu value two weeks old) and a combination of the two, refit each week using only data at least two weeks old.

"""Toy: a Google-Flu-Trends-style model on synthetic data (seed 3). My sketch, not Google's method or data.
3,000 candidate 'queries': 2,990 are noise or winter-seasonal (think 'high school basketball'); 10 follow flu."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
W, TRAIN = 364, 156                                    # 7 years of weeks: train on 3, test on 4
t = np.arange(W); season = np.cos(2*np.pi*(t - 3) / 52)
flu = np.clip(2.0 + 1.5*season + rng.normal(0, .25, W), .3, None)
flu[190:202] += [.5, 1, 2, 3.5, 5, 6, 5.5, 4, 2.5, 1.5, 1, .5]   # off-season outbreak in test years
X = np.empty((3000, W))
for j in range(2990):                                  # noise (2/3) or winter-seasonal (1/3), unrelated to flu
    X[j] = rng.normal(0, 1, W) if j % 3 else season*rng.uniform(.5, 1.5) + rng.normal(0, .8, W)
for j in range(2990, 3000): X[j] = 0.5*flu + rng.normal(0, .2, W)
X[2990:, 260:] *= 2.0                                  # week 260: a search-box change doubles the real queries' volume
corr = np.array([np.corrcoef(X[j, :TRAIN], flu[:TRAIN])[0, 1] for j in range(3000)])
top = np.argsort(-corr)[:45]
print("top 45 queries by training correlation: %d real flu queries, %d winter noise or chance" % ((top >= 2990).sum(), (top < 2990).sum()))
print("best single-query training correlation: %.3f" % corr[top[0]])
agg = X[top].mean(0)                                   # one feature: mean frequency of the 45 queries
c = np.polyfit(agg[:TRAIN], flu[:TRAIN], 1); gft = np.polyval(c, agg)
lag = np.r_[flu[0], flu[0], flu[:-2]]                  # 'CDC' number, always 2 weeks old
comb = np.full(W, np.nan)
for s in range(TRAIN, W):                              # refit each week using only data at least 2 weeks old
    ix = np.arange(2, s - 1); A = np.c_[gft[ix], lag[ix], np.ones(len(ix))]
    comb[s] = np.array([gft[s], lag[s], 1]) @ np.linalg.lstsq(A, flu[ix], rcond=None)[0]
def mae(p, a, b): return np.abs(p[a:b] - flu[a:b]).mean()
print("\nmean absolute error (flu units)   train   test, before   test, after")
print("                                   0-155     156-259        260-363")
for name, p in (("query model", gft), ("lagged value only", lag), ("combined, refit weekly", comb)):
    tr = "%7.3f" % mae(p, 0, TRAIN) if name != "combined, refit weekly" else "    n/a"
    print("%-32s %s %10.3f %14.3f" % (name, tr, mae(p, TRAIN, 260), mae(p, 260, W)))
print("\noff-season outbreak, weeks 190-201: true peak %.2f, query-model peak %.2f" % (flu[190:202].max(), gft[190:202].max()))
print("after week 260, query model above truth in %d of %d weeks" % ((gft[260:] > flu[260:]).sum(), W-260))
print("mean error after week 260: %+.3f (query), %+.3f (combined)" % ((gft[260:] - flu[260:]).mean(), (comb[260:] - flu[260:]).mean()))
top 45 queries by training correlation: 10 real flu queries, 35 winter noise or chance
best single-query training correlation: 0.945

                                   0-155     156-259        260-363
query model                        0.173      0.475          0.236
lagged value only                  0.360      0.472          0.336
combined, refit weekly               n/a      0.440          0.267

off-season outbreak, weeks 190-201: true peak 7.18, query-model peak 2.50
after week 260, query model above truth in 85 of 104 weeks
mean error after week 260: +0.204 (query), +0.120 (combined)

Reading the output. Only 10 of the 45 chosen queries are real: the other 35 are noise or winter-seasonal series that happened to rank well on three years of data, which is the 50 million versus 1,152 problem in miniature. The query model fits the training years well (error 0.173) and beats the lagged baseline there (0.360), as the paper's validation would have shown. It then misses the off-season outbreak almost completely (its peak is 2.50 against a true 7.18) because the winter series carry the signal, which is the 2009 failure. After the "search change" the query model sits above the truth in 85 of 104 weeks, a mean error of +0.204; the combined model, which learns from recent errors, reduces the bias to +0.120 but does not remove it. Data like this has no single cure; it needs monitoring (watch the sign of the error), a baseline to compare with, and a refit schedule.

Try it yourself

Change [:45] in the script to [:10] and [:200] and see what happens to the training error, the number of real queries chosen and the error after week 260. Then change the first candidate pool from 3,000 to 300 and see whether the off-season failure disappears. Write down which of the paper's three failure modes each change affects.

Common misconception

"Big data failed." What failed was a particular design: a model whose features were selected by the largest search available, validated on one season and run for years with no comparison to the baseline it was meant to improve on. Lazer et al. say the opposite of "abandon it": "there is indeed valuable signal to be extracted", and the right approach is an "all data revolution" that combines new sources with traditional ones.

Systems connection

This is a data-contract failure. A feature that depends on another team's product (search results) has a dependency whose behaviour changes without notice; the equivalent in services is an upstream API that changes semantics. Instrument inputs as you would a dependency: see metrics, and postmortems for turning an incident into a learning. The ML-specific version of this is in Designing with ML & LLMs.

Key takeaways

  • GFT (Ginsberg et al., 2009) chose 45 of 50 million queries using 1,152 training points; it validated at mean correlation 0.97 on 42 weeks.
  • It missed the 2009 off-season H1N1 wave (30 to 40 times too low in Olson et al.), then ran high for 100 of 108 weeks from August 2011 (Lazer et al.).
  • The causes: matching by chance and by season, platform changes (algorithm dynamics), and a target that is itself a proxy.
  • A two-week-old CDC number plus seasonality beat GFT (MAE 0.311 against 0.486); the combination did best (0.232).
  • Keep a baseline in the loop, monitor the sign of the error, and treat third-party data as a dependency that can change.

Check your understanding

The original GFT paper found the best fit using the top 45 of 50 million candidate queries, with 1,152 training points. What is the main statistical risk this design carries?

  1. The queries were too long to count
  2. The CDC data was too accurate
  3. With so many candidates, some match the target by chance or by shared seasonality, without any causal link to flu
  4. The regression had too many coefficients
The final model had one variable, so coefficient count is not the problem. The risk is the selection among 50 million: Lazer et al. note the odds of finding unrelated terms that match were high, and the authors themselves removed basketball queries by hand.

GFT scored a mean correlation of 0.97 on 42 held-out weeks, yet missed the spring 2009 H1N1 wave. Which explanation fits?

  1. The validation weeks were one ordinary season, so the test never contained an off-season outbreak
  2. A correlation of 0.97 is always unreliable
  3. The validation weeks leaked the answer
  4. The model had too few training weeks to fit at all
The validation window was a single mild season, and the model leaned on winter-seasonal queries, so it had nothing to say about flu out of season. Option 2 overstates the point; the weeks were not leaked, the test was just not hard enough.

Lazer et al. argue that "algorithm dynamics" may explain the 2011 to 2013 overestimate. What does the term mean?

  1. The machine-learning algorithm slowly converged
  2. Flu viruses mutate quickly
  3. CDC staff changed how they report
  4. Changes to the search service, and to how users use it, altered the data that the model was trained on
The authors point to Google adding suggested searches (June 2011) and diagnoses in results (February 2012). They call it likely, not proven. The other options describe different effects.

In Lazer et al.'s comparison, mean absolute error was 0.486 for GFT, 0.311 for lagged CDC data and 0.232 for GFT combined with lagged CDC data. What is the best conclusion?

  1. GFT had no signal and should be discarded
  2. The signal in GFT was real but is better used alongside, not instead of, the traditional data
  3. Lagged data is always better than search data
  4. The combination is guaranteed to stay accurate as the platform changes
The combination beat both. Option 1 contradicts it. Nothing guarantees stability: the toy shows that a combined model reduces but does not remove drift bias.
