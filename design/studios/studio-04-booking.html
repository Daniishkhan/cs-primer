Design a Hotel Booking System

Design a system that sells hotel rooms by the night across a million properties. It must never sell the same room night twice, except when the business tells it to, search must stay fast under hundreds of searches per booking, and other websites sell the same rooms at the same time.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 45 to 60 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the framework: clarify, estimate, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, failure modes, trade-offs, and a spoken summary. A rubric at the end separates mid-level from senior signals. All numbers on this page are assumptions to reason with, not measurements.

📐 The brief

An interviewer says: "Design a hotel booking system like Booking.com. Travellers search for hotels by city and dates, pick a room type, and book it. Hotels manage their rooms and prices, and also sell them on other websites."

  • The cardinal sin is selling the same room night twice, but the business also wants to oversell a little, because some guests never turn up.
  • Browsing is hundreds of times more common than booking. A stay of three nights touches three separate inventory counts, and they must change together.
  • Builds on ticketing (holds and the waiting room), payments, isolation levels and caching.
  • You have 45 to 60 minutes. Follow the framework.

  • 1.8 billion inventory rows, about 73 GB. The table is small enough to fit one large disk, but not one machine's write and read capacity with replicas and availability targets: shard it by hotel_id so a whole stay is one shard and one transaction.
  • 23 bookings per second on average, 230 at a 10x peak, 58 row updates per second (a 2.5 night stay updates 2.5 rows). The write load is tiny. That is the point: booking is a correctness problem, not a throughput problem, so use a boring relational database and spend your effort on invariants.
  • 11,600 searches per second on average, 35,000 at peak, 500 times the writes. Serving those as range queries on the inventory database would put 35,000 multi-night scans per second on the system that must stay correct. Searches go to a separate, derived index and cache.
  • Staleness is a cliff, not a slope. A hot room type taking 0.5 bookings per minute with one room left has a 22% chance of being sold out within a 30-second cache lifetime and 63% within 2 minutes; with 10 rooms left the chance is essentially zero. So cached availability is wrong exactly when it matters, for the last rooms. Show "only 1 left" loosely from the cache, and confirm exactly at the hold step.
  • Overbooking is a calculation. With 100 rooms and 8% cancellations or no-shows, selling 103 means a 0.9% chance that someone must be walked (relocated), expected 0.011 guests per night; selling 106 raises that to 14% and 0.24 guests per night; 110 is hopeless. The business picks the rate where the expected cost of walking guests is below the revenue from the extra rooms. The design supports the knob and logs every walk.

-- channels (inbound and outbound)
POST /channels/{ch}/reservations   {external_id, hotel, room_type, nights, ...}   # dedupe on (ch, external_id)
POST /channels/{ch}/ari-updates    {hotel, room_type, night, available, rate, version}   # outbound push
  • Search results are hints, offers are quotes, holds are promises. Only a hold decrements inventory. The response to a hold says exactly what is guaranteed: this room type, this price, until expires_at.
  • Idempotency keys on holds, bookings and cancels (payments chapter): a mobile client retries on a bad connection and must not take two rooms or charge twice.
  • Booking returns 202 and a state because payment can be slow or ambiguous; the client polls or gets a push. Never present "confirmed" until payment has been captured or authorised.
  • Inbound channel reservations carry an external id, and the platform treats a repeat of the same id as the same reservation, since channel integrations resend.
  • Outbound updates carry a version per (hotel, room_type, night) so a receiver can drop an older update that arrives late.

  • Sharding by hotel_id is the central decision: a stay of several nights, its hold, its booking and its outbox event are all on one shard, so "update all nights or none" is a single local transaction. If you sharded by date, a three-night stay would span shards and need distributed commit (atomic commit).
  • Available = total + overbook allowance - sold - held. Keeping held as a separate column from sold lets the reaper release holds without touching confirmed bookings, and lets reports separate demand from sales.
  • The alternative model is a row per physical room per night with a unique constraint on (room_id, night). It fits when guests choose a specific room, but it makes search harder (you must count free rooms) and cannot express overbooking naturally; we use counts, and assign rooms at check-in.

5High-level architecture

Draw the system. Which path must be strongly consistent, which can be stale, and where do the external channels attach?

Compare with a worked answer

Booking a stay:

  1. Search reads the cached result page or the availability index and returns hotels with approximate stock and price.
  2. Hold: the booking service runs one transaction on the hotel's shard: for each night, a conditional update that increments held only if capacity remains; all nights succeed or the transaction rolls back. It writes a hold with an expiry and an outbox event, and replies with a price lock.
  3. Book: the guest pays; the payment service authorises the card with an idempotency key. On success the booking transaction converts the hold: held - 1, sold + 1 per night, booking state confirmed.
  4. Changes flow through the outbox to the availability index (seconds) and to channel sync, which pushes the new count to other sites.

6Deep dive 1: no double bookings

Two guests click 'book' for the last double room on the same night at the same millisecond. Show the failing design, then three designs that work. What changes for a three-night stay?

Compare with a worked answer

The classic bug is read, check, write: two requests both read "1 left", both pass the check, both write. At read committed the second transaction is not blocked by the first one’s read, so both pass the check (a lost update; snapshot isolation would catch a plain same-row write but not a check spread over several rows, which is write skew; isolation levels). The runnable model below replays the worst interleaving, then shows the fix: a conditional update whose guard and change are one atomic statement, wrapped around all nights of the stay.

"""Three ways to book 3 nights, run on SQLite (stdlib). Same data, same 12 buyers for the last 2 rooms."""
import sqlite3, threading

def fresh():
    db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:", check_same_thread=False, isolation_level=None)
    db.execute("CREATE TABLE inv(hotel INT, rtype TEXT, night INT, total INT, sold INT, PRIMARY KEY(hotel,rtype,night))")
    db.executemany("INSERT INTO inv VALUES(1,'dbl',?,10,8)", [(n,) for n in range(1, 8)])   # 2 rooms left each night
    db.execute("CREATE TABLE booking(id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, hotel INT, rtype TEXT, first_night INT, nights INT)")
    return db

def left(db, n): return db.execute("SELECT total-sold FROM inv WHERE night=?", (n,)).fetchone()[0]

# 1. read-then-write, replayed as the worst interleaving: every buyer reads before anyone writes
db, lock = fresh(), threading.Lock()
seen = [left(db, 2) for _ in range(12)]                       # 12 buyers all see "2 left"
ok = 0
for s in seen:
    if s > 0: db.execute("UPDATE inv SET sold=sold+1 WHERE night=2"); ok += 1
print(f"1. read then write       : confirmed {ok} buyers for 2 rooms; sold={db.execute('SELECT sold FROM inv WHERE night=2').fetchone()[0]} / total 10")

# 2. conditional update, all nights in one transaction (all-or-nothing)
def book(db, first, nights):
    db.execute("BEGIN IMMEDIATE")
    for n in range(first, first + nights):
        c = db.execute("UPDATE inv SET sold=sold+1 WHERE hotel=1 AND rtype='dbl' AND night=? AND sold<total", (n,))
        if c.rowcount == 0:
            db.execute("ROLLBACK"); return False
    db.execute("INSERT INTO booking(hotel,rtype,first_night,nights) VALUES(1,'dbl',?,?)", (first, nights))
    db.execute("COMMIT"); return True

db = fresh()
res = [book(db, 1, 3) for _ in range(12)]
print(f"2. conditional UPDATE    : {sum(res)} booked, {res.count(False)} refused; nights 1-3 sold = {[db.execute('SELECT sold FROM inv WHERE night=?',(n,)).fetchone()[0] for n in (1,2,3)]}")

# 3. a failure in the middle of a stay leaves nothing behind: night 3 is full, nights 1-2 roll back
db = fresh(); db.execute("UPDATE inv SET sold=10 WHERE night=3")
r = book(db, 1, 3)
print(f"3. night 3 sold out      : booked={r}; nights 1-2 sold still {[db.execute('SELECT sold FROM inv WHERE night=?',(n,)).fetchone()[0] for n in (1,2)]} (rolled back)")

# 4. overbooking as policy: the same guard with a per-type limit above physical capacity
db = fresh(); db.execute("ALTER TABLE inv ADD COLUMN limit_ INT"); db.execute("UPDATE inv SET limit_=total+2")
def book_over(db, first, nights):
    db.execute("BEGIN IMMEDIATE")
    for n in range(first, first + nights):
        if db.execute("UPDATE inv SET sold=sold+1 WHERE night=? AND sold<limit_", (n,)).rowcount == 0:
            db.execute("ROLLBACK"); return False
    db.execute("COMMIT"); return True
res = [book_over(db, 1, 3) for _ in range(12)]
print(f"4. policy limit total+2  : {sum(res)} booked: 2 free rooms + 2 of headroom (sold={db.execute('SELECT sold FROM inv WHERE night=1').fetchone()[0]} vs 10 rooms): the guard is a business number, not a bug")
1. read then write       : confirmed 12 buyers for 2 rooms; sold=20 / total 10
2. conditional UPDATE    : 2 booked, 10 refused; nights 1-3 sold = [10, 10, 10]
3. night 3 sold out      : booked=False; nights 1-2 sold still [8, 8] (rolled back)
4. policy limit total+2  : 4 booked: 2 free rooms + 2 of headroom (sold=12 vs 10 rooms): the guard is a business number, not a bug

Read the four lines. (1) Twelve buyers all saw "2 left"; the naive code confirmed all twelve and the sold count reached 20 against 10 rooms. (2) The conditional UPDATE ... WHERE sold < total let exactly two succeed. Each statement locks the row, evaluates the guard against the latest value and changes it, so the check and the write cannot be separated. The stay is one transaction; the nights are updated in ascending order so two stays never deadlock by locking in opposite orders. (3) If night 3 is sold out, the earlier nights roll back and nothing is left half done. (4) Overbooking is the same guard with a different number: limit = total + 2.

  • Put the invariant in the database, the only component that sees every write. A cache, a queue or a lock service may help performance but must not be the thing that prevents double booking. A CHECK (sold + held <= limit) constraint is a cheap second line of defence.
  • Hot hotels. A hotel in a city with a huge event has thousands of people on the same room type and nights. Row-level contention limits you to roughly a thousand updates per second per row, and the transactions fail fast with a clear "sold out" rather than queueing. For the extreme case, a waiting room in front of the booking step admits people at a safe rate, as in the ticketing chapter.
  • Cross-shard stays (a trip across several hotels) are a saga, not a transaction: hold each hotel independently, pay once, and compensate by releasing holds if one fails (sagas).
  • Retries are safe because the hold carries an idempotency key and the conditional update would otherwise count twice: dedupe at the hold insert, in the same transaction as the counters.

7Deep dive 2: holds with expiry, payment and overbooking

A guest picks a room and goes to enter a card. Do you reserve it for them? For how long, and what happens if payment succeeds after the hold has expired? How do you decide how much to overbook?

Compare with a worked answer

Without holds, the guest enters a card for a room that sells out under them. A hold converts "probably available" into "yours for ten minutes". The mechanics are the ones in the ticketing chapter (a TTL and a reaper), adapted to nights. Here the hold increments held on each night's row inside the same guarded transaction as deep dive 1.

Hold designHowProsCons
No holdCheck availability at payment timeSimpleGuest pays, then hears 'sold out'; support tickets

  • Length: 10 to 15 minutes, extended once while a payment is in flight. The reaper runs every few seconds with an index on expires_at; a late release of a few seconds costs almost nothing here (a room night is not a concert seat), so a sweep every 5 seconds is fine.
  • Release is idempotent: flip the hold from active to expired with a compare-and-set, and only the transaction that changed the row decrements held. Running the reaper twice is harmless.
  • The race: payment succeeds after the hold expired. Authorise before converting; at conversion, try to take the inventory again atomically. If rooms remain, confirm. If not, void the authorisation and tell the guest the room is gone, with alternatives. Never capture first and hope; an unknown payment outcome is resolved by reconciliation (payments, sagas: compensate with a void).
  • Price lock: a hold records the quoted price and the cancellation policy id; the booking uses the snapshot, not today's price.
  • Overbooking is a business policy, implemented as a number. The estimates show selling 103 rooms of 100 gives a 0.9% chance of walking someone; the platform stores overbook_pct per hotel and room type, defaulting to zero, and the guard in deep dive 1 uses total + allowance. Walks (relocating a guest to an equivalent hotel with compensation) are a first-class workflow with a cost that feeds back into the chosen percentage. Do not overbook sold-out event nights, premium room types, or when forecast no-shows are low. Regulations and brand policies cap it.

8Deep dive 3: search over availability

35,000 searches per second ask 'hotels in Lisbon for these three nights'. Why can't they hit the inventory database? Design the index and the cache, and say how wrong they are allowed to be.

Compare with a worked answer

Search is a different workload on a different, derived dataset. The question "which of 3,000 Lisbon hotels have a free room for every night from 12 to 15 June?" must be answered in tens of milliseconds, 35,000 times per second.

  • Partition the index by city or geohash (partitioning), so a query touches one or a few shards, ANDs masks for each candidate hotel, applies filters (guests, price, rating) and returns the top results by a ranking score. 3,000 hotels per city and 3 nights is microseconds of bit operations.
  • Keep the index fresh from the log. Every inventory change writes an outbox row in the same transaction (CDC and the outbox); a consumer updates the mask bit and the price. Typical lag is seconds, and the whole index can be rebuilt from the inventory table if corrupted.
  • Cache result pages with a short TTL (5 to 30 seconds) keyed by city, dates, guests and filters (caching, distributed cache). Coalesce concurrent misses so a conference week does not stampede the index. Cache hit rates are high because many people search the same popular cities and dates.
  • How wrong can it be? The estimate shows the cliff: with one room left, a 30 s cache is stale 22% of the time. So: (1) results show coarse stock ("few left") not exact numbers; (2) the offers call re-checks the hotel exactly from the database for the chosen hotel only; (3) the hold step is the real arbiter, and "just sold out" is handled gracefully with alternatives; (4) price shown in search is "from", the binding price is quoted at the hold.
  • Never let search stale data cause an oversell. It can only cause a disappointed click. That is the whole contract between the two paths.
  • Ranking and personalisation sit on top of the candidate set and are out of scope here; they only need the candidates and their prices.

9Deep dive 4: channel managers, cancellations and the money

The hotel sells the same room on three other websites through a channel manager. How do you keep inventory consistent with systems you do not control? How does a cancellation release rooms and refund money correctly?

Compare with a worked answer

Hotels distribute inventory through channel managers and property management systems. Each partner has its own copy of availability; there is no shared transaction. This is the same problem as any distributed inventory, with an unreliable network and a legal obligation to honour confirmed reservations.

ModelHowProsCons
AllotmentGive each channel a fixed quota of roomsNo double booking by constructionUnsold rooms stranded in one channel; hotels dislike it
Real-time authorityOne system (the PMS or us) owns the count; every sale calls it synchronouslyExactLatency and an availability dependency on every partner; many partners cannot do it
Shared pool, eventual sync, with buffer (typical)Each system sells from its copy and pushes changes; reconciliation fixes drift; overbooking allowance absorbs racesWorks with poor partners; high sell-throughOccasional oversell, handled by a walk workflow
  • Outbound: every inventory change is an outbox event (same transaction); channel sync turns it into a per-channel update (availability, rate, restrictions) with a queue per channel and a rate limit that respects that channel's API. Updates carry the version; the sender coalesces rapid changes to the latest value and retries with backoff; the receiver (or we, when we are the receiver) ignores an update older than what it has.
  • Inbound: a channel reservation arrives as a webhook or by polling. Deduplicate on (channel, external_id), then apply it through the same guarded update. If the guard fails, the channel already confirmed the guest, so we must accept it and record a negative margin: availability goes below the limit, we alert, and the walk workflow starts. The overbooking allowance is the shock absorber for exactly these races.
  • Reconciliation: nightly (and on demand) compare our counts with each channel's reported availability for the next 365 nights; push corrections; alert if drift is more than a threshold for a hotel. Detecting drift needs a metric: fraction of hotel-nights where a partner disagrees (events and dashboards).
  • Cancellation is a state machine with a compare-and-set, not a delete: confirmed -> cancelled succeeds for exactly one request (retries see the same result). In the same transaction, for each night in booking_night, decrement sold; write an outbox event so the index and channels learn that rooms came back. The refund runs afterwards as a step with its own idempotency key, because it talks to the provider and can fail independently (saga).
  • Which policy applies? The one in the booking's policy_snapshot at the time of booking, and the deadline evaluated in the hotel's local time zone. Free cancellation until 18:00 on the day before check-in is a classic source of bugs; store the deadline as an absolute instant with its zone.
  • Modification is hold-new then release-old in one transaction (or cancel plus rebook with a guard), never release-then-take, which can lose the room to someone else in between.
  • Money: authorise at booking, capture at the policy time or check-in; a timeout from the provider means an unknown outcome and goes to reconciliation, not to a retry that might charge twice (payments); keep amounts in minor units with an explicit currency.

  • Group bookings (10 rooms): the same guarded update with qty = 10 per night; larger transactions and a higher chance of failing the guard; consider partial offers.
  • Global audience and regions: a hotel's shard lives in one home region; searches are served from regional index copies; bookings route to the home region (extra latency for far travellers, accepted for correctness).   • Flash promotions: a sudden surge on a few hotels is the ticketing problem: waiting room, per-hotel admission rate and fairness (chapter 15).

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"This is two systems with different guarantees. Booking is tiny but must be exactly right: about 23 bookings a second, 230 at a peak, and 1.8 billion inventory rows, one per hotel, room type and night. Search is 500 times bigger, around 11,600 a second and 35,000 at peak, and can be a few seconds stale. For booking I keep a count per room type per night, shard the database by hotel so a whole stay is one transaction, and enforce 'no double booking' with a guarded conditional update, sold plus held below the limit, over all nights in order. If any night fails, everything rolls back. I'd show the broken read-check-write version and why a distributed lock around it is not a fix, because the database is the only place that sees every write. A hold increments a held counter with an expiry and a reaper, the same pattern as ticketing, with a price lock; when payment succeeds after the hold expired I retake inventory atomically or void the authorisation. Overbooking is just the guard's limit as a business number: at 8% cancellations, selling 103 rooms of 100 has under a 1% chance of walking anyone. Search reads a derived index, a bitmask per hotel with 365 bits, partitioned by city, fed by the outbox and CDC, plus a short-TTL cache; the staleness maths says the last room is wrong 22% of the time at 30 seconds, so search shows coarse stock and the hold is the arbiter. Channels are external replicas: versioned outbound updates, deduplicated inbound reservations that we must accept even when the guard fails, a walk workflow, and nightly reconciliation. Cancellation is a compare-and-set on the booking state that returns nights and starts an idempotent refund. I'd alert on the invariant audit, hold age and index lag. If room-level choice or flash promotions came in, I'd add reservation rows and a waiting room."

Notice the shape: two paths → invariant in the database → hold and policy → derived search → external replicas → state machines.

Key takeaways

  • Booking is a correctness problem and search is a scale problem. Build them as two systems with two guarantees.
  • Put the invariant in the database: a guarded conditional update across all nights of a stay, in one transaction on one shard.
  • Shard by hotel so a stay never needs a distributed transaction.
  • Holds turn 'probably available' into a promise; expiry must be idempotent and payment after expiry must be compensated.
  • Overbooking is a number in the guard and a cost in the business model. Quantify it.
  • Search reads a derived index and cache that may be stale; the hold step is the arbiter.
  • External channels are replicas you do not control: version, dedupe, reconcile, and keep a buffer for races.
