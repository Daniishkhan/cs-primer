Design Photo Sharing (Instagram)

Design a service where people upload photos, follow each other and scroll a feed. Writes are modest and reads are enormous; the interesting parts are the upload pipeline, the CDN, a feed that survives accounts with a hundred million followers, and counters that survive a viral post.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 45 to 60 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the framework: clarify, estimate, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, failure modes, trade-offs, and a spoken summary. A rubric at the end separates mid-level from senior signals. All numbers on this page are assumptions to reason with, not measurements.

📐 The brief

An interviewer says: "Design a photo sharing app like Instagram. Users post photos, follow other users, see a feed of photos from people they follow, like and comment, and search for people and hashtags."

  • Some accounts have hundreds of millions of followers. When one of them posts, millions of people open the app within minutes.
  • Photos come from phones: 2 to 5 MB originals, shown at screen sizes from a thumbnail to a full-screen image.
  • Builds on the news feed chapter, object storage, video streaming (for the CDN) and the Instagram IDs case.
  • You have 45 to 60 minutes. Follow the framework.

Estimate uploads, stored bytes per photo and per day, image views and edge bandwidth, feed reads, and the like rate on a viral post. Which numbers force architectural choices?

  • Writes are small, reads are huge. About 350 uploads per second on average and 1,000 at peak, against 1.4 million image views per second at peak. One service per path, and the read path wins every argument about cost.
  • Variants are cheap relative to originals: four resized versions add 283 KB to a 3 MB original, about 9%. Store them eagerly instead of resizing on every request; the CPU saved pays for the disk many times over.
  • 98 TB per day, about 36 PB per year before replication, and 54 PB per year with erasure coding. This is cold-heavy storage with a small hot working set: new photos are viewed intensely for days, then rarely. Tier on age (object storage).
  • Edge bandwidth of roughly 670 Gbit/s at peak. No origin can serve that, so a CDN is a requirement, not an optimisation. The CDN hit ratio is the most important number in the system: at 95% the origin still has to deliver 33 Gbit/s; at 99%, 6.7 Gbit/s.
  • 23,000 feed loads per second, half a million post hydrations per second. Each feed page names 20 posts; the post bodies, authors and counts must come from caches, not from a database per post.
  • One viral post can receive 16,000 likes per second. A single row can absorb about a thousand updates per second (it is held for about a millisecond per update), so one counter fails by an order of magnitude. Sixteen shards bring the busiest to about 1,070 per second, right at that line; 64 gives comfortable headroom.

3API

Define the key endpoints. How does a photo get from a phone to storage without passing through your application servers? Which calls must be idempotent?

Compare with a worked answer 
POST /photos            {content_type: "image/jpeg", bytes: 3145728, sha256}
                        -> {photo_id, upload_url (presigned PUT, 15 min), headers}
PUT  {upload_url}       body=original bytes            # straight to object storage
POST /posts             {photo_id, caption, idempotency_key}
                        -> {post_id, state: "processing"|"ready"}
GET  /feed              ?cursor=..&limit=20  -> {posts:[{post_id, author, image:{...}, like_count, ...}], next_cursor}
GET  /posts/{id}        GET /users/{id}/posts?cursor=
PUT  /posts/{id}/likes/me     DELETE /posts/{id}/likes/me          # idempotent by (post, user)
POST /posts/{id}/comments     {text, idempotency_key}   GET .../comments?cursor=
GET  /search/users?q=ali       GET /tags/{tag}/posts?sort=recent|top&cursor=
GET  https://img.cdn/{photo_id}/{variant}.jpg          # variant: t150 s320 m640 l1080
  • Presigned upload keeps 3 MB bodies off the app tier. At 1,000 uploads per second that is 3 GB/s of traffic the API fleet would otherwise proxy. The API only issues a short-lived URL scoped to one object key and size, like the pattern in the object storage chapter.
  • Two steps, upload then create the post. A phone may upload and never post; unreferenced objects are deleted by a lifecycle rule after 24 hours. Posting an unknown or unfinished photo_id is refused.
  • Likes are PUT and DELETE of a resource, so repeating the call changes nothing. The mobile client will retry on a flaky network; a POST that increments would double count.
  • Image URLs are immutable and built from ids (photo_id plus variant), so the CDN can cache them for a year. A changed image is a new id.
  • Cursors, not offsets, on every list; the feed cursor is a post id (see deep dive 2), so a page boundary does not move as new posts arrive.

  • IDs. A 64-bit id is time in the high bits, then a shard or machine id, then a sequence (the layout from the case and chapter 7). Sorting by id is sorting by time, no extra column or index is needed for "newest first", and a cursor is just "ids below X".
  • Post shard key is the author. "All posts by user" is one shard; the cost is that one celebrity's posts live on one shard. That is fine for storage (they post a few times a day); the read heat is handled by caches.
  • The like table is sharded by post, the counter is not the like table. Keeping them separate lets the counter be sharded and the table remain the source of truth for idempotency.

5High-level architecture

Draw the system. Which components are on the upload path, which on the view path, and which work asynchronously?

Compare with a worked answer

Posting a photo:

  1. The app asks for an upload URL; the upload service checks quota and rate limit (rate limiter) and returns a presigned PUT.
  2. The phone uploads the original straight to object storage, then calls POST /posts. The post row is written with state processing and an event goes to the log.
  3. A worker validates and decodes the image in a sandbox, strips location metadata, writes the four variants, and marks the post ready. Then fan-out workers insert the post id into followers' timelines (small accounts) or leave it to be merged at read time (celebrities).
  4. The search worker indexes the hashtags and the caption.

Viewing the feed: the feed service reads the viewer's timeline ids, merges in recent posts from followed celebrities, hydrates posts, authors and counts from caches, and returns 20 items with image URLs. The phone then fetches each image variant from the CDN; the CDN fetches from object storage on a miss.

6Deep dive 1: the upload pipeline, image variants and the CDN

Why presign? What do you generate, when, and what could go wrong when you decode strangers' images? How do you keep the CDN hit ratio above 99%?

Compare with a worked answer

Direct upload with a presigned URL moves the bytes off the application tier. The server's job is authorisation, quota and recording intent; the object store's job is bytes and durability.

Check the magic bytes, cap pixel dimensions (a small file can decode to billions of pixels, a decompression bomb), decode in an isolated worker with memory and time limits (the same isolation problem as the code judge studio), then re-encode rather than serve the original, which also strips embedded scripts and EXIF location (injection and data protection).

  • Format by capability. Serve AVIF or WebP when the client says it can (Accept header), JPEG otherwise. That multiplies variants by the formats (4 x 3), still small beside the originals. Cache keys must include the format.
  • CDN hit ratio from design, not luck: immutable URLs with a one-year cache lifetime; a small whitelist of variants; an origin shield tier so only one CDN node per region goes to the origin on a miss and misses for one object are coalesced.   • Hot photos need no special case at the CDN: a viral image is the best possible cache workload. The special case is in the application caches (deep dive 3).

7Deep dive 2: the feed, IDs and celebrity accounts

A user with 10 million followers posts. What does a fan-out on write do to your system? Where would you draw the line between push and pull, and what does the reader do at read time?

Compare with a worked answer

This is the news feed chapter applied; here are the numbers for this product. Average account: 200 followers. Posts per day: 30 million. Fan-out on write for everyone is 30M x 200 = 6 billion timeline inserts per day, 69,000 per second on average and about 210,000 at peak. That is a healthy cache workload. Now one celebrity with 10 million followers posts once: 10 million inserts. At 100,000 inserts per second of worker capacity the last follower gets the post 100 seconds late, and the burst starves every other account's fan-out queue behind it (head-of-line blocking).

StrategyWrite costRead costVerdict
Fan-out on write (push) for allFollowers per post; celebrity post is millions of insertsOne cache readGreat for ordinary accounts, breaks on celebrities
Fan-out on read (pull) for allOne insert per postQuery every followed account's recent posts and merge: hundreds of lookups per feed load at 23,000 loads/sToo much read work; slow tail latency
Hybrid (our choice)Push for accounts below a follower threshold (for example 100,000); none for aboveOne timeline read plus merge of recent posts from the few celebrities the viewer followsBounded work on both paths
  • The threshold is a dial. Choose it so the push queue stays under a latency target. Accounts crossing the line are flipped to pull; recent posts remain in followers' timelines, and new ones are merged on read.
  • Read-time merge is cheap because a viewer follows only a handful of celebrities: fetch each one's last N post ids from a hot cache (they are the same keys for millions of viewers, so they live in memory on many replicas), merge by post id, hydrate the winners. IDs sort by time (see the data model, case), so merging is comparing integers and the cursor is an id.
  • Prioritise the fan-out queues. Separate queues per tier so a giant job cannot delay normal ones; chunk a big fan-out into jobs of 10,000 followers so it is interruptible; prefer active users first (users who opened the app in the last 7 days get inserted first, dormant ones get their timeline rebuilt on next open). This is the inactive users idea.
  • Hot reads on celebrity posts. Millions open the same post within minutes: post metadata and counts are cached at several layers, with a short TTL (1 to 5 seconds) at the edge and replicated copies in the application cache so no single cache node holds a hot key (hot-key replication, cache chapter); the Twitter timelines case works through the push-versus-pull split for large accounts.
  • Deletes and privacy are read-time checks. A deleted or newly private post may still sit in thousands of timelines. Hydration drops anything that fails a state check; cleaning the timelines is a background job.

8Deep dive 3: likes, comments and hot-post counters

A photo gets 1 million likes in a minute. What breaks in a naive design? Compare four counter designs, and say what is source of truth and what is a derived number.

Compare with a worked answer

Two facts have different consistency needs. Whether I liked this post must be exact and idempotent: a unique row (post_id, user_id). How many likes it has is a number humans read approximately ("1.2M"); it can lag a few seconds, but it must not lose updates or hot-spot one row. The estimate above showed 16,667 likes per second on one post against a single-row ceiling near 1,000 per second.

DesignWrite pathRead pathHot post behaviour
COUNT(*) on readInsert like rowCount rowsReads scan millions of rows; unusable
Single counter rowUPDATE count = count + 1Read one rowRow lock serialises at about 1,000/s; the post's likes time out
Sharded counter (our choice)Pick a random sub-counter of N and increment it; increment is atomic in the in-memory storeSum N sub-counters, cache the sum for a second or twoLoad per sub-counter is rate / N; the demo shows 16 shards bring the busiest to about 1,070/s and 64 to 300/s
Async aggregationAppend like events to a log; a consumer batches them every second into one increment per postRead the aggregated valueBounded write rate (one update per post per second) at the cost of up to a second of lag; pairs well with sharded counters
  • Adaptive shard count. Most posts get a handful of likes and need N = 1. A rate detector (the same sliding-window logic as the rate limiter, or a top-k sketch from trending) splits a post to 16 or 64 sub-counters when its rate passes a threshold, and merges back when it cools. Splitting an increment-only counter is trivial: new increments go to the new set, the old total stays as one more addend.
  • The like row is written first and the counter follows. The insert is idempotent (unique key); only when the insert actually created a row does an increment event go out, through an outbox so a crash cannot skip it (streams and CDC). Unlike and delete are the same in reverse. Counters will drift from rare failures, so a reconciliation job recomputes from the like rows for any post whose counter was touched, and always for posts that hit a threshold.
  • Display rounding is a feature. Above ten thousand, show "12.3K". Nobody can tell the difference, and it lets you cache the count for seconds. Own like state ("filled heart") comes from the like row (or a per-user Bloom filter or recent-likes cache), not from the counter.
  • Comments are append-only rows keyed by (post_id, comment_id), with comment ids time-sortable (same id design). Show the first page and a count; load more by cursor. A hot post's comment writes are spread across shards by hashing comment_id within the post's key range, or buffered through the log, and readers see the most recent page from a cache with a short TTL. Moderation (spam, abuse) is a stream consumer that can hide a comment, and reads check the hidden flag.
  • Likes at the edge of reality: a bot farm can create a million likes. Rate-limit per user and per device, down-weight suspicious accounts in ranking, and keep the raw rows so you can remove them (abuse).

9Deep dive 4: search and hashtags

How do you find posts for #sunset in under 200 ms when millions of posts carry it? What is different about searching users by name?

Compare with a worked answer

Two indexes with different shapes. Hashtags are exact tokens: an inverted index mapping a tag to post ids, newest first, built when the post is created. User search is prefix and fuzzy matching on names (the typeahead chapter), ranked by closeness in the social graph.

OptionHowProsCons
Database query (LIKE '%tag%')Scan captionsNo new systemCollapses at any real size
Hashtag as a sharded key-value listKey = tag (or tag + day); value = post idsCheap, simple, fast newest-firstHot tags (#love) become hot keys; no ranking by relevance
Search engine (inverted index, sharded)Index captions, tags, user names; query with filters and scoringPrefix, fuzzy, ranking, facetingAnother system to run; near-real-time (seconds) indexing; deletes must be propagated
  • Index asynchronously from the same event log that drives fan-out: a post event creates index entries within seconds; a delete event removes them. The index is a derived view, rebuildable from the posts (CDC).
  • Hot tags. Shard a popular tag's list by time bucket, for example one list per tag per hour; "recent" reads the newest buckets, no bucket becomes a hot key, and old buckets are cold. Writes land only in the current bucket, so spread that bucket over a few shards (hash of post id).
  • "Top" posts for a tag is not newest-first: compute it periodically from engagement over a window using a heavy-hitters job (top-k and trending) and store the result as a short list per tag; do not sort millions of posts at query time.
  • User search uses a prefix index on username and display name with a popularity prior (followers, mutuals); a viewer's own follow graph boosts matches (people you follow first), computed from a small candidate set, not the whole user table.
  • Deletes and privacy again: a private account's posts must not appear in tag search, and a removed post must disappear quickly. Treat the index as a candidate generator and filter by current post state at read time (the same "check at read" rule as the feed).
  • Abuse: banned or sensitive tags can be blocked at query time; tag stuffing (30 tags on a photo) is rate-limited by policy.

SLOs (SLOs):

  • Feed load: 99.9% of requests succeed, p99 under 400 ms server-side.
  • Image delivery: 99.95% availability at the edge, hit ratio above 98% for variants (an alert at 96%).
  • Upload to visible: p95 under 10 s from upload completion to the post being ready to followers.
  • Durability: zero loss of acknowledged uploads; a periodic sample read-back verifies originals.

Rollout of a new format (say AVIF at a new quality): add it as a new variant key, backfill hot photos first from a throttled queue so it cannot starve live resize jobs, serve it only to clients that advertise support, and compare image bytes and decode failures against the control group (deployments). Roll back by removing the format from the CDN rule; the old variants are still there.

11Trade-offs and "if requirements change"

What did this design give up? Then: what if the product adds video, 24-hour stories, a ranked (not chronological) feed, or private accounts?

Compare with a worked answer
We choseWe gave upBecause
Eager variants9% more storage and some wasted jobsInstant views and no CPU on the read path
Hybrid fan-outA more complex read path for celebrity followsBounded work on both sides
Sharded, eventually-consistent countersExact live countsContention is the real limit; nobody notices a second of lag

  • Video: upload becomes multipart; processing becomes transcoding into several bitrates and segments, which is minutes of CPU per upload; delivery uses adaptive streaming (video streaming). The feed, counters and search carry over unchanged.
  • Stories (24-hour expiry): a TTL on the post and its variants, and a lifecycle rule; the feed becomes a tray of authors with new stories; storage is reclaimed automatically.
  • Ranked feed: the timeline cache becomes a candidate set (hundreds of ids), a ranking service scores them with features (affinity, recency, engagement) and the cursor must be stable per session; precompute for active users. The ranking pipeline is a black box in this studio (see chapter 9).
  • Private accounts: every read path checks follow-approval: feed hydration, tag search, direct post URLs and CDN image URLs. Image URLs for private posts need signed URLs or short-lived tokens, which lowers CDN hit ratio for them.
  • Multi-region: originals replicated to a second region; timelines and counters regional; the follow graph is global read-mostly (multi-region).

12Say it out loud: a five-minute summary

Say your design aloud in five minutes, then compare with this model.

Compare with a worked answer

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"Writes are small and reads are enormous: about 350 uploads a second against 1.4 million image views a second at peak, so I'd design for the read path. A phone gets a presigned URL and uploads the original straight to object storage; the post is created in a processing state and an event goes to a log. Workers decode in a sandbox, re-encode, strip location data, and write four eager variants, which cost only 9% extra storage. Images are served from a CDN with immutable URLs and an origin shield, and the hit ratio is the number I'd watch: 95% still means 33 gigabits a second at the origin, 99% means under 7. Post ids are 64-bit and time-sortable with a shard id inside, so feed cursors are just ids. For the feed I'd use a hybrid: push into timeline caches for ordinary accounts, about 70,000 inserts a second, but not for accounts over a hundred thousand followers, whose recent posts are merged in at read time. Likes are an idempotent row per user per post as truth, with a derived counter spread over 16 or 64 sub-counters for hot posts, since a single row tops out near a thousand updates a second and a viral photo asks for sixteen thousand; counts are cached and rounded. Hashtag and user search are derived indexes fed from the same event log, with hot tags bucketed by time. Deletes and privacy are checked at read time. Biggest risks are the celebrity stampede, the resize backlog and counter drift, and I'd alert on queue age, CDN hit ratio and counter divergence. If we add video, upload and delivery change but the rest holds."

Notice the shape: read-heavy numbers → direct upload → async variants → CDN → ids and hybrid feed → sharded counters → derived search → read-time checks.

Self-check rubric

Tick what your own design (before reading the answers) covered. The first items are what a solid mid-level answer has; the later ones are what separates a senior answer.

  • Asked clarifying questions and scoped out video, stories and ads explicitly.
  • Did estimates that expose the asymmetry: hundreds of uploads per second against over a million image views per second, and used it.
  • Used a presigned direct upload and explained what it saves (bytes off the app tier), including the orphan cleanup.
  • Chose eager resized variants with a cost argument (about 9% extra storage) and described format negotiation.
  • Made the CDN central, with immutable URLs, an origin shield, and the origin load at 95% versus 99% hit ratio.
  • Treated uploaded images as untrusted input: size limits, sandboxed decoding, re-encoding, metadata stripping.
  • Designed time-sortable IDs with a shard id, and used them for cursors and merging.
  • Proposed a hybrid feed with a follower threshold, a read-time merge for celebrities, and prioritised fan-out queues.
  • Separated the like row (truth) from the counter (derived), and made likes idempotent.
  • Used sharded counters for hot posts with numbers (rate per shard) and an adaptive shard count.
  • Reconciled counters and explained drift, display rounding and short-TTL caching.
  • Designed hashtag and user search as derived indexes fed from the event log, with hot-tag bucketing and a top list computed offline.
  • Applied read-time checks for deletes and privacy rather than chasing every cached copy.
  • Named failure modes with mitigations (resize backlog, CDN or origin overload, cache loss) and wrote SLOs with alert signals.

Key takeaways

  • The shape of the load decides the design: tiny write rate, huge read rate. Invest in the read path and the CDN.
  • Presigned URLs keep bytes off your servers; treat every uploaded image as hostile until decoded in isolation.
  • Eager variants cost less than resizing on read; immutable URLs make the CDN do almost all the work.
  • Time-sortable ids with an embedded shard make feeds, cursors and routing simple.
  • Hybrid fan-out bounds the work for both ordinary and celebrity accounts; the threshold is a dial.
  • A like is a row; a count is a derived, sharded, cached number. Hot posts need N counters, not one.
  • Search is a derived index off the event log; deletes and privacy are always checked at read time.
