Design a Distributed Message Queue

A message queue at scale is a very specific piece of engineering: a distributed, replicated, append-only log with a position per reader. This studio builds one from the numbers up: how fast sequential disks let it go, how replication decides what counts as committed, how a group of workers shares the load, and what 'exactly once' honestly means.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 45 to 60 minutes. Each of the nine stages follows the framework of chapter 1 and opens with a question. Write your own answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. A rubric at the end separates what a mid-level answer covers from what a senior answer adds. All numbers are assumptions to reason with, not measurements of any real system.

📐 The brief

An interviewer says: "Design a distributed message queue: producers publish messages to named topics and consumers read them, at very large scale, with durability."

  • Assume an internal platform shared by hundreds of teams: 2 million messages per second at peak, about 1 KB each, a few hundred topics, and several consumer groups reading each important topic.
  • Teams want to replay history (rebuild a downstream store, backfill a new consumer), not just receive messages once and lose them.
  • A produced message that was acknowledged must survive the loss of a machine. Ordering matters for related messages (all events of one user or order).
  • You may not use an existing queue product as the answer: design the thing itself.

Draw on append-only logs and indexes, logs and exactly-once, Raft, replicated state machines, queues and backpressure, durability and estimation. The design follows the well-known shape of Apache Kafka; the numbers are assumptions.

  • Replication multiplies the disk writes, not the client traffic: 2 GB/s in becomes 6 GB/s of disk writes with a replication factor of 3 (plus network between brokers). With sequential disk bandwidth assumed at 400 MB/s per broker and a 50% headroom rule, that is about 30 brokers. Disk bandwidth, not CPU, sets the cluster size (memory hierarchy).
  • Reads are cheap if consumers keep up: they read the tail from the operating system's page cache, and the broker sends file bytes straight to the network socket without copying them through user space (zero-copy sendfile). A consumer group that reads a week-old backlog goes to disk instead and competes with the writers; that is the case to design for (stage 9).
  • Batching is the throughput lever: without it, 2M requests a second; at 100 messages per batch, 20,000 requests of 100 KB. The producer waits a few milliseconds ("linger") to fill a batch, trading a bounded latency for a large reduction in requests, syscalls and per-record overhead. Compression (applied per batch) multiplies the effect for text-like data.
  • Partitions: 200 partitions at 10 MB/s each is the floor for ingest. In practice choose more (a few thousand for the whole cluster, tens per hot topic) because parallelism of consumers is capped by partition count. But each partition has a cost: file handles, replication state, election work on failure. Order of magnitude, not hundreds of thousands per broker.
  • Storage at full rate for 7 days with 3 copies: thousands of terabytes. That number alone motivates tiered storage (stage 9) and per-topic retention instead of one policy for all.

  • Partition key: chosen by the producer. hash(key) mod partitions keeps all events for one user together and in order. Adding partitions later changes the mapping, so ordering by key is broken across the change; choose the count with growth in mind or accept it as a migration.
  • Hot keys: a single key cannot be spread across partitions without losing its order. The usual answer is to salt only if the consumer does not need strict per-key order, or to move the heavy key to its own topic.

5High-level design

Sketch brokers, partitions, the controller and consumer groups. Which components are on the data path, and which are not?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Produce. The producer hashes the key to a partition, finds that partition's leader from cached metadata, and sends a batch. The leader appends it to the active segment, assigns offsets, and followers fetch the new bytes. When enough in-sync replicas have it, the leader acknowledges.
  2. Consume. A consumer in a group is assigned some partitions. It fetches from each leader starting at the committed offset, processes the batch, and commits the new offset.
  3. Control. A small quorum of controllers (Raft; see Raft deep dive) stores topic and partition metadata and decides leaders when a broker fails. Brokers register with it and learn changes from its replicated metadata log. Metadata is itself a log.
  4. Groups. One broker acts as the group coordinator for a group (chosen by hashing the group id onto a partition of the offsets topic), tracking members by heartbeat and triggering rebalances.
  5. Cold data. Closed segments past a local-retention age are copied to object storage; the broker keeps only an index.

The data path has no central component: the controller and coordinator are off it. A controller outage stops changes (new topics, elections) but does not stop existing leaders from serving, which is a big blast-radius advantage.

6Deep dive 1: the storage engine, segments, indexes and why it is fast

How do you store a partition so that writes are sequential, reads by offset are quick, retention is cheap and the tail is served from memory?

Compare with a worked answer

A partition is a directory of segment files. Each segment holds a contiguous range of offsets, named by its first (base) offset: 00000000000000368769.log. Only the last, active segment is written. It rolls when it reaches a size (about 1 GB is the common default) or an age.

FileContainsPurpose
.logRecord batches, back to back, in offset orderThe data. Append-only and immutable once the segment closes
.indexA sparse map: offset to byte position, one entry for every few KB of log (roughly every 4 KB by default)Find an offset: binary search the segment list by base offset, binary search the index for the nearest earlier entry, scan forward a few KB (sparse index)
.timeindexTimestamp to offsetSeek by time (for 'replay from 2 pm') and time-based retention

Why a sparse index and not one entry per record: a dense entry of about 8 bytes per 1 KB record is under 1% of the log, but on a broker holding tens of terabytes that is hundreds of gigabytes of index, too much to keep in memory. A sparse index with one 8-byte entry per 4 KB of log is four times smaller again (about 0.2% of the log, roughly 2 MB for a 1 GB segment), and a lookup touches only one segment’s index, so the part that matters stays cached.

Design choiceAlternativeWhy the chosen one wins here

  • Retention is deleting whole segments whose newest record is older than the limit, or when total size exceeds a cap. Because the unit is a file, it costs nothing.
  • Log compaction is an alternative cleanup policy for state-like topics (latest value per key): a background cleaner rewrites old segments keeping only the last record for each key, and a record with a null value (a tombstone) marks a key for deletion after a grace period. Offsets stay unchanged; there are just gaps. Use it for changelogs and the offsets topic, not for event history.
  • Crash recovery: on restart, a broker re-checks the tail of the last segment, truncates any partial batch, and rebuilds indexes if needed. After truncation, a follower must discard records the new leader does not have (leader epochs, below).
  • Throughput check: the 400 MB/s per disk figure is sequential. Many partitions per disk is fine if the writes remain mostly large sequential appends across batches; hundreds of thousands of tiny concurrent appends would turn the pattern random (durability).

7Deep dive 2: replication, ISR, acks and leader election

A partition has three replicas. When is a write 'committed'? What exactly do acks=all and min in-sync replicas promise, and what does the controller do when a leader dies?

Compare with a worked answer

Each partition has a leader and followers that fetch from it, like consumers. The leader tracks the in-sync replica set (ISR): the replicas that have kept up within a time bound. A record is committed when every replica in the ISR has it; the offset up to which that is true is the high watermark, and consumers can only read up to it. This prevents a consumer from seeing a record that a leader failure would erase.

OptionPromiseCost / riskUse when
acks=0None: fire and forgetLost messages on any failure; lowest latencyMetrics samples you can lose
acks=1Leader wrote itIf the leader dies before followers copy, an acknowledged message is lost. The demo in stage 2 shows it: the leader holds m4, the followers do notLow-value logs
acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 (rf=3)Committed on at least 2 replicas before ack; the write is rejected when fewer than 2 are in syncLatency of the slower of the 2 replicas; lower availability for writes if 2 replicas are down (stage 2: write rejected). Survives any single broker loss without lossDefault for important data
Quorum replication (Raft per partition)A majority must append; leader election built inNaturally handles slow replicas and elections; but one Raft group per partition (thousands) is heavy, and a majority of 3 equals ISR 2 anywayStrongly consistent metadata stores, small data
Wait for ISR vs wait for a fixed majorityISR adapts: a dead replica leaves the set so writes continue with fewer copiesNeeds the controller to be correct; minimum ISR sets the floorLarge logs where adaptivity beats fixed-size quorums
  • Why ISR instead of a plain majority: with rf=3 and a majority rule, you tolerate 1 failure. With an ISR that can shrink to the leader alone, you could tolerate 2 failures for availability, but at the risk of acknowledging with a single copy. The knob min.insync.replicas bounds that risk. This gives a continuum between "available" and "safe" instead of one fixed point (logs).
  • Leader election: the controller (the Raft-replicated metadata quorum) learns of a broker failure from a missed heartbeat or session expiry, picks a new leader for each affected partition from the ISR, bumps the leader epoch, and publishes the change. Because the new leader came from the ISR it already has all committed records. Choosing a replica outside the ISR (unclean election) restores availability but can lose acknowledged data; keep it off by default.
  • Leader epochs fix the truncation problem. A follower that was ahead of the new leader (it had uncommitted records) asks the new leader for the end offset of its epoch and truncates to it, so replicas converge instead of diverging.
  • Producers on retry hit 'not leader', refetch metadata, and resend; idempotence makes the resend safe.
  • Zombie leaders: a leader that is cut off but alive might still accept writes. It only counts as committed if the ISR acknowledges, and the controller's epoch rejects the old leader's replicas: the same fencing idea as before (fencing).
  • Rack awareness: place replicas of a partition in different racks or zones, so one failure domain loses at most one copy (blast radius).
  • Controller quorum: 3 or 5 nodes running Raft over a metadata log (Raft); this replaced an external coordination service in modern Kafka, removing a second system to operate.

8Deep dive 3: consumer groups, offsets, rebalancing and delivery semantics

How do many workers share a topic with no duplicates in steady state? What happens when a worker joins or dies, and where do duplicates and losses come from?

Compare with a worked answer

A consumer group divides the partitions of a topic among its members: each partition is read by exactly one member at a time. A group of N workers over P partitions has useful parallelism min(N, P). Different groups are independent and each sees all messages.

Membership: each member heartbeats to the group coordinator. If it misses the session timeout (or the poll loop stalls longer than the max-poll interval), the coordinator removes it and triggers a rebalance, which reassigns partitions. The demo below uses 12 partitions and compares an eager reassignment with a sticky one when a fourth worker joins.

"""Keys -> partitions, and how many partitions move when a consumer group rebalances."""
import zlib
P = 12
def part(key): return zlib.crc32(key.encode()) % P
keys = [f"user{i}" for i in range(10000)]
counts = [0]*P
for k in keys: counts[part(k)] += 1
print("keys per partition (10,000 keys):", counts)
def eager(members):
    ms = sorted(members); return {p: ms[p % len(ms)] for p in range(P)}
def sticky(prev, members):
    ms = sorted(members); a = {p: o for p, o in prev.items() if o in ms}
    target = P // len(ms) + (1 if P % len(ms) else 0)
    load = {m: sum(1 for o in a.values() if o == m) for m in ms}
    for p in range(P):
        if p not in a or load[a[p]] > target:
            if p in a: load[a[p]] -= 1
            m = min(ms, key=lambda x: load[x]); a[p] = m; load[m] += 1
    return a
a3 = eager(["c1", "c2", "c3"])
a4_e = eager(["c1", "c2", "c3", "c4"])
a4_s = sticky(a3, ["c1", "c2", "c3", "c4"])
moved = lambda a, b: sum(a[p] != b[p] for p in range(P))
print("3 -> 4 consumers, eager round-robin moves", moved(a3, a4_e), "of", P, "partitions")
print("3 -> 4 consumers, sticky/cooperative moves", moved(a3, a4_s), "of", P, "partitions")
print("after sticky:", {m: sorted(p for p, o in a4_s.items() if o == m) for m in sorted(set(a4_s.values()))})
print("hot key demo: one key at 40% of traffic ->", max(sum(1 for k in ['k']*40+keys[:60] if part(k)==p) for p in range(P)), "of 100 msgs on one partition")

  • Eager rebalance (stop the world): every member gives up all partitions, then re-joins. In the demo 9 of 12 move and the whole group pauses. Large groups can stall for many seconds.
  • Cooperative/sticky rebalance: only partitions that must move are revoked: 3 of 12 here. Combined with static membership (a restarting worker keeps its identity and partitions for a grace period), a rolling deploy causes no rebalance storm.
  • Hot-key line: with one key sending 40% of traffic, one partition gets about 45 of 100 messages, so one consumer is the bottleneck regardless of group size. Parallelism is capped by the hottest partition.

  • The honest summary: end-to-end exactly-once delivery is not possible over an unreliable network; what exists is exactly-once effect through idempotence and atomic commit (chapter 17).
  • Ordering guarantees: within a partition, in offset order, as long as a producer has one in-flight batch per partition or uses idempotence (which keeps order across retries up to a bound on in-flight batches). Across partitions, none. Across producers, order is the order of arrival at the leader. A consumer that processes records concurrently in threads loses order unless it partitions the work by key again.
  • Offset storage: committed offsets are written to an internal, compacted topic keyed by (group, topic, partition): the same log machinery stores its own state.
  • Slow consumers: a consumer that cannot keep up does not slow producers (the log buffers up to retention). It just lags. The risk is lag exceeding retention: data deleted before it is read. Alert on lag as a time, not just a count.

9Deep dive 4: retention, tiered storage and backpressure

7 days at 2 GB/s is thousands of terabytes on expensive local disks. How do you keep long retention cheaply, and how do you keep one noisy client from harming others?

  • Recommendation: tiered storage with a few hours of local data, and per-topic retention. The tiered feature was introduced as early access in Kafka 3.6 and declared production-ready in Kafka 3.9 (KIP-405; the 3.9 release announcement says so, but check the limits of your own version). Catch-up readers then hit object storage and no longer evict the page cache that live consumers rely on, a real operational win.
  • Math: if local data is 6 hours instead of 7 days, local disk need falls by 28 times (168 h / 6 h) before replication; the 3 local copies are paid for only on the recent window, while object storage has its own durability.
  • Backpressure: a log is a buffer, so slow consumers are handled by lag. The risks are on the producer and broker side.
    • Quotas per client id or tenant: bytes/s produced and fetched, and request-time share. A broker that exceeds a quota delays its response rather than erroring, which slows the client without a retry storm (overload).
    • Producer buffer limits: when the client's buffer is full, the send call blocks or fails; the application chooses to drop, block or spill.
    • Bounded request queues at the broker, shedding when full (queues and backpressure).
    • Replication throttles so re-replicating a lost broker does not starve live traffic.
  • Catch-up reads and cache: a consumer reading a three-day-old backlog evicts hot pages. Remedies: tiered storage, per-client fetch quotas, and separate "analytics" clusters (via mirroring).

  • Disaster recovery: an asynchronous mirror in a second region. RPO is the mirror lag; consumer offsets need translating across clusters (DR).

11Trade-offs, and what changes if requirements change

What did this design give up? What changes if the interviewer asks for per-message acknowledgements, strict global order, or a million partitions?

Compare with a worked answer
  • What we gave up: per-message features. Because the unit is a partition log and a consumer tracks a position, there are no per-message acknowledgements, no priorities and no individual redelivery. A work queue that needs "retry this one message in 5 minutes" is a different shape (delayed retry topics or a different product). We accepted this for throughput and replay.
  • Ordering only within a partition. If the requirement becomes a strict global order, you have one partition and one leader for the whole topic: a ceiling of one machine's bandwidth (in our numbers the 2 GB/s ingest is about five times one broker's assumed 400 MB/s disk, before replication). Alternatives: order by key and accept it, or sequence downstream.
  • Latency vs throughput: batching and acks=all cost milliseconds. If a use case needs sub-millisecond, relax acks and put it on a dedicated cluster.
  • A million partitions: each costs file handles and controller state; the metadata log and election time grow. Design topics around ordering needs, not as a consumer-count trick. If you must, spread across multiple clusters.
  • Per-message acknowledgement queue (like a job queue): you need per-message state (visibility timeouts, delivery counts). Build it over a database or choose a broker whose model is the work queue; it will be an order of magnitude lower in throughput.
  • Multi-region active-active: topics per region, mirrored; avoid a stretched cluster (cross-region latency in every acks=all write). Conflicts are the consumers' problem (multi-region).
  • Exactly-once everywhere: costs transactions' latency and some throughput. Offer it as an opt-in, not the default.
  • Cheaper retention: move to tiered storage first; then shorten.

12A model five-minute verbal summary

Explain the design in five minutes, in the framework's order. Record yourself.

Compare with a worked answer

Model answer, about 650 words spoken

"I'd treat this as a log, not a queue: messages are retained and each consumer group keeps its own position, which gives replay for free. Assumptions: 2 million messages a second at about a kilobyte, so 2 GB/s in; with three replicas that is 6 GB/s of disk writes, and at 400 megabytes a second of sequential bandwidth per broker and 50% headroom, about 30 brokers. Disk bandwidth sets the size, not CPU, and the tail is served from the page cache with zero-copy sends. Batching is the throughput lever: 100 messages per batch turns two million requests a second into twenty thousand.

The model: topics split into partitions; a partition is an append-only log, the unit of ordering, replication and parallelism. Producers hash a key to pick the partition, so all events for one user stay in order, and talk straight to the partition leader. Each partition is a set of segment files, about a gigabyte each, with a sparse offset index and a time index, so lookup is two binary searches and a short scan; retention is deleting old files, and compaction is an alternative for latest-value-per-key topics.

Replication: followers fetch from the leader. A record is committed when all in-sync replicas have it; consumers read only up to that high watermark. With acks=all and min in-sync replicas of two, an acknowledged write survives any single broker loss, and when fewer than two are in sync, writes are refused rather than silently weakened. I ran that: with acks=1 the leader held a message its followers did not. A Raft-based controller quorum, off the data path, elects new leaders from the in-sync set and bumps a leader epoch so a returning replica truncates divergent records. Unclean election is off.

Consumers: a group splits partitions between members; parallelism is capped by partition count and by the hottest key. I'd use cooperative, sticky rebalancing and static membership; the demo moved 3 of 12 partitions instead of 9. Offsets live in an internal compacted topic. Delivery is at-least-once by default, so consumers must be idempotent; effectively-once exists inside the system through idempotent producers and transactions, and for external stores I'd store the offset with the result.

Operations: tiered storage keeps hours locally and the rest in object storage, so disks are sized for bandwidth, and catch-up readers stop evicting the cache. Quotas delay over-limit clients. I'd page on under-replicated partitions, ISR shrink, disk projected full and controller health, roll upgrades one broker at a time, and place replicas across zones. What we gave up: per-message acknowledgement, priorities and global order; if those are needed, it is a different product. Where would you like to dig?"

Saying it in an interview

The shape of a strong summary

Stage 12 has a model five-minute answer. Notice its shape: log not queue → numbers → partition and segment model → replication and commit rule → consumer groups and semantics → operations → what we gave up.

Key takeaways

  • It is a log with a position per reader, not a delete-on-consume queue; that gives replay and cheap fan-out.
  • Sequential disk bandwidth and batching set the cluster size, not CPU. The page cache and zero-copy serve the tail.
  • A partition is the unit of order, replication and parallelism; key hashing gives per-key order only.
  • Committed means all in-sync replicas have it; acks=all plus min in-sync replicas is the durability dial, and unclean election trades loss for availability.
  • A Raft controller off the data path elects leaders from the ISR; leader epochs make truncation and fencing safe.
  • Cooperative rebalancing and static membership prevent stop-the-world pauses; hot keys cap parallelism.
  • Exactly-once is an effect of idempotence plus atomic commit; external effects need idempotency too.
  • Tiered storage and quotas keep long retention affordable and neighbours safe.
