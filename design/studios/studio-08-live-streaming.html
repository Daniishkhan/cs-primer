Design Live Streaming with Chat

A live broadcast cannot buffer its way out of trouble. Video has to reach a million people a few seconds after it happens, and a chat room beside it has to feel alive without sending two hundred million messages a second. This studio designs both halves, shows how they differ, and makes the latency numbers explicit.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 45 to 60 minutes. Each of the nine stages follows the framework of chapter 1 and opens with a question. Write your own answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. A rubric at the end separates what a mid-level answer covers from what a senior answer adds. All numbers are assumptions to reason with, not measurements of any real system.

📐 The brief

An interviewer says: "Design a live-streaming platform with chat: broadcasters stream video, viewers watch it live and chat while they watch."

  • Assume a big platform: a peak of 5 million concurrent viewers across the site, and the largest single stream reaching 1 million viewers with an active chat of about 200 messages a second.
  • A viewer expects video within a few seconds of what the broadcaster sees, because chat reacts to it. Spoilers in chat matter if the video is 30 seconds behind.
  • Streams end; viewers then expect clips of the best moments and a replay (VOD).

Draw on video streaming (transcoding, segments, CDNs), chat and presence, approximate counting, Netflix Open Connect for CDN economics, Slack realtime for chat connection scale, and estimation.

  • Video egress dominates by bytes: a 1M-viewer stream at 3 Mbit/s average is 3 Tbit/s, 375 GB/s. No origin can do that: it must be a CDN, with the origin seeing only cache misses. The cost of this egress is the platform's biggest bill (CDN economics).
  • Short segments multiply requests: halving the segment time doubles requests. At 1 s segments a 1M-viewer stream is a million requests a second at the edge. Hit ratio then decides the origin's life (figures here are for 2 s segments, as in the script): 90% leaves 50,000 requests a second on origin per rendition; 99.9% leaves 500. Everyone watches the same newest segment, which is ideal for caching, but a thundering herd at each segment boundary needs request coalescing at the edge.
  • Chat is smaller in bytes and harder in structure: 200 messages a second to a million viewers is 200 million deliveries a second, 240 Gbit/s of chat. That is 8% of video egress for text; worse, each delivery is a separate small packet over a separate connection. Sampling to a readable 20 per second cuts it by 10× (24 Gbit/s). Nobody can read 200 messages a second anyway.
  • Connections: 1M WebSocket connections for one room, at 100,000 per gateway server, is 10 servers for the room, and 50 for 5M platform-wide viewers (chapter 10).
  • Transcoding ladder: five renditions total 10.5 Mbit/s for a 6 Mbit/s ingest, about 1.8× the bitrate, but a big compute multiple; transcoding is per stream, not per viewer, so a 5M-viewer platform needs about as much transcoding as it has live streams (tens of thousands), not millions.

5High-level design

Draw the video path and the chat path as two separate systems that share only a room id. Where does time get spent?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Ingest. The broadcaster's encoder connects to the nearest ingest point (primary), with a backup ingest in another zone. The ingest validates the stream key and passes frames to the transcoder.
  2. Transcode and package. The transcoder produces a ladder of renditions with aligned keyframes, so a player can switch between them at segment boundaries. The packager cuts each rendition into 2-second segments, each made of sub-second parts, and writes them to the origin and to object storage.
  3. Deliver. Players fetch playlists and parts through the CDN. Everyone requests the same newest part; the edge coalesces simultaneous requests into one origin fetch.
  4. Chat. The player also opens a WebSocket to a chat gateway. A message goes to the room's shard, which applies moderation and rate limits, then publishes the accepted message through a fan-out tree to every gateway holding a viewer in that room.
  5. Counts and clips. Gateways report presence into approximate counters. A clip request is served by reading already-stored segments.

Time budget: the video path spends its seconds on the encoder, segmentation and buffering; the chat path spends milliseconds, but crucially chat is usually ahead of the video, which is why low video latency matters for the experience (stage 7).

Transcoding. Real-time encoding of a ladder is compute-heavy and cannot lag behind the live edge. Options:

  • Recommendation: hardware or fast software transcoding for a full ladder on streams above a viewer threshold, source-only for small ones, and an automatic upgrade when a stream grows. Keyframes at a fixed interval (say 2 s, matching the segment length) so renditions align.
  • Stateful workers need a scheduler: a stream is assigned to a worker; if the worker dies, a replacement picks up the incoming feed from the ingest, which buffers a few seconds (job scheduling).
  • Ingest failover: the encoder sends to a primary and, if configured, a backup stream (dual ingest). The packager picks the better input and switches at a keyframe boundary, keeping segment numbering continuous so players do not see a discontinuity. If the broadcaster's connection drops, the stream stays "live" for a grace period (say 30 to 60 s), with the last frames or a slate, and resumes on reconnect; after the grace period the stream is ended and the VOD is finalised.
  • Zone loss at ingest: DNS or the ingest API steers broadcasters to healthy points, and the encoder's auto-reconnect finishes the job. The stream key alone identifies the stream, so reconnecting anywhere re-joins the same pipeline (blast radius).

  • What LL-HLS changes (from the specification's extensions): the server publishes each segment as short partial segments as they are encoded, so a player need not wait for the full segment; a blocking playlist reload lets the client ask for "the playlist that contains part N" and the server holds the response until it exists, removing polling delay; a preload hint names the next part so the client can request it before it exists; delta updates and rendition reports shrink playlists and speed bitrate switches. Net effect: the unit of latency falls from a segment to a part.
  • Why the CDN still works: parts and segments are immutable files with the same URL for everyone; only the playlist changes. Edges must hold blocking playlist requests and coalesce them: all viewers wait on the same next part, and one origin fetch satisfies them all.
  • Recommendation: LL-HLS (or low-latency DASH) for the audience, since the stream is one-to-many and cacheable, so it scales on infrastructure the platform already runs; WebRTC (WHIP) for browser-based ingest and for a small number of interactive guests. Keep the player able to degrade to normal-latency HLS.
  • CDN fan-out for live: a tiered cache (edge, regional mid-tier, origin shield) so that a miss at an edge goes to a mid-tier, not the origin. With 99.9% hit ratio origin sees about 2,000 part requests a second for a 1M-viewer stream (output above). Pre-position the stream: tell the CDN to prefetch when a stream starts to be big. Use several CDNs and steer between them by measured quality (chapter 13).
  • Thundering herd at start: a streamer announces, and 500k people arrive in a minute; each needs a playlist and the init segment. Cache the init segment with a long TTL, rate-limit new connections gradually, and tolerate more buffering on join.
  • Spoilers: chat is only slightly delayed relative to broadcaster. Some platforms delay the chat display to match the player (a chat delay offset), or show the stream's own timestamps. A design note, not a technical necessity.

8Deep dive 3: a chat room with a million viewers

200 messages a second times a million viewers is 200 million deliveries a second. Why not deliver everything, and what do you do instead? How are rooms sharded, and where does moderation go?

Compare with a worked answer

Naive fan-out is hopeless: one chat server cannot push to a million sockets, a single room is a hot key, and no human can read 200 messages a second. This is the same shape as a celebrity's post, only synchronous (chapter 10 and fan-out in the feed).

OptionMechanismProsCons
Deliver every message to every viewerRoom server writes to all socketsSimple semantics200M deliveries/s; unreadable; one hot server
Sample on the server, then fan outAccept all messages, choose about 10 to 30 a second to display (priority for moderators, subscribers, then a random sample of the rest), publish the chosen onesCuts fan-out 10×; readable; protects the systemA viewer's own message may not be shown to others; show it locally to the sender so it feels delivered
Per-viewer throttling at the gatewayEach gateway drops messages beyond a rateProtects slow clients and gatewaysDifferent viewers see different chats; fine for huge rooms, odd for small ones
Tiered roomsSmall rooms deliver everything; above a size threshold switch to samplingBest experience for small roomsMode switching logic and testing
Split a giant room into shardsEach viewer is in one of N chat shards of the same streamEach shard is a normal-sized room; low loadViewers do not see each other's messages; breaks the 'shared moment'. Sometimes used with a global highlight channel
  • Recommendation: tiered rooms with sampling above a threshold. The script in stage 2 ran a priority sample: moderators always shown, subscribers and ordinary viewers given reserved shares, 20 messages out of 200 in a second. A strict priority order starved ordinary viewers in a first version of the demo, which is why the shares are reserved.
  • Fan-out tree: a room shard publishes accepted messages to a pub/sub layer; each of the roughly 10 gateways holding that room's viewers (stage 2) subscribes once and writes to its local sockets. Fan-out cost is gateways × message rate, not viewers × message rate, at the root. Batch a gateway's writes into one frame every 100 to 200 ms to cut syscalls (event loops).
  • Sharding: rooms are partitioned by room id across room shards (consistent hashing). A giant room gets several shards: ingest messages are spread across shards for moderation and sampling, and each shard samples a share of the output. Gateways are not tied to rooms; a gateway subscribes to the rooms its connections need.
  • Moderation has to be fast and layered:
    • Synchronous, cheap: rate limit per user (slow mode), length limits, blocklist and regex filters held in memory, banned-user check, link rules. Microseconds, on the room shard.
    • Asynchronous, heavier: machine-learning classifiers and human review work off a stream of messages. When they decide, they emit a MOD event that deletes the message from clients and bans the user. Some harm is seen briefly; that is the trade for latency.
    • Channel modes: followers-only, subscribers-only, verified-only: a switch raising the cost of abuse. Broadcaster-appointed moderators act through the same event path.
    • Moderation actions are idempotent events keyed by message id, so a reconnecting client replays recent MOD events and does not see deleted content (abuse).
  • Connection surges: a raid brings 100k new chat connections in seconds. Gateways accept connections with a bounded rate, join rooms lazily, and the server controls initial history size (send the last 50 sampled messages).
  • Delivery semantics: best effort. A reconnecting client does not replay missed chat; it receives a recent backlog. That is a conscious product choice that removes per-user acknowledgement (compare with 1:1 chat, where delivery state is the product).

9Deep dive 4: viewer counts, clips and the VOD afterwards

How do you show an approximate viewer count that updates every few seconds for a million people? How can a viewer clip the last 30 seconds in under 10 seconds? What turns a stream into a replay?

Compare with a worked answer

Viewer counts. Exact concurrent counts of a million moving connections are expensive and unnecessary.

  • Recommendation: sharded gateway counters for the live number (summed every 5 seconds, smoothed), with HyperLogLog for unique viewers in analytics. The count is allowed to be off by a percent and a few seconds old; display it rounded for large numbers.

Clips. The live segments are already in object storage. A clip request names a window (for example, the last 30 seconds as of "now minus player latency"):

  • The clip service finds the segments that cover the window and creates a clip manifest pointing to them, so a clip is available almost immediately (a reference, no copying).
  • Edges that fall inside a segment need trimming: either re-encode only the first and last segment (a few seconds of work) or snap to keyframes. Re-encode into a shareable MP4 asynchronously for downloads and social sharing.
  • Keep a short-term ring buffer of the highest-quality renditions so clips are not limited to the lowest. Retain referenced segments as long as a clip exists (reference counting) even if the VOD expires.
  • Abuse: rate-limit clip creation per user; apply the same moderation to clip titles; check copyright rules for the clipped content.

VOD. When the stream ends (or after the grace period), build a VOD manifest listing all segments in order, mark the stream ended, and optionally re-encode into a more efficient ladder offline (VOD can afford slower, better encoding; see chapter 13 for the DAG). Keep the live segments until the VOD is ready, then switch the pointer. Retention policy: keep VOD for a period (days or weeks) unless the broadcaster saves it. Storage: if a channel streams 6 hours and the stored ladder totals 10.5 Mbit/s, that is roughly 6 × 3600 × 10.5 / 8 = 28 GB per stream (about 16 GB more if the 6 Mbit/s source is kept too); with tens of thousands of simultaneous streams, retention is a significant cost line, so tier older VOD to colder storage and drop upper renditions after a while.

11Trade-offs, and what changes if requirements change

What did you give up? What if the product needs sub-second latency for everyone, a shared chat where everyone sees everything, or a 10× bigger event?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Gave up: sub-second latency for the audience (we chose 2 to 5 s) and a complete shared chat for giant rooms (sampling). In return we use the CDN for video and keep chat bounded.
  • Sub-second for everyone: move the audience to WebRTC, or a hybrid; the price is a stateful media-server tier proportional to viewers, and no HTTP caching. Likely feasible only for premium or small interactive streams, or for a very specific event type (auctions, betting) where it is worth the cost.
  • Full chat for everyone: possible for small rooms; for giant rooms, shard the room and accept different views, or provide a "highlights" channel of the top messages. Fully reading 200 messages a second is not a human capability.
  • 10× event: video scales mostly by buying more CDN capacity and negotiating peering; chat needs more gateways and shards, which scale linearly; transcoding is per stream and is unaffected. The first thing to break is likely the join surge at the start: plan admission and pre-warming.
  • Cost levers: fewer renditions for small streams; hardware encoders; keep fewer days of VOD; use a peering/embedded CDN for the biggest streams (case).
  • Interactivity (polls, predictions, co-streaming): small-state, real-time features can ride on the chat connection: a poll is a message type with aggregated results.
  • Multi-region: ingest near the broadcaster, replicate segments to origin shields near viewers; chat rooms live in one home region with edge gateways near users; a cross-region pub/sub brings messages home (multi-region).
  • Security and abuse: stream keys are secrets (rotation, revocation); hash-signed playback URLs deter hotlinking; ingest accepts only authenticated connections (keys and secrets).

12A model five-minute verbal summary

Say it aloud. Start with the tension between latency and scale.

Compare with a worked answer

Model answer, about 650 words spoken

"The core tension is low latency versus scale. Viewers want video within a few seconds because chat reacts to it, and the biggest stream has a million viewers. So I'd build two systems that share only a room id: a video path, which is one-to-many and cacheable, and a chat path, which is many-to-many and not.

Numbers: a million viewers at 3 megabits is 3 terabits a second, 375 gigabytes a second, which only a CDN can do; the origin sees only misses, so at a 99.9% hit ratio about 2,000 part requests a second. Chat is 200 messages a second times a million viewers, 200 million deliveries and 240 gigabits a second, for text nobody can read at that rate. A million sockets is about ten gateways at 100,000 each.

Video path: the broadcaster sends RTMP or SRT to a primary ingest with a backup. A transcoder makes a ladder with aligned keyframes, only for streams above a viewer threshold, since most have a handful of viewers. A packager cuts two-second segments made of sub-second parts and writes them to the origin and to object storage. For delivery I'd use low-latency HLS: partial segments, blocking playlist reload and preload hints turn the latency unit from a segment into a part, giving roughly two to five seconds, against twenty to thirty for plain HLS, and it still rides CDN caches with request coalescing at the edge. WebRTC gets under a second but needs a stateful media-server tier per viewer, so I'd use it only for browser ingest and a few interactive guests, not the audience. In my budget script, the player's buffer was the biggest item for plain HLS, which is why shrinking segments is what matters.

Ingest failover: dual ingest, switching at a keyframe boundary with continuous numbering, and a grace period when the broadcaster disconnects before the stream is ended.

Chat: tiered rooms. Small rooms deliver everything; above a threshold, a room shard moderates and samples about twenty messages a second, with reserved shares for moderators, subscribers and ordinary viewers, then publishes through a pub/sub tree so the cost is gateways times messages, not viewers times messages. Rooms are sharded by id; a giant room gets several shards. Moderation is layered: cheap synchronous filters on the shard, such as slow mode, blocklists and rate limits, and asynchronous classifiers and humans that emit delete and ban events. Delivery is best effort, with a recent backlog on reconnect.

Viewer counts come from sharded gateway counters, summed every few seconds; clips are manifests over segments already in object storage; and the VOD is the same segments with a final manifest. SLOs are measured in the player: start time, rebuffer ratio, glass-to-glass latency. Trade-offs: we gave up sub-second latency for the audience and a complete shared chat in giant rooms. Where would you like to dig?"

Saying it in an interview

The shape of a strong summary

Stage 12 has a model five-minute answer. Notice its shape: the tension → numbers → video path → protocol choice → ingest failover → chat fan-out and moderation → counts, clips, VOD → SLOs → trade-offs.

Key takeaways

  • Low latency fights scale; every choice sits on that line. Say so first.
  • Video is one-to-many and cacheable (CDN, immutable parts); chat is many-to-many and not. Design them as two systems sharing a room id.
  • LL-HLS shortens the unit of latency from a segment to a part (blocking reload, preload hints) and keeps CDN scaling; WebRTC is sub-second but costs a stateful tier.
  • Transcoding scales with streams, not viewers: give a full ladder only to streams that grow.
  • Ingest failover is dual ingest, a keyframe-aligned switch and a grace period.
  • For giant chat rooms, sample what is displayed, fan out through a tree, and moderate in layers with fast synchronous filters.
  • Counts are approximate; clips and VOD reuse segments already in storage.
  • Measure SLOs at the player: start time, rebuffering and glass-to-glass latency.
