Design an Ad Click Aggregator

Counting clicks sounds like a counter. At ten billion a day, with duplicates, bots, late phones and a bill at the end of the month, it is a lesson in what exactly-once really means. This studio designs two paths from one log: a fast one for dashboards and an exact one for billing, and shows how to keep both honest.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 45 to 60 minutes. Each of the nine stages follows the framework of chapter 1 and opens with a question. Write your own answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. A rubric at the end separates what a mid-level answer covers from what a senior answer adds. All numbers are assumptions to reason with, not measurements of any real system.

📐 The brief

An interviewer says: "Design a system that counts ad clicks. Advertisers see clicks per ad per minute on a dashboard, and they are billed per click."

  • Assume a large ad network: 10 billion clicks per day, with a peak about three times the average, and about 10 million active ads.
  • Two consumers with different needs: dashboards want fresh numbers (within about a minute) and tolerate small errors; billing wants exact numbers and tolerates delay of hours.
  • Clicks arrive from browsers and apps over unreliable networks. Duplicates, bots and late arrivals are normal.

Draw on top-k and trending (windows, event time, lambda versus kappa), logs and exactly-once, column stores, batch processing, streams and estimation.

5High-level design

Draw the minimal pipeline for the dashboard, then add the second path that makes billing exact. What does each path trust?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Ingest. The click edge verifies the token signature, stamps ingest time, and appends to a partitioned log. It never talks to the aggregate store, so a downstream outage cannot lose clicks: the log buffers them (backpressure).
  2. Speed path. A stream job reads the log, drops duplicates by click id, applies cheap fraud rules, counts per (ad, minute) in event-time windows, and upserts window results into the OLAP store.
  3. Query. The dashboard reads the OLAP store (column store) and shows recent minutes as provisional.
  4. Batch path. The log is archived hourly as columnar files. A nightly job recomputes, from the raw data, deduplicated and fraud-scored counts for the closed day and writes the billing ledger. It also diffs against the stream's numbers.

This is a lambda design in the plain sense: two paths computing the same thing for different guarantees (chapter 12 compares lambda and kappa). The reason here is not fashion; it is that billing needs a result that can be recomputed and audited, whatever bugs or late data the stream saw.

6Deep dive 1: dedup and fraud filtering at 350k events per second

Where can duplicates come from, how do you detect them without unbounded memory, and where does fraud filtering sit? What are the alternatives?

Compare with a worked answer

Sources of duplicates: client or network retries; the user double-clicking; the edge writing to the log twice after a timeout; the stream job replaying after a crash. They need different defences, and the click id handles the first three.

  • Recommendation: exact keyed state in the stream job (partition by ad id, so duplicates always meet), with a window of 24 hours, plus a full dedup in batch for billing. The batch job is the real source of truth, and the stream state only has to be good enough for dashboards. State is checkpointed to durable storage so it survives restarts.
  • Why partition by ad id: duplicates of one click always share an ad id, so all copies reach one task. (Partition by click id would also work but loses aggregation locality.)
  • Fraud filtering, qualitatively: several layers, cheapest first.
    • Structural: invalid or expired token signature, impossible timing (a click within milliseconds of the impression), a click without an impression.
    • Rate and reputation: too many clicks from one IP, device or account per window; known data-centre IP ranges and bot user agents.
    • Behavioural and model-based scores: run later in batch with more context (click-to-conversion patterns, device graphs) and used to credit advertisers retroactively.
    • Tag, do not delete: store a flag and reason on each event so decisions can be audited and reversed. Count raw, valid and billable separately.
  • Industry guidelines distinguish "general" invalid traffic (easy to detect, such as known bots) from "sophisticated" invalid traffic (needs deeper analysis). A real design keeps both classes of detection and a dispute process.

7Deep dive 2: windows, late events, watermarks and exactly-once into the aggregate store

A click that happened at 12:00:58 may arrive at 12:01:20, or at 12:40. When do you close the 12:00 window, what happens to latecomers, and how do you make the write to the aggregate store safe against crashes and replays?

Compare with a worked answer

Event-time windows need a notion of "no more events for this window are expected": the watermark. A common heuristic: watermark = latest event time seen minus an allowed lateness (the demo below simplifies by taking the latest arrival time instead, a fair stand-in for a steady stream). When the watermark passes the end of a window, the window is closed and emitted. Later events for that window are late. The demo below simulates three minutes of clicks with random delays (a few arrive 20 to 70 s late), 5% duplicates, a 30 s lateness allowance, and an idempotent sink.

"""Ad clicks: minute windows with dedup, a watermark, late events and an idempotent sink."""
import random
random.seed(7)
W, LATENESS = 60, 30
events, eid = [], 0
for i in range(2000):
    t = random.uniform(0, 180); eid += 1
    ad = random.choice(["adA"]*6 + ["adB", "adC"])
    arrive = t + (random.expovariate(1/3) if random.random() < 0.9 else random.uniform(20, 70))
    events.append((arrive, f"c{eid}", ad, t))
    if random.random() < 0.05: events.append((arrive + 0.5, f"c{eid}", ad, t))   # client/network retry duplicate
events.sort()
seen, counts, late_dropped, dups, wm = set(), {}, 0, 0, 0.0
sink = {}                                              # (ad, window) -> count; written by upsert, so replays are harmless
for arrive, cid, ad, t in events:
    wm = max(wm, arrive - LATENESS)                    # watermark: we assume events are at most LATENESS seconds late
    if cid in seen: dups += 1; continue
    seen.add(cid)
    w = int(t // W) * W
    if w + W <= wm:                                    # window already closed and emitted
        late_dropped += 1; continue
    counts[(ad, w)] = counts.get((ad, w), 0) + 1
    for k in [k for k in counts if k[1] + W <= wm]:    # emit closed windows
        sink[k] = counts.pop(k)
sink.update(counts)
truth = {}
seen2 = set()
for _, cid, ad, t in events:
    if cid in seen2: continue
    seen2.add(cid); k = (ad, int(t//W)*W); truth[k] = truth.get(k, 0) + 1
print(f"\nevents delivered {len(events)}, duplicates dropped {dups}, late (after watermark) {late_dropped}")
print("stream total", sum(sink.values()), "vs exact batch total", sum(truth.values()),
      f"-> error {1-sum(sink.values())/sum(truth.values()):.2%}")
for k in sorted(truth)[:6]: print(k, "stream", sink.get(k, 0), "batch", truth[k])

# crash and replay: the same finished windows are written a second time
incr = {}
for k, v in sink.items(): incr[k] = incr.get(k, 0) + v          # naive "count += v" sink, first run
for k, v in sink.items(): incr[k] = incr.get(k, 0) + v          # replay after a crash
upsert = dict(sink); upsert.update(sink)                        # idempotent upsert keyed by (ad, window)
print("\nafter replaying every window write:")
print("  increment sink total:", sum(incr.values()), "(double counted: customers overbilled)")
print("  upsert sink total   :", sum(upsert.values()), "(unchanged)")

  • The last block is the exactly-once lesson: a sink that adds to a counter doubles every number when a finished window is written twice after a crash (3,894 versus 1,947). A sink that overwrites the row for (ad, window) with the window's final count is idempotent: repeating it changes nothing.

  • Why state needs a checkpoint: on crash the job restores state and the log offset from the last checkpoint and replays from there. Replay is safe only because the sink writes are idempotent and dedup state was restored with the same checkpoint.

8Deep dive 3: hot ads, and querying for advertisers

One ad receives 3,500 clicks/s. What goes wrong, and how do you keep partitions balanced without breaking counts? Then: how do advertisers query 90 days by minute without scanning everything?

Compare with a worked answer

Hot keys. Partitioning by ad id makes one task process every click of the hottest ad, and a viral campaign can exceed what one task or one aggregate row can take. Options:

OptionMechanismProsCons
Local pre-aggregation (combiner)Each edge or stream task counts per (ad, minute) in memory for a second or so, then emits one partial countCuts traffic into the hot partition by 100× or more; no change in correctness since counting is associativeAdds a second of latency; partial counts must carry ids for dedup, so dedup happens before this step
Key saltingAppend a random salt 0..k-1 to a hot ad's key, aggregate per salted key, then merge the k partials in a second stageSpreads load across k tasksNeeds to know which ads are hot (detect by counters, or salt all ads cheaply); two-stage plan; dedup must still be exact

Dedup is done first (by click id, on the unsalted ad key), then counts are salted and merged. Counting is a commutative, associative operation, so partial sums merge exactly. (Sketches are for approximate counts; billing wants exact counts, so we do not use them here, but they fit the dashboard's top-k view, see chapter 12.)

Querying. The access pattern is: filter by ad and time range, group by minute or hour, optionally by one or two dimensions, read a few columns.

StoreFitNotes
Column-oriented OLAP database, time-partitionedBest: reads only needed columns, compresses well, vectorised scans, supports rollupsNeeds a design for upserts of late windows; query concurrency limits
Time-series databaseGood for per-ad seriesMany series (10M ads) is a cardinality problem

Maintain hourly and daily rollups so long ranges read 60 or 1,440 times fewer rows (90 days by hour is 2,160 rows per ad, not 129,600). The API chooses the rollup from the requested granularity and range.
  • Query concurrency: 100k advertisers refreshing dashboards. Cache recent results by (ad, range, granularity) for 30 s; limit the maximum range at fine granularity; return the final flag.
  • Multi-tenancy: enforce that an advertiser can only query their own ads at the API layer (authorization).

9Deep dive 4: batch reconciliation and billing correctness

Why is the stream not the billing source? Design the nightly path, what it compares, and how disagreements are handled. Lambda or kappa?

Compare with a worked answer

Billing needs a number that can be explained, repeated and defended in a dispute. Streams are subject to watermark cutoffs, bugs fixed later, and fraud rules that improved after the fact. A batch job over immutable raw data has none of those problems: it can be re-run.

ArchitectureHowProsCons
Lambda: stream plus batchStream gives fast approximate numbers; batch recomputes exact numbers from raw data; the batch result replaces the stream's for closed periodsTwo independent computations check each other; batch is simple (batch processing)Two code paths to keep consistent; duplicated logic is the classic source of drift
Kappa: one streaming code path, replay for correctionsReprocess from the log with the same job when logic changesOne implementationNeeds long log retention; replay of big windows is slow; exactness still depends on stream semantics
Batch only, hourlyRun the batch job every hourVery simpleNo minute-level freshness
Stream only with a very large allowed latenessWaitOne pathSlow dashboards, no independent check
  • Recommendation: lambda-style for billing. To reduce drift, share the logic (the definition of a valid click, window assignment) as one library used by both paths, and test them against the same sample inputs.
  • The nightly job: read the day's raw files (plus a margin of late data), deduplicate by click id, join fraud labels, count billable clicks per (campaign, day), and write to the billing ledger with a version. Re-running writes a new version; the ledger keeps both. Closing the day happens after a defined cut-off for late data (say 3 hours).
  • Reconciliation: compare, per ad and hour, the stream's count to the batch count. Expected difference: the late-event tail (about 2.65% in our demo before corrections). Alert when the difference exceeds a threshold, such as the 3% in the SLO below, or a sudden change in the ratio; that points to a bug or an outage in one path. Differences are explained, not hidden.
  • Correcting the dashboard: after the batch run, write the exact numbers into the OLAP store (upsert) and set final: true. Advertisers may see the number for a given minute change slightly after a day; the UI says so.
  • Disputes and credits: late fraud findings produce a credit in a later period instead of rewriting an invoice already sent. The ledger is append-only, like the money systems in the payments design.
  • Late arrivals after the cut-off: counted in the next day's batch as an adjustment, or dropped under a published policy. Decide and write it down.

11Trade-offs, and what changes if requirements change

What do you give up, and how would the design change if the SLA moved to real-time bidding, unique-user counts, or a 100× larger volume?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Gave up: a single pipeline. Two paths cost engineering effort and cause advertisers to see numbers change. We chose that over unexplained billing. We also gave up per-event queries in the dashboard; they are served from rollups.
  • Freshness vs accuracy: the watermark is a dial. A shorter delay shows data faster and undercounts more; the batch fixes it.
  • Unique clicks / unique users per ad: exact distinct counts do not add across windows. Use HyperLogLog sketches for dashboards (chapter 12) and exact distinct in batch for billing. Sketches merge by union, which makes rollups possible.
  • Real-time budget capping (stop serving an ad once the daily budget is spent): this needs a low-latency counter read by the ad server, not the dashboard. A separate small, fast counter (sharded, approximate, per-region) with overshoot tolerated, and billing still comes from the exact path.
  • 100× volume (1 trillion clicks a day): the log, stream and OLAP tiers scale by partitioning; pre-aggregation at the edge becomes mandatory (count per ad per second at each edge). Raw storage and dedup state dominate cost: shorten the dedup window, sample raw detail, keep full raw only for billable clicks.
  • Dimension explosion: adding more group-by dimensions multiplies rows. Pre-aggregate only the common combinations; query the rest from raw data on demand.
  • Regulation and privacy: store hashed device identifiers, expire raw events on a schedule, and support deletion in the raw store (data protection).
  • Multi-region ingest: ingest locally, replicate logs to a central region for aggregation, accept inter-region lag in dashboards; billing is computed centrally.

12A model five-minute verbal summary

Explain the design in five minutes, ending on trade-offs.

Compare with a worked answer

Model answer, about 650 words spoken

"The hardest requirement is exact billing from an event stream that is duplicated, out of order and late. So the design has two consumers with different guarantees: a dashboard that wants numbers within a minute and tolerates small errors, and billing that wants exact numbers and tolerates hours.

Numbers: 10 billion clicks a day is 115 thousand a second on average and about 350 thousand at peak. Raw events are around 200 bytes, so 2 terabytes a day, which goes to object storage as columnar files and is the source of truth. Aggregates are tiny by comparison: minute counts per ad, with maybe 2% of ads active in a minute, about 0.3 billion rows a day and 12 gigabytes. A hot ad at 1% of peak is 3,500 clicks a second on one key.

Flow: a click goes to a stateless edge that verifies a signed token containing a system-generated click id, appends to a log partitioned by ad id, and redirects the user. The log is the buffer, so a downstream outage cannot lose clicks. A stream job deduplicates by click id with 24 hours of keyed state, applies cheap fraud rules and tags rather than deletes, counts per ad per minute in event-time windows, and upserts the results into a column-oriented store that serves dashboards, with rollups for long ranges.

Deep dives. Late events: the watermark is a dial between freshness and accuracy. I ran a simulation: with 30 seconds of lateness, duplicates were all removed but 2.65% of clicks arrived too late, which is why billing doesn't come from the stream. Exactly-once into the store: make the write idempotent, an upsert keyed by ad and window. In the simulation, replaying the writes doubled an incrementing sink but left the upsert unchanged. Hot ads: pre-aggregate at the edge or in the first stage, and salt keys for ads above a threshold, merging partial sums, because counting is associative; dedup happens first so exactness holds. Billing: a lambda-style nightly job recomputes from raw data, deduplicates, joins fraud labels, and writes a versioned ledger; it reconciles against the stream, and a divergence over a threshold pages us. The two paths share one library defining a valid click to avoid drift.

Failures: if the stream is down the log holds the backlog; a bug is repaired by replaying a range, which is safe because writes are idempotent. Trade-offs: two code paths and numbers that change slightly after the fact, in exchange for a bill we can explain. Saying it in an interview

The shape of a strong summary

Stage 12 has a model five-minute answer. Notice its shape: two consumers with different guarantees → numbers → log, stream, store → late events and idempotent sink → hot keys → exact batch path → failures → trade-offs.

Key takeaways

  • Two consumers want different guarantees: dashboards want fresh and approximate, billing wants exact and can wait. That justifies two paths.
  • A server-generated, signed click id makes duplicates detectable and forgeries rejectable.
  • A log between the edge and the processors absorbs spikes and outages, so no click is lost to downstream trouble.
  • A watermark trades freshness for completeness; the late tail is real (2.65% in the demo), so billing recomputes from raw data.
  • Make sinks idempotent: an upsert keyed by (ad, window) is safe to replay; an incrementing sink double-counts.
  • Pre-aggregate and salt hot keys; exact counts merge because addition is associative.
  • Reconcile stream against batch continuously; tag fraud, do not delete, and credit later findings in a new period.
