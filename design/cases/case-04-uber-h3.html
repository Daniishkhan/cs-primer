Uber's H3 Hexagonal Grid

A ride marketplace needs to ask the same question millions of times: how many riders and drivers are near here, right now? Uber's answer, published in the 2018 post "H3: Uber's Hexagonal Hierarchical Spatial Index", was to cut the globe into hexagonal cells at sixteen resolutions, give every cell a 64-bit index, and then do analytics and pricing on cells instead of on raw latitude and longitude. This case explains why hexagons, what "aperture 7" means, and why twelve pentagons are unavoidable.

The pattern in one sentence

Turn continuous space into a hierarchy of discrete cells whose neighbours are uniform, so that "near", "count in this area" and "smooth over neighbours" become integer lookups instead of geometry.

Context

The Uber post says marketplace events (ride requests, trip starts, food orders) are bucketed into cells for city-wide analysis, and that supply and demand are measured per cell in each city to set prices; it also names dispatch as a purpose and gives, as its example, detecting two ride requests near a uberPOOL driver. The post lists the alternatives it considered and why each fell short: square grids on a Mercator projection (significant size distortion and two kinds of neighbour), postal-code zones (irregular shapes and sizes that change for unrelated reasons), and zones drawn by operations staff (they need frequent updates and define edges arbitrarily). It does not discuss geohash or S2 in the page I fetched, so I do not compare H3 with them here (general: see the geohash and quadtree options in chapter 14).

Cell
One tile of the grid, identified by a 64-bit integer index.
Resolution
The size level of the grid; H3 has 16, numbered 0 (coarsest) to 15, per the post and docs.
Aperture
How many children a cell has at the next finer resolution. H3's is 7.
Base cell
A resolution-0 cell. There are 122 of them (110 hexagons, 12 pentagons).
Gnomonic projection
A map projection from the sphere's centre onto a flat plane; H3 uses one per face of an icosahedron (a 20-faced solid) placed around the Earth.

The mechanism: why hexagons

The post's argument is about movement and smoothing. People move continuously through a city; a grid whose cells have uniform neighbours produces less error from quantising their position as they cross cell boundaries, and a ring of cells approximates a radius well. Figure 6 of the post compares neighbour distances: a hexagon has one centre-to-neighbour distance, a square has two (edge neighbours and corner neighbours) and a triangle has three. The post says this greatly simplifies analysis and smoothing over gradients. The geometry is easy to check yourself, as in the toy below.

Code below is mine: it checks the neighbour-distance claim, checks a formula for the cell counts against the H3 resolution table, and shows the simplest analytics (a count per cell). It is not H3's code, and the cell names are placeholders.

"""Why hexagons, and what aperture 7 means (my arithmetic; checked against numbers in the H3 docs)."""
import math
# 1. Distances from a cell centre to its edge-or-corner neighbours, unit centre spacing for edge neighbours.
square = sorted(math.hypot(dx, dy) for dx in (-1, 0, 1) for dy in (-1, 0, 1) if (dx, dy) != (0, 0))
hexa = sorted({round(math.hypot(math.cos(math.radians(60 * k)), math.sin(math.radians(60 * k))), 6) for k in range(6)})
print("square neighbours:", len(square), "distinct distances:", sorted({round(d, 3) for d in square}))
print("hexagon neighbours: 6, distinct distances:", hexa)

# 2. Aperture 7: each step down has 7x the cells, 1/7 the area, edge length scaled by 1/sqrt(7).
def cells(res):                      # 12 pentagons + 110 hexagons at res 0, then 7x per level (+ pentagon bookkeeping)
    return 2 + 120 * 7 ** res
for r, doc in [(0, 122), (5, 2_016_842), (9, 4_842_432_842), (15, 569_707_381_193_162)]:
    print(f"res {r:2d}: formula {cells(r):>18,}  docs {doc:>18,}  match={cells(r) == doc}")
print("edge length ratio per level: 1/sqrt(7) =", round(1 / math.sqrt(7), 4), "| res 9 edge km:", 1281.256011 / math.sqrt(7) ** 9)

# 3. Bucketing events into cells (the simplest 'marketplace analytics': counts per cell).
from collections import Counter
events = [("req", "cell_A"), ("req", "cell_A"), ("driver", "cell_A"), ("req", "cell_B")]
by_cell = Counter((kind, cell) for kind, cell in events)
for (kind, cell), n in sorted(by_cell.items()): print(cell, kind, n)

The mechanism: aperture 7, 64-bit indexes and pentagons

H3 places a grid on each of the 20 faces of an icosahedron using gnomonic projections; the post says it does not unfold the icosahedron. Resolution 0 has 122 base cells, with ten per face in the post's counting (some cells belong to more than one face, so the per-face count does not sum to 122). Each finer resolution has one seventh the area of the coarser one, which the docs call aperture 7: seven children per cell, with unit length scaling by sqrt(7). Indexes are 64-bit integers (the post), and an ancestor is found by truncating the index, which the docs say takes only a few bitwise operations; nearby places also tend to have numerically close indexes.

Hexagons cannot be split exactly into seven smaller hexagons. The docs say H3 approximates the split by alternating the grid's orientation, so a child is only approximately inside its parent. The docs distinguish: containment within the index is exact (a child's parent is always the cell you truncate to), but in geography it is approximate, and exact boundaries need a point-in-polygon check. Cell borders at one resolution are not approximate.

Why pentagons? A solid made only of hexagons cannot close into a sphere; the post says twelve pentagons, one at each icosahedron vertex, are needed. The docs state there are exactly 12 pentagons at every resolution, centred on the icosahedron's vertices; the pages I read give no pentagon area, so I make no claim about how a pentagon compares in size with a hexagon. The post adds that the icosahedron is oriented (using R. Buckminster Fuller's orientation) so the vertices fall in water, keeping pentagons out of most land analysis. Cell sizes from the docs' table: resolution 5 has 2,016,842 cells with an average hexagon area of about 253 km2; resolution 9, about 4.84 billion cells and 0.105 km2 (average edge about 0.2 km); resolution 15 under one square metre. The post's compact-set example: California at resolution 6 is 10,633 hexagons uncompacted and 901 when compacted (using coarser cells wherever a whole parent fits).

How the marketplace uses cells

As the post states it: events are bucketed into cells so that analysts can look at a city as a map of counts; supply and demand are measured per hexagon to set prices; and cell sets can be clustered into neighbourhood-like shapes where checking membership is as efficient as a set lookup. Two further points are mine (general), not claims about Uber's internals: first, because cell neighbours are uniform, a smoothing step such as "average the surge signal with the cells next door" is symmetrical in every direction, which a square grid does not give; second, choosing the resolution is a product decision: too coarse and a price boundary crosses a whole neighbourhood, too fine and each cell has too few events for stable counts. For a full ride-matching design with nearest-driver queries, see chapter 14, and build a simpler index yourself in lab 5.

What they learned or changed

  • Put a stable grid between the world and the analysis. Instead of zones that change for unrelated reasons, a global fixed grid gives everyone the same cells, so datasets from different teams can join on the cell index (general).
  • Accept an approximate hierarchy for exact neighbours. H3 gives up perfect parent/child nesting to keep hexagons; the post and docs are explicit about the distortion.
  • Make the index an integer. A 64-bit key is cheap to store, join and shard; the hierarchy lives in the bits.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: tiling a city with a honeycomb

Imagine a honeycomb laid over a city map. You stop asking "how many drivers within 800 m of this exact point?" and ask "how many in this cell and its six neighbours?". Every neighbour is the same distance away, so no direction is favoured, and you can zoom out by looking at the larger cell that contains your cell.

Where it breaks down: a honeycomb tiles a flat sheet perfectly; a sphere forces twelve five-sided cells, and a big cell is not exactly the union of seven small ones.

Key takeaways

  • Discretise space into cells so that location becomes a key.
  • Hexagons have one neighbour distance, which makes rings and smoothing uniform.
  • Aperture 7: seven children, one seventh of the area, edge length divided by sqrt(7), 16 resolutions, 64-bit indexes.
  • Twelve pentagons per resolution are the price of covering a sphere.
  • Pick the resolution to match the decision: counts need enough events per cell, prices need cells small enough to be local.

Use it in an interview

Say: "I'd index locations into a hierarchical grid, so a driver's position is a cell ID. Nearby search is the cell plus its ring of neighbours, and analytics are counts per cell. I'd pick a cell size from the pickup radius, and prefer hexagons or a similar grid because every neighbour is the same distance away, which matters when I step outward ring by ring." Then say what you give up: "events are quantised to cells, and I'd check a precise distance on the shortlist."

Don't: name-drop a library or say "we'd use Uber's thing". Interviewers want the requirement-to-choice chain: a radius query at high update rates, uniform neighbours, cheap roll-ups. Be ready to compare with geohash prefixes and a quadtree, and to say that you would test the resolution against real densities. The hexagon fact worth citing is the uniform neighbour distance, which you can derive on the whiteboard.

Check your understanding

What is the key geometric argument for hexagons in the Uber post?

  1. Hexagons are easier to draw
  2. From a hexagon's centre all neighbours are at one distance; squares have two distances and triangles three
  3. Hexagons tile a sphere without any other shape
  4. Hexagons use fewer bits
Uniform neighbour distance simplifies analysis and smoothing; it does not remove the need for pentagons.

What does aperture 7 mean?

  1. Seven resolutions
  2. Each cell has seven children at the next finer resolution, each with one seventh the area
  3. Seven neighbours per cell
  4. Seven pentagons
Area per cell divides by 7 and edge length by sqrt(7) at each finer resolution.

Why does H3 contain exactly 12 pentagons at every resolution?

  1. Hexagons alone cannot close up around a sphere; one pentagon sits at each icosahedron vertex
  2. Pentagons are faster to compute
  3. Uber needed pentagons for airports
  4. It is a bug in the indexing
The icosahedron has 12 vertices; the docs and post state 12 pentagons per resolution.

How accurate is parent/child containment?

  1. Exact in geography
  2. Exact in the index, but only approximate in geography
  3. Exact only at resolution 0
  4. Not defined
The docs say logical containment in the index is exact; geographic containment is approximate, so exact boundaries need a point-in-polygon check.

According to the post, how are cells used for pricing?

  1. Each cell has a fixed price
  2. Supply and demand are measured per hexagon in each city to set prices
  3. Drivers choose their cells
  4. Prices depend on postal codes
The post says supply and demand are measured per hexagon; it gives no more detail on the pricing mechanism.
