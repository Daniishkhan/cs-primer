Trade-offs, Communication & Design Docs

A design only exists for others once you have communicated it. This chapter is about the human side of design: thinking aloud so people can follow your reasoning, driving a discussion without steamrolling it, answering "what if" challenges, admitting what you do not know, drawing diagrams people can read, and naming the trade-offs behind every choice. It also sketches how design skill grows from mid-level to staff, and how interviews are generally evaluated.

Why this matters

Two engineers can reach the same architecture and leave very different impressions: one narrates the questions, numbers and rejected alternatives; the other draws a finished diagram in silence. The first shows how they think and lets a colleague correct them early. Communication is also the real work of a design review at your job, where the audience decides whether your idea ships. The skills here are not packaging for the content; they are part of the content.

Think aloud, and signpost

The person listening cannot see your reasoning, only what you say. Silence reads as being stuck, and a conclusion without its reasons gives them nothing to evaluate or correct. So narrate: the question you are asking yourself, the options you see, and why you pick one.

Signposting means telling the listener where you are in the structure, so they can follow and interrupt at natural points. It costs a few words and buys a great deal of clarity:

  • "I will start by clarifying requirements, then estimate, then sketch the API and data, and spend most of our time on the hardest part." (an agenda, up front)
  • "That gives me the requirements. Let me confirm: reads are anonymous, deletes are rare. Is that right?" (a checkpoint)
  • "Next I will estimate; I will round aggressively." (a transition)
  • "There are three ways to generate ids. I will compare them, then pick one." (announcing a comparison)
  • "I will park the abuse question and come back to it in failure modes." (deliberately deferring)

Two further habits. Say your assumptions as assumptions ("I will assume 10 million daily users; tell me if that is off") so they can be challenged. And say the "so" after each number or fact ("700 reads per second, so one primary is fine"), because the connection is what shows judgment.

Driving versus following

In a design discussion there are two failure modes. Following too passively means waiting for the other person to tell you what to do next, and the discussion drifts. Driving too hard means monologuing through your plan, ignoring signals, and leaving no room for the other person's concerns. A senior engineer drives with consent.

Too passiveToo domineeringDriving well
Starting"Where should I begin?"Begins drawing at onceProposes an agenda and asks if it suits

InterruptionsAbandons the planTreats them as obstaclesFollows the new thread, then returns: "Back to where we were..."
Checking inNeverNeverPauses at transitions: "Does that match your expectations?"

If the interviewer or colleague steers you somewhere, treat it as information about what they care about, and go there. Drive the structure; follow the interest.

Handling "what if" pushback

"What if traffic is ten times higher?" "What if this node dies?" "What if we need strict ordering?" These are not attacks. They are the most efficient way for the other person to probe how well you understand your design. Some are asked to test depth, some to see whether you can adapt, and some because the real requirement just changed. A reliable four-part reply:

  1. Restate the change in numbers or scenarios ("Ten times is 7,000 reads a second at peak.").
  2. Locate the pressure point ("The first thing to break is the metadata database's read capacity, then cache memory.").
  3. Adapt the design minimally ("Add read replicas and shard the cache by id. The write path is unchanged.").
  4. State the new cost ("Replica lag means a fresh paste may be missing for a moment; we can pin the author's reads to the primary for a few seconds.").

Analogy: a stress test, not a trial

Picture an engineer inspecting a bridge. Asking "what if a truck twice as heavy crosses?" is not an accusation that the bridge is bad; it finds where the margin lies. A good designer answers the way a good structural engineer would: here is the member that carries the load, here is its limit, here is how we would strengthen it.

Where it breaks down: a bridge's physics is settled, whereas in design the "right" reinforcement depends on the requirement, so you often need to ask back: "do we need that, or is it a hypothetical?"

When the challenge exposes a real flaw, say so plainly and fix it: "You are right, the cache invalidation I described would leave stale data after a delete. Let me change that." Changing your design in response to a good point is a strength. Defending a flaw is the weakness.

Admitting uncertainty well

You will be asked about things you do not know or cannot recall exactly. How you handle that is a signal. Weak responses are bluffing (stating a guess as a fact), freezing, or dismissing the question. Strong ones separate what you know, what you can reason out, and what you would verify:

  • Say what you do know: "I have not used that database in production, but I know the family: LSM-based, partitioned by key."
  • Reason from first principles: "If it is LSM-based, writes should be cheap and compaction will cost reads, so I would watch read amplification."
  • Mark confidence: "I believe the default replication factor is three, but I would check."
  • Say how you would find out: "I would benchmark with our actual key distribution before committing."

Numbers deserve the same honesty. "Roughly 100,000 simple operations per second per node, give or take a factor of three" is better than a precise figure you invented. Chapter 2 showed why the error bars are large. State them.

Drawing clear diagrams

A diagram is a communication tool, not a record of your knowledge. A good one lets someone understand the flow in ten seconds and lets you point at it while you talk.

Habits that help:

  • Left to right (or top to bottom) for the request path, clients first, storage last.
  • Label arrows with what flows ("order event", "read by id") and, where you have it, how much.
  • Name boxes by role, and add the technology only when it matters. "Metadata store" says more than the product name.
  • Distinguish kinds of things consistently: databases, queues, caches, external systems. Colour on a whiteboard is a luxury; shapes or a legend work. This course uses green clients, blue stateless services, purple stores, orange queues and logs, cyan caches, grey externals and red for failure.
  • Draw incrementally. Start with the minimal path, then add components as you justify them. A diagram that grows with the argument is easier to follow than one dropped in complete.
  • Number the steps of a flow so you can say "step 3" and be understood.
  • Leave room. You will need to add a box, and cramming the first draft makes the rest unreadable.

Free tools such as Excalidraw make this fast; whichever you use, practise until drawing a box does not interrupt your sentence (see the Toolbox page).

Common trade-off axes

Every design choice sits on some axis between two goods. Naming the axis, your position on it, and the reason is the form of a trade-off statement. These come up repeatedly; the links show where each was developed.

A usable template for any choice: "We chose A over B because of requirement R and number N. It costs us C. If R changes to R′, or N grows to N′, we would revisit." Keep it to that. Longer trade-off monologues are usually padding.

Writing a design doc

The same reasoning, written down, is a design doc. The architecture course covers the craft in depth, including decision records, alternatives and templates in documenting decisions, so we will not repeat it. What matters here is how a doc relates to a live design discussion:

  • The eight framework steps are a natural skeleton: context and requirements, estimates, interfaces, data, architecture, hard parts, risks and operations, alternatives and open questions.
  • A doc has to stand alone, so lead with the decision you need and the context a cold reader lacks; in a discussion you can read the room and fill in gaps.
  • Alternatives considered are the most valuable section, because they record why not, which is what a future maintainer will otherwise re-argue.
  • Record open questions and risks explicitly. A doc that claims certainty it does not have loses trust.
  • Keep it short enough to be read. A one-page doc that gets reviewed beats a thirty-page one that does not.

The Toolbox has a one-page and a full template.

Mid-level, senior, staff: what changes

An opinion, not a rubric

Companies define levels differently and I am not describing any particular one. What follows is my view, from seeing designs reviewed, of how answers tend to differ as the person grows. Treat it as a way to notice what to practise, not as a scorecard.

DimensionMid-level answerSenior answerStaff-level answer
Scope and ambiguityAsks a few questions, then builds what the prompt describesShapes the problem: identifies the core flow and the hardest requirement, proposes scopeQuestions whether this is the right problem, ties it to the product goal, and splits it into phases
DesignReasonable components in a sensible arrangementComponents derived from numbers; the simplest design that worksConsiders the organisation: team boundaries, migration path from today's system, who owns what
DepthCorrect on well-known partsGoes deep on the hard part, compares options, knows the failure modes of the chosen oneSpots the subtle risks (correctness under failure, hot spots, data growth) and the unknown unknowns, and proposes how to learn about them
Trade-offsNames some when askedVolunteers them, with what would change the decisionFrames them as options with cost and reversibility; recommends the cheapest experiment
OperationsMentions monitoringSLOs, alerts, rollout, runbooks, blast radiusCost to run, on-call burden, long-term maintenance, deprecation
Business senseBuilds what is askedRelates choices to user impact and costWeighs engineering effort against value; says "do not build this yet" when right

Notice what is not on the list: knowing more technologies. The differences are mostly in scope, judgment about risk, and awareness of context. A mid-level engineer can know every box in the catalogue; a senior one knows when each is the wrong answer; a staff one knows when the whole project is.

A weak and a strong exchange

Here is a short stretch of the same conversation done two ways. The prompt is the pastebin from chapter 1, and the interviewer has just asked about storage.

Weak

Interviewer: How would you store the pastes?

Candidate: I would use MongoDB, because it scales well and it is flexible.

Interviewer: What if reads go up a hundredfold?

Candidate: We add more servers and scale horizontally.

Interviewer: What breaks first?

Candidate: ... I would probably need to shard. Or use Kafka?

Strong

Interviewer: How would you store the pastes?

Candidate: The access is lookup by id, about 700 reads a second at peak, 10 KB each, 11 TB a year. I would split it: a relational table for metadata, since we need the delete token and expiry rules to be transactional, and object storage for the bodies, which are large and immutable.

Interviewer: What if reads go up a hundredfold?

Candidate: That is 70,000 reads a second. The metadata database would be first to struggle, so I would put a cache in front, which a 20 to 1 read ratio makes effective, and add replicas. Bodies would move behind a CDN. The cost is staleness after deletes, which I would bound with a short TTL.

Interviewer: What if deletes must be instant?

Candidate: Then the CDN cannot hold private content for long, or we need purge calls. I would ask how strict "instant" has to be before paying for that.

What made the second better? It used numbers from the earlier steps, split the problem by access pattern, located the first pressure point, adapted the design minimally, stated the cost, and asked a clarifying question instead of guessing. None of it required deeper knowledge than the first answer; it required structure.

How interviews are generally evaluated

Every company runs its own process and I will not claim to know any specific rubric. In general terms, design interviews tend to look for a few broad things, and you can see how this chapter and the framework map onto them:

  • Handling ambiguity: do you ask good questions, state assumptions, and define scope?
  • Technical reasoning: are the numbers sensible, the choices justified, the design able to meet its requirements?
  • Depth: can you go beyond a diagram into the hard part and handle follow-ups?
  • Trade-offs and judgment: do you see alternatives, costs and failure modes?
  • Communication and collaboration: is your thinking easy to follow, and do you respond well to feedback?

The same list is a reasonable description of how a design review at work goes, which is why practising for one improves the other. Do not try to game it. The best preparation is learning to design well and to explain it clearly.

Try it yourself

  1. Record yourself solving the pastebin (or any prompt) for 20 minutes. Count the pauses over five seconds and the "so" statements. Aim for fewer of the first and more of the second.
  2. Ask a friend to throw three "what if" challenges at your design. Reply using the four-part pattern (restate, locate, adapt, state the cost).
  3. Take a diagram you drew earlier and redraw it with every arrow labelled and every box named by role. Notice what you could not label: that is something you did not understand yet.
  4. Write your pastebin design as a one-page doc using the Toolbox template, with a section for alternatives considered.

Distributed systems connection

Many trade-offs are the distributed-systems trade-offs in plain clothes: "consistency versus latency" is the tension behind impossibility results, and "durable versus fast" is the cost of synchronous replication. Having the vocabulary (linearizable, quorum, at-least-once, idempotent) lets you state a trade-off in one precise sentence instead of three vague ones, which is a communication skill as much as a technical one.

Key takeaways

  • Think aloud and signpost: state the agenda, your assumptions, the options, the choice and the "so". Silence hides your reasoning.
  • Drive with consent: propose the structure, follow the other person's interest, and return to the plan afterwards.
  • Answer "what if" by restating in numbers, locating the first pressure point, adapting minimally, and naming the new cost.
  • Handle uncertainty by separating what you know, what you can reason out and what you would verify. Never bluff a number.
  • Draw for the listener: roles, labelled arrows, numbers, built up incrementally.
  • State trade-offs as "A over B because of R and N; it costs C; we would revisit if...".
  • In my view, levels differ less in knowledge than in scope, risk judgment, operations and business sense. Interviews, in general terms, probe ambiguity handling, reasoning, depth, trade-offs and communication.

Check your understanding

An interviewer asks "what if traffic grows 10x?" Which reply is strongest?

  1. "We scale horizontally."
  2. "I would redesign it with microservices."
  3. "That is 7,000 reads a second at peak. The metadata database is the first thing to strain, so I would add a cache and replicas; the cost is brief staleness."
  4. "That would not happen."
The strong reply restates the change as a number, locates the pressure point, adapts the design minimally and names the new cost. The others assert without reasoning.

You are asked about a technology you have not used. What is the best approach?

  1. Say what you know about its family, reason from first principles about what that implies, mark your confidence and say how you would verify
  2. Guess confidently so you seem expert
  3. Say you cannot answer and stop
  4. Change the subject
Separating knowledge, reasoning and verification plan shows honesty and competence. Bluffing risks being caught and erodes trust in everything else you said.

Which is a well-formed trade-off statement?

  1. "There are trade-offs between speed and consistency."
  2. "We use a cache because everyone does."
  3. "Consistency and availability cannot both be had."
  4. "We chose asynchronous replicas over synchronous for lower write latency; the cost is that a crash can lose the last few writes, and we would revisit if the data became financial."
A good statement names the choice, the reason, the cost and the condition that would change it, all specific to the design. The others are generic or unreasoned.

What does "driving with consent" mean in a design discussion?

  1. Never letting the other person interrupt
  2. Proposing the structure and where to go deep, checking in at transitions, and following their interest when they steer
  3. Waiting to be told each next step
  4. Skipping the agenda to save time
Driving supplies structure; consent keeps the discussion collaborative. Both pure passivity and pure monologue are failure modes.

Why is the "alternatives considered" section often the most valuable part of a design doc?

  1. It makes the doc longer
  2. It shows the author is thorough
  3. It records why other options were rejected, which future maintainers would otherwise have to re-argue
  4. It is required by every company
The chosen design is visible in the system itself, but the reasons for not choosing alternatives disappear unless written down.
