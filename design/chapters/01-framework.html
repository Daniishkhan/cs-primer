A Framework for Design Problems

An open-ended problem and a blank whiteboard are paralysing until you have a sequence to follow. This chapter gives you one: eight steps, in a deliberate order, with a time budget for a 45 to 60 minute session. For each step you get why it comes where it does, the mistakes people make, and what a good version looks like. It ends with a pastebin designed start to finish in miniature, and a timer you can practise with.

Why this matters

Most weak design answers fail on sequence, not on knowledge: jumping to databases before knowing the read rate, drawing for twenty minutes and never discussing failure, or going deep on the wrong part. A framework does not replace thinking; it spends your attention in the right order so that each step produces the inputs the next one needs. Treat it as scaffolding you will eventually stop noticing, the way a pilot stops noticing the checklist.

The eight steps, and the clock

The order is not arbitrary. Each step consumes the output of the previous one: requirements bound the estimate; the estimate tells you which problems are real; the API and data model fix what the system must do and store; the high-level design arranges components to meet the numbers; deep dives spend leftover time where the risk is; failure and trade-offs test the result. Skipping ahead means guessing at an input.

Real design reviews at work look different again (more writing, more asynchronous discussion, see chapter 4), but the thinking order is the same.

Functional and non-functional requirements

Functional requirements say what the system does: "a user can post a paste and share a link". Non-functional requirements (sometimes "quality attributes") say how well: how fast, how available, how consistent, how durable, how secure, how cheap. Functional requirements decide which endpoints and entities exist. Non-functional requirements decide almost everything else: the architecture is mostly a response to them. Two systems with identical features but different non-functional needs (a pastebin versus a payments ledger) end up with very different designs.

Turning vague words into numbers

Requirements arrive as adjectives. Your first job is to convert each into something you could test. Ask, or assume and state, a number.

The numbers do not have to be true; they have to be stated. A stated assumption can be challenged and adjusted ("we will have 100M users, not 10M"), and when it changes you know exactly which later decisions to revisit. An unstated assumption silently makes the whole design wrong.

Workload shapes

Beyond raw size, the shape of traffic steers the architecture. Identify which of these describes your problem; most real systems are a mix, but one usually dominates.

Step 1: Clarify requirements and scope

Why first. Everything downstream depends on what you are building. "Design a chat app" could mean a two-person messenger or a global platform with media, search and bots, and the right designs differ by orders of magnitude. Spending the first five minutes on questions is not stalling; it is the cheapest way to avoid designing the wrong system.

What good looks like.

  • A few high-leverage questions, grouped: who uses it and for what (the core flow); scale (users, volume); quality (latency, availability, consistency, durability); constraints (existing systems, budget, regions).
  • You propose answers, not only questions: "I will assume 10M daily users and anonymous reads; tell me if that is off."
  • You write down the agreed requirements and an explicit out of scope list, then read them back.
  • You identify the single hardest requirement ("this is essentially a read-heavy problem with a strict expiry rule") and say so. It will guide the rest.

Common mistakes. Asking twenty questions with no priority, so the interviewer drowns and you never get to design. Asking none and silently assuming. Treating the prompt as a feature list instead of finding the core flow. Forgetting non-functional requirements entirely, which is the single most common omission.

Step 2: Estimate

Why here. The estimate is how requirements become engineering constraints. It tells you whether one machine suffices, whether you are bound by QPS, storage or bandwidth, and whether caching can pay off. Doing it before the architecture is what makes the architecture derived rather than asserted. Chapter 2 is entirely about doing this well; here we just place it.

What good looks like. Round numbers, assumptions spoken aloud, units on everything, and each result followed by "so...": "700 reads per second at peak, so one database could almost serve this, but a cache will make latency predictable". Aim for the answer to be right within a factor of ten and keep it to about four minutes.

Common mistakes. False precision ("231.48 QPS"). Computing everything and using none of it. Forgetting the peak factor, replication, or that "per second" and "per day" differ by 105. Doing arithmetic silently while the interviewer watches; narrate it.

Step 3: API

Why before data. The API is the contract, and it names the nouns and verbs your data model must support. Sketching three to five endpoints forces you to decide what operations really exist, which in turn shows the access patterns: "read by id" versus "list by user, newest first" imply different storage. Architecture course chapter 9 has the craft; here you need only the essentials.

What good looks like. Method, path, key parameters, response codes; pagination for lists (cursor-based rather than offset for large data); idempotency keys on anything a client might retry (safe retries); a word on authentication and rate limiting. Mention the read path and write path separately.

Common mistakes. Designing twenty endpoints. Making creation non-idempotent so a timeout produces duplicates. Returning unbounded lists. Mixing internal service calls with public API without saying which is which.

Step 4: Data model

Why now. You know the operations (API) and the sizes (estimate), so you can choose how to store data. The key question is the access pattern: how is each entity looked up, how often, how big, and how long does it live? Store choice follows from that, as choosing storage argued.

What good looks like. A short list of entities with primary keys; a stated partition key if the data will not fit on one node, plus a note on whether it could become hot; separation of small, structured, queryable metadata from large blobs; a reason for each store ("relational for metadata because we need transactions on the owner relationship; object storage for bodies because they are large and immutable"). Mention indexes you will need for the listed access patterns.

Common mistakes. Choosing a database by brand. Putting everything in one store without discussing size. Sharding at 200 writes per second. Forgetting that the partition key decides both load balance and which queries are cheap.

A tiny example of deriving a number for the data model: how long must a pastebin key be? With 1 million pastes a day for ten years, we can compute rather than guess.

"""How long must a pastebin key be? Derive it from a number, not a habit."""
import math

pastes_per_day = 1_000_000
years = 10
total = pastes_per_day * 365 * years
print(f"pastes over {years} years: {total:,}")

for length in (6, 7, 8):
    space = 62 ** length                    # base62: a-z A-Z 0-9
    used = total / space
    # chance a random key collides with an existing one when we are full to `used`
    print(f"base62 length {length}: {space:,} keys, {used:.4%} used at the end")

# random 7-char keys: expected retries per insert near the end of life
space = 62 ** 7
p_collision = total / space
print(f"collision chance per insert at end of life: {p_collision:.2%}"
      f" -> {1/(1-p_collision):.3f} attempts on average")
print(f"bits needed for a counter: {math.ceil(math.log2(total))}")
pastes over 10 years: 3,650,000,000
base62 length 6: 56,800,235,584 keys, 6.4260% used at the end
base62 length 7: 3,521,614,606,208 keys, 0.1036% used at the end
base62 length 8: 218,340,105,584,896 keys, 0.0017% used at the end
collision chance per insert at end of life: 0.10% -> 1.001 attempts on average
bits needed for a counter: 32

Seven base62 characters leave a 0.1% collision chance even at end of life, so random keys with a unique constraint and a retry are a sound, simple choice. Six would be 62 times fuller and still workable; eight is wasteful. The number made the decision.

Step 5: High-level design

Why now. With requirements, numbers, interfaces and data in hand, the boxes mostly place themselves. Drawing earlier produces decoration; drawing now produces a diagram where every component answers a stated need.

What good looks like.

  • The smallest design that meets the numbers. If one primary and a cache suffice, say so; do not add a message bus for the look of it.
  • Boxes labelled by role, arrows labelled by what flows, and the colours or notation explained.
  • A walk through the write path and the read path in sentences, pointing at the diagram and quoting numbers.
  • Stateless services in front, state behind them, and a clear statement of where each piece of state lives (chapter 3 catalogues the blocks).

Common mistakes. The "everything" diagram with 20 boxes and no flow. Drawing the target architecture for 100x scale, then having no time for the part that matters. Boxes whose purpose you cannot state in a sentence. Silence while drawing: narrate (chapter 4).

Step 6: Deep dives

Why it takes the most time. This is where the evaluator learns whether you can really engineer. Every design has one or two hard problems: how to avoid double-selling a seat, how to fan out a celebrity's post, how to generate unique ids across regions. A broad diagram that never goes deep shows only vocabulary.

How to choose what to dive into. Pick by risk and by the interviewer's interest: the requirement that is hardest to meet, the part where the naive approach breaks at your numbers, or the part the interviewer asks about. Say it aloud: "The riskiest part is the hot-key problem for popular pastes; I will go deep on that unless you would prefer something else."

What good looks like. Two or three options, each with a one-line mechanism and its price, then a recommendation and "I would change my mind if...". Use the vocabulary of earlier courses and link ideas in your head to their chapters: consistent hashing, quorum, idempotency, outbox, fencing, backpressure. A comparison table is the classic form, and that is how the worked designs present them.

Common mistakes. Diving into something unimportant because you know it well. Presenting one option as if it were the only one. Reciting a textbook algorithm without tying it to the numbers. Failing to say what you would measure to confirm the choice.

Step 7: Failure modes and operations

Why it is a separate step. Designs are usually drawn for the happy path. Real systems spend much of their life partly broken, so a design that does not say what happens when a node, a dependency, a region or a deploy goes wrong is not finished. The operational view (SLOs, alerting, rollout) is what separates a diagram from a service. See the reliability course.

What good looks like. Walk the diagram and ask, for each box, "what if this is down, slow, or wrong?" State the blast radius and the degraded mode. Name one or two SLIs and an SLO (SLOs and error budgets), the alert you would page on (burn-rate alerts), and how you would ship changes safely (canary rollouts). Mention timeouts, retries with budgets, and load shedding (resilience patterns). For anything security-sensitive, name the trust boundaries and the abuse cases.

Common mistakes. Saying "we replicate it, so it is highly available" and stopping. Ignoring correlated failure (the replicas share a bug, a region, a deploy). Describing recovery that depends on the thing that broke. Omitting this step because time ran out; keep four minutes in reserve.

Step 8: Trade-offs and wrap-up

Why last. Now you can evaluate the design you actually made. Trade-offs are the evidence of judgment: what you chose, what you gave up, and what would make you choose differently. End deliberately; do not trail off.

What good looks like. Three short points: what you optimised for and what that cost ("we accept seconds of staleness for cheap reads"); what changes at 10x or with a new requirement ("if deletes must be instant, we drop CDN caching"); the biggest remaining risks. Then a three-sentence summary and an invitation: "Where would you like to probe?"

Common mistakes. Claiming the design has no downsides. Listing generic trade-offs ("consistency versus availability") without connecting them to this system. Ending mid-thought.

The framework applied: a pastebin in miniature

Here is the whole framework on a deliberately small problem: "Design a pastebin, where users paste text and share a link." Use the step buttons to move through the eight phases. Notice that each panel uses what the previous ones produced: the estimate says QPS is tiny, so the design has no queue and no shard; the id analysis from step 4 settles the deep dive in step 6.

Step through

A pastebin, end to end

  1. Clarify. Five minutes of questions produce a short, testable list, including what is out of scope. The assumptions are stated, so they can be challenged.
  2. Estimate. About 35 writes/s and 700 reads/s at peak. QPS is not the problem, so no sharding; storage grows about 11 TB/year with replicas, so plan for it.
  3. API. Three endpoints. The create call carries an idempotency key, because clients on bad networks retry and we do not want duplicate pastes.
  4. Data model. Metadata is small and queryable; bodies are blobs. The id is both the primary key and the partition key, and random ids spread load evenly.
  5. High-level design. Reads try the cache, then metadata and object store; writes go to the metadata database and object store. The CDN fronts popular pastes. This is the smallest design that meets the numbers.
  6. Deep dive. The one real decision is the id. Three options compared in one line each, a pick, and the condition that would change it.
  7. Failure and operations. Walk each box: what if it is down or slow? What do we alert on? Note the degraded mode for each.
  8. Trade-offs and wrap-up. What we gave up, what changes at 100x, and the main open risk (abuse). Then summarise in three sentences.

A real session would add more detail at each step, and the pastebin is easy. The structure, though, is the same for a payment system. In the worked designs from chapter 5 on, you will see exactly this sequence with the depth turned up.

Practise with the timer

This timer paces a full session. Pick a problem (try "design a URL shortener" or any prompt from the worked designs), start the clock, talk aloud, and press Next phase each time you move on. At the end you get planned versus actual minutes, which tends to reveal your habit: most people overspend on clarifying or on the diagram and starve failure modes. The "fast demo" box runs it thirty times quicker so you can see how it behaves.

Try it yourself

Run the timer with a friend for 45 minutes on "design a pastebin". Your friend plays the interviewer and may answer questions only with numbers or yes/no. Afterwards compare the timer summary with your own sense of where time went. Then repeat with "design a URL shortener"; it should feel faster and you should find yourself reusing the id analysis.

Go deeper: adapting the framework optional

The sequence flexes. For a storage-engine style question ("design a key-value store") the data model and deep dives dominate and the API is trivial. For a workflow question (payments, booking) the failure modes belong inside the deep dive, not at the end, because correctness under failure is the design. For an ML system ("design a recommender") add an explicit step for data and model lifecycle (see chapter 20). For a design review at work, step 1 is a written problem statement and step 8 becomes "alternatives considered" in a design doc (documenting decisions). The skill is knowing why each step exists so that you can reorder with intent.

Distributed systems connection

Step 1's non-functional requirements are where the theory bites. "Highly available" and "strongly consistent" can conflict during a partition (impossibility results), and your answer to "may a user see stale data?" decides whether you can use asynchronous replicas (replication in practice), quorums, or a consensus-backed store (coordination services). Make that conversation explicit in step 1 and every later step gets easier.

Key takeaways

  • Eight steps, in order: clarify, estimate, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, failure and operations, trade-offs. Each consumes the previous step's output.
  • Turn vague words ("scalable", "real-time", "available") into stated numbers. A stated assumption can be corrected; a silent one cannot.
  • Identify the workload shape (read-heavy, write-heavy, bursty, fan-out, skewed, bandwidth-bound, compute-bound, correctness-critical) early. It steers the architecture more than raw size does.
  • Draw the smallest design that meets your numbers, then spend the largest block of time (about a quarter to a third) on the one or two hard parts.
  • Reserve time for failure modes and trade-offs. They are the clearest evidence of judgment and the most often skipped.
  • Narrate: assumptions, numbers, options, the choice and what would change it.

Check your understanding

Why does estimation come before the high-level design?

  1. Interviewers like arithmetic
  2. The numbers show which problems are real at this scale, so the architecture is derived from them instead of guessed
  3. You cannot draw boxes without knowing exact QPS
  4. It lets you skip the API step
Estimates tell you whether one machine suffices, whether you are limited by QPS, storage or bandwidth, and whether a cache pays off. Exact numbers are not needed, only the right order of magnitude, but you do need them before choosing components.

A product manager says the system must be "real-time". What is the best response?

  1. Add WebSockets everywhere
  2. Use a message queue
  3. Say that real-time is expensive and ask for a bigger budget
  4. Ask what delay is acceptable at which percentile, and propose a number to confirm
"Real-time" might mean under 200 ms or within a minute, and those lead to completely different designs. Converting it to a testable number comes first; technology follows.

Peak traffic is 700 reads/s and 35 writes/s with 11 TB per year of replicated data. Which design choice is best supported by these numbers?

  1. One database primary with a cache and plenty of disk planning; no sharding yet
  2. Shard the database into 64 partitions immediately
  3. A global multi-master deployment
  4. A stream-processing cluster for every write
These rates are within a single well-tuned primary, so sharding adds complexity without a requirement. Storage will grow, which is a reason to put bodies in object storage and plan capacity, not a reason to shard on day one.

With 12 minutes of deep-dive time, how should you choose the topic?

  1. The component you know best
  2. The one with the most boxes
  3. The riskiest or hardest part at your numbers, ideally agreed with the interviewer
  4. Whichever comes first on your diagram
Deep dives exist to show you can handle the hard part. Pick the requirement the naive design fails, say why, and check the interviewer agrees. Diving where you are comfortable is a common way to waste the most valuable minutes.

Which is the best example of a trade-off statement at the wrap-up?

  1. "Everything is scalable and consistent."
  2. "We accept up to a cache-TTL of stale content after a delete to keep reads cheap; if deletes must be instant we would drop CDN caching and pay more at the origin."
  3. "There are trade-offs between consistency and availability."
  4. "We could have used a different database."
A good trade-off statement is specific to the design, names what was given up, and says what would change the decision. The generic statements apply to every system and show no judgment.

Which workload shape most directly suggests precomputing results rather than computing on read?

  1. Write-heavy ingestion
  2. Compute-bound batch
  3. Read-heavy with fan-out, where the same answer is read many times
  4. Correctness-critical transfers
When the same result is read far more often than it changes, computing it once and storing it saves work, at the cost of storage and staleness. The worked news-feed design is built around exactly this trade.
