Design a Distributed Job Scheduler

Delayed and recurring jobs, leases, retries, priorities and at-least-once execution. "Run this later" sounds like a timer; at scale it is a durable store, a due-time index, a lease protocol and a fairness policy, and the interesting bugs live where those meet.

Why this matters

Almost every product ends up with a scheduler: send the reminder in 24 hours, retry the webhook, expire the hold, rebuild the report every night. It is a small system whose correctness story is subtle. A job must not be lost when a machine dies, must not run in two places as though it were alone, and a stampede of "midnight" jobs must not take down the thing that serves customers. It is also the cleanest place to apply leases and fencing, at-least-once with idempotent effects and leader election to one concrete problem.

The problem

"Design a service that lets teams run work at a later time or on a schedule. They submit a job, say when it should run, and the system runs it reliably. Some jobs recur. It has to survive machines dying."

"Reliably" hides the whole design. Does it mean "never lose a job", "never run a job twice", or "run it within a second of its time"? You cannot have the second without paying for it, and the third conflicts with the second under failures. We pin these down below using the framework.

Clarify requirements

QuestionAssumed answer
Kinds of job?Three: one-off (run as soon as possible), delayed (run at a given time, minutes to months ahead) and recurring (cron expressions, with a time zone).
Who runs the work?Our fleet of workers calls the tenant's handler (an HTTP endpoint or a registered function). The scheduler decides when and where; the handler owns what.
Scale?About 500M executions per day across 20,000 tenants (teams or customers), a few thousand cron entries per busy tenant, handlers that take about 2 s on average and rarely more than a few minutes.
Timing accuracy?A job starts within about 1 s of its due time at p99 when the system is healthy, and we expose the lag as a metric. Not hard real time.
Delivery semantics?At-least-once. A job may run more than once after a failure, so handlers must be idempotent. We provide a stable idempotency key per scheduled run.
Retries?Configurable per job: attempts, exponential backoff, then a dead-letter state a human can inspect and redrive.
Priorities and tenants?Several priority classes; one tenant must not be able to starve the others.
Durability?An accepted job survives the loss of any single machine or zone. Acknowledge the submit only after it is durably stored.

Functional. Submit, cancel, reschedule and inspect a job; create and edit cron schedules; run handlers with retries; expose status and history; dead-letter and redrive.

Non-functional. No accepted job is lost; start-time lag p99 under 1 s; the control plane (submit, status) stays available when the data plane is backed up; tail behaviour at round times such as the top of the hour is bounded; fairness across tenants; every worker failure is recoverable without a human.

Out of scope. Multi-step workflow engines with branching and compensation (that is sagas, built on top of a scheduler), sandboxing untrusted code (see the code-judge studio), and a fully general batch cluster scheduler. We do not promise exactly-once execution, because it is not achievable at the boundary with the outside world; we promise exactly-once triggering for cron and effectively-once effects via idempotency.

Estimate

The numbers to extract are the steady rate, the size of the pending set (which decides whether a database index is enough), the size of the "due soon" window (which decides what must sit in memory), the worker fleet, and the size of the top-of-the-hour herd. Reading the output. About 5,800 executions per second on average and 17,000 at an ordinary peak is a modest rate by the standards of other chapters; the store has to take roughly 29,000 writes per second on average (insert, claim, a heartbeat, complete), which is within reach of a sharded SQL or wide-column store but not of one primary, so the job table is partitioned. The pending set is the surprise: most pending rows are long-dated ones (my assumption: 5% of jobs are due about a month out), around 770 million rows or a few hundred gigabytes. That is a perfectly fine disk workload and an awkward memory workload, which is exactly why only the next minute or so of due jobs, about 350,000 rows or under 200 MB, should live in the in-memory index. The worker arithmetic is Little's law (concurrency = rate × time in system): 16,500 busy slots on average, so a few hundred hosts, and about 775 at the peak. And the last block is the one to remember: two million cron jobs sharing 0 * * * * start in the same second unless we spread them. Spread over five minutes the same work is a calm 6,700 starts per second.

  • Submit is idempotent, via an idempotency key, for the usual reason: a client that times out must be able to retry without creating two jobs (API design).
  • The claim returns a fence. A strictly increasing number per job, bumped on every claim. Every later call from the worker carries it, and the store rejects calls with an old fence. This is the whole defence against a worker that was paused and wakes up after its lease was reassigned.
  • run_key is stable across retries. It is derived from the job (or, for cron, from the cron id and the scheduled fire time), so a handler can use it as the idempotency key for its own side effects.
  • Misfire policy is explicit on crons, because "what happens if the scheduler was down at 09:00" has three defensible answers (Deep dive 4).

Data model

  • Jobs table (the durable source of truth), partitioned by job_id hash: job_id, tenant, queue, priority, due_at, state, attempts, fence, lease_until, owner, run_key, payload_ref. States: scheduled → ready → running → done, with running → scheduled (retry, with a new due_at) and running → dead. A secondary index on (partition, state, due_at) serves the due scan. Large payloads go to object storage and the row keeps a reference.
  • Cron table: cron_id, tenant, expr, tz, template, misfire, last_fire_time. Tiny compared with jobs (tens of thousands of rows).
  • Run history: append-only, job_id, attempt, worker, started, ended, outcome, in a cheaper store with a retention period. Kept apart so the hot table stays small.
  • Which store? The jobs table needs conditional updates ("claim if still queued"), a range index on due time and cheap horizontal partitioning. A partitioned SQL database or a wide-column store with lightweight transactions fits (sharding). A pure message queue does not, as Deep dive 1 explains. A coordination service such as ZooKeeper or etcd is for the handful of leases and leader keys, not for hundreds of millions of rows.

High-level design

The central choice: the database is the truth and every queue is a cache of it. If the due index or a ready queue is lost, the scheduler rebuilds it by scanning rows with state in (scheduled, ready, running). That makes recovery boring, and it is why we do not treat a message broker as the system of record: a broker that loses messages loses jobs, while a derived queue that loses messages only costs a rescan. The life of one job:

Step through

One job, from submit to done

  1. Submit. The API validates, stores the row with due_at and state scheduled, and only then acknowledges. Durability is decided here.
  2. Load. The partition scheduler that owns this slice of the table keeps an in-memory index of jobs due within the next minute or so, refilled by a range scan on (partition, state, due_at). Jobs due next month are not in memory at all.
  3. Release. When the clock reaches due_at the scheduler moves the id into the ready queue for its priority and tenant (Deep dive 4). Releasing is idempotent; the row, not the queue, says what is real.
  4. Claim. A worker takes an item and does a conditional update in the store: set running, owner, lease_until = now + L, and fence = fence + 1, only if the job is still claimable. The returned fence is the worker's proof of ownership.
  5. Heartbeat. While the handler runs, the worker extends the lease every L/3 seconds, carrying its fence. A long job survives; a dead worker stops heartbeating.
  6. Complete. The worker reports success with its fence; the store accepts it only if the fence still matches. State becomes done (or scheduled with a new due time on failure).
  7. The crash path. If the worker dies, heartbeats stop, the lease expires, and the job becomes claimable again with a higher fence. If the old worker was merely slow and wakes up, its calls carry fence 1 and are rejected.

Deep dive 1: the durable store versus a queue, and the due-time index

A scheduler is not "a queue with a delay". Queues are optimised for order of arrival; a scheduler needs order of due time, and due times are inserted wildly out of order (one job in a minute, the next in a month). Cancelling or rescheduling a job in the middle is also ordinary here, which most brokers do not support well. Hence a store of record plus an index on due time, with queues only as the last hop.

Recommendation: the last row, because the estimate told us the pending set is hundreds of millions of rows but the hot window is a few hundred thousand. You get the durability of a database without having every poller hammer the head of a global index.

The timing wheel and bucketed due times

A timing wheel (Varghese and Lauck, 1987) is a circular array of slots, one per tick. A timer due d ticks from now goes into slot (now + d) mod W with a rounds counter d div W; each tick, the wheel advances one slot, decrements the rounds of everything in it and fires what reaches zero. Insert is O(1) and each tick touches only one slot (the hashed-wheel variant with a rounds counter, in the paper's terms), compared with O(log n) per insert for a heap. At scheduler scale the same idea appears as time buckets in the database: bucket = floor(due_at / 60 s) is stored in the row, indexed, and the scheduler scans whole buckets one minute ahead.

This is a toy single-level wheel with eight slots, so you can see rounds in action, followed by the hash that partitions jobs across schedulers. Real implementations use larger wheels or several stacked ones (seconds, minutes, hours), and the single level is already enough to see why insert and fire are cheap.

The idea

  1. add(job, due): slot ← due mod 8; rounds ← delay ÷ 8
  2. tick: advance; fire jobs with rounds = 0, decrement the rest
  3. partition ← crc32(job id) mod 4; count jobs per partition
"""A single-level timing wheel (bucketed due-time index) with a rounds
counter, plus hash partitioning of jobs across schedulers."""
import zlib
from collections import defaultdict

# ── 1. The wheel ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TICK = 1          # seconds per bucket
WHEEL = 8         # slots in the wheel (tiny, for the demo)


class Wheel:
    """Slots hold [job, rounds]. A job farther than WHEEL ticks away
    keeps a 'rounds' counter: how many full laps to skip."""

    def __init__(self):
        self.slots = [[] for _ in range(WHEEL)]
        self.now = 0

    def add(self, job, due):
        delay = max(0, due - self.now)
        slot = (self.now + delay) % WHEEL
        rounds = delay // WHEEL
        self.slots[slot].append([job, rounds])

    def tick(self):
        self.now += 1
        index = self.now % WHEEL
        fire = []
        keep = []
        for item in self.slots[index]:
            if item[1] == 0:
                fire.append(item[0])        # its lap has come round
            else:
                item[1] -= 1                # another lap to wait
                keep.append(item)
        self.slots[index] = keep
        return fire


# ── 2. Drive it: jobs due at t = 2, 2, 5, 11, 20 ──────────────────────
w = Wheel()
for name, due in [("a", 2), ("b", 2), ("c", 5), ("d", 11), ("e", 20)]:
    w.add(name, due)
for t in range(1, 22):
    fired = w.tick()
    if fired:
        print(f"t={t:>2}  fire {fired}")

# hash(job_id) picks a partition; each partition owns its own wheel.
P = 4
counts = defaultdict(int)
for i in range(10_000):

print("\njobs per partition (crc32 mod 4):", dict(sorted(counts.items())))

Jobs a and b share a slot and fire together; d, due at 11 with a wheel of 8, waits one extra round and fires at exactly 11; e, due at 20, waits two. Partitioning the job table by a hash of the id (as in partitioning) gives each scheduler instance a disjoint slice, so no two instances scan the same rows and the due index of each is small. A partition's owner is itself assigned by a lease (the next section) or by the coordination service, and when it dies another instance takes the slice and rebuilds its wheel from the table. Because the table is the truth, the rebuild is a range scan.

Deep dive 2: claiming work with leases, heartbeats and fencing

Every worker will crash or stall at some point: a process killed by the orchestrator, a long garbage-collection pause, a network partition that leaves it healthy but unreachable. The scheduler cannot tell "dead" from "slow" (failure detection), so it uses time. A lease says "this job is yours until time T unless you renew". If no renewal arrives by T, the scheduler may give the job to someone else. The full protocol, and the proof that a lease alone is unsafe, are in leases and fencing tokens; here is how it lands in a scheduler.

  • Claim is a conditional update. In SQL: UPDATE jobs SET state='running', owner=:w, lease_until=now()+:L, fence=fence+1, attempts=attempts+1 WHERE job_id=:id AND (state IN ('scheduled','ready') OR (state='running' AND lease_until <= now())). One row, one atomic step. If zero rows change, someone else won.
  • Pick the lease length from the handler, not from taste. Short leases (tens of seconds) give fast recovery but need steady heartbeats; long leases tolerate a slow network but delay recovery. Renew at roughly a third of the lease so one missed heartbeat is harmless. Jobs with a long expected runtime ask for a longer initial lease.
  • Time source. The lease expiry is judged by the store's clock, not the worker's. The worker only needs a roughly correct duration, not agreement on absolute time (time revisited).
  • Fencing closes the gap. A worker that stalls past its lease still believes it owns the job. When it wakes it will try to write, and its write must be refused. Every state change carries the fence and the store checks it, so the stale worker is rejected. The diagram shows the exact interleaving.

Here is the same protocol as a deterministic simulation: a store with atomic claim, heartbeat, complete and fail, the stall above, a job that always fails, and an idempotent handler.

The idea

  1. claim ← oldest due job, queued or running with lease expired
  2. claim: lease ← now + 10; fence ← fence + 1; complete(fence) must match
  3. fail: attempts ≥ max → dead letter; else due ← now + uniform(0, 2^n)
  4. effect(key) ← skip if key already seen, else apply once
"""Tiny deterministic durable scheduler: leases, heartbeats, fencing
tokens, backoff with jitter, dead letter, and an idempotent handler."""
import random

# ── 1. The durable job table ──────────────────────────────────────────
class Store:
    """Stands in for the job table. Every method is one atomic
    conditional update."""

    def __init__(self):
        self.jobs = {}

    def add(self, jid, due, max_attempts=4):
        self.jobs[jid] = dict(
            state="queued",
            due=due,
            attempts=0,
            fence=0,                # bumps on every claim: the fencing token
            lease_until=0,
            owner=None,
            max=max_attempts,
        )

    def claim(self, now, worker, lease=10):
        """UPDATE ... WHERE state='queued' AND due<=now (or lease expired)
        ORDER BY due LIMIT 1."""
        cands = []
        for k, j in self.jobs.items():
            ready = j["state"] == "queued" and j["due"] <= now
            lapsed = j["state"] == "running" and j["lease_until"] <= now
            if ready or lapsed:
                cands.append((j["due"], k))
        if not cands:
            return None
        _, k = min(cands)
        j = self.jobs[k]
        j.update(
            state="running",
            owner=worker,
            lease_until=now + lease,
            fence=j["fence"] + 1,
            attempts=j["attempts"] + 1,
        )
        return k, j["fence"]

    def heartbeat(self, k, fence, now, lease=10):
        j = self.jobs[k]
        if j["fence"] != fence:
            return False            # someone else owns it now
        j["lease_until"] = now + lease
        return True

    def complete(self, k, fence, now):
        j = self.jobs[k]
        if j["fence"] != fence or j["state"] != "running":
            return False            # fenced out: a newer claim exists
        j["state"] = "done"
        return True

    def fail(self, k, fence, now, rng):
        j = self.jobs[k]
        if j["fence"] != fence:
            return "fenced"
        if j["attempts"] >= j["max"]:
            j["state"] = "dead"
            return "dead-letter"
        backoff = min(60, 2 ** j["attempts"])
        # Full jitter: a random wait in [0, backoff] stops retries syncing.
        j.update(state="queued", due=now + rng.uniform(0, backoff))
        return f"retry in {j['due'] - now:.1f}s (cap {backoff}s)"

# ── 4. At-least-once delivery, effect applied once ────────────────────
print("\n-- idempotent handler: at-least-once delivery, effect applied once")
done = set()
log = []


def pay(job, key):
    if key in done:
        log.append(f"{job}: duplicate {key}, skipped")
        return
    done.add(key)
    log.append(f"{job}: charged once under {key}")


pay("j1", "inv-42")

Notice what the fence protects and what it does not. It stops A from marking the job done or changing its state. It cannot stop A from already having sent the email before it was fenced, because the outside world does not check our fence. That is why the last block exists: delivery is at-least-once, so effects must be idempotent.

At-least-once plus idempotent handlers

Between "the handler did its effect" and "the worker told the store" there is always a window where a crash makes the job run again. Exactly-once execution would need the effect and the acknowledgement to be one atomic step across two systems, which in general you do not have (failure and retries; the broader treatment is logs and exactly-once). So the contract is: the scheduler delivers at least once, passes a stable run_key, and the handler makes its effect idempotent with it. Typical ways: a unique-key insert or an upsert keyed by run_key; passing run_key as the idempotency key to a payment or email provider; a conditional write ("set status = sent where status = pending"). If a handler's effect cannot be made idempotent, the honest alternative is at-most-once: record "started" first and never retry, accepting that a crash loses the job. That is a product decision to expose, not hide.

Deep dive 3: retries, backoff and dead-letter

A failed job should usually be retried, but retries are also how a small outage becomes a large one: every failing job comes back at once. The rules that keep it sane follow resilience and overload practice.

  • Exponential backoff with full jitter. After attempt n, wait a random time between 0 and min(cap, base × 2n). Without the randomness all failures from one outage retry in lockstep. The simulation above uses exactly this ("retry in 0.6s (cap 2s)"). Try the backoff experiment if you want to see the synchronisation.
  • Classify failures. A handler returns "retryable" (timeout, 503), "permanent" (400, validation) or "retry after T" (429 with a header). Permanent errors skip retries and go straight to dead-letter; retrying a malformed payload five times is pure waste.
  • Bound attempts and total age. After max_attempts, or when a job is older than its deadline, move it to dead. The dead-letter state is just a row state plus an alert, with a UI to inspect and redrive (reset attempts, set due now) after the bug is fixed.
  • Retries are lower priority than new work when the system is saturated, or a retry storm starves fresh jobs. A per-tenant retry budget, such as "retries may use at most 20% of this tenant's concurrency", gives the same protection as a retry budget in a client.
  • Poison jobs. A job that crashes the worker (out of memory, segfault) never reports a failure; the lease just expires, over and over. Counting claims (attempts rises on every claim, not every reported failure) lets the dead-letter rule catch it.

Deep dive 4: priorities, fairness across tenants, and cron

Priorities and tenants

Two different problems share one queue. Priority says some jobs matter more (a password-reset email beats a weekly digest). Fairness says one tenant's flood must not delay another tenant's trickle. Strict priority queues alone make low priority wait forever and do nothing about a single tenant filling the top class. The usual shape is a two-level scheme:

SchemeHowGood forRisk
One FIFO queueOrder of due timeSimplicityA tenant that enqueues 100,000 jobs makes everyone wait behind it
Strict priority classesAlways serve the highest non-empty classUrgent workStarvation of low classes; no isolation inside a class
Weighted classes plus per-tenant round robinPick a class by weight (say 60/30/10), then rotate through tenants inside itPriority and fairness; low classes still progressNeeds per-tenant queues or virtual queues; weights are policy to tune
Per-tenant concurrency capsEach tenant may hold at most c running slotsHard isolation of capacity; also protects tenants' own downstreamsWastes capacity if caps sum to less than the fleet unless borrowing is allowed

Recommendation: weighted classes with per-tenant round robin, plus per-tenant concurrency caps with the ability to borrow idle capacity. Isolation of noisy tenants is the same idea developed in multi-tenancy and isolation, which also covers the security side (one tenant's handler URL must not become a path into another's data). In the scheduler, tenant identity is a first-class field of every row and every queue key, not something derived later.

The idea

  1. arrivals ← 2,000 jobs for A, then 20 each for B and C
  2. FIFO: each tick run the first 10 jobs in arrival order
  3. round robin: each tick take one job per tenant in turn until 10 are used
  4. print the tick when each tenant's last job finished
"""Fairness: one tenant floods 2,000 jobs. FIFO vs per-tenant round robin
with 10 execution slots per tick."""
from collections import deque

# ── 1. The workload ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLOTS = 10                    # execution slots per tick


def arrivals():
    # tick 0: tenant A dumps 2000 jobs; B and C each submit 20 right after
    jobs = [("A", i) for i in range(2000)]
    jobs += [("B", i) for i in range(20)]
    jobs += [("C", i) for i in range(20)]
    return jobs


# ── 2. FIFO: the flood sits in front of everyone ──────────────────────
def fifo():
    q = deque(arrivals())
    t = 0
    done = {}                       # tenant -> tick its last job finished
    while q:
        t += 1
        for _ in range(min(SLOTS, len(q))):
            tenant, i = q.popleft()
            done[tenant] = t
    return done


# ── 3. Round robin: one job per tenant in turn ────────────────────────
def round_robin():
    queues = {}
    for tenant, i in arrivals():
        queues.setdefault(tenant, deque()).append(i)
    t = 0
    done = {}
    while any(queues.values()):
        t += 1
        budget = SLOTS
        while budget and any(queues.values()):
            for tenant, q in queues.items():
                if q and budget:
                    q.popleft()
                    budget -= 1
                    done[tenant] = t
    return done


# ── 4. Compare ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
print("tick at which each tenant's last job finished (10 slots per tick)")
print("  FIFO       :", fifo())
print("  round robin:", round_robin())
tick at which each tenant's last job finished (10 slots per tick)
  FIFO       : {'A': 200, 'B': 202, 'C': 204}
  round robin: {'A': 204, 'B': 7, 'C': 7}

Under FIFO the two small tenants wait behind a 2,000-job flood and finish at ticks 202 and 204. With a per-tenant rotation they finish at tick 7, and the flooder's total completion time barely changes (204 against 200), because the fleet was busy either way. That is the essence of fairness: it reorders, it does not create capacity.

Exactly-once cron triggering

A cron entry must fire once per scheduled time. Two failures break that: no one fires it (the single scheduler is down) and two fire it (two schedulers both think they are in charge). The standard answer combines two mechanisms with different jobs.

  1. Leader election for efficiency. One instance at a time holds a lease in the coordination service and runs the cron loop (or one leader per range of cron ids). Failover happens when the lease expires. This keeps the work from being done N times in the common case.
  2. A deterministic unique key for correctness. The leader does not "launch" a run; it inserts a job row whose key is (cron_id, scheduled_fire_time). A second insert of the same key fails. So even if a paused old leader wakes up and tries to fire the same minute, or the fencing check in the coordination layer is imperfect, the database refuses the duplicate.

The lesson generalises: use consensus or leases to get liveness and efficiency, and idempotent writes to get safety. Do not rely on the lease alone for safety, for the reason in the fencing chapter.

The idea

  1. enqueue(key ← cron id + fire time); if key exists → duplicate, ignore
  2. next fire ← minute rounded up to the next multiple of 15
  3. two leaders enqueue the same fire time; the second is ignored
  4. missed ← every fire time in (last, now]; skip / fire once / fire all
"""Exactly-once cron triggering: leader election for efficiency, a unique
(cron_id, fire_time) key for correctness."""
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

# ── 1. Enqueue guarded by a unique key ────────────────────────────────
jobs = {}                     # the job table; primary key (cron_id, fire_time)


def enqueue(cron_id, fire_time, leader):
    # The key comes from the schedule, not from who is asking, so two
    # leaders computing the same fire time collide on purpose.
    key = (cron_id, fire_time.isoformat())
    if key in jobs:
        return f"{leader}: insert {key[1]} -> duplicate, ignored"
    jobs[key] = dict(state="queued", by=leader)
    return f"{leader}: insert {key[1]} -> created"


# ── 2. Computing the next fire time ───────────────────────────────────
def next_fire(after):
    """Next time matching '*/15 * * * *'."""
    base = after.replace(second=0, microsecond=0)
    return base + timedelta(minutes=15 - base.minute % 15)


# ── 3. A stale leader tries the same fire ─────────────────────────────
t = datetime(2025, 1, 1, 9, 7)
fire = next_fire(t)
print("next fire after 09:07 is", fire.time())
print(enqueue("report", fire, "leader-1"))
print(enqueue("report", fire, "leader-2"))
print("  ^ stale leader, same time-derived key")

# ── 4. Catch-up after downtime: skip, fire once, or fire all ──────────
print("\ncatch-up after the scheduler was down from 09:00 to 10:40 "
      "(job fires every")
print("15 min):")
last = datetime(2025, 1, 1, 9, 0)
now = datetime(2025, 1, 1, 10, 40)
missed = []
f = next_fire(last)
while f <= now:
    missed.append(f)
    f = next_fire(f)
print("  missed fire times:", [m.strftime("%H:%M") for m in missed])
print("  policy skip     : run nothing, wait for", f.strftime("%H:%M"))
print("  policy fire-once: run once for", missed[-1].strftime("%H:%M"))
print("  policy fire-all : run", len(missed), "times, oldest first")
next fire after 09:07 is 09:15:00
leader-1: insert 2025-01-01T09:15:00 -> created
leader-2: insert 2025-01-01T09:15:00 -> duplicate, ignored
  ^ stale leader, same time-derived key

catch-up after the scheduler was down from 09:00 to 10:40 (job fires every
15 min):
  missed fire times: ['09:15', '09:30', '09:45', '10:00', '10:15', '10:30']

  policy fire-once: run once for 10:30
  policy fire-all : run 6 times, oldest first

The second half of the output is the misfire policy you promised in the API. After downtime of an hour and forty minutes, a 15-minute job has six missed fire times. A metrics rollup probably wants fire once (do the latest, the aggregates cover the gap); a billing job may want fire all, oldest first; a "send today's digest" job wants skip. Make it a field, document the default, and write the missed times into the run history.

Two further cron details deserve a sentence each. Time zones and daylight saving: store the zone with the schedule and compute the next fire time in that zone, deciding explicitly what a 02:30 job does on the day clocks skip it and on the day 01:30 happens twice. And compute the next fire time when the previous one is created, not by a separate clock tick, so the chain of keys is a pure function of the expression and the last time.

Deep dive 5: the top of the hour

People pick round numbers. If two million entries say 0 * * * *, then at 09:00:00 the scheduler wants to start two million jobs in the same second: the estimate printed 2,000,000 starts per second against a normal 5,800. Everything downstream is hit at once: the claim path in the store, the worker fleet, and the tenants' own handlers, which are the real victims (a webhook receiver that normally sees 10 calls a second gets 10,000). Mitigations, from cheapest to most structural:

  • Jitter the start. Offset each run by a per-job random delay in a window the tenant accepts (30 seconds to 5 minutes for most background work; none for jobs that really need the minute). Derive the offset from a hash of the cron id, so it is stable across restarts, and the job's run_key still uses the nominal time.
  • Spread at creation time. When a tenant creates a schedule with no preference, pick the minute for them ("hourly" becomes 17 * * * *). Defaults shape fleet behaviour far more than documentation does.
  • Rate-limit the release. The ready-queue release step has a global and a per-tenant rate, so a herd is smoothed into a ramp and lag shows up honestly as lag (rate limiter).
  • Pre-scale. Known peaks (the start of every hour) can scale the worker fleet ahead of time, but only after you have flattened them; scaling for a spike you could have spread is the expensive route.

The idea

  1. peak(starts) ← largest count of starts in any one second
  2. no jitter: all 200,000 start at second 0
  3. random jitter: start ← uniform(0, window) for window 30 s and 300 s
  4. hash jitter: start ← crc32(job id) mod 300 (same after any restart)
"""Cron herd: 200,000 jobs scheduled '0 * * * *'. Compare peak starts per
second with and without jitter."""
import random
import zlib
from collections import Counter

# ── 1. Measuring the peak ─────────────────────────────────────────────
N = 200_000


def peak(starts):
    """Return (busiest second's start count, seconds touched)."""
    per_second = Counter(int(t) for t in starts)
    return max(per_second.values()), len(per_second)


# ── 2. No jitter: everything starts at :00 ────────────────────────────
rng = random.Random(1)
p, n = peak([0.0] * N)
print(f"no jitter       peak {p:>8,} starts/s over {n} second(s)")

# ── 3. Random jitter over a window ────────────────────────────────────
for window in (30, 300):
    p, n = peak([rng.uniform(0, window) for _ in range(N)])
    print(f"random jitter {window:>3}s peak {p:>8,} starts/s over {n} seconds")

# ── 4. Hash jitter: the same offset after every retry or restart ──────
offsets = [zlib.crc32(f"job-{i}".encode()) % 300 for i in range(N)]
p, n = peak(offsets)
print(f"hash jitter   300s peak {p:>8,} starts/s over {n} seconds")
print("  (stable across restarts)")
no jitter       peak  200,000 starts/s over 1 second(s)
random jitter  30s peak    6,886 starts/s over 30 seconds
random jitter 300s peak      742 starts/s over 300 seconds
hash jitter   300s peak      747 starts/s over 300 seconds
  (stable across restarts)

With 200,000 entries, no jitter is 200,000 starts in one second. A random 30-second window brings the peak to about 6,900 per second, and a 5-minute window to about 740. Deriving the offset from a hash of the id (the last line) gives the same smoothing, with the property that the offset does not change when a job is retried or the scheduler restarts. Peak-to-average drops by two orders of magnitude for a trivial change.

Observing lag

The most important single metric is scheduling lag: now − due_at for the oldest job still waiting, per partition and per priority class. It rises before anything else breaks, whatever the cause (starved workers, a slow store, a herd). Next: start delay (claimed_at − due_at) as a distribution, queue depth by class and tenant, lease expirations per minute (a sudden rise means workers are dying or too slow to heartbeat), retry and dead-letter rates, and duplicate-suppression counts (the cron unique key firing, handler idempotency hits), which should be nonzero but small. Percentiles come from histograms (metrics), not averages.

SLOs. 99% of jobs start within 1 s of due time (excluding jittered ones, which are measured from the jittered time); no accepted job is lost (verified by a periodic reconciliation of accepted versus terminal counts); submit availability 99.95%. Alert on lag burn rate and on a nonzero count of jobs that are running with an expired lease for longer than a threshold (burn-rate alerting). Roll out scheduler changes one partition at a time, with the old and new release able to read the same rows, and run a chaos check that kills a worker mid-job and verifies exactly one effect.

Trade-offs & alternatives

  • At-least-once versus at-most-once. We chose at-least-once because losing a job is usually worse than running it twice, and pushed the cost to handlers. For non-idempotent effects the alternative is at-most-once with "started" recorded first.
  • Database polling versus a broker. A database gives cancel, reschedule and inspection; a broker gives raw throughput. Many systems use both: the store of record decides, a broker moves ready ids to workers.
  • Push versus pull to workers. We chose workers pulling with claims, which gives natural backpressure and no registry of worker addresses. Push is lower latency but needs health tracking and can overload a slow worker.
  • Short versus long leases. Short means quick recovery and more heartbeat traffic; long means slow recovery and fewer writes. A per-job lease tied to expected runtime avoids a global compromise.
  • If we needed sub-millisecond precision, this is the wrong tool: you would run the timers in-process, accept loss on restart and not promise durability. If jobs were workflows with dependencies, add a workflow layer that stores state transitions as events and uses the scheduler only for timers (sagas).
  • Where it breaks down. Very large fan-in schedules (millions of timers per tenant), jobs whose runtime far exceeds any sensible lease, and effects with no idempotency story. Each needs a limit or a different product, not a bigger cluster.

What a strong answer adds

  • Separates when to run (the scheduler) from what to run (the handler), and states the delivery guarantee up front: at-least-once, with idempotency keys.
  • Uses the estimate to split the pending set (disk, hundreds of millions) from the hot window (memory, hundreds of thousands), and designs a tiered due index around that.
  • Explains why a lease needs a fencing token, and draws the pause-then-wake interleaving.
  • Treats the database as the truth and every queue as a rebuildable cache.
  • Gets cron right: leader election for efficiency, a deterministic unique key for correctness, and an explicit misfire policy and time-zone story.
  • Anticipates the top-of-the-hour herd with numbers and proposes jitter derived from a hash.
  • Combines priority classes with per-tenant fairness and caps, and says that fairness reorders but does not add capacity.
  • Names scheduling lag as the headline SLI and lists the supporting metrics.

Follow-up questions

How would you support cancelling a job that is already running?

Cancellation is a state change, not a kill. Mark the row cancel_requested; the worker learns about it on its next heartbeat response (the store returns a flag) and the handler must cooperate (check a context and stop). If the handler does not respond within a grace period, the worker's lease is allowed to lapse and the fence prevents any later completion from being accepted. Be honest in the API: cancellation is best effort once a job has started.

How do you handle a job that takes six hours?

Do not make a six-hour lease. Keep the lease short and heartbeat steadily, so a crash is noticed within a minute. Better, make the job resumable: the handler checkpoints progress (an offset, a cursor) in the job row or its own store, so a re-claim continues instead of restarting. If it cannot be checkpointed, split it into shorter chained jobs.

Why not just use cron on one machine?

It is a single point of failure, it loses runs while down, it has no retry or backoff, no per-tenant fairness and no visibility. It is a fine answer for one small team's maintenance task. The distributed design earns its complexity when there are many tenants, strict reliability needs and a fleet of workers; say that, rather than treating complexity as a virtue.

Two workers claim the same job at the same instant. What happens?

The claim is a single conditional update on the row, executed atomically by the store for that partition, so one wins and the other sees zero rows changed. In a replicated store the claim goes through the partition's leader or uses a compare-and-set, never a read-then-write across replicas. The loser simply picks another job.

How do you scale to ten times the load?

Add partitions (resharding the job table by hash, ideally with consistent hashing so only a fraction moves), add scheduler instances so each owns fewer partitions, and add workers. The things that do not scale linearly are hot tenants and the cron leader; shard crons by id range, and cap or isolate the tenants whose volume is a large share of the total.

How would you test that no job is lost or duplicated under failures?

Write an invariant checker (every accepted job reaches exactly one terminal state; every effect key appears once) and run the scheduler under a deterministic simulation or a chaos harness that kills workers and schedulers, pauses processes beyond their lease and partitions the store (testing distributed systems). Lab 6 builds a small version and tests it with crashes.

Distributed systems connection

The scheduler is a compact tour of coordination patterns. A lease is a time-bounded lock; a fence is the monotonic token that makes an expired lock harmless; leader election is a lease on a name; and the unique cron key is an idempotent write that makes the whole arrangement safe even when the lease fails. See leases and fencing, coordination services and logs and exactly-once. The ready queues are the pattern in queues and backpressure, scaled out.

Key takeaways

  • A scheduler is a durable store plus a due-time index; queues are derived caches of the store. Only the next minute or so of jobs needs to live in memory, in a timing wheel or time buckets, partitioned by job id.
  • Workers claim with a conditional update that sets a lease and bumps a fence; heartbeats extend the lease; every later call carries the fence so a stalled worker is rejected.
  • Delivery is at-least-once. Hand handlers a stable run key and make effects idempotent; exactly-once execution is not on offer, exactly-once triggering and effectively-once effects are.
  • Retry with exponential backoff and full jitter, classify errors, bound attempts, count claims to catch poison jobs, and dead-letter with a redrive path.
  • Combine priority classes with per-tenant rotation and concurrency caps. Fairness reorders work; it does not create capacity.
  • Cron uses leader election for efficiency and a unique (cron_id, fire_time) key for correctness, plus an explicit misfire policy and time zones.
  • Round-number schedules create herds; jitter derived from a hash of the id cuts the peak by orders of magnitude. The headline metric is scheduling lag.
