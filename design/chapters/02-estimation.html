Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

Before choosing a single component you need to know how big the problem is: how many requests per second, how many terabytes, how many gigabits, how many machines. This chapter gives you the numbers worth remembering, the handful of formulas that turn "10 million users" into capacity, sanity checks that catch your own mistakes, and three fully worked examples as Python you can run. It finishes with a calculator you can play with.

Why this matters

Estimation is how a vague requirement becomes an engineering constraint. "Handle a lot of traffic" cannot be designed for; "700 reads per second at peak and 11 TB per year" can. Good estimates take two minutes, are right within a factor of ten, and each one justifies a decision: one database or many, cache or no cache, CDN or not. The senior habit is not precision. It is noticing which numbers matter and being able to say "that is 100 times more than one machine can do, so we must partition".

Numbers to know

Powers of two and ten

Data sizes are quoted in powers of ten, but memory and disks come in powers of two, and the two are close enough that you can mix them when estimating (they differ by 2.4% at kilo and about 10% at peta). Memorise the ladder:

Power of 2AboutNameHandy example
210 = 1,0241031 KBA short text message with metadata is a few hundred bytes
2201061 MBA compressed phone photo is roughly 2 to 3 MB
2301091 GBMany hours of compressed music, or roughly an hour of standard-definition video
24010121 TBA single large SSD or a few spinning disks
25010151 PBNeeds a cluster; a thousand 1 TB disks

Counts of things use the same ladder: thousand (k), million (M), billion (G), trillion (T). The most useful conversion in this whole chapter is time.

The one number to tattoo on your hand

There are 86,400 seconds in a day, which is roughly 105. So "N per day" is roughly "N divided by 100,000 per second". A million events a day is about 12 per second; a billion a day is about 12,000 per second. A month has about 2.6 million seconds (call it 2.5 M) and a year about 31.5 million (about π × 107).

The latency ladder

You met these in the memory hierarchy and the tail-latency discussion in networking performance. The exact figures change with hardware, so remember the ratios: each rung is roughly 10 to 1000 times slower than the one above it.

Three consequences recur in every design. Avoid network hops on the hot path (each costs the equivalent of thousands of memory reads). Do sequential rather than random disk access where you can (log-structured storage exists because of this). And do not make cross-region synchronous calls casually: 150 ms is an eternity in a request that has a 200 ms budget.

Rough throughput per box

Order of magnitude only

The figures below are rules of thumb for a modern server doing simple operations. Real values vary by a factor of ten or more with hardware, schema, request size, tuning and versions. Use them to decide "one box, ten boxes or a thousand?", never to size a purchase. When it matters, measure your own workload (load testing and capacity planning).

In-memory key-value server (e.g. one Redis node)On the order of 100,000 simple operations/sMostly single-threaded per node; pipelining can raise it. Memory-bound: RAM is the capacity limit (caching).

The methods

Users to QPS

Start with DAU, daily active users. Multiply by the number of actions each does per day to get actions per day, divide by 86,400 for the average per second. The average is almost never what matters: traffic is not flat. Multiply by a peak factor to get peak QPS. For consumer apps with a daily rhythm, a peak 2 to 5 times the daily average is a common planning assumption; spiky products (ticket sales, sports, news) can be 10 to 100 times. State your factor.

Read to write ratio

Split the actions into reads and writes. Reads usually dominate: loading a feed is a read, posting is a write; a profile is viewed far more often than edited. Ratios of 10:1 to 100:1 are common; telemetry and logging invert it. The ratio decides whether caching and replicas help (read-heavy) or whether write capacity is the constraint (write-heavy), as the workload-shape table in chapter 1 showed.

Storage

New bytes per day = writes per day × bytes per write. Multiply by 365 for a year, by the replication factor (3 copies is a common default for durability; erasure coding can bring overhead to about 1.5 times, replication), and by the retention in years. Add overhead for indexes and metadata if the data is structured (often 20 to 100 percent; say which you assume). Remember that raw size and replicated size answer different questions: a single node must hold one replica's share, while the fleet holds all copies.

Bandwidth

Peak bandwidth = peak read QPS × bytes per response × 8 for bits. Do ingress (writes) and egress (reads) separately. Compare with one NIC (1 to 10 Gbit/s) and decide if you need a CDN. If a cache or CDN absorbs a fraction h (the hit ratio) of requests, the origin sees (1 − h) of the load, so going from 90% to 99% hits cuts origin traffic tenfold.

Cache size and the 80/20 rule

Access patterns are usually skewed: a small fraction of items get most of the traffic. The rough "80/20 rule" says about 20% of the data serves about 80% of reads. It is a heuristic, not a law (real skew is often steeper, following a Zipf-like distribution), but it gives a cheap first sizing: cache size ≈ hot fraction × the data that is actually being read within the window. For a feed or pastebin, the window is recent days, not all history. If the result fits in a few nodes' RAM, caching is clearly viable. Hit ratio then depends on how skewed things truly are; measure it (caching, hot spots).

Machine counts

Servers = peak QPS ÷ (per-box capacity × target utilisation) + spares. Target utilisation of around 50 to 70 percent leaves room for bursts and for losing nodes; running hot makes latency explode, as the queueing chapter shows (queueing theory). To survive losing one of k zones, you must provision enough that the remaining k − 1 zones can carry peak: with three zones that means 1.5 times the minimal capacity. A related identity, Little's law, gives concurrency: requests in flight = arrival rate × time in system. At 5,000 requests/s with 40 ms latency, about 200 are in flight at once, which tells you thread or connection counts.

Sanity checks and error bars

Estimates fail in boring ways. These checks catch most of them:

  • Units. Write units on every number. "Per second" versus "per day" and bits versus bytes cause most 105 and 8x mistakes.
  • Cross-check two ways. Estimate storage from users × items per user, and from writes per day × days. If they disagree by 10x, one assumption is wrong.
  • Compare with a known system. One machine does thousands of requests per second, so "500 servers for 100 QPS" is wrong, and "1 server for 1,000,000 QPS" is wrong. Bound your answer between "obviously too small" and "obviously too large".
  • Check against physical limits. 50 Gbit/s does not come out of one NIC. 100 TB does not fit in RAM on one machine.
  • Ask what would change the decision. If doubling the estimate would not change the design, stop refining it.

Estimates multiply, and so do their errors. If each of four inputs might be off by a factor of two in either direction, how wrong can the product be? Let us simulate it rather than guess:

"""How wrong is a product of guesses? Each factor is uncertain by up to 2x either way."""
import random

rng = random.Random(42)
nominal = {"DAU": 10e6, "actions/user/day": 20, "KB/action": 10, "peak factor": 3}

def trial():
    value = 1.0
    for v in nominal.values():
        value *= v * 2 ** rng.uniform(-1, 1)      # log-uniform within 2x either side
    return value

base = 1.0
for v in nominal.values():
    base *= v
results = sorted(trial() for _ in range(20_000))
pick = lambda p: results[int(p * len(results)) - 1]
print(f"nominal answer:  {base:.3g}")
print(f"5th percentile:  {pick(0.05):.3g}  ({pick(0.05)/base:.2f}x)")
print(f"median:          {pick(0.50):.3g}  ({pick(0.50)/base:.2f}x)")
print(f"95th percentile: {pick(0.95):.3g}  ({pick(0.95)/base:.2f}x)")
print(f"spread 95th/5th: {pick(0.95)/pick(0.05):.1f}x")
nominal answer:  6e+09
5th percentile:  1.6e+09  (0.27x)
median:          6.01e+09  (1.00x)
95th percentile: 2.26e+10  (3.76x)
spread 95th/5th: 14.1x

Four factors each uncertain by 2x give a middle 90% range spanning roughly 14x. That is the honest meaning of "within a factor of ten": it is what you get from sensible guesses, and it is enough. You are choosing between designs that differ by 100x or 1000x in capacity, so a 10x error bar is usually fine, and you should state the range ("between 300 and 3,000 reads/s") when the decision sits near a boundary.

Worked example toolkit

Below are three complete estimates as runnable Python. They share this small toolkit, which does nothing but arithmetic, so every assumption is visible in the script. Save it as toolkit.py or paste it into each script.

SECONDS_PER_DAY = 86_400          # roughly 10^5; we use the real number in code
def human(n, unit=""):
    for div, suf in ((1e15, "P"), (1e12, "T"), (1e9, "G"), (1e6, "M"), (1e3, "k")):
        if abs(n) >= div:
            return f"{n/div:.3g} {suf}{unit}"
    return f"{n:.3g} {unit}".strip()

def qps(dau, actions_per_user_per_day):
    return dau * actions_per_user_per_day / SECONDS_PER_DAY

def servers(peak_qps, qps_per_box, headroom=0.5):
    """Boxes needed if each should run at `headroom` of its max, plus one spare."""
    import math
    return math.ceil(peak_qps / (qps_per_box * headroom)) + 1

Example 1: a pastebin

10 million daily users; one in ten creates a paste each day; each paste is read about 20 times; a paste is about 10 KB; keep 5 years; three replicas; peak is 3x average. We cache the hottest 20% of the last 30 days of pastes.

"""Example 1: a pastebin. 10M DAU; 1 in 10 users creates a paste a day; each paste is read ~20 times."""
from toolkit import *

dau = 10_000_000
writes_per_day = dau * 0.1               # 1 in 10 users creates a paste
reads_per_write = 20                     # read-heavy: each paste viewed ~20 times
reads_per_day = writes_per_day * reads_per_write
PEAK = 3                                 # peak-to-average factor

w_avg = writes_per_day / SECONDS_PER_DAY
r_avg = reads_per_day / SECONDS_PER_DAY
print(f"writes/day {human(writes_per_day)}, reads/day {human(reads_per_day)}")
print(f"avg write QPS {w_avg:,.0f}, peak {w_avg*PEAK:,.0f}")
print(f"avg read  QPS {r_avg:,.0f}, peak {r_avg*PEAK:,.0f}")

paste_kb = 10                            # average paste ~10 KB of text
raw_per_year = writes_per_day * 365 * paste_kb * 1e3
repl = 3
print(f"new data/day {human(writes_per_day*paste_kb*1e3,'B')}")
print(f"raw/year {human(raw_per_year,'B')}, x{repl} replicas = {human(raw_per_year*repl,'B')}")
print(f"5-year footprint (x3) {human(raw_per_year*repl*5,'B')}")

egress = r_avg * PEAK * paste_kb * 1e3 * 8
print(f"peak egress {human(egress,'bit/s')} (before CDN/cache)")

hot = 0.2                                # 80/20: 20% of pastes get 80% of reads
hot_set = writes_per_day * 30 * hot * paste_kb * 1e3
print(f"cache the hottest 20% of 30 days of pastes: {human(hot_set,'B')}")
print(f"app servers (2k QPS/box at 50% load): {servers(r_avg*PEAK + w_avg*PEAK, 2000)}")
writes/day 1 M, reads/day 20 M
avg write QPS 12, peak 35
avg read  QPS 231, peak 694
new data/day 10 GB
raw/year 3.65 TB, x3 replicas = 10.9 TB
5-year footprint (x3) 54.8 TB
peak egress 55.6 Mbit/s (before CDN/cache)
cache the hottest 20% of 30 days of pastes: 60 GB
app servers (2k QPS/box at 50% load): 2

What the numbers decide. 35 writes and 700 reads per second at peak are small: a single primary with a cache and one or two app servers is enough. The 20:1 read ratio makes a cache worthwhile, and a 60 GB hot set fits in one cache node's RAM. The real long-term issue is storage: 11 TB a year replicated, which is a reason to put paste bodies in object storage and keep the database for metadata (the design from chapter 1). Peak egress is only 55 Mbit/s, so no CDN is required for capacity (one may still help latency worldwide).

Example 2: a chat service

50 million daily users each send 40 messages a day. Messages average 200 bytes with metadata. Messages have 1.5 recipients on average (mostly one-to-one, some groups). 20% of daily users are connected at peak; peak is 4x the average; each gateway server can hold 100,000 idle connections, which we load to half. Clients send a presence heartbeat every 30 seconds.

"""Example 2: a chat service. 50M DAU, 40 messages sent per user per day."""
from toolkit import *

dau = 50_000_000
sent_per_user = 40
PEAK = 4
avg_group = 1.5                          # recipients per message (mostly 1:1, some groups)

msgs_day = dau * sent_per_user
w_avg = msgs_day / SECONDS_PER_DAY
print(f"messages/day {human(msgs_day)}; avg {w_avg:,.0f}/s; peak {w_avg*PEAK:,.0f}/s")
print(f"deliveries at peak {w_avg*PEAK*avg_group:,.0f}/s")

msg_bytes = 200                          # text + metadata, no media
per_year = msgs_day * 365 * msg_bytes
print(f"text/year {human(per_year,'B')}; with 3 replicas {human(per_year*3,'B')}")

concurrent = dau * 0.2                   # 20% of DAU connected at peak
per_box = 100_000                        # connections per gateway box (tuned, idle sockets)
print(f"concurrent connections {human(concurrent)}; gateways at 50% of {per_box:,}: {int(concurrent/(per_box*0.5))+1}")

heartbeat_s = 30
hb_qps = concurrent / heartbeat_s
print(f"presence heartbeats {hb_qps:,.0f}/s -- more than the messages themselves")
messages/day 2 G; avg 23,148/s; peak 92,593/s
deliveries at peak 138,889/s
text/year 146 TB; with 3 replicas 438 TB
concurrent connections 10 M; gateways at 50% of 100,000: 201
presence heartbeats 333,333/s -- more than the messages themselves

What the numbers decide. About 93,000 messages per second at peak is beyond one database primary but ordinary for a partitioned store, so partition by conversation. Storage of 146 TB a year of text is manageable on a partitioned log-structured store. The surprise is that presence heartbeats (333,000 per second) outnumber messages: the load is dominated by connection state, not message content. That tells you where to look in the chat design (chapter 10): cheap in-memory presence, batching heartbeats, and about 200 gateway servers whose connections you must rebalance on deploys. A different estimate (5,000 messages per second) would have hidden this entirely.

Example 3: photo sharing

20 million daily users each view 30 photos and upload 0.2 photos a day. An original is about 2 MB after compression, plus three delivered sizes with a typical 200 KB thumbnail. Peak is 2.5x average. Originals use erasure coding at about 1.5x overhead. A CDN serves 95% of view traffic. The hot set is the most-viewed 20% of the past week's uploads.

"""Example 3: photo sharing. 20M DAU, view 30 photos, upload 0.2 per user per day."""
from toolkit import *

dau = 20_000_000
views = 30
uploads = 0.2
PEAK = 2.5

up_day = dau * uploads
view_qps = dau * views / SECONDS_PER_DAY
print(f"uploads/day {human(up_day)} ({up_day/SECONDS_PER_DAY:,.0f}/s); views avg {view_qps:,.0f}/s, peak {view_qps*PEAK:,.0f}/s")
print(f"read:write ratio {dau*views/up_day:.0f}:1")

orig_mb, thumb_kb = 2.0, 200             # original after compression, delivered version
store_year = up_day * 365 * (orig_mb*1e6 + 3*thumb_kb*1e3)   # original + 3 sizes
print(f"object storage/year {human(store_year,'B')} (x1.5 for erasure coding = {human(store_year*1.5,'B')})")

peak_bps = view_qps * PEAK * thumb_kb * 1e3 * 8
print(f"peak delivery bandwidth {human(peak_bps,'bit/s')}")
hit = 0.95
print(f"origin bandwidth if CDN hit ratio {hit:.0%}: {human(peak_bps*(1-hit),'bit/s')}")

hot_photos = up_day * 7 * 0.2            # hottest 20% of last week's photos
print(f"cache set for hot thumbs: {human(hot_photos*thumb_kb*1e3,'B')} across {human(hot_photos)} photos")
print(f"metadata rows/year {human(up_day*365)}; at 1 KB each {human(up_day*365*1e3,'B')} -> shard or not? "
      f"{'yes' if up_day*365*1e3 > 2e12 else 'one big primary may still do'}")
uploads/day 4 M (46/s); views avg 6,944/s, peak 17,361/s
read:write ratio 150:1
object storage/year 3.8 PB (x1.5 for erasure coding = 5.69 PB)
peak delivery bandwidth 27.8 Gbit/s
origin bandwidth if CDN hit ratio 95%: 1.39 Gbit/s
cache set for hot thumbs: 1.12 TB across 5.6 M photos
metadata rows/year 1.46 G; at 1 KB each 1.46 TB -> shard or not? one big primary may still do

What the numbers decide. The ratio is 150 reads for every write, so this is read-heavy, but bytes dominate over requests: 17,000 views per second is easy; 28 Gbit/s of delivery is not. No origin can serve that directly, so a CDN is not an optimisation but a requirement, and with a 95% hit ratio the origin sees about 1.4 Gbit/s, which a small fleet handles. 3.8 PB of images a year says object storage (chapter 8), and metadata at about 1.5 TB per year says a sharded relational or key-value store is plausible but not urgent. Compare with the pastebin: the same method, a different dominant constraint. That is the point of estimating.

Common estimation mistakes

  • Using the average where the peak matters (or the reverse for storage).
  • Forgetting replication, indexes and overhead when sizing storage.
  • Computing a bandwidth number and never comparing it with a link.
  • Treating the 80/20 rule as exact. If the cache is cold or the keys are uniformly random, caching helps much less.
  • Presenting a number with no unit or no "so".

Distributed systems connection

Estimates tell you whether you need distribution at all. Below a few thousand writes per second and a few terabytes, one well-run primary with replicas is usually simpler and safer than a distributed store (choosing storage). Above that, you pay the costs from the earlier courses: partitioning (consistent hashing), replication lag, consensus latency. Estimating honestly protects you from taking on those costs before you need them.

Key takeaways

  • A day is 86,400 s, roughly 105: "N per day" is about N / 105 per second. A million a day is about 12/s.
  • Remember latency ratios (cache, memory, SSD, data-centre round trip, ocean crossing) and rough per-box throughput, always as orders of magnitude.
  • The pipeline: DAU × actions → per day → ÷ 86,400 → × peak; then × size for bandwidth and storage; × replicas and years; ÷ per-box capacity and utilisation for machines.
  • Use 80/20 for a first cache size, and size over the window that is actually read.
  • Sanity-check units, cross-check two ways, compare with physical limits. Four 2x-uncertain inputs give about a 14x spread, which is enough to choose a design.
  • Every number must end with a "so": the decision it supports.

Check your understanding

A service gets 50 million requests per day. About how many per second on average?

  1. About 50
  2. About 5,000
  3. About 600
  4. About 50,000
50,000,000 / 86,400 is about 580, so roughly 600. Using 10^5 for a quick estimate gives 500. Each million per day is about 12 per second, so 50 million is about 600.

Peak read traffic is 20,000 responses/s of 500 KB each. Which statement is correct?

  1. That is about 80 Gbit/s, far beyond one 10 Gbit/s link, so a CDN or many front ends are needed
  2. That is about 80 MB/s, trivial for one server
  3. That is about 10 Gbit/s, exactly one link
  4. Bandwidth does not depend on response size
20,000 × 500 KB = 10 GB/s = 80 Gbit/s (multiply bytes by 8). Mixing up bits and bytes is the classic error; the answer is eight times larger than the MB figure.

You need capacity for 12,000 peak QPS. One box handles 2,000 QPS flat out, and you run boxes at 50% load. How many boxes, with one spare?

  1. 6
  2. 7
  3. 12
  4. 13
At 50% load each box does 1,000 QPS, so 12,000 / 1,000 = 12, plus one spare gives 13. Forgetting the utilisation target (answer 7) leaves no headroom for bursts or for a failed node.

A system is spread over three availability zones and must survive losing any one of them at peak. How much capacity relative to the bare minimum must you provision?

  1. 1.0 times
  2. 1.5 times, so the remaining two zones can carry the peak
  3. 2 times
  4. 3 times
If two zones must carry the full load, each zone must be able to carry half, so the three together hold 1.5 times the minimum. Replicating everywhere (3 times) is overkill for stateless capacity.

Your estimate used five inputs, each of which could be 2x off. What is the right attitude toward the result?

  1. Add more decimal places
  2. Discard it; it is useless
  3. Treat it as an order of magnitude with a wide error bar, and refine only the inputs that would change a decision
  4. Assume errors cancel out so it is accurate
Uncertainties multiply, so the range can be more than ten times wide. That is still enough to choose between designs that differ by 100x. Spend effort only on inputs near a decision boundary.

In the chat example, presence heartbeats outnumbered messages. What does that teach?

  1. Estimate every kind of traffic, because the dominant load may not be the obvious one
  2. Heartbeats are always the bottleneck
  3. Chat needs no database
  4. Messages can be ignored
Heartbeats happen to dominate in that example because of the assumptions. The lesson is general: compute all the traffic classes (messages, presence, receipts, media) before deciding where the design must be clever.
