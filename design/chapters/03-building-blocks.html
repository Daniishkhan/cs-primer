The Building Blocks, Revisited

Almost every system you will design is assembled from the same two dozen parts. You have met each one in depth. This chapter is the catalogue you keep in your head during a design: for every block, what it buys you, what it costs, how it fails, and when to leave it out. It points back to the chapter that taught each one and spends its words on choosing.

Why this matters

A design answer is a set of choices among these blocks, and the quality of the answer is the quality of the reasons. If you can state, for each box you draw, the specific thing it buys at your numbers and the specific thing it costs, you are doing design. If you can only say it is standard, you are decorating. The most valuable skill here is also the least glamorous: knowing when not to add a block.

The reference architecture

Here is the typical shape. It is not a template to copy; it is a map showing where each block sits so you can add one only when a requirement calls for it. A small product may need just the gateway, a service and one relational database. Each additional box on this diagram exists to answer a particular pressure: latency, scale, durability, asynchrony, search, or coordination.

The edge: DNS, CDN, load balancers and gateways

DNS and GeoDNS

Buys: names that survive changing IPs, and with GeoDNS or latency-based routing, steering users to the nearest region. Costs: answers are cached by resolvers for the TTL and some resolvers ignore low TTLs, so DNS failover takes minutes in practice, not seconds. Fails: by serving a dead address for a while. Avoid relying on it for fast failover; pair it with health-checked load balancers or anycast. Taught in DNS.

CDN

Buys: latency (bytes served near the user) and origin offload, which at high bandwidth is a necessity: the photo example in chapter 2 needed one for capacity, not speed. Costs: staleness until TTL or purge, cache-key mistakes that leak or fragment content, and a bill per byte. Fails: a popular object expiring everywhere at once can stampede the origin; collapse concurrent misses (cache stampedes). Avoid for private, per-user or very small dynamic responses. Background: performance; at the extreme, a company can place its own caches inside ISPs (Netflix Open Connect).

L4 and L7 load balancers, API gateways

Buys: a stable front door, health checks, spreading load, TLS termination; at L7 also routing by path or header, retries and per-client limits. A gateway adds authentication, quotas and request shaping in one place. Costs: a hop, a shared dependency, and a place where a bad rule or deploy affects everything. Fails: by being the single point of failure (run several, behind anycast or DNS) and by configuration errors. Choose L4 for raw speed and non-HTTP protocols, L7 when you need content-aware routing (load balancers, cross-cutting concerns). Do not put business logic in the gateway.

Compute

Stateless services

Buys: scaling by adding instances and recovering by replacing them, because any instance can serve any request. Costs: every piece of state has to be somewhere else (a store, a cache), so each request pays network hops. Fails: by overload and retry amplification; protect them with timeouts, budgets and load shedding (resilience). Watch for hidden state: in-process sessions, local caches that must agree, local files. Whether to split into many services at all is a separate question (monolith or microservices); a well-structured single deployable is a legitimate answer.

Workflow engines

Buys: durable, resumable multi-step processes (book, charge, notify) with timers, retries and compensation, without hand-rolling a state machine. Costs: a new platform to run, rules about deterministic workflow code, and a place where long-lived versions pile up. Fails: workflows stuck on a failing step, or replays that repeat side effects if the steps are not idempotent. Avoid for two-step flows where a queue and a table suffice. The patterns are sagas and distributed transactions.

Schedulers

Buys: delayed and recurring work, and placing work on machines. Costs: a scheduler is itself a distributed system with leader election and clocks; "exactly once" execution is really at-least-once plus idempotent handlers. Fails: missed runs after leader failover and duplicate runs after a lease expires without fencing (leases and fencing). Avoid if an event can trigger the work. Designed in chapter 17; the OS-level idea is in scheduling.

Data stores

The central question, from choosing storage, is the access pattern: what is looked up, how often, how big, with what consistency. Start with the most general store that meets your numbers, and move to specialised ones when a measured requirement demands.

Caches

Buys: memory-speed reads and a database spared the repeated work. Costs: staleness, invalidation logic (famously hard), and a second place the data lives. Fails: cold starts, stampedes when a hot key expires, hot keys that overload one node, and cached data outliving a permission change. Avoid when writes dominate, the data is cheap to compute, or the hit ratio will be low. Taught in caching; designed in chapter 6.

Relational databases

Buys: transactions, constraints, joins and ad-hoc queries. For most products these are the right default, and a single primary with replicas goes a long way. Costs: scaling writes past one node means sharding, which gives up cross-shard transactions and joins. Fails: by failover pauses, replica lag showing stale data, and long transactions or hot rows causing contention. Avoid as the sole store for very high write rates keyed by one id. See SQL, ACID, replication, sharding.

Key-value and wide-column stores

Buys: horizontal scale for storage and writes, keyed access, often tunable consistency. Costs: you design tables around queries; there are no joins; transactions are limited or absent. Fails: hot partitions from a skewed key and slow compactions. Avoid when you do not yet know the access patterns. Background: LSM trees and partitioning.

Document databases

Buys: an entire aggregate (an order with its lines) stored and read as one unit, with a flexible schema. Costs: relationships across documents are your problem; unbounded growth inside one document hurts. Fails: by oversized documents and inconsistent duplicates. Avoid for heavily relational data. See data models.

Search indexes

Buys: relevance ranking, text, fuzzy and faceted queries that B-trees cannot do. Costs: it is a derived copy of the source of truth, always slightly behind, which you must keep fed (change data capture). Fails: by lagging or diverging, and by expensive reindexing. Avoid when lookups are by exact key, and never treat it as the primary store. See specialised stores and chapter 11.

Object storage

Buys: very cheap, very durable storage of large immutable blobs, scaling to huge sizes without capacity planning. Costs: per-request latency in the tens of milliseconds, no in-place edits, and request charges. Fails: slow tail requests and per-prefix limits. Avoid for small frequently-updated records. The usual pattern is metadata in a database, bytes in object storage, with a pointer between them. Designed in chapter 8.

Moving data and work

Message queues versus logs

A queue hands each message to one worker and deletes it when acknowledged: ideal for work distribution, retries, and smoothing bursts. A log keeps an ordered, partitioned, replayable history that many consumers read independently at their own pace. Choose a queue for tasks ("send this email"); choose a log when several systems need the same events, when you need to replay after a bug, or when ordering by key matters. Costs of both: asynchronous semantics, at-least-once delivery (so consumers must be idempotent, safe retries), and backlog to monitor. Fails: consumer lag, poison messages, rebalances. A queue absorbs a burst only if the backlog is allowed to grow, so set its age limit. See queues and backpressure, streams and CDC and logs and exactly-once; building one is Studio 6.

Stream processors

Buys: continuous computation over events: counters, windows, joins, alerts. Costs: managed state, handling of late and out-of-order events, and an always-on pipeline to operate. Fails: state loss on restart, skew on one key, and double counting without careful delivery semantics. Avoid if hourly batch freshness is acceptable. Compare with batch and see the sketches used for heavy hitters in chapter 12.

Batch processing

Buys: simplicity and throughput on large data, with easy reprocessing when logic changes. Costs: latency measured in minutes to hours. Fails: late or silently incomplete runs. Prefer it for reports, reconciliation and backfills: the payments design relies on it to catch what the real-time path misses (chapter 16).

Coordination and protection

Coordination services

Buys: a small, strongly consistent store for leader election, locks, membership and configuration, built on consensus. Costs: low throughput, an ensemble to keep healthy, and subtle semantics around session expiry. Fails: loss of quorum, and clients that wrongly believe they still hold a lock. Avoid using it as a general database, and avoid it entirely if a lease or a conditional write in a store you already run can do the job. If you use locks, pair them with fencing tokens. See coordination services and leases and fencing.

Rate limiters

Buys: per-client fairness, abuse protection, and a guard for downstream capacity. Costs: shared counters or approximate local ones, a little latency, and false positives. Fails: the limiter itself becoming a dependency, so decide up front whether it fails open (allow) or closed (block). Avoid for trusted internal traffic where load shedding is the right tool. Designed in chapter 5; related patterns in resilience.

How to choose: five habits

  1. Start boring. In my opinion the best first answer is almost always: a stateless service, one relational database with a replica, and a cache only if the numbers demand it. Add every other block because of a number or requirement, and say which.
  2. Separate the source of truth from derived copies. Caches, search indexes, materialised views and analytics stores are rebuildable. The database or log is not. Knowing which is which tells you what you can lose.
  3. Match the store to the access pattern, not the brand. Say the pattern aloud ("lookups by id, 20:1 reads, 10 KB values") and let the choice follow.
  4. Prefer asynchronous only where the caller can wait or tolerate delay. A queue in a user-facing path turns a failure into a silent backlog.
  5. Count the moving parts. Each block adds failure modes, deploys, dashboards and on-call load. A design with seven well-chosen blocks beats one with fifteen decorative ones.

Resume-driven design

A block can be popular and still wrong for your problem. "We need Kafka because we have events" is not a reason; "three independent consumers need to replay 7 days of orders after a bug" is. Be especially suspicious of any block you cannot explain in terms of the numbers from your estimate.

Try it yourself

Pick a system you use (a ride app, a streaming service, a bank app). On paper, redraw the reference architecture for it, but include only the blocks it needs. For each block you keep, write one line: "needed because ...". For each you drop, write why not. Compare with a friend's choices: disagreements usually reveal a hidden assumption about scale or consistency.

Distributed systems connection

Almost every row in the table is a place where distribution shows its teeth: the cache and replica rows are about consistency, the queue and log rows about delivery guarantees, the coordination row about consensus, and the scheduler row about leases and clocks. When you add a block, you are choosing which of those problems to take on.

Key takeaways

  • For each block know four things: what it buys, what it costs, how it fails, and when to leave it out.
  • Start with a stateless service, one relational database and a replica; add blocks only when a number or requirement forces you, and say which.
  • Keep the source of truth separate from derived copies (caches, search indexes, analytics); derived data can be rebuilt.
  • Queues distribute work; logs retain replayable history for many readers. Both mean at-least-once delivery and idempotent consumers.
  • Coordination services and schedulers are distributed systems in their own right; prefer a lease or conditional write you already have.
  • Every extra box adds failure modes and operations. Fewer, well-justified blocks beat many decorative ones.

Check your understanding

When is a log a better fit than a queue?

  1. When each task should be processed by exactly one worker and then deleted
  2. When several independent consumers need the same ordered events, or you need to replay history
  3. When messages are tiny
  4. When you want no consumers to track position
A log retains events and lets each consumer keep its own position, so multiple systems can read and replay. A queue is for distributing tasks once. Message size is not the deciding factor.

Why should a search index never be your system of record?

  1. It cannot store text
  2. It is too slow to read
  3. It is always more expensive than a database
  4. It is a derived copy that lags and can diverge, so the authoritative data must live elsewhere and be able to rebuild it
Search indexes are optimised for queries, updated asynchronously, and rebuilt from the source when needed. Treating them as primary risks permanent loss or inconsistency.

A product has 300 writes/s and 5 TB of data. Which first-pass design is most defensible?

  1. A stateless service with a relational primary and replica, bodies in object storage if large, and a cache only if reads need it
  2. A sharded wide-column store with a global consensus layer
  3. A stream processor in front of every write
  4. A workflow engine for each request
300 writes/s is far below one primary's capacity, and 5 TB can be managed with care. Heavier machinery adds failure modes with no number justifying it.

What is the main risk of relying on DNS TTLs for fast failover?

  1. DNS cannot return more than one address
  2. DNS is not secure
  3. Resolvers and clients cache answers, some beyond the TTL, so clients keep using the dead address for a while
  4. DNS requires TLS
Caching at resolvers and clients, which sometimes ignore low TTLs, makes DNS failover slow and unpredictable. Use health-checked load balancers or anycast where seconds matter.

A rate limiter's backing store goes down. What must the design have decided in advance?

  1. Nothing; limiters never fail
  2. Whether to fail open (allow traffic) or fail closed (block), based on what each choice risks
  3. To restart the limiter every minute
  4. To switch to a CDN
Failing open risks overload or abuse; failing closed turns a limiter outage into a full outage. Which is right depends on the system, but the decision must be made deliberately, as the rate-limiter design discusses.
