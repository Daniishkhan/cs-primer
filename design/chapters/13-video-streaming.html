Design Video Streaming

Upload, transcoding pipelines, adaptive bitrate, CDNs and the economics of bytes. A video service is two systems joined at a segment store: a batch pipeline that makes uploads playable, and a cache hierarchy that moves petabytes a day to the viewers who want them.

Why this matters

Video is the clearest case where the interesting engineering is not the clever part. A feed or a chat service worries about QPS; a video service worries about bytes, and bytes are priced. The whole design bends around two numbers, computed in the estimate: the storage you pay for once per upload, and the egress you pay for on every minute watched, which is two orders of magnitude bigger. Once you see that, the pipeline, the segment format, the CDN strategy and even the ladder you encode are all consequences.

The problem

"Design a video streaming service like YouTube or Netflix: creators upload videos, and hundreds of millions of people watch them, on any device and any network, without buffering."

The vagueness hides three different systems. There is an ingest system (take a huge, unreliable upload and make it playable), a delivery system (move petabytes to the right place at the right quality), and a thin catalogue (titles, owners, counts, recommendations). The first is a batch pipeline, the second is a caching problem priced in dollars per gigabyte, and the third is ordinary web engineering. A good answer says so in the first minute and then spends the time on the first two. We follow the method of chapter 1; the block vocabulary is in chapter 3.

Estimate

The estimate answers four questions: how much does an hour of video cost to store once the ladder is encoded, how much compute does transcoding take, how much do viewers pull every day, and what the CDN hit ratio is worth in dollars. Every input is an assumption; the bitrates are illustrative H.264-class values and are not any service's real ladder. Prices are illustrative and labelled as such.

Decisions from the output.

  • It is a delivery problem, not a storage problem. New storage is about 1.5 PB a day (541 PB a year before redundancy, which erasure coding in the object store keeps near 1.4x), which is large but ordinary. Viewers pull about 112 PB a day. That is roughly 76 bytes out for every byte stored: the ratio that justifies a CDN and every caching decision below.
  • The ladder costs about 0.65 of the source. An hour's ladder is 8.7 GB against a 13.5 GB original (the original is assumed to be a high-bitrate upload), so keeping the original is cheap insurance: you can re-encode with a better codec in two years without asking creators to upload again. A second codec adds a further 5 GB per hour, which you only pay for on videos popular enough to repay the bandwidth savings.
  • Transcoding is a parallel-compute farm. At six CPU-hours per content-hour (an assumption; real encoders vary by an order of magnitude with preset and codec) the farm averages ~17,000 busy cores and needs headroom for a 2x peak. It is spiky, preemptible work, ideal for spot or batch capacity.
  • Ingest QPS is tiny. About 6 uploads a second on average, 17 at peak. The upload service is bandwidth-bound (83 Gbit/s average), not request-bound.
  • Egress sets the economics. At 31 Tbit/s peak, the same bytes cost $5.6M a day at an illustrative public-cloud egress price and $0.23M a day on amortised own hardware: a 25x spread. That spread is why companies at the top of the market build their own edge (Netflix Open Connect) and everyone else negotiates CDN contracts.
  • Hit ratio is worth millions. Taking the edge hit ratio from 90% to 99% cuts what the origin must serve from 1.04 to 0.10 Tbit/s average. The skew table shows how: with Zipf exponent 1.0, caching just 1% of titles serves about 68% of requests, and 20% serves about 89%.
  • Segment requests dominate request counts. At 4-second segments, about a million segment GETs per second on average. That is a CDN-scale request rate; the origin must never see it.

Three details are design choices. The upload is a resumable protocol: chunk uploads are idempotent (the same byte range written twice is the same bytes), the server answers a status query with what it holds, and complete is the only call that has a side effect beyond storage. Object stores give you the primitive (multipart, with parts that retry independently and an atomic complete: see the multipart section of chapter 8); open protocols such as tus and the Google resumable-upload convention put a resumable session in front of it. Playback never touches our API per segment: after one metadata call the player talks to the CDN, with a signed, expiring URL standing in for authorisation (data protection). And beacons are fire-and-forget events, batched client-side, that feed quality monitoring and counts.

Data model

  • Video metadata: videos(video_id, owner_id, title, description, status, duration_s, visibility, created_at, manifest_key, rungs[], codecs[]). Status is a small state machine: uploading, processing, ready, failed, removed. A relational store partitioned by video_id (or a wide-column store) is fine: this is a few hundred million rows of small records, read through a cache (caching). Choosing the store is boring; that is the point.
  • Upload sessions: uploads(upload_id, owner_id, size, parts_received bitmap, expires_at), short-lived, in a fast key-value store with a TTL; abandoned uploads are reaped and their parts aborted.
  • Raw store: object store keyed raw/{video_id}, kept for re-encoding. Cold-tiered after processing (see the tiering section of chapter 8).
  • Segment store: object store keyed v/{video_id}/{codec}/{rung}/seg_{n}.m4s plus rung playlists and the master manifest. Keys are deterministic, so a retried encode task overwrites its own output with identical bytes. Segments are immutable, which makes them perfectly cacheable (cache forever; change the video, change the key).
  • Job state: a workflow table or an orchestrator's own state (task graph, per-task status, attempts). Small, transactional, and the only place pipeline progress lives.
  • Counters: approximate view counts in a sharded counter store; exact, auditable counts in an event log (deep dive 4).

High-level design

The write path is a pipeline: upload, durable raw store, queue, workers, segment store. The read path is a cache hierarchy: player, edge, shield, segment store. The metadata database and the catalogue API are the small, conventional piece in the middle. Here is the main path, once from upload to the first segment:

Step through

A video, from upload to playback

  1. Start a resumable upload. The client asks the upload service for a session, gets an upload URL and a chunk size, and starts sending chunks (for example 8 MB each) in parallel. Nothing about the video is visible anywhere yet.
  2. Chunks land in the raw store. Each chunk is a part of a multipart upload into the raw object store (chapter 8). After a dropped connection the client asks which parts exist and sends only the missing ones. The final complete call stitches the parts into one original.
  3. Enqueue the pipeline. Completing the upload writes the video row with status processing and enqueues one job that carries the video id and the original's location. The job is idempotent: running it twice produces the same outputs under the same keys.
  4. Probe and split. A worker inspects the file (codec, resolution, duration, audio tracks), rejects what we cannot handle, and cuts the video at keyframes into segments of a few seconds to a minute, without re-encoding.
  5. Encode the ladder in parallel. One task per (segment, rung) pair fans out across many workers. Each task is independent, retryable and idempotent, and writes its output to a deterministic key. Thumbnails extract alongside.
  6. Package and publish. Per rung, encoded chunks become HLS/DASH segments and a rung playlist; then the master manifest is written. That write is the commit point: the metadata row flips to ready and the watch page can show the video.
  7. Play: manifest first. The player asks the watch API for the video, gets a master manifest URL (signed, if access is controlled) and fetches it through the CDN. The manifest lists the rungs and where their segments live.
  8. Play: segments and ABR. The player downloads segments, measures how fast they arrive, and picks the rung for the next one. The first viewer in a region misses the edge cache; the miss goes to the origin shield and, if needed, the segment store, and fills the cache on the way back.
  9. Play: hits and switches. Later viewers hit the edge. When a viewer's bandwidth dips, the next segment comes from a lower rung (same time range, aligned boundaries), so the switch is invisible apart from sharpness.

Deep dive 1: upload and the transcoding DAG

Upload

Treat a large upload like a distributed transaction that the network will interrupt. The client chunks the file, sends chunks in parallel (a few at a time, tuned to the link), and persists a map of confirmed chunks. After a failure it asks the server what it has and resumes. The server writes each chunk as a part into the raw store and records nothing visible until complete. Design points:

  • Chunk size. Large enough to amortise request overhead (several MB), small enough that a retry is cheap. Mobile clients benefit from smaller chunks and fewer parallel streams; broadband clients from larger.
  • Integrity. A checksum per chunk and one for the whole file, verified on complete (the end-to-end checksum argument from chapter 8).
  • Direct-to-storage. Issue pre-signed part URLs so bytes go from the client to the object store, not through our upload servers. The service handles sessions and metadata only. This removes the upload fleet from the bandwidth path.
  • Abuse. Quotas per account and size caps, rate limits on session creation (chapter 5), and a malware or content scan stage before publish. Hostile files target the decoder: run transcoding in a sandbox with strict resource limits.

The pipeline as a DAG

Transcoding one 8-minute video on one machine takes long, and a 3-hour film takes hours. The fix is the same as in batch processing: split the work so it parallelises. Video has a convenient property: if you cut at keyframes (frames that do not depend on earlier frames), each segment can be encoded independently. The job becomes a DAG.

Run it. The simulation schedules the same one-hour video as a DAG, with different encode granularities, on different worker counts. The job costs are assumptions chosen so that the ladder totals about six CPU-hours, consistent with the estimate.

"""Transcoding as a DAG of jobs, scheduled greedily on W identical workers (deterministic list scheduling).
Job costs are in worker-seconds and are ASSUMPTIONS: encoding cost per second of video grows with resolution."""
import heapq

def build(video_s, seg_s, rungs):
    cost_per_video_s = {"240p": 0.15, "360p": 0.25, "480p": 0.4, "720p": 0.8, "1080p": 1.6, "1440p": 2.9}   # worker-seconds per second of video
    jobs, deps = {}, {}
    jobs["probe"] = 5; deps["probe"] = []
    n = -(-video_s // seg_s)
    jobs["split"] = video_s * 0.01; deps["split"] = ["probe"]       # cut at keyframes, no re-encode
    for s in range(n):
        for r in rungs:
            j = f"enc:{r}:{s}"
            jobs[j] = seg_s * cost_per_video_s[r]; deps[j] = ["split"]
    for r in rungs:
        jobs[f"pkg:{r}"] = n * 0.2; deps[f"pkg:{r}"] = [f"enc:{r}:{s}" for s in range(n)]    # write HLS/DASH segments + rung playlist
    jobs["thumbs"] = 20; deps["thumbs"] = ["split"]
    jobs["manifest"] = 1; deps["manifest"] = [f"pkg:{r}" for r in rungs] + ["thumbs"]       # master playlist: the commit point
    return jobs, deps

def schedule(jobs, deps, workers):
    done, running, t = {}, [], 0.0                     # running: heap of (finish_time, job)
    pending = set(jobs); free = workers
    while pending or running:
        ready = sorted(j for j in pending if all(d in done for d in deps[j]))
        for j in ready:
            if free == 0: break
            pending.discard(j); free -= 1
            heapq.heappush(running, (t + jobs[j], j))
        t, j = heapq.heappop(running); done[j] = t; free += 1
    return t

def critical_path(jobs, deps):
    memo = {}
    def f(j):
        if j not in memo: memo[j] = jobs[j] + max((f(d) for d in deps[j]), default=0)
        return memo[j]
    return max(f(j) for j in jobs)

rungs = ["240p", "360p", "480p", "720p", "1080p", "1440p"]
video_s = 3600
for seg_s in (600, 60, 6):
    jobs, deps = build(video_s, seg_s, rungs)
    total = sum(jobs.values())
    print(f"critical path (infinite workers): {critical_path(jobs, deps)/60:.1f} min")
    print(f"1 hour of video, encode unit = {seg_s:>3} s -> {len(jobs):>5} jobs, {total/3600:5.2f} CPU-hours of work")
    for w in (1, 8, 64, 512):
        print(f"   {w:>4} workers: finished in {schedule(jobs, deps, w)/60:7.1f} min")
print("\nwith enough workers the finish time approaches the critical path; more workers cannot beat it.")

Read the output. With one worker, an hour of video takes 6 hours however it is cut. With 64 workers and 10-minute chunks it still takes about 30 minutes, because the largest chunk of the slowest rung is now the critical path; cut finer and 64 workers finish in under 9 minutes. The critical path line is the floor that more workers cannot beat: probe and split at the front, the longest encode, package and publish at the back. Finer chunks lower the floor but add scheduling overhead and slightly worse compression at chunk edges (rate control has less context), so the usual range is a few seconds to a minute. A three-hour film takes the same wall-clock time if you give it three times the workers: the pipeline is horizontally scalable per video as well as across videos.

OrchestrationA queue plus ad hoc workers; a workflow engine with durable stateA workflow engine (or a queue plus a task table) with per-task status. You need retries, timeouts, partial progress and a UI that says why a video is stuck. A bare queue loses the graph.

Failure handling. Each task has a timeout, a retry budget and a dead-letter path. A video that fails after its retries goes to failed with a reason the creator can see (corrupt file, unsupported codec); it is not retried forever. A poison file that crashes the decoder must not take down a worker pool: sandbox and cap each task. See resilience patterns for timeouts, bulkheads and retry budgets.

Deep dive 2: segments, manifests and adaptive bitrate

Streaming protocols look exotic but the idea is plain HTTP: cut the video into small files, describe them in a text file, and let the player download them like any other web resource. There is no streaming server holding a connection per viewer. That is why a CDN works so well: a segment is a static, cacheable object.

The two dominant formats are HLS (Apple's HTTP Live Streaming, specified in RFC 8216) and MPEG-DASH (an ISO standard, ISO/IEC 23009-1). They differ in manifest syntax (an .m3u8 playlist versus an XML .mpd) and historically in container (MPEG-TS versus fragmented MP4). With the Common Media Application Format (CMAF), both can reference the same fragmented-MP4 segments, so you encode and store one set of media and publish two small manifests. The client does the adaptation

The server has no idea how fast a viewer's network is right now; the player does. So adaptive bitrate (ABR) logic lives in the client. After each segment the player measures throughput (bytes over download time) and the playback buffer (seconds of video downloaded but not yet played), then picks the rung for the next segment. There are two families: throughput-based (pick the highest rung under a safety fraction of the estimated bandwidth) and buffer-based (pick by buffer level: big buffer, step up; small buffer, drop), the latter studied in a well-known paper by Huang and colleagues (SIGCOMM 2014). Real players blend them. The trade-off is three-way: quality (higher rungs), stability (fewer switches, which viewers notice) and rebuffering (the worst outcome). Here is a simplified player on a bandwidth trace with a dip:

"""A throughput + buffer ABR client (simplified) over a fixed bandwidth trace. Deterministic.
Rule: estimate throughput as an EWMA of segment download rates; pick the highest rung whose bitrate is under 0.8 x estimate;
be conservative (drop to the lowest rung) when the buffer is under 5 s; do not step up unless the buffer is over 10 s."""
ladder = [("240p", 0.43), ("360p", 0.73), ("480p", 1.33), ("720p", 2.63), ("1080p", 5.13)]   # Mbit/s incl. audio
SEG = 4.0                                                                                     # seconds per segment
# bandwidth in Mbit/s for each second of wall-clock time: good, then a dip (train tunnel), then recovery
trace = [8]*40 + [1.0]*25 + [0.3]*10 + [6]*60

def simulate(use_buffer_rule):
    t, buf, est, rung = 0.0, 0.0, None, 0
    played_s = stall_s = 0.0
    log, started = [], False
    for seg in range(30):
        # choose
        if est is None: pick = 0
        else:
            pick = max([i for i, (_, b) in enumerate(ladder) if b <= 0.8 * est], default=0)
            if use_buffer_rule:
                if buf < 5: pick = 0
                elif pick > rung and buf < 10: pick = rung
        rung = pick
        size_mbit = ladder[rung][1] * SEG
        # download: integrate the trace until size is delivered
        got, dl_start = 0.0, t
        while got < size_mbit:
            bw = trace[min(int(t), len(trace)-1)]
            step = min(1.0 - (t % 1.0), (size_mbit - got) / bw)
            got += bw * step; t += step
        dl = t - dl_start
        # playback drains the buffer while we download
        if started:
            drain = min(buf, dl); stall = dl - drain
            buf -= drain; stall_s += stall
        buf += SEG
        if not started: started = True
        thr = size_mbit / dl
        est = thr if est is None else 0.6 * est + 0.4 * thr
        log.append((seg, ladder[rung][0], round(t, 1), round(buf, 1), round(thr, 2)))
    return log, stall_s

The second run is the point. The throughput-only player reaches 1080p, then the dip arrives (8 Mbit/s falls to 1 and then 0.3), its estimate lags, it keeps requesting a rung the network cannot carry, and the buffer empties: about 3 seconds of stalled video in this toy trace. Adding the buffer rule (stay conservative when the buffer is low; do not step up unless it is comfortably full) cost nothing in quality here and avoided the stall. Notice how the estimate lags reality even then: the player was still on 720p while the link delivered under 1 Mbit/s. Real players add startup tricks (begin low, ramp quickly), segment abandonment (cancel a slow download and fetch a lower rung), and per-device caps (no 4K rung on a phone). All of it is client code you ship and tune with the beacon data, so ABR is also a release-engineering problem: roll out a new algorithm to 1% and compare rebuffer rates (deployments).

Deep dive 3: CDN delivery, cache hit ratio and the economics of bytes

Everything in the estimate says the CDN is the product. Segments are immutable, uniform, cacheable objects, so a standard HTTP cache hierarchy works (HTTP caching, performance): edge caches near viewers, a regional or shield layer that merges misses, and the origin (the segment store). Three levers decide cost and quality.

1. Hit ratio, and what skew does for you

Video popularity is heavily skewed. The table in the estimate models it as a Zipf distribution: at exponent 1.0, a cache holding the top 1% of titles answers about 68% of requests, and the top 20% about 89%. Two consequences. First, the head is easy to cache and the tail is hopeless: the long tail (the many videos watched by a handful of people) has hit ratios near zero at any edge, so you accept those misses and make them cheap. Second, the exponent matters more than the cache size: a service with a few mega-hits (a new season) is far cheaper to serve than one with a flat catalogue. Measure your own, because the answer can move the whole business case.

Hit ratio can be raised without a bigger cache:

  • Origin shield / tiered caching. Many edges ask one parent for the same missing segment, so the origin sees one request, not hundreds. This turns thundering misses on a new release into a single fetch (and uses request coalescing: concurrent misses for the same key wait for one fetch, the same remedy as for a cache stampede).
  • Cache key hygiene. Signed URLs put a token in the query string; if the token is part of the cache key, every viewer has a distinct key and hit ratio collapses. Cache on the path, validate the signature at the edge, and ignore the token in the key.
  • Pre-warm / pre-position. For a predictable release you do not wait for the first viewers to miss. Push the popular rungs to edges before the launch, ideally during off-peak hours when the links are cheap. This is the heart of Netflix's Open Connect design, where appliances placed inside ISP networks are filled off-peak with the titles forecast to be popular, and viewers are steered to them; the case study has the sources, and the broader idea of caches placed inside access networks is in ISP caches.
  • Popularity-aware eviction. Plain LRU lets a one-off long-tail view evict a hot segment. Admission policies that require a second request before caching (and size-aware eviction) protect the head; see chapter 6.
  • Fewer rungs in cache. Every extra rung multiplies the working set. Edge caches can hold only the rungs actually requested in that region, and evict the 1440p rung that nobody there can play.

What does not move is the shape: egress cost is linear in bytes, so every optimisation that removes bytes (a better codec, a lower default rung on mobile, a higher hit ratio) is worth the same fraction of the bill. 3. Steering and failure

Which edge serves a viewer is a decision made by DNS, anycast or an application-level redirect that consults health and load (load balancing, DNS steering). Players can also fail over: if the manifest lists two base URLs, a player that sees errors from one switches to the other mid-stream. That client-side failover is what makes a CDN outage a quality blip rather than an outage. The latency tail here is the segment fetch that misses all the way to origin; protect it with hedging and timeouts (tail latency).

Deep dive 4: metadata, counts, thumbnails, DRM and live

Metadata is the easy part, so keep it easy

The watch page needs one small record and a signed manifest URL. Cache the record aggressively; stale for a few seconds is fine except for state changes that matter (a video removed for a legal reason must disappear fast, which is a purge, not a TTL). The one correctness rule: never expose a video as ready before its manifest and all rungs exist, which is why the manifest write is the commit point and the status flip follows it.

View counts are approximate, on purpose

A hot video is one counter incremented by tens of thousands of players a second: the textbook hot key. Incrementing one row is a contention disaster (row locks). The approach:

  • Count events, not rows. Players send "play started" and "30 seconds watched" beacons. The platform defines a view (for example, at least 30 seconds, deduplicated per viewer and window), which doubles as spam defence.
  • Buffer and aggregate. Beacons flow into a log (streams), stream processors sum per video per minute with a windowed aggregation, and write the sum, not the events, to the serving store. Hot keys are spread across several partial counters and merged on read.
  • Serve an approximate number. The count on the page is the last aggregate plus, optionally, a short in-memory delta. It lags by seconds to minutes and may be corrected later. Nobody is harmed by seeing 1,203,411 instead of 1,203,950.
  • Approximate structures where they help. "Unique viewers" is a cardinality problem (HyperLogLog), and "trending now" is a heavy-hitters problem (count-min sketch and windows), both treated in chapter 12. The same beacon stream feeds both.
  • But money is exact. If counts decide what a creator is paid or what an advertiser is billed, the numbers come from a separate path: an append-only event log, deduplicated and reconciled, in the spirit of the click-aggregation problem in the payments chapter. Display counts are cheap and approximate; billing counts are expensive and exact. Saying so explicitly is a strong signal.

Thumbnails and previews

Thumbnails are generated in the pipeline: a poster frame (selected by a simple heuristic such as skipping black frames, or chosen by the creator), a sprite sheet of small frames for scrubbing, and perhaps a short animated preview. They are small images, served through the same CDN, in the same pipeline DAG and under the same deterministic-key rule. Because there are billions of thumbnail views against millions of video plays, they make up a surprisingly large share of CDN requests even though they are tiny in bytes, so cache and size them with the same care.

DRM in a paragraph

Premium content must not be copyable from a cache or a network capture. The usual answer is to encrypt the segments once and make the player obtain a decryption key from a licence server that checks entitlement. Three systems cover the device world (Widevine, Apple's FairPlay and Microsoft's PlayReady), and common encryption (CENC, with the CBCS mode in common use) lets one set of encrypted segments be decrypted by any of them given the right licence. The CDN keeps serving ciphertext; only the licence path needs strong authentication, per-user entitlement and key management (keys and secrets). Be honest about its limits: DRM raises the cost of piracy, it does not stop a determined person from recording the screen.

SLOs. Playback is measured by the viewer's experience, not by server uptime: video start failure rate under 0.5%, startup time p50 under 2 s and p95 under 5 s, rebuffering ratio under 1% of watch time, and a quality SLI (fraction of watch time at or above 720p on adequate links). These come from client beacons, which is why the beacon pipeline is operationally critical even though it is off the playback path. Pipeline SLO: 95% of videos playable within 10 minutes of upload completion and fully encoded within 30. Alert on burn rate (alerting) for the playback SLIs and on queue age for the pipeline. Roll out encoder and ladder changes on new uploads first, compare quality scores and bitrate on a sample, and only then backfill old videos. Change client ABR logic like any client release: percentage rollout with automatic rollback on rebuffer ratio.

Trade-offs & alternatives

  • Storage versus compute versus bandwidth. Encoding more rungs and codecs spends compute and storage to save bandwidth. Encoding lazily (on the first request for a rung) saves storage and compute on the unwatched tail at the cost of a slow first view; the common compromise is eager for a core ladder, lazy for the rest.
  • Startup versus quality. Short segments and a low first rung start fast and look soft for a moment. This is a product choice, not a technical one.
  • Own edge versus rented. Building your own edge pays off only at huge, predictable volume with a small hot set; below that the capital and relationships do not repay. An opinion, with the cost arithmetic above as the support.
  • If the content were short clips and the audience mostly mobile, use shorter segments, aggressive low-rung defaults and more reliance on the first-mile quality; a lower startup matters more than 4K.
  • If everything were live, the pipeline becomes a real-time system and caching works on a window of seconds; see the live studio.
  • If rights restricted content by region, the entitlement check moves to the manifest and licence path, and the CDN must honour geo rules without destroying the cache key.

What a strong answer adds

  • Does the egress arithmetic early and says that delivery, not storage or request rate, is the design's centre of gravity.
  • Names the segment idea (plain HTTP and a manifest) and why it makes CDNs applicable, and keeps boundaries aligned across rungs so switching is seamless.
  • Draws the transcoding job as a DAG, explains keyframe-aligned splitting and the critical path, and makes every task idempotent with deterministic output keys and a manifest-last commit.
  • Puts adaptive bitrate in the client, can describe throughput-based and buffer-based logic and the stability/quality/rebuffer trade-off.
  • Quantifies the CDN hit ratio with a popularity-skew model and lists levers: shield, request coalescing, cache-key hygiene with signed URLs, pre-positioning, admission policy.
  • Separates approximate display counts from exact billing counts, and points to sketches for uniques and trending.
  • Measures experience with client beacons and defines SLIs in viewer terms, not server terms.
  • Keeps DRM, thumbnails and live in proportion: one paragraph each, with the correct reason for each separate path.

Follow-up questions

A new episode of a hugely popular show releases at 9 p.m. How do you avoid melting the origin?

Pre-position: the title is known in advance, so push the popular rungs to edges and shields before release, off-peak. At the edge, request coalescing turns a flood of concurrent misses for the same segment into one upstream fetch, and the shield does the same for many edges. Rate-limit the origin so overload degrades to a lower rung rather than errors. Release time is the one time a launch plan beats a reactive cache.

How does a viewer in the middle of a video switch from 1080p to 480p without a visible glitch?

Segment boundaries are aligned across rungs and each starts at a keyframe. The player finishes segment N at 1080p, then requests segment N+1 from the 480p rung, whose first frame is a keyframe at the same timestamp. The decoder is re-initialised from the new rung's init segment if needed. The viewer sees a drop in sharpness, not a pause.

What if the upload service crashes with 90% of a 4 GB file uploaded?

The client's next call asks for the session status; the server reports the parts it has durably stored (parts live in the object store, not in the crashed process's memory), and the client sends only the missing ones. The session is stateless apart from a small record in a key-value store. Completion is idempotent, so a retried complete does not enqueue a second pipeline.

How would you count views for a video that goes viral?

Never increment one row per view. Send beacons, deduplicate by a viewer/window definition, aggregate per minute in a stream processor with the hot key split across several partial counters, and serve the merged total a few seconds behind. For uniques use a HyperLogLog. Keep a separate exact path for anything billed. If asked how stale is acceptable: seconds to minutes for display; zero tolerance for payouts.

Why not stream with a long-lived connection from a media server instead of segments?

Per-viewer connections to stateful servers cannot be cached, so every viewer costs origin capacity and bandwidth, and failover means reconnect and resync. Segments over HTTP are stateless and cacheable, so the CDN absorbs nearly all traffic and the origin scales with the catalogue, not the audience. The price is a few seconds of latency, which is fine for VOD and is the central tension in live.

How do you re-encode the whole library with a better codec?

Because we kept the originals, it is a background batch of the same DAG, run at the lowest priority on preemptible capacity, oldest-popular-first: encode only the videos whose view counts repay the compute and bandwidth. Publish the new rung set additively in the manifest so old players keep working, and watch bitrate and quality scores on a sample before expanding.

Distributed systems connection

The pipeline is a task graph with idempotent tasks and a commit point, so it exercises the same ideas as effectively-once processing and the manifest-last pattern of the object store. The CDN is a hierarchy of caches that depends on immutable keys to be correct without invalidation. And the view-count path is the clearest example of choosing an approximate answer because the exact one is a coordination cost you do not need (coordination avoidance).

Key takeaways

  • Do the egress arithmetic first: viewing moves roughly two orders of magnitude more bytes than uploading stores, so delivery drives cost and design.
  • Streaming is plain HTTP: a manifest plus small, immutable, keyframe-aligned segments per rung. That makes every segment a cacheable object and puts adaptation in the client.
  • Transcoding is a DAG: probe, split at keyframes, encode every (segment, rung) in parallel, package, publish. Tasks are idempotent with deterministic keys, and the manifest write is the commit point.
  • Cache hit ratio is the lever: model popularity skew, then use a shield with request coalescing, clean cache keys with signed URLs, pre-positioning and admission policy.
  • ABR trades quality, stability and rebuffering; a buffer-aware rule avoids stalls that a throughput-only rule causes when bandwidth dips.
  • View counts shown to users are approximate and asynchronous; counts that pay people are exact and come from a separate log. Say which is which.
  • Measure the viewer, not the server: SLIs for start failures, startup time, rebuffering and quality come from client beacons.
  • Live reuses the delivery layer but is a real-time pipeline with a thundering herd on the newest segment: a different design, the next studio.
