Design a Payment System

Idempotency, double-entry ledgers, payment service providers, reconciliation and exactly-once money. The core of the design is one habit, applied at every network call: say what state the payment is in if no reply ever arrives, and have a way to ask.

Why this matters

A payment system is the part of an engineering organisation where mistakes have a unit of currency attached. Almost every technique in this course appears in it in its strictest form: idempotency, state machines, transactions, outboxes, reconciliation, signing, and designing for the case where you do not know what happened. The exercise to take away is not a list of components. It is the habit of asking, at every network call, what if I never hear back, and having a written answer.

The problem

"Design a payment system for an e-commerce platform. Customers pay with cards; merchants get paid; every cent must be accounted for."

Ambiguities worth surfacing: are we the merchant integrating one or more payment providers, or are we building the provider? How are refunds and disputes handled? What does 'every cent accounted for' mean operationally (the answer is: a ledger and a daily reconciliation)? We assume the common case: a platform with its own checkout that uses an external payment service provider (PSP) to talk to the card networks, and builds its own payment service and ledger around it. Building the PSP's side (connecting to acquirers and networks, holding card data at scale) is a different and far larger system.

Clarify requirements

Functional. Create a payment (idempotent); authorize; capture (full or partial); void; refund; receive provider events; notify merchants by webhook; keep a ledger; reconcile with settlement files. Non-functional. No double charge, no lost payment, exact arithmetic, an audit trail for every state change, recoverable from any crash, and operable by a small team (the reconciliation breaks must be a handful a day, not thousands).

Estimate

Payments are low-QPS and high-stakes: the estimates are about latency of external calls, storage over years and how many exceptions humans must handle. The same script shows why money is never a float.

The idea

  1. rate ← per_day ÷ 86,400; peak ← rate × 6; in_flight ← peak × latency
  2. rows ← payments × 4; bytes ← rows × row size; keys ← payments × 500 B
  3. breaks/day ← payments × mismatch rate (human reconciliation work)
  4. show float drift (0.1 + 0.2, sum 0.1 a thousand times) vs integer cents
  5. split: floor each share, give leftover cents to the largest remainders

# ── 5. Splitting an amount exactly ────────────────────────────────────
def split(total_minor, weights):
    """Split an integer amount by weights; the shares sum to the total exactly.

    Largest-remainder method: give everyone the rounded-down share, then hand
    the leftover units, one each, to the shares that lost the most to rounding.
    """
    weight_sum = sum(weights)
    shares = [total_minor * w // weight_sum for w in weights]
    leftover = total_minor - sum(shares)          # always < len(weights)

    def lost_to_rounding(i):
        return (total_minor * weights[i]) % weight_sum

    order = sorted(range(len(weights)), key=lost_to_rounding, reverse=True)
    for i in order[:leftover]:                    # biggest loss first
        shares[i] += 1
    return shares

three_ways = split(10_000, [1, 1, 1])
fee_split  = split(5, [70, 20, 10])

print(f"split a 5-cent fee 70/20/10:     {fee_split} sum={sum(fee_split)}")

Decisions from the output.

  • Throughput is ordinary; correctness is not. 3,500 payments a second at peak and ~14,000 PSP calls a second is a modest, horizontally scalable service tier, but it is not a single database primary's easy problem once the ledger is counted (~14,000 rows a second at peak): shard the ledger by account or merchant, and keep each payment's records on one shard so the state change and its ledger entries commit in one local transaction.
  • The external call dominates latency and ties up capacity: by Little's law, ~1,000 requests are in flight at peak at 300 ms each, and many more at the 3-second tail. Timeouts, bulkheads and a circuit breaker per PSP are not optional (resilience), because a slow PSP fills your threads long before it fails.
  • Storage is small but forever. About 15 TB a year of append-only ledger rows. Partition by time, archive cold partitions, never delete: retention rules commonly run to seven years or more, and ledgers are append-only for correctness, not just policy.
  • Idempotency keys are cheap (tens of GB for a day's keys) and held for at least a day, usually longer, because retries can arrive late.
  • Reconciliation is a join of two big files, not a stream: about 7.5 GB of settlement data a day against 200M ledger rows is a sort-merge that finishes in minutes. At a mismatch rate of one in 100,000, that is still 500 exceptions a day, which is why automatic classification of breaks matters more than the matching.
  • Floats corrupt money quietly (the second half of the output). The fix is integers in minor units, and explicit allocation when splitting.

GET  /v1/payments/{id}          -> current state + history

-- inbound from the PSP: ack fast, process asynchronously
POST /webhooks/psp   (signature header, timestamp)

-- outbound to merchants
POST {merchant_url}  (our signature header)
  { event_id, type: "payment.captured", data }  -> merchant returns 2xx

Notes. amount_minor is an integer count of the currency's smallest unit; the currency code travels with every amount. The API can answer 202 processing, because honest answers about unknown outcomes are part of the contract. The idempotency key is required on every mutating call; its design, including the lock and the stored response, is the subject of the Stripe idempotency case study, and the general API rules are in API design; I use those rather than re-derive them. Webhooks are the asynchronous channel in both directions, with signatures, covered in deep dive 4.

Data model

  • payments: (payment_id, merchant_id, amount_minor, currency, state, version, psp_key, psp_ref, captured_minor, refunded_minor, created_at, updated_at). version makes each transition a compare-and-set. psp_key is derived from payment_id and stable across retries.
  • idempotency_keys: (merchant_id, key, request_hash, state: started|completed, response, locked_until, expires_at) with a unique constraint on (merchant_id, key). Same database as payments so creating the payment and completing the key can share a transaction.
  • ledger_entries: (entry_id, txn_id, account_id, amount_minor, currency, payment_id, kind, created_at). Append-only, double-entry: every txn_id sums to zero per currency, enforced by a check at write and audited nightly. Unique on (payment_id, kind) so replaying a transition cannot post twice. Designed in the ledger studio; I do not repeat it.
  • outbox: (event_id, aggregate_id, type, payload, created_at, published_at), written in the same transaction as the state change.
  • psp_events: every inbound webhook, raw, keyed by the PSP's event id (unique), with a processed flag. Raw storage means a bug in our handler is recoverable by replay.
  • recon_items: the daily reconciliation output: one row per compared item with a classification and a resolution.
  • Partitioning: payments, keys and ledger rows for one merchant (or one payment) co-located, so the transactions that matter are local. Cross-shard flows (a marketplace split between a buyer's payment and several sellers) go through a ledger transaction on one shard or a saga.

High-level design

Who is involved

A card payment crosses more parties than most engineers expect, and most of what makes the design hard lives in the gaps between them. In simple terms:

ActorWhat it does for a payment
CardholderThe customer. Enters card details in a form that goes to the PSP, not to us.
MerchantThe business being paid. In a marketplace, our platform acts for many merchants.
Payment service (us)Owns the payment's lifecycle, the idempotency contract, the ledger, notifications and reconciliation. Never holds card numbers.
PSP (payment service provider)Provides the API: stores card data in a vault and returns tokens, routes authorizations to the acquirer, reports outcomes, provides settlement reports, sends webhooks.
AcquirerThe merchant's bank (or its processor): submits transactions to the card networks and receives funds on the merchant's behalf.
Card networkVisa, Mastercard and similar: the rails and rules between acquirers and issuers, including clearing and dispute rules.
IssuerThe cardholder's bank: decides whether to approve (funds and fraud checks), places the hold, and later pays out through the network. Also receives disputes.

In simplified order: authorization is an online question from the merchant's side to the issuer, "will you honour this amount on this card?", and an approval puts a hold on the cardholder's available funds or credit but moves no money. Capture says "now take it": the merchant (through the PSP) confirms the amount to charge, which can be less than the authorized amount. Clearing and settlement are the batch, mostly overnight, process by which captured transactions are exchanged through the network and money actually moves, net of fees, issuer to acquirer to merchant, usually taking one to a few business days. Authorizations that are never captured eventually expire (commonly within days; it varies by network and merchant category). A refund is a new, separate movement of money back, referencing the original capture; it is not an "undo". A chargeback is a dispute raised by the cardholder with the issuer: the issuer pulls the funds back through the network, the merchant is notified and may submit evidence, and the outcome (won or lost) moves money again, often with a fee. The window to raise one is long, often months, and rules differ by network and reason. These are the facts that shape the state machine below; precise timings and rules are network- and contract-specific, so check your PSP's documentation rather than hard-coding any of them.

The system

Two properties of the diagram carry the design. First, the PSP is an unreliable remote system with its own state: whatever we believe about a payment, the PSP may believe something different, and the reconciler and resolver exist to bring the two together. Second, the middle row (payment, ledger and outbox in one database transaction) is the only place where our truth changes. Here is the main path, including the case everyone forgets:

Step through

Creating a payment when the PSP times out

  1. The request. The merchant app posts the payment with an Idempotency-Key it generated for this purchase. The key is created once per purchase attempt, before the first send, and reused on every retry.
  2. Idempotency gate. The API looks the key up. New key: insert it with a fingerprint of the request body and a lock. Seen key with the same body: return the stored response (or 409 in progress if another request is still running). Seen key with a different body: reject with 422.
  3. Persist the intent. Before touching the PSP, commit the payment row in state authorizing with a psp_key derived from our payment id. If we crash after the PSP call we will know we owe an enquiry. No database transaction stays open across the network call.
  4. Call the PSP. The adapter sends the authorization with the PSP-side idempotency key and a timeout (a few seconds). The PSP talks to the acquirer, the card network and the issuer; that chain is not ours to see.
  5. Timeout: unknown. No answer arrives. We cannot tell whether the request was lost before the PSP, or the PSP authorized and the reply was lost. Marking the payment failed would risk a customer charged for an order we cancelled. It becomes unknown.
  6. Resolve by asking. A resolver queries the PSP by psp_key. Three answers: authorized, declined, or never heard of it. The last means the first request never arrived and a retry with the same key is safe. In every case the payment moves on.
  7. Commit the outcome. One database transaction: the payment goes to authorized (a compare-and-set on the previous state), the ledger entries are inserted (unique per payment and kind), and an outbox row is written. All or nothing.
  8. Tell the world. The outbox publisher emits payment.authorized to the merchant as a signed webhook and to internal consumers, retrying until acknowledged. It may deliver twice; every consumer dedupes on the event id.
  9. The retry gets the same answer. If the merchant's original request had timed out and was retried with the same key, it now gets the stored response. The merchant may have retried three times; there is one payment.

Every transition is a compare-and-set on (payment_id, version), so two actors racing (a capture request and a void; a webhook and a resolver) cannot both win; and each is idempotent: applying the same event again leaves the payment where it already is. That property, not a messaging guarantee, is what gives the system its exactly-once effect, as the first deep dive shows.

Deep dive 1: idempotency end to end, and exactly-once money

There is no exactly-once delivery across a network (you met the argument in the failure chapter); you get at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, which has the same effect. In a payment system that has to hold at every boundary, not only at the front door:

BoundaryWhat can duplicate or be lostThe idempotency mechanism
Merchant to our APIClient retries after a timeout, double-click, a load balancer retryIdempotency key: stored with a request fingerprint, lock and the response. Same key and body: replay the stored response. Different body: 422. In progress: 409 (or wait). Keys expire after a window (a day or more).
Our API to our databaseTwo concurrent requests with the same keyUnique constraint on (merchant_id, key); the loser reads the winner's row
Us to the PSPOur retry after a timeout; a crash between the PSP's answer and our commitA stable PSP-side idempotency key derived from payment_id (never random per attempt). Retrying with it returns the first result instead of charging again
PSP to us (webhooks)At-least-once, out-of-order, delayedDedupe on the PSP event_id (unique); treat the webhook as a hint and fetch current state when order matters
State change to ledgerA replayed transition posts twiceUnique (payment_id, kind) on ledger transactions, and the transition itself is a compare-and-set on the state
State change to the outside worldA crash between 'commit' and 'notify'Outbox: the event is committed with the state change, and a publisher delivers at least once; consumers dedupe on event id

The pattern that makes the rows hang together: persist intent, then act, then record the result, all idempotently. The outbox pattern is treated in streams and CDC and the delivery semantics in logs and exactly-once; the point here is to apply it consistently. Two details that matter in practice:

  • Derive the PSP key from your own identity, and persist it before the call. If the key were generated at call time and the process died, you would have no way to ask the PSP about a request you cannot name.
  • Never hold a database transaction open across the PSP call. A three-second external call inside a transaction holds row locks and a connection for three seconds, multiplied by your concurrency. Commit the intent, release, call, then commit the outcome. The state machine is what makes this safe: a crash anywhere leaves a recognisable state (authorizing) with enough information to continue.

Deep dive 2: unknown outcomes, timeouts and reconciliation

Unknown outcomes

The defining difficulty of payments is a call that returns nothing. There are three possible worlds behind a timeout: the request never reached the PSP; the PSP processed it and the answer was lost; or the PSP is still processing. From the outside they look identical, and the two wrong reactions are both disastrous: assume failure and retry with a new key (the customer may be charged twice), or assume success and ship the goods (maybe nobody paid). The right reaction is to admit the state (unknown), then resolve it by asking with a query that is cheap and safe: look up the payment by our key. The three answers map cleanly: approved, declined, or "no record of this key", and the last one is the permission to retry with that same key. Where the PSP offers no lookup by merchant reference, you cannot safely resolve a timeout by anything but waiting for the webhook and the settlement file, which is a good reason to choose a PSP that does. A resolver runs continuously with backoff on payments stuck in unknown, escalating after a bound (say, 30 minutes) to a human queue.

The simulation implements the core of this: idempotency keys on the front door, a stable PSP key, a PSP that sometimes loses the request and sometimes loses the reply, the state machine with unknown, a double-entry ledger written with the state change, an outbox, a resolver, and finally a reconciliation run against a deliberately damaged settlement file. (It posts the authorization hold to the ledger at authorization time to keep the example short; a real ledger design is in the ledger studio.)

The idea

  1. PSP: may lose request or reply; stores each charge under OUR key
  2. create(key) → same payment on replay; authorize → result | unknown
  3. resolver: for each unknown, ask PSP.query(key) and apply the answer
  4. 2,000 orders + client retries, then 3 resolver passes (SAME key)
  5. check: charges == orders; ledger sums to 0; owed == authorized
  6. reconcile ours vs settlement file → missing, extra, amount mismatch
"""A payment service in miniature: idempotency keys, a state machine, a flaky
PSP with UNKNOWN outcomes, a double-entry ledger, an outbox, reconciliation.
Deterministic (seeded). Amounts are integer minor units (cents)."""
import random
from collections import Counter

# ── 1. A flaky PSP that dedupes on our key ────────────────────────────
class PSP:
    """The external processor. It stores charges under the idempotency key WE
    send, so retrying with the same key can never create a second charge."""

    def __init__(self, rnd):
        self.rnd = rnd
        self.charges = {}       # psp_key -> (status, amount)
        self.calls = 0

    def authorize(self, key, amount):
        self.calls += 1
        r = self.rnd.random()
        if key not in self.charges:
            if r < 0.08:
                # Lost on the way in: the PSP never saw it, nothing happened.
                raise TimeoutError("request lost before the PSP saw it")
            status = "declined" if r < 0.15 else "authorized"
            self.charges[key] = (status, amount)
        if 0.15 <= r < 0.30:
            # Lost on the way out: the PSP committed, the answer never arrives.
            raise TimeoutError("PSP committed, but the response was lost")
        return self.charges[key][0]

    def query(self, key):
        """Cheap, safe lookup by our key: how an unknown gets resolved."""
        status, _ = self.charges.get(key, ("unknown_to_psp", 0))
        return status

    def settlement_file(self, drop_one=None):
        rows = [(key, amount)
                for key, (status, amount) in self.charges.items()
                if status == "authorized"]
        if drop_one is not None and rows:
            rows.pop(drop_one)                  # a row the PSP forgot to settle
        return rows


# ── 2. Our service: idempotent create, state machine, ledger, outbox ──
class Payments:
    def __init__(self, psp):
        self.psp = psp
        self.pay = {}           # payment_id -> {state, amount, psp_key, ...}
        self.idem = {}          # client idempotency key -> payment_id
        self.ledger = []        # (payment_id, account, signed amount)
        self.outbox = []        # (event type, payment_id), published later

    def _post(self, pid, debit, credit, amount):
        """Double entry: every movement has two sides that sum to zero."""
        self.ledger.append((pid, debit, +amount))
        self.ledger.append((pid, credit, -amount))

    def create(self, idem_key, amount, merchant):
        if idem_key in self.idem:
            return self.idem[idem_key]          # a replay gets the SAME payment
        pid = f"pay_{len(self.pay) + 1:06d}"
        # The PSP key is derived from OUR id, so it is stable across retries and
        # we can always ask the PSP about it, even after a crash.
        self.pay[pid] = dict(state="created", amount=amount,
                             merchant=merchant, psp_key=pid)
        self.idem[idem_key] = pid
        return pid

    def authorize(self, pid):
        p = self.pay[pid]
        if p["state"] != "created":
            return p["state"]                   # only created -> * is allowed
        p["state"] = "authorizing"              # persist intent BEFORE the call

        result = None
        for _attempt in range(3):
            try:
                result = self.psp.authorize(p["psp_key"], p["amount"])
                break
            except TimeoutError:
                result = None                   # unknown, NOT failed

        if result is None:
            p["state"] = "unknown"              # resolver asks the PSP later
            return "unknown"
        self._finish(pid, result)
        return p["state"]

    def _finish(self, pid, result):
        """State change + ledger lines + outbox event: ONE transaction."""
        p = self.pay[pid]
        if result == "authorized":
            p["state"] = "authorized"
            self._post(pid, debit="auth_holds_receivable",
                       credit="merchant_pending", amount=p["amount"])
            self.outbox.append(("payment.authorized", pid))
        elif result == "declined":
            p["state"] = "declined"
            self.outbox.append(("payment.declined", pid))
        elif result == "unknown_to_psp":
            p["state"] = "created"              # PSP never saw it: retry

    # ── 3. Resolver: settle UNKNOWN by asking, never by guessing ──────
    def resolve_unknown(self):
        resolved = 0
        for pid, p in self.pay.items():
            if p["state"] == "unknown":
                self._finish(pid, self.psp.query(p["psp_key"]))
                resolved += 1
        return resolved


def states(svc):
    return dict(Counter(p["state"] for p in svc.pay.values()))

Reconciliation

Reconciliation is the safety net that catches everything the real-time design misses: bugs, operator errors, PSP incidents, and the rare unknown that never resolved. Daily, join three sources: our payments and ledger, the PSP's API view, and the PSP's settlement files (and, one level further, the bank statements for actual money received). The classes of break and what each means:

BreakMeaningTypical resolution
In our records, not in the PSP's fileWe think a payment succeeded; the PSP has no trace (or it is not settled yet)First check timing (cut-off times, time zones, weekends); then void/cancel ours, or investigate the PSP
In the PSP's file, not in oursThe PSP charged the customer and we lost the payment. The most dangerous classCreate the missing record from the PSP data and notify the merchant, or refund; alert immediately and find the cause

DuplicateTwo PSP charges for one orderRefund one; fix the idempotency hole that allowed it

Four practices separate a reconciliation that works from one that produces noise. Match on stable references (the psp_key and the PSP's id), not on amount and time. Never edit history: fix breaks with new, compensating ledger entries that cite the break. Automate the boring classes (known fee patterns, timing differences that clear next day), so humans see only the unexplained. And measure it: the number of open breaks, their age, and their value are SLIs. A break older than a few days is an incident. The cost is real and unglamorous; it is also the reason an auditor and a CFO trust the numbers.

Daily settlement, in one line

Money arrives from the PSP in batches (net of its fees and disputes), so the ledger models receivable from the PSP separately from owed to merchants. When a settlement file lands and matches, entries move funds from the first account to the second. The ledger design, including multi-currency accounts and how to prove balance, is in the ledger studio.

Deep dive 3: money as data, currency and rounding

  • Integers in minor units. $49.99 is the integer 4999 with currency USD. Every amount in the API, database and ledger is an integer; formatting happens at the edge. The napkin output shows why: 1.1 times 3 is 3.3000000000000003, and adding 0.1 a thousand times gives 99.9999999999986.
  • Minor units differ by currency. The ISO 4217 table gives each currency's exponent: USD and EUR have 2, JPY has 0 (one yen is the smallest unit), and some currencies (for example the Kuwaiti dinar) have 3. So the currency is part of the amount, and code that multiplies by 100 is a bug waiting for its first yen. Store (amount_minor, currency) as an inseparable pair.
  • Never mix currencies in arithmetic. Adding USD and EUR amounts is a type error. A conversion is an explicit step that records the rate, its source and time, and the rounding rule used. Ledgers are per-currency: a transaction balances in each currency separately, and an FX conversion is a pair of transactions through a conversion account.
  • Rounding is a policy, not a detail. A 2.9% fee on $4.99 is a fraction of a cent. Decide where rounding happens (once, at the end, on the fee), which rule (half-up, half-even), and write it down per use. Where a total must be split (a $100.00 order across three sellers, a fee split 70/20/10), use largest-remainder allocation so the shares are integers that add up to the total: the napkin script shows $100.00 three ways as 3,334 + 3,333 + 3,333.
  • Percentages and tax are computed with integer arithmetic and an explicit rounding mode, tested with the edge values (half cents, large amounts that would overflow 32 bits: 64-bit integers or arbitrary precision for ledger sums).
  • Decimal types are not a free pass. Using a decimal type in the database is fine for storage, but the application still needs the rounding policy and the currency exponent. Integer minor units make the policy explicit.

Deep dive 4: card data, fraud and webhooks

PCI scope and tokenization

The PCI Data Security Standard governs anyone who stores, processes or transmits cardholder data, and the cost of compliance scales with how many of your systems touch the card number. The design goal is to touch it with none. The browser or mobile SDK collects the card details in a form hosted by the PSP (an embedded frame or SDK fields) and sends them straight to the PSP, which returns a token (pm_tok_...) that stands for the card in the PSP's vault. Our servers, logs and databases only ever see the token and non-sensitive attributes (brand, last four digits, expiry). A token is useless without the PSP account that minted it, so a leak of our database does not leak cards. This typically shrinks our compliance scope to the lightest self-assessment level (SAQ A in PCI terms, though which one applies depends on exactly how the PSP fields are embedded; confirm with your PSP and assessor), and the diagram's dashed line from the browser to the PSP is the whole trick. The broader treatment of data classification, tokenization and encryption is in data protection. Related details: never log request bodies near payment endpoints, scrub error messages, and keep the PSP's API credentials as rotated secrets (keys and secrets).

Fraud checks, at concept level

Before and during authorization, a risk engine scores each attempt from signals: velocity (many attempts per card, device or account), device and network reputation, mismatches between billing country, IP and shipping, history of this customer, and the issuer's own checks (card verification and address checks, whose results come back with the authorization). The score maps to an action: allow, challenge (extra authentication such as 3-D Secure, which also shifts dispute liability in many cases), hold for review (authorize but delay capture), or block. Design points: the check runs inline within a tight latency budget (tens of milliseconds) so it must be fast and degrade safely (if the risk service is down, fail to a conservative rule set, not to 'allow all' and not to 'block all'); every decision is logged with its inputs for audit and model training; and the feedback loop matters most, because chargebacks and confirmed fraud are the labels. False positives cost sales and false negatives cost chargebacks and fees, so thresholds are a business decision with a dial, per merchant segment.

Webhooks, both directions

Webhooks are how the asynchronous world tells you what happened. Inbound from the PSP: verify the signature (an HMAC over the body and a timestamp with a shared secret, compared in constant time, with a tolerance window against replays: applied cryptography); persist the raw event keyed by the PSP's event id; return 2xx quickly and process asynchronously; dedupe; and do not trust ordering. A captured event may arrive before authorized, or twice. The robust handler treats the event as a notification and fetches the current object from the PSP API before applying the state transition, so a stale or reordered event cannot move a payment backwards. Outbound to merchants: the outbox feeds a delivery service that signs each request, retries on non-2xx with exponential backoff and jitter for days, bounds concurrency per endpoint, and disables a persistently failing endpoint with a notice. Provide event ids for dedupe, a way to replay events from a dashboard, and the same state through the API, because merchants' endpoints will be down at the worst moment and polling must always work as a fallback.

Trade-offs & alternatives

  • One PSP versus several. Several give resilience, negotiating leverage and local payment methods, at the cost of an adapter per provider, differing semantics for idempotency and lookups, and reconciliation per provider. Start with one behind a clean adapter interface.
  • Authorize then capture versus a single step. Separate steps suit goods that ship later and any flow with a risk review, and cost an expiry to manage. Immediate capture is simpler for digital goods.
  • Synchronous versus asynchronous API. A synchronous 201 is a nicer experience; the honest 202 is safer when the PSP is slow. Offer both with a clear contract.
  • Strong consistency in one shard versus distributed transactions. Keep a payment and its ledger entries on one shard so that the transaction is local; use sagas for flows across payments (sagas). A cross-shard two-phase commit is possible but is the costlier choice (atomic commit).
  • Build versus buy. Buying the PSP is usually right; building the ledger is usually right too, because it is your business's record. The costly middle is rebuilding what the PSP does well.
  • If we were the PSP, the problem becomes network connectivity, HSM-protected key management, card vaults, 24x7 settlement operations and certification: an order of magnitude larger, and a different chapter.

What a strong answer adds

  • Names the actors correctly (merchant, PSP, acquirer, network, issuer) and separates authorization, capture, clearing and settlement, and refund and chargeback as distinct events with different effects.
  • Puts the payment in an explicit state machine with unknown as a first-class state and compare-and-set transitions.
  • Applies idempotency at every boundary, not only the API: client, PSP, webhooks, ledger, outbox.
  • Says exactly-once is at-least-once plus idempotent effects, and persists intent before calling the PSP, never holding a transaction across the call.
  • Resolves timeouts by querying the PSP with a stable key and knows the three answers; reconciles daily against settlement files, classifies breaks and fixes them with compensating entries.
  • Uses integer minor units with the currency exponent, an explicit rounding policy and largest-remainder allocation.
  • Keeps card numbers out of scope with PSP-hosted fields and tokens, and explains why that reduces risk and compliance cost.
  • Treats webhooks as hints: verify, dedupe, fetch current state, and retries with backoff for outbound.

Follow-up questions

A payment request times out. What exactly do you do?

Mark the payment unknown, not failed. Return 202 processing to the merchant. A resolver queries the PSP by our stable key: if approved or declined, finish the transition; if the PSP has no record, it is safe to retry with the same key. Escalate to a human if it stays unknown past a bound. Settlement-file reconciliation is the final backstop.

How do you stop a client double-clicking Pay from charging twice?

The client generates one idempotency key per purchase attempt and reuses it on every send. The server stores the key with a request fingerprint and returns the stored response for repeats; a concurrent duplicate gets 409 in progress. Below that, the PSP-side key (derived from the payment id) prevents a second charge even if our own dedupe failed.

Why not use a database transaction across your DB and the PSP?

The PSP is an external system that cannot join our transaction, and holding locks across a seconds-long network call would cripple throughput. Instead we persist intent, call, then record the outcome, with each step idempotent and the unknown state covering the gap. That is a saga with a resolver, not a two-phase commit.

How do you handle a refund of a payment that was partially captured and split across sellers?

A refund references the capture and may not exceed what has been captured less what has been refunded (enforced by a conditional update on refunded_minor). Allocation to sellers uses the same largest-remainder rule as the original split so pennies reconcile. The ledger posts compensating entries per seller; nothing is edited. The PSP refund call uses its own idempotency key derived from the refund id.

A webhook says 'captured' but we show 'authorizing'. What now?

Do not apply it blindly. Fetch the payment from the PSP, which is the source of truth for what the PSP did, then apply the transitions that bring us there in order (authorizing to authorized to captured), each idempotent. Log the anomaly: it usually means the resolver or an earlier webhook is late, which is worth measuring.

How would you add a second PSP for resilience without risking double charges?

Behind the adapter interface, route by merchant, region or cost. Fail over only when the first attempt is known not to have been processed: a definitive decline is not a failover case, and a timeout is resolved by querying the first PSP before trying the second. Give each PSP its own derived key, record which one holds the payment, and reconcile per PSP.

Try it yourself

In the flow script, make the PSP lose 30% of replies and see how many payments the resolver must settle and how quickly the pass count converges. Then break the design on purpose: generate the PSP key from a fresh random value on every attempt, and watch the number of distinct PSP charges exceed the number of orders. Finally, add a refund operation with a conditional update on refunded_minor and include it in the ledger check.

Distributed systems connection

This chapter is the exactly-once discussion made concrete: an unreliable remote system with its own state, retries, and a requirement of no duplicates. The unknown state is the practical face of the Two Generals problem, resolved by asking rather than guessing, and the reconciler is an anti-entropy process between two replicas of the truth that cannot be kept in lockstep.

Key takeaways

  • Payments are low-QPS and high-stakes. Design every external call for 'what if I never hear back': persist intent first, act, record the outcome, all idempotently.
  • Model the lifecycle as a state machine with compare-and-set transitions. Timeout is unknown, not failure; authorize, capture, void, refund and dispute are different events with different money effects.
  • Idempotency is needed at every boundary: client key, stable PSP key, webhook event ids, unique ledger postings and an outbox. Exactly-once money is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects.
  • Resolve unknown outcomes by querying the PSP with the stable key (approved, declined, or unknown-to-PSP means retry is safe). Reconcile daily against settlement files; fix breaks with compensating ledger entries, never edits.
  • Money is an integer in minor units with its currency. Decide rounding explicitly and split with largest-remainder allocation.
  • Never touch card numbers: PSP-hosted fields and tokens keep servers out of PCI scope.
  • Treat webhooks as hints: verify signatures, dedupe, fetch current state, process asynchronously; deliver your own with signatures, retries and replay.
