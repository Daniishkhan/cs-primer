Design Ticketing & Flash Sales

Seat holds with expiry, contention on hot inventory, queues and fairness. When 2 million people want 50,000 seats, the engineering is in the gate, the hold and the saga: a calm inventory, a seat that returns itself when time runs out, and a refund path for every step that can fail.

Why this matters

Ticketing is what happens when demand for a scarce, perishable resource is wildly larger than supply, and everyone shows up in the same ten seconds. Most systems are designed to spread load out; a flash sale concentrates it on a handful of rows, and then asks that nobody be sold the same thing twice and nobody be locked out by someone who walked away. It forces three skills into one design: holding a resource with an expiry, throttling people instead of requests, and compensating when payment fails after you have promised something.

The problem

"Design a ticketing system: users browse events, pick seats and buy tickets. A popular concert goes on sale at noon and 2 million people want 50,000 seats."

The first sentence is an e-commerce site. The second is the real problem: a 40-to-1 stampede on inventory that cannot be oversold. Everything unusual about this design flows from that ratio. The related problem of booking a room for a date range is a close cousin: see the hotel booking studio.

Decisions from the output.

  • 40 fans per seat is the design constraint. Unthrottled, 2M people in a minute means ~200,000 seat-map reads a second and on the order of 120 hold attempts per good seat. The stampede would hit the one row that matters hardest, so a database cannot be the thing that absorbs it.
  • The waiting room sets a rate far below capacity. Admitting 300 people a second with 15% reaching a hold gives ~45 holds a second against a shard that could do ~2,000. That is deliberate. The goal of the gate is not maximum throughput, it is a calm inventory: fans spend their five minutes shopping, seats sell out in about 23 minutes, and the last person admitted sees "sold out" quickly rather than hammering a dead page.
  • Only about a fifth of the crowd is ever admitted. The other 80% learn the event is sold out while still in the queue. That is the honest outcome of 40:1 demand; the engineering goal is to tell them quickly and cheaply, and to make the order they were served in defensible (fairness section).
  • The expiry rate is tiny. At worst 83 holds a second lapse; a reaper sweep every 5 seconds examines hundreds of rows via an index on expiry. The reaper is not a performance problem, but a 5-second delay frees seats late, which matters only in the last minutes of a sale.
  • GA partitions well. Spread over 200 sections, contention on a counter falls from ~45 a second to ~0.2 a second per section: per-section partitioning (below) removes the hot row entirely for GA.

API

POST /v1/events/{id}/queue                 {captcha_proof}      -> 202 {queue_token, position_hint}
GET  /v1/events/{id}/queue                 (queue_token)        -> {status: waiting|admitted|closed, retry_after_s, admission_token?}

GET  /v1/events/{id}/availability          (cached 1-2 s)       -> {sections: [{id, price_minor, available_approx}]}
POST /v1/events/{id}/holds                 (admission_token)    {section, seat_ids[] | quantity}  Idempotency-Key: ...
                                                                -> 201 {hold_id, seats, expires_at} | 409 {unavailable: [seat_ids]}
POST /v1/holds/{id}/checkout               {payment_method_token}  Idempotency-Key: ...  -> 202 {order_id, status: "payment_pending"}
GET  /v1/orders/{id}                       -> {status: payment_pending|confirmed|failed, tickets?}

Design points. Queue and admission are separate calls and tokens, so the heavy traffic (polling) never touches the booking API. Availability is approximate and cached; POST /holds is authoritative. Holds are idempotent via a key, so a retried tap returns the same hold. Quantity holds serve GA: no seat ids, just "three in section 112". Checkout is asynchronous because the payment provider can be slow: the response is payment_pending and the client polls or is notified. Idempotency on every mutating call follows API design and the pattern in the Stripe case.

Data model

  • Seats (assigned): seats(event_id, seat_id, section_id, state: free|held|sold, hold_id, hold_expires_at, order_id, version), primary key (event_id, seat_id), with an index on (event_id, hold_expires_at) where state='held' for the reaper. 50,000 rows for the biggest event: tiny.
  • Section counters (GA): sections(event_id, section_id, capacity, available, version). A hold decrements available with a guard; a lapse increments it. Holds for GA are rows of their own: holds(hold_id, event_id, section_id, qty, expires_at, state).
  • Holds and orders: holds as above (the unit of expiry and extension), orders(order_id, hold_id, user_id, total_minor, currency, payment_state), tickets(ticket_id, order_id, seat_id, barcode). Orders live in a general transactional store; they are not the bottleneck.
  • Placement: partition by event_id, and for the very biggest events by (event_id, section_id), so one event's stampede lands on its own shards and cannot hurt other events (sharding). Small events share shards freely.
  • Waiting-room state: a place per fan (token), the admit pointer, rate counters: a small in-memory store or even signed tokens with the state in the token, which scales with no store at all.

High-level design

The flow end to end:

Step through

One purchase through the on-sale

  1. Join the queue. At on-sale time the fan hits a static waiting-room page served from the edge. Joining runs a bot check and a per-identity rate limit, and returns a place: a signed token containing the event id and a random or arrival-based rank. No database sees this traffic.
  2. Wait. An admission controller releases fans at a rate chosen from the inventory service's measured capacity and error rate. The page polls its status endpoint with jitter, or listens on a lightweight stream; both are cheap and cacheable.
  3. Be admitted. When the fan's turn comes, they receive a short-lived signed admission token. The booking API rejects hold requests without one, so the waiting room cannot be bypassed by going straight to the API.
  4. Choose seats. The seat map shows availability by section from a cache that is a second or two stale; for general admission, only counts. It is advisory: the next step decides.
  5. Hold. One conditional write marks the seats held with a hold id and an expiry (say ten minutes), but only if each is free or its previous hold has lapsed. Either all requested seats are held or none are. This is the only step that creates exclusivity.
  6. Authorize payment. The checkout authorizes the card for the order total but does not capture. The hold is extended once while an authorisation is in flight, so a slow bank does not cost the fan their seat.
  7. Confirm. On success, one conditional write moves the seats from held to sold, checking the hold id and that it has not lapsed; then capture. If the confirm fails (the hold lapsed), the authorisation is voided: the compensation.
  8. Fail. If the payment is declined, the saga's compensation releases the hold immediately so other fans can buy the seats, rather than waiting for the expiry.
  9. Abandon. If the fan simply walks away, nothing happens until the expiry passes. Then the seats are free by definition (the hold-check in step 5 treats a lapsed hold as free) and the reaper tidies the rows and publishes the availability change.

The pieces to notice: the waiting room and the admission controller are separate from booking so that the gate's load never competes with inventory, and the controller reads the inventory's load as its control signal (back-pressure applied to people, overload). Exclusivity is decided in exactly one place, the conditional write in step 5, and nothing earlier is authoritative.

Deep dive 1: holds with expiry (a TTL and a reaper)

A hold is the promise "these seats are yours for ten minutes". The risk of a process-held lock is that the process dies; the solution is to make the hold data with an expiry, not a lock. Two rules make it robust:

  • The check is in the write. The hold statement is a single atomic conditional update: set held, hold_id, expires_at = now + 10 min where state = 'free' or (state = 'held' and hold_expires_at <= now). A lapsed hold is free by definition, even if no process has noticed. So correctness never depends on the reaper being alive or fast.
  • The reaper is an optimiser. It scans an index on expiry, flips lapsed rows to free, bumps availability counters and publishes a change so caches and the waiting room's controller learn about returned inventory. If it stalls, the only effect is that availability displays lag; no seat is stuck or double sold.

Use the database's clock (or a single timestamp source) for now, not each app server's, for the same reason the rate limiter used the store's clock: skewed clocks would let two servers disagree about whether a hold has lapsed. The confirm step uses the same conditional style: set sold, order_id where hold_id = X and state = 'held' and hold_expires_at > now. If it matches zero rows the hold lapsed (or was released) and the order cannot proceed. Extension while paying is a conditional update too (only if it has not lapsed, and only once).

This is the same idea as a lease (leases and fencing), and the same one used to reserve a driver in ride matching: exclusivity that expires by itself, with a token (here the hold id) that proves you are the current holder.

Deep dive 2: contention on a hot event

All 40 fans want the best 5,000 seats. Which concurrency-control technique do you use for the hold? The mechanisms are in isolation and MVCC and locking; here is how they fare on this workload.

ApproachHowUnder a stampedeVerdict
Pessimistic row lockSELECT ... FOR UPDATE the seat rows, check, update, commitCorrect. Every contender queues on the lock; a transaction that waits on a held lock for the duration of an app round trip stacks up waiters, exhausts connections and deadlocks when two fans lock overlapping multi-seat sets in different ordersFine only if the transaction is one short statement or you lock in a canonical order; never hold a lock across a user think-time
Optimistic (version) checkRead the row and its version, then UPDATE ... WHERE version = v; retry on failureCorrect, with no waiting, but under high contention most attempts lose and retry: wasted work that grows with contendersGood when contention is moderate; a retry storm when it is not
Single conditional UPDATEOne statement: UPDATE seats SET state='held' WHERE seat_id IN (...) AND (state='free' OR lapsed), check rows affected equals requested, else roll backCorrect and atomic; the row lock is held for microseconds inside one statement. Contenders fail fast with 409 and pick other seatsThe default for assigned seats
Per-section / per-event partitioning with a single writerRoute every hold for a section to one worker or shard that processes requests serially (an actor or a queue)No lock contention at all: the serial order is the lock. Throughput per section is capped by one thread (fast, as it is memory-resident), and sections scale out independentlyBest for GA counters and for the very hottest events; costs routing and failover logic

My recommendation, as an opinion: the single conditional UPDATE for assigned seats on a sharded relational store, with per-section serialisation added for events large enough to need it. Two refinements matter more than the choice. First, multi-seat holds must be all or nothing (a family of four wants four adjacent seats): do them in one statement or one transaction that touches rows in seat id order, which removes the deadlock cycle. Second, reduce contention at the source: let the server choose ("best available in section 112, 4 seats together") rather than the client picking from a stale map. The simulation below shows why.

The script runs a complete on-sale: fans are shuffled into a waiting room and admitted at a fixed rate, choose from a seat map cached for two seconds (so many pick the same attractive seat), hold with a TTL, pay (25% fail), and the reaper sweeps. It runs twice: with a read-then-write hold (the check and the write are separate steps, and another fan's write can land in between) and with a compare-and-set hold. An oversell is a seat paid for by more than one order.

"""Simulated on-sale: waiting room + seat holds with TTL + a reaper + payment failures. Simulated time, seeded.
Compares a read-then-write hold (racy under interleaving) with a compare-and-set hold. Counts OVERSELLS: a seat sold
to more than one order."""
import random, heapq
from collections import deque

class Inventory:
    """seat -> (state, order, version, expires). All methods atomic, like a single-row UPDATE ... WHERE state='free'."""
    def __init__(self, n): self.seat = {i: ["free", None, 0, 0.0] for i in range(n)}
    def hold_cas(self, i, order, now, ttl):
        s = self.seat[i]
        if s[0] == "free" or (s[0] == "held" and s[3] <= now):    # a lapsed hold counts as free even before the reaper runs
            s[:] = ["held", order, s[2] + 1, now + ttl]; return s[2]
    def sell(self, i, order, version, now):                       # only the current holder, unexpired, right version
        s = self.seat[i]
        if s[0] == "held" and s[1] == order and s[2] == version and s[3] > now: s[0] = "sold"; return True
        return False
    def reap(self, now):
        n = 0
        for s in self.seat.values():
            if s[0] == "held" and s[3] <= now: s[0], s[1] = "free", None; n += 1
        return n

def simulate(mode, seed=1, seats=500, fans=12000, admit_per_s=40, ttl=60, pay_fail=0.25):
    rnd, inv = random.Random(seed), Inventory(seats)
    queue = deque(range(fans)); rnd.shuffle(queue)     # lottery order: fair against arrival-time bots
    events, now = [], 0.0                                          # (time, kind, payload)
    sold_to = {}                                                   # seat -> list of orders that believe they bought it
    stats = dict(admitted=0, holds=0, lost_race=0, paid=0, pay_failed=0, expired=0, gave_up=0)
    next_reap = 5.0
    snap = [[]]; next_snap = 0.0
    def shop(order):
        # choose a seat, preferring low numbers (everybody wants the best seats)
        for attempt in range(12):
            if not snap[0]: break
            i = snap[0][min(int(rnd.expovariate(1 / max(1, len(snap[0]) / 1.5))), len(snap[0]) - 1)]   # best-ranked free seat in a slightly stale seat map
            if mode == "naive":
                if inv.seat[i][0] == "free":                       # read ...
                    heapq.heappush(events, (now + rnd.uniform(0.0, 0.05), "naive_write", (order, i)))   # ... write lands later
                    return
            else:
                v = inv.hold_cas(i, order, now, ttl)
                if v:
                    stats["holds"] += 1
                    heapq.heappush(events, (now + rnd.uniform(5, 90), "pay", (order, i, v))); return
                stats["lost_race"] += 1
        stats["gave_up"] += 1
    while (queue or events) and now < 3000:
        now += 0.05
        if now >= next_snap: snap[0] = [i for i, x in inv.seat.items() if x[0] == 'free']; next_snap += 2.0   # the seat map is cached for 2 s
        for _ in range(int(admit_per_s * 0.05 + rnd.random())):    # the gate admits at a fixed rate
            if queue: stats["admitted"] += 1; shop(queue.popleft())
        if now >= next_reap: stats["expired"] += inv.reap(now); next_reap += 5
        while events and events[0][0] <= now:
            _, kind, p = heapq.heappop(events)
            if kind == "naive_write":
                order, i = p; s = inv.seat[i]; s[0], s[1] = "held", order; stats["holds"] += 1     # overwrites a rival's hold
                heapq.heappush(events, (now + rnd.uniform(5, 90), "pay", (order, i, None)))
            else:
                order, i, v = p
                if rnd.random() < pay_fail: stats["pay_failed"] += 1; inv.seat[i][0] = "free" if inv.seat[i][1] == order else inv.seat[i][0]; continue
                ok = inv.sell(i, order, v, now) if mode == "cas" else True
                if ok:
                    if mode == "naive": inv.seat[i][0] = "sold"
                    sold_to.setdefault(i, []).append(order); stats["paid"] += 1
    oversold = sum(len(set(o)) - 1 for o in sold_to.values() if len(set(o)) > 1)
    sold = len(sold_to)
    return stats, oversold, sold, now

for mode in ("naive", "cas"):
    st, over, sold, t_end = simulate(mode)
    print(f"{mode:<7}{st['admitted']:>9}{st['holds']:>7}{st['lost_race']:>10}{st['pay_failed']:>9}{st['gave_up']:>8}{st['expired']:>8}{sold:>11}{st['paid']:>12}{over:>10}{t_end:>10.0f} s")

naive      12000   3179         0      783    8821    2639        500        2396      1896       342 s

The read-then-write version sells the 500 seats to 2,396 paying orders: 1,896 oversells. The compare-and-set version sold 490 seats to exactly 490 orders and oversold nothing. The 80,000 'lost race' lines are the price: with a stale seat map, fans keep trying seats that have just gone. That is wasted work, not wrong answers, and it is exactly what server-side best-available assignment and per-section serialisation reduce. The 10 unsold seats at the end are the toy's fans running out of retries, not an inventory leak. (Two things the toy does not model: real payment latency and a shared database; the principle, that the guard must be inside the atomic write, carries over unchanged.)

Deep dive 3: the virtual waiting room

The waiting room turns a stampede of requests into a queue of people. It exists because the inventory cannot be made fast enough, and because rejecting 80% of an angry crowd with errors is a worse product than telling them where they are in line.

  • Edge-served. The waiting page is static and cached at the CDN. The only dynamic calls are join and status. If 2M fans poll every 10 seconds, that is 200,000 requests a second of tiny, cacheable responses: trivial for an edge, ruinous for a database. Add jitter to the polling interval and return a retry_after so the crowd does not synchronise (overload and retry storms).
  • Tokens, not sessions. A place in line is a signed token (event id, rank, issue time, an HMAC). The status endpoint verifies the signature and compares the rank with the admit pointer: no per-fan row in a database. The admission token is another short-lived signed token that booking verifies (sessions and tokens).
  • The admit rate is a control loop. Start from the napkin number and adjust from signals: hold latency p99, conditional-update conflict rate, DB CPU. Raise the rate additively while healthy; cut it multiplicatively when the signal degrades (AIMD, as in TCP congestion control: TCP). Reserve a small share for people mid-checkout so admitting new fans never starves those who already hold seats.
  • Ordering policy. Strict arrival order rewards whoever has the fastest connection and the most scripts. For an on-sale, many systems instead open the queue some minutes before the sale and then randomise everyone who arrives in that window, so being early by seconds confers nothing; after that window, arrival order applies. The simulation used the randomised version. Whichever you pick, publish the rule.
  • Close it cleanly. When inventory is exhausted, flip the queue to closed and let every waiting client see it on its next poll: a cheap, global, truthful message. Stop admitting before the last seats are gone, accounting for those in flight.
  • The waiting room is itself a system to protect. Rate-limit join, bound token lifetimes, and make sure a failure in the waiting room fails closed (nobody admitted) rather than open (everybody admitted). It is the only thing between the crowd and the inventory.

Deep dive 4: fairness, bots and the money path

Fairness and bots

Scalpers use automation to buy faster and in volume. No design stops this entirely; the aim is to make it expensive and to keep an honest fan's chance reasonable. The toolbox, with the threat analysis in abuse and denial of service:

Say plainly that this is an arms race; a defence in depth, with the strongest control being identity (a real, verified person) rather than a clever puzzle. Fairness also has a systems meaning: a fan who is mid-checkout must not lose their place because a bigger fan joined; extension while paying protects them.

Payment holds and compensation

The purchase spans two systems that do not share a transaction: our seat store and the payment provider. The order of operations is a design decision. Hold seats first (cheap, reversible, expires on its own). Then authorize the card, which reserves funds without moving them (payments). Then confirm the seats as sold, and only then capture. Each step has a defined undo, which is the shape of a saga (sagas):

StepIf it failsCompensation
1. Hold seatsNothing to undoNone; return 409 with what is unavailable
2. Authorize cardDeclined, timeout, 3-D Secure failureRelease the hold now (do not make others wait for the TTL)
3. Confirm seats (held to sold)Hold had lapsed during a slow authorisationVoid the authorisation; tell the fan honestly; the extension-while-paying rule makes this rare
4. CaptureProvider error or unknown outcomeRetry idempotently; reconcile with the provider; never issue tickets before capture succeeds or is certain
5. Issue ticketsCrash after captureOutbox drives ticket creation; idempotent, retried until done

The pivot is step 3: after seats are confirmed sold, we must move forward (capture, issue), not backward. That is why confirming seats comes before capture but after a successful authorization; the only way to reach 'sold' without money is an authorization we hold and can capture. The unknown-outcome problem (a timeout on capture) is the central topic of the next chapter. For now: use the order id as the idempotency key with the provider so a retry cannot double-charge.

Trade-offs & alternatives

  • Fairness versus throughput. A random lottery is fairest and slightly wasteful; FIFO is simpler to explain and easier to game.
  • Hold length. Long holds let careful buyers finish but strand inventory behind abandoners; short holds punish slow payers. Ten minutes is a product trade, tunable per event, and shorter during the final scramble.
  • Exactness versus speed on GA. Sub-counters scale; a single counter is exact. For the last tickets, switch to the exact path.
  • If there were no waiting room, you would need orders of magnitude more inventory capacity for a few minutes a year, which is the wrong thing to buy. The waiting room converts a capacity problem into a policy.
  • If sales were mostly low demand, skip all of it: one table and one conditional UPDATE is the whole design. Complexity should follow the 40:1 ratio.
  • If tickets were resalable, add a ledger of ownership transfers; the inventory model changes from seats to tokens with an owner.

What a strong answer adds

  • Opens with the demand-to-supply ratio and the number of admitted fans, not with a seat table.
  • Makes the hold a row with an expiry and puts the lapsed-check inside the atomic write, so correctness does not depend on the reaper.

Follow-up questions

What if two people click the same seat in the same millisecond?

They both send a conditional update. The database serialises the two statements on that row; the first sets it held, and the second finds it is no longer free and affects zero rows, so it gets a 409 and a fresh availability list. There is no window in which both succeed, because the check and the write are one atomic statement.

What if the user pays after their hold expired?

The confirm step is conditional on the hold id and an unexpired time, so it fails if someone else now holds the seat. We then void the authorisation and tell the fan. To make it rare, extend the hold once when an authorisation is in flight, so only genuinely slow payments are affected. Never capture first and hope.

How would you scale a single huge GA section?

Split the counter into sub-counters, each holding a slice of capacity, and decrement one chosen at random; if empty, try the others. Throughput scales with the number of stripes. The last few tickets are a rebalancing problem, so near sell-out switch to a single exact counter, and accept that the final stretch is slower.

How do you make the queue fair for people on slow connections?

Open the queue before the sale and randomise everyone who arrives in the window, so connection speed within the window does not matter. After the window, order by arrival. Publish the rule. Then protect people mid-checkout with a bounded hold extension.

How do you keep the seat map right-ish without hammering the database?

Serve availability by section from a cache refreshed every second or two, populated by the reaper's and holds' change events, not by the clients' reads. Treat it as advisory: the hold write is the truth, and a 409 returns current availability so the client recovers in one round trip.

What changes if the event is a whole season with thousands of dates?

Per-event partitioning already isolates dates. Most events are small; use the simple path for them and enable waiting room and per-section serialisation only for events flagged hot (by expected demand or by a live signal). Avoid a design where every event pays for the stampede machinery.

Try it yourself

In the sale script, set pay_fail to 0.5 and watch expired holds rise but oversells stay at zero. Then reduce the hold TTL to 20 seconds with payment taking up to 90, and see how many paying fans lose their seat: that is why you extend holds while a payment is in flight. Distributed systems connection

The oversell in the naive run is a lost update, the same anomaly dissected in the isolation chapter, and the cure is the same: make the check and the write one atomic step. Holds with expiry are leases (leases and fencing), the waiting room is admission control with a feedback loop, and the purchase is a saga whose pivot is the moment the seat is confirmed.

Key takeaways

  • The defining number is demand per seat (40 here). It makes the inventory shard a protected resource and the waiting room a policy engine rather than an optional extra.
  • A hold is a row with an expiry. Put the lapsed-hold check inside the atomic conditional write, so correctness never depends on the reaper; the reaper only tidies and publishes.
  • For assigned seats use one conditional UPDATE (atomic, microsecond lock); lock multi-seat sets in id order; shard by section for the hottest events; let the server choose best-available.
  • The waiting room is edge-served, token-based, jittered, controlled by the inventory's health (AIMD), and fails closed. Publish the ordering rule.
  • Bots are an arms race: identity, per-identity limits and a randomised entry window beat clever puzzles.
  • The purchase is a saga: hold, authorize, confirm, capture, each with a compensation; extend the hold while payment is in flight; use idempotency keys with the provider.
  • GA is a counter, not a set of rows; stripe it for throughput and use an exact path for the last tickets.

Check your understanding

Why is the 'hold lapsed' check placed inside the atomic hold statement rather than only in a background reaper?

  1. It is faster
  2. The reaper cannot read the database
  3. So correctness never depends on the reaper running; a lapsed hold is free by definition
  4. To avoid using a clock
If the reaper stalls, seats would otherwise be stuck. With the check in the write, the reaper is only an optimiser.

In the on-sale simulation the read-then-write hold oversold heavily and the compare-and-set hold oversold nothing. What is the lesson?

  1. The guard must be part of the same atomic write as the update
  2. Compare-and-set is slower
  3. Fans should be admitted faster
  4. Use more seats
A separate read and write leaves a window in which another fan's write lands between them (a lost update).

Why admit fans through a waiting room at a rate well below the inventory's raw capacity?

  1. To make fans wait for fun
  2. Because the CDN is slow
  3. To reduce the seat count
  4. To keep the inventory calm and honest, so the sale completes fast and the rest are told quickly rather than hammering it
The aim is a stable, correct sale; demand is 40x supply so most people cannot be served anyway, and a controlled queue is a kinder, cheaper outcome than errors.

A fan's card authorisation succeeds after their hold expired and another fan now holds the seat. What happens?

  1. Both get the seat
  2. The confirm (conditional on hold id and expiry) fails; the authorisation is voided and the fan is told
  3. The second fan is removed
  4. The payment is captured anyway
The compensation for a failed confirm is to void the authorisation; extending the hold while paying makes this rare.

Why must multi-seat holds touch rows in a canonical order?

  1. It improves cache hit rate
  2. It sorts the seat map
  3. Two fans locking overlapping sets in different orders can deadlock; a global order breaks the cycle
  4. It reduces payment fees
Deadlock needs a cycle of waits; sorting by seat id makes every transaction acquire in the same order.
