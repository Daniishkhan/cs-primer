Start Here

You have spent seven courses learning how the parts work. This capstone teaches the other half of the craft: deciding which parts to use, in what shape, for what reason, and saying all of that out loud to another engineer. Design problems have no single right answer, so we practise something different from coding: reasoning you can defend.

Why this matters

Almost every senior engineering decision is a design decision made under ambiguity: a half-defined requirement, a deadline, numbers you have to guess. Doing it well is a learnable skill with a method, and the same skill is what interviewers probe in a system-design round. This course teaches the method once (chapters 1 to 4), then applies it fifteen times to different problems and eight more times in timed studios, until the rhythm is automatic.

How a design problem differs from a coding problem

A coding problem has a specification, a checker and (usually) a right answer. A design problem arrives as a sentence like "design a news feed" or "design a ticketing system". The sentence hides dozens of decisions: how many users, how fresh, how correct, how much money, how many engineers. Nobody can mark your answer correct or incorrect, because there is no fixed answer to compare against. What a reader of your design can judge is your reasoning.

Coding problemDesign problem
The promptPrecise, with examples and constraintsOne vague sentence; finding the real problem is part of the task
The answerOne output is right; others are wrongMany designs are defensible; some are clearly poor for the stated needs
What is judgedCorrectness, then efficiencyWhether each choice follows from a requirement or a number, and whether you saw the risks
Wrong turnsA failing testA design that cannot meet its own numbers, or that you cannot justify
SkillsAlgorithms, data structures, careful codeAsking questions, estimating, choosing building blocks, anticipating failure, communicating
You are done whenTests passThe trade-offs are named and the stakeholder agrees (or the clock runs out)

Two consequences shape everything that follows. First, a design is derived, not asserted. "We will use Kafka" is an assertion. "Writes peak near 90,000 per second, which one database primary cannot take, and the consumers must be able to replay after a bug, so we put a partitioned log in front" is a derivation. If you cannot point to the requirement or number that forced a choice, you do not yet have a reason. Second, there is no free lunch: every choice costs something, and a strong answer says what.

Cargo-cult answers

The most common failure is naming popular technologies in a plausible order ("load balancer, cache, queue, sharded database") without saying which problem each solves at this scale. It can sound fluent and still show no judgment. We will keep asking one question of every box you draw: what number or requirement made this necessary?

How this course is organised

The method (chapters 1 to 4)

A framework with timings, estimation with runnable examples, a catalogue of building blocks, and communication. Read these first, in order.

Worked designs (chapters 5 to 19)

Fifteen systems in five groups, from a rate limiter to a payment system. Each follows the same twelve-section rhythm so you can compare them.

Labs, cases and studios

Labs build the key mechanisms in Python. Case studies tell what real systems did, with sources. The eight studios are practice sessions you do yourself.

The rhythm of a worked design

Every design from chapter 5 on has the same sections, in the same order, so your own answers settle into the same shape: the problem, clarify requirements, estimate, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, failure modes and operations, trade-offs and alternatives, what a strong answer adds, follow-up questions, and finally a quiz and key takeaways. Notice that the first seven mirror the eight steps of the framework. Notice also that nothing in the estimate section is decorative: each number ends up justifying a later decision.

How a studio works

A studio is a practice session you run before reading the answer. You get a prompt, a place to type your own notes (saved only in your browser), and the problem broken into stages: requirements, numbers, API, data, architecture, failures. For each stage you attempt it yourself, then open a "reveal" to compare against a model answer. There is a self-check rubric at the end. The worked designs teach you how to think; the studios check whether you can do it unassisted. Do not skip the attempt. Reading a good answer feels like understanding, and the gap between those two is exactly what the studios measure.

A note on company names and numbers

Throughout, we avoid claims about how specific companies are built unless a case study with sources backs them up. When we say "large social networks typically do X", read it as a common pattern, not a statement about any one firm. Numbers such as "one database primary handles roughly a few thousand writes per second" are orders of magnitude, labelled as such. Your job in a design session is to be right to within a factor of ten, quickly, and to say your assumptions.

How to practise

Reading about design is like reading about swimming. The skill is in the doing, and several parts of it (organising your thoughts, estimating under pressure, noticing you have talked for four minutes without a diagram) only show up when you perform.

  1. Practise out loud. Talk to an empty room, a rubber duck or a recording. Silence in your head feels like thinking; in a real session it feels like stalling. Narrating makes your reasoning visible, to others and to you.
  2. Practise against the clock. Use the timer in chapter 1. A 45-minute session forces trade-offs about what to skip. If you always finish early, you are not going deep enough.
  3. Practise with a friend. One plays the interviewer or product manager, who answers questions and pushes back, and also takes notes on what was unclear. Swap roles: playing the interviewer teaches you what a good answer sounds like faster than anything else.
  4. Draw it. A whiteboard, paper or a free tool such as Excalidraw. Practise diagrams until drawing a box does not interrupt your sentence.
  5. Redo, don't just review. After a session, write down the two weakest moments and redo only those. A fifteen-minute redo beats a second full run.

Try it yourself: a five-minute baseline

Before reading further, set a timer for five minutes and answer aloud: "Design a service that shortens URLs." Record yourself. Do not look anything up. Then listen back and note (1) which questions you asked before drawing anything, (2) whether you gave a single number, (3) how many boxes you could justify by a requirement. Keep the note and repeat the exercise after chapter 4. The difference is the point of the course.

Distributed systems connection

Design questions are where the distributed-systems ideas stop being theorems and start being choices. Whether to accept stale reads, how to make a retry safe, which node is allowed to decide: these are trade-offs about latency, availability and cost, and you now know the vocabulary to state them. The ones you will reach for most are in replication, consistency models and logs and exactly-once.

Key takeaways

  • Design problems are ambiguous by design and are judged on reasoning, not on matching a single answer.
  • Derive every choice from a requirement or a number. If you cannot say why a box is there, take it out.
  • Every choice has a cost; a strong answer names it and says what would change the decision.
  • Courses 0 to 6 are the library. This course links back to them rather than re-teaching, so follow the links when something is rusty.
  • Chapters 1 to 4 teach the method; chapters 5 to 19 apply it with a fixed rhythm; the studios test you without a safety net.
  • Practise out loud, against a clock, with another person, and redo the weak moments.

Check your understanding

What is the main thing an evaluator can judge in a design answer?

  1. Whether it matches the one correct architecture for the problem
  2. How many technologies you mentioned
  3. Whether each choice follows from a requirement or a number, and the risks were seen
  4. How quickly you finished drawing
There is no single correct architecture, so judgment rests on the reasoning behind the choices. Naming many technologies without a reason is the cargo-cult pattern, and speed of drawing matters only if it leaves time for the reasoning.

Which statement is an example of a derived design choice rather than an asserted one?

  1. "Peak writes are about 90k/s, beyond one primary, so we partition by user ID"
  2. "We will use a microservice architecture because it scales"
  3. "We need a cache because every large system has one"
  4. "We will use the database most teams use"
The first ties a decision (partitioning) to a number (write rate) and a limit (one primary). The others cite popularity or a vague virtue instead of a requirement.

You get stuck on a detail in a worked design that relies on an earlier idea, such as quorum reads. What is the intended way to proceed?

  1. Skip it; it is not important to the design
  2. Wait for the course to re-explain it in full
  3. Look it up on the web
  4. Follow the link to the chapter that taught it, then return and see why this design needs it
The designs link to the earlier courses on purpose, to spend their space on how the pieces combine. The earlier chapters are the reference library.

Why does a studio ask you to attempt each stage before revealing the model answer?

  1. To save the model answer for later
  2. Because reading a good answer feels like understanding, but only an attempt shows whether you can produce one unassisted
  3. Because the model answers are optional
  4. To make the studio take longer
Recognition is easier than recall. The attempt reveals gaps that reading would hide, which is the whole point of practice.
