Lab 6: A Durable Job Scheduler

"Run this later" sounds like a timer. At scale it is a durable store, a due-time index, a lease protocol, a retry policy and an idempotent effect, and the interesting bugs live where those meet. You build each piece on a simulated disk and simulated time, then break the whole thing with hundreds of seeded chaos runs: workers that die, workers that freeze past their lease and carry on, a store that loses power mid-write, and three cron replicas that disagree about who is the leader. Each job's effect still happens exactly once, and each cron fires once per period.

Why this lab matters

Every product grows a scheduler, and every scheduler is first written as a timer and an in-memory list. It then loses jobs on a restart, runs a job twice when a worker pauses, retries in lockstep against a struggling dependency, and fires the nightly report three times when someone deploys three replicas. Chapter 17 designed the fixes; this lab makes you build each one and then try to break it with a few hundred seeded runs in which workers die, freeze past their leases and carry on, and the store itself loses power in the middle of a write. The point to leave with: durability, liveness and safety are three different mechanisms: a log, a lease, and a fence plus an idempotent effect.

Simulated time, simulated disk, simulated chaos

Nothing sleeps and nothing touches a file. Disk is a Python object with an OS cache in front of a durable list: fsync moves lines across, and crash(rng) throws the cache away, sometimes leaving a torn half-line. disk.fail_at = n makes the n-th disk operation raise Crash mid-write. Time is a number passed as now. The chaos harness run_chaos(seed, handler) is given: it runs workers that die before and after the effect, freeze past their lease, and meet a store and a bank that lose power, until a given time, then lets the system heal. A failing property prints its seed: python3 check.py solution 4 17 replays it.

What you'll build

Five milestones:

  1. A durable job store: a checksummed write-ahead log under a dict that survives simulated crashes, a heap-backed due-time index rebuilt on recovery, and idempotent submit.
  2. Leases and fencing: claim a job for a lease on simulated time; an expired lease can be re-claimed; every claim bumps a fencing token and a stale worker's heartbeat or completion is refused (leases and fencing tokens).
  3. Retries: exponential backoff with full jitter, a maximum number of attempts, a dead-letter queue with redrive, and poison jobs that kill every worker that touches them.
  4. Exactly-once effects: an effect ledger keyed by the job, not the attempt, so that over 300 seeded chaos runs, with duplicate executions everywhere, each job's effect happens once.
  5. Cron: one trigger per period with three scheduler replicas, using a leader lease for efficiency and unique firing ids plus fencing for safety; and jitter that spreads the top-of-the-hour herd.

Milestone 1: a durable store and a due-time index

An accepted job must survive the loss of any machine (chapter 17's durability requirement), so the job table is the truth and everything else, the due index and any ready queue, is a cache rebuilt from it. The simplest honest model of a durable table is a write-ahead log: every change is one record appended and fsynced before it is applied to the in-memory dict, so that whatever a caller was told succeeded survives the crash (crash recovery, durability and fsync). Three details are the whole milestone:

  • Atomic multi-key records. put_many writes one line, so a crash leaves either all of it or none. Later milestones depend on this (a job and its cron cursor; an effect and its ledger entry).
  • Torn writes. A crash can leave half a line. Each line carries a CRC32 of its body, and recovery stops at the first line that does not verify.
  • Truncate on recovery. If you stop replay at the bad line but leave it on disk, the next append lands after the garbage and is lost on the following restart. Recovery must cut the log at the last good record.

The due-time index answers "which job is claimable next?". Chapter 17's estimate says the pending set is hundreds of millions of rows but the next minute is a few hundred thousand, so only the near future needs an in-memory structure; a binary heap of (ready_at, job_id) is enough. Jobs get rescheduled (retries, heartbeats extend leases), so you cannot delete from the middle of a heap: leave the old entry and treat an entry as stale unless at[job_id] still equals its time (lazy invalidation). A queued job is ready at its due time; a running job is ready when its lease expires, so one index serves both new work and recovery.

def encode_record(rec):
    body = json.dumps(rec, sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":"))
    return f"{zlib.crc32(body.encode()):08x} {body}"
def decode_record(line):
    if len(line) < 10 or line[8] != " ":
        return None
    crc, body = line[:8], line[9:]
    try:
        if f"{zlib.crc32(body.encode()):08x}" != crc:
            return None
        return json.loads(body)
    except ValueError:
        return None
class LogDict:

    def __init__(self, disk):
        self.disk = disk
        self.data = {}
        self._recover()

    def _recover(self):
        """Replay the durable lines in order. At the first damaged line stop and truncate the disk there
        (self.disk.truncate(n)), so that later appends do not follow garbage."""
        good = 0
        for line in self.disk.read_all():
            rec = decode_record(line)
            if rec is None:
                break
            self._apply(rec)
            good += 1
        self.disk.truncate(good)

    def _apply(self, rec):
        for k, v in rec.get("set", {}).items():
            self.data[k] = v
        for k in rec.get("del", []):
            self.data.pop(k, None)

    def put_many(self, sets=None, deletes=()):
        """Atomically set and delete several keys: ONE record, then fsync, then apply to memory."""
        rec = {"set": dict(sets or {}), "del": list(deletes)}
        self.disk.append(encode_record(rec))
        self.disk.fsync()
        self._apply(json.loads(json.dumps(rec)))
class DueIndex:

    def __init__(self):
        self.heap = []
        self.at = {}

    def add(self, job_id, t):
        self.at[job_id] = t
        heapq.heappush(self.heap, (t, job_id))
        if len(self.heap) > 4 * len(self.at) + 64:                 # drop stale entries now and then
            self.heap = [(tt, j) for j, tt in self.at.items()]
            heapq.heapify(self.heap)

    def discard(self, job_id):
        self.at.pop(job_id, None)

    def pop_ready(self, now):
        while self.heap:
            t, j = self.heap[0]
            if self.at.get(j) != t:
                heapq.heappop(self.heap)
                continue
            if t > now:
                return None
            heapq.heappop(self.heap)
            del self.at[j]
            return j
        return None

    def ready(self, now):
        """All job ids with ready_at <= now, in (ready_at, id) order, without removing them."""
        return [j for t, j in sorted((t, j) for j, t in self.at.items() if t <= now)]
def submit(self, job_id, due, payload=None, max_attempts=5, base=1.0, cap=60.0, key=None):
    if self.db.get("job:" + job_id) is not None:
        return False
    self._save({"id": job_id, "key": key or job_id, "payload": payload, "due": due, "state": "queued",
                "attempts": 0, "fence": 0, "lease_until": 0.0, "owner": None,
                "max_attempts": max_attempts, "base": base, "cap": cap, "error": None})
    return True

Milestone 2: claiming work with leases and fencing tokens

A worker cannot be told apart from a slow worker, so the scheduler uses time: claim says "this job is yours until now + lease unless you renew", and once the lease lapses anyone may claim it again. That is liveness. Safety needs more, because the original worker may wake up after its pause still believing it owns the job. Every claim therefore bumps a fencing token stored with the job, every later call (heartbeat, complete, fail) must present the token, and the store refuses an old one. The sequence is the one in chapter 17:

Each claim is a single durable write, so a crash cannot leave a half-claimed job. The lease is judged by the store's clock (the now you are given), never the worker's. A heartbeat extends the lease only for the current fence; a completion for an old fence changes nothing. The check drives 60 random interleavings of four workers with random pauses and restarts against a shadow model, and demands: claims come in (ready time, id) order, never early and never while a lease is live, fences rise by exactly one per claim even across restarts, and each job completes exactly once.

def claim(self, now, worker, lease_s):
    while True:
        jid = self.index.pop_ready(now)
        if jid is None:
            return None
        rec = self.get(jid)
        if rec is None or ready_at(rec) is None or ready_at(rec) > now:
            continue
        if rec["state"] == "running" and rec["attempts"] >= rec["max_attempts"]:
            # a poison job: every worker that took it died. Stop feeding it to workers.
            rec.update(state="dead", owner=None, error="lease expired after max_attempts claims")
            self._save(rec)
            continue
        rec.update(state="running", owner=worker, lease_until=now + lease_s,
                   fence=rec["fence"] + 1, attempts=rec["attempts"] + 1)
        self._save(rec)
        return dict(rec)
def heartbeat(self, job_id, fence, now, lease_s):
    rec = self.get(job_id)
    if rec is None or rec["state"] != "running" or rec["fence"] != fence:
        return False
    rec["lease_until"] = now + lease_s
    self._save(rec)
    return True
def complete(self, job_id, fence):
    rec = self.get(job_id)
    if rec is None or rec["state"] != "running" or rec["fence"] != fence:
        return False
    rec.update(state="done", owner=None)
    self._save(rec)
    return True

Milestone 3: retries, backoff, jitter and the dead-letter queue

A failed job should usually be retried, and retries are also how a small outage becomes a large one, because every failed job comes back at once. Chapter 17's rules, in code: wait min(cap, base × 2attempt−1) at most, but pick uniformly at random in [0, that] (full jitter) so the retries of one outage spread out; give up after max_attempts and park the job in a dead-letter state a human can inspect and redrive; skip retries entirely for permanent errors.

Two subtleties. The random draw must be reproducible after a restart, so the store derives it from (seed, job id, attempt) (JobStore.jitter, given), not from global state. And poison jobs, which crash the worker that runs them, never report a failure: the lease just expires, over and over. Because attempts rises on every claim, not every reported failure, the claim you wrote in milestone 2 can notice a running job with an expired lease that has already been handed out max_attempts times and send it to the dead-letter queue instead of feeding it to another worker.

def backoff_delay(attempt, base, cap, u):
    return u * min(cap, base * 2.0 ** (attempt - 1))
def fail(self, job_id, fence, now, error, retryable=True):
    rec = self.get(job_id)
    if rec is None or rec["state"] != "running" or rec["fence"] != fence:
        return "fenced"
    rec["error"] = error
    rec["owner"] = None
    if not retryable or rec["attempts"] >= rec["max_attempts"]:
        rec["state"] = "dead"
        self._save(rec)
        return "dead"
    rec["state"] = "queued"
    rec["due"] = now + backoff_delay(rec["attempts"], rec["base"], rec["cap"], self.jitter(job_id, rec["attempts"]))
    self._save(rec)
    return "retry"
def dead_letters(self):
    return sorted(k[4:] for k, r in self.db.items() if k.startswith("job:") and r["state"] == "dead")
def redrive(self, job_id, now):
    rec = self.get(job_id)
    if rec is None or rec["state"] != "dead":
        return False
    rec.update(state="queued", attempts=0, error=None, due=now, owner=None)
    self._save(rec)
    return True

Milestone 4: idempotent handlers and an effect ledger

Between "the handler did its effect" and "the worker told the store" there is always a window, and a crash in it means the job runs again. Fencing cannot help: it stops the stale worker from marking the job done, not from having already paid the customer. So the contract (chapter 17, deep dive 2) is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects. Here the effect is a deposit into a bank, and the idempotency mechanism is a ledger that lives in the same atomic store as the balance, so one record contains both the new balance and the proof that this job's deposit happened.

The key is where it goes wrong. It must be the same on every attempt of the same job, which means the job's stable run key, never an attempt number or a worker name. The given handle_deposit_attempt_key is the classic bug: it has a ledger and still double-pays on every retry because each retry looks like a new job. The given handle_deposit_unsafe has no ledger at all. The check runs all three through the same 100 chaos runs and demands that your handler survives and that both of the others do not: it prints how many runs they corrupt, so you can see the test has teeth. For payments the same idea is the idempotency key of the Stripe case.

class EffectLedger:

    def __init__(self, db):
        self.db = db

    def done(self, key):
        return ("ledger:" + key) in self.db

    def apply_once(self, key, writes):
        """If `key` is not in the ledger: in ONE put_many write `writes` (a dict of key -> value) plus the ledger
        entry 'ledger:<key>', and return True. If it is already there: change nothing and return False."""
        if self.done(key):
            return False
        sets = dict(writes)
        sets["ledger:" + key] = 1
        self.db.put_many(sets)
        return True
def deposit(self, key, acct, amount):
    return self.ledger.apply_once(key, {"bal:" + acct: self.balance(acct) + amount})
def handle_deposit(job, bank):
    p = job["payload"]
    return bank.deposit(job["key"], p["acct"], p["amount"])

Milestone 5: cron, a leader lease and the herd

A recurring schedule must fire once per period. Two failures break that: nobody fires it (the single scheduler is down) and two fire it (two replicas both believe they lead). Chapter 17's answer uses two mechanisms with different jobs, and the milestone is to keep them separate:

  • A leader lease, for efficiency. Replicas call lease.acquire(name, now, ttl) on every tick; at most one holds it, and the holder gets a fencing token that rises with every change of leader. Only the leader fires, so three replicas do not do three times the work.
  • A unique key plus the token, for safety. The leader does not "launch" a run; it creates a job whose id is cron_id@fire_time, in the same atomic write that moves the cron's cursor and records its token. A second creation of the same firing returns duplicate; a token below the highest seen returns fenced. So even a leader that froze for ten seconds and wakes up with an old token and an old view of the cursor cannot double-fire or move time backwards.
  • Catch-up. The cursor is durable, so a new leader fires every period it missed (exactly one job each), not just the latest.

The other half is the herd. Two million crons that all say 0 * * * * start in the same second (chapter 17's estimate: 2,000,000 starts per second without jitter, 6,700 with five minutes of spread). The offset must be stable per cron, derived from a hash of its id, not re-drawn each period: a randomly shifted cron would run 23 or 37 minutes after its last run depending on luck, and the period is a promise.

def cron_jitter(cron_id, spread_s):
    if spread_s <= 0:
        return 0.0
    h = hashlib.blake2b(cron_id.encode(), digest_size=8).digest()
    return int.from_bytes(h, "big") / 2.0 ** 64 * spread_s
def fire_times(period, offset, after, upto):
    import math
    k = math.floor((after - offset) / period) + 1
    out = []
    while offset + k * period <= upto:
        t = offset + k * period
        if t > after:
            out.append(t)
        k += 1
    return out
def fire_cron(self, cron_id, fire_t, due, payload, token):
    if token < self.db.get("meta:cron_token", 0):
        return "fenced"
    jid = f"{cron_id}@{fire_t}"
    if self.db.get("job:" + jid) is not None:
        return "duplicate"
    rec = {"id": jid, "key": jid, "payload": payload, "due": due, "state": "queued", "attempts": 0, "fence": 0,
           "lease_until": 0.0, "owner": None, "max_attempts": 5, "base": 1.0, "cap": 60.0, "error": None}
    self.db.put_many({"job:" + jid: rec, "cursor:" + cron_id: fire_t, "meta:cron_token": token})
    self._reindex(rec)
    return "created"
def tick(self, now):
    token = self.lease.acquire(self.name, now, self.ttl)
    if token is None:
        return 0
    return self.fire_due(now, token)
def fire_due(self, now, token, stale_cursors=None):
    made = 0
    for c in self.crons:
        after = (stale_cursors or {}).get(c["id"], c["start"] - 1e-6) if stale_cursors is not None else self.store.cursor(c["id"], c["start"] - 1e-6)
        for t in fire_times(c["period"], c["offset"], after, now)[:self.max_catchup]:
            r = self.store.fire_cron(c["id"], t, t + cron_jitter(c["id"], self.spread), {"cron": c["id"], "t": t}, token)
            if r == "fenced":
                return made
            if r == "created":
                made += 1
                self.created += 1
    return made

Look under the hood

  • Where the chaos comes from. Run run_chaos yourself and print out['log']: you will see claims, freezes, exec events on a fence that is no longer current, complete events with False, and store-crash events in which the store lost power right after a durable write, so the caller never saw the acknowledgement. Those lost acknowledgements are why a worker cannot know whether its claim, its heartbeat or its completion took effect, and why every one of them must be safe to repeat.
  • The three mechanisms answer three questions. Did my write survive a crash? The log. Who is allowed to act now? The lease, and the fence to enforce it at the one place that knows. What if the action happens twice anyway? The ledger. Remove any one and the chaos check finds it: the mutations I ran against this check (no fsync, no truncate, a fence that is not checked, a ledger keyed by attempt, a non-atomic cursor) are each caught by a specific property.
  • "Exactly once" is a statement about effects. The harness counts about 4,100 handler executions for 3,600 jobs in 300 runs, with several hundred duplicate executions. The ledger makes the effect exactly once; delivery stays at-least-once, as in logs and exactly-once.
  • Lease length is a trade-off you can measure (experiment 2).

Stretch goals

  • A timing wheel. Replace the heap with a hashed wheel of one-second buckets and compare the work per insert and per fire (chapter 17, deep dive 1).
  • Log compaction. Write a snapshot record and drop the log before it; crash during compaction and show recovery is still correct.
  • Per-tenant fairness. Make claim rotate between tenants and show that a 2,000-job flood no longer delays other tenants (the round-robin experiment in chapter 17).
  • Misfire policies. Add skip and fire_once to cron and test each after an hour-long outage.
  • A retry budget. Cap the share of worker capacity that retries may use, so a retry storm cannot starve fresh jobs.
  • Lost-ack hunting. Add a deliberate bug (apply the effect, then record it in a second write) and use the replay command to find the smallest failing seed and minimise its log.

Distributed systems connection

The lease and the fencing token are the protocol of leases and fencing tokens, here implemented where it matters: in the store that arbitrates. The leader lease for cron is the efficiency-versus-safety split that coordination services exist to provide, and the log that survives a torn write is the same discipline as the replicated logs behind exactly-once processing. The design that ties them together is chapter 17; the retry herd is the overload story of overload and load shedding.

What you learned

  • A durable store is a log with an ordering rule: append, fsync, then apply; checksums make torn writes detectable and recovery must truncate at the first damaged line.
  • One index serves new work and recovery if you key it by "ready at": due time for queued jobs, lease expiry for running ones. Heap entries are invalidated lazily.
  • A lease buys liveness, a fencing token buys safety, and the token is enforced by the store, not the worker. Expiry is judged by the store's clock.
  • Backoff with full jitter spreads retries; attempts count claims so that poison jobs end up in the dead-letter queue; the draw is deterministic per (job, attempt).
  • Delivery is at-least-once, so effects must be idempotent: a ledger entry committed atomically with the effect, keyed by something that is the same on every attempt.
  • Cron: the lease makes one replica do the work; the unique firing id, the fencing token and the atomic cursor make a mistaken second firer harmless; stable jitter spreads the herd without changing the period.

Check your understanding

A worker claims a job with fence 3, freezes for longer than its lease, and wakes up. Meanwhile another worker claimed the job (fence 4) and finished it. What should happen to the first worker's complete(job, 3)?

  1. It succeeds: the work was done first
  2. It is refused because the store's current fence is 4 (and the job is no longer running)
  3. It succeeds only if the lease has not been reclaimed
  4. It crashes the store
The store, which holds the fence, is the arbiter. The lease handed the job to someone else (liveness); the fence stops the old owner from interfering (safety). The first worker may already have done the side effect, which is why effects must also be idempotent.

Why must recovery truncate the log at the first damaged line instead of just stopping replay there?

  1. To save disk space
  2. Because the checksum would otherwise fail again
  3. To remove duplicates
  4. Otherwise later appends land after the garbage and are lost on the next restart
Replay always stops at the first bad line. A new record written after a torn line would sit behind it, acknowledged but unreachable on the next recovery: a lost write.

An effect ledger is keyed by job_id + '#' + attempt. The worker crashes after the effect, and the job is retried. What happens?

  1. The ledger sees the key and skips the second effect
  2. The retry fails permanently
  3. The effect happens again, because the second attempt has a different key; the key must be the same on every attempt
  4. The store detects the duplicate by its fence
A key that includes the attempt makes every retry look like a new job. The key has to be derived from what is the same across attempts, here the job's run key. The lab's chaos runs corrupt balances in 93 of 100 runs with this bug.

Three scheduler replicas each run the cron loop, but only the lease holder fires. A deployment pauses the leader for 10 s with a 6 s lease. What protects against the paused leader firing a period the new leader already fired?

  1. The unique job id cron@fire_time and the fencing token checked by the store; the lease alone cannot, because the paused leader still believes it leads
  2. The lease: the old leader's clock will notice
  3. Nothing; duplicates are expected
  4. The worker, which deduplicates by payload
Leases give efficiency in the common case; they are not enough for safety, for the reason in the fencing chapter. The store refuses the stale token and also refuses a second job with the same id, so the late firing becomes a no-op.
