Lab 3: Feed Fan-out Simulator

Chapter 9's central argument is that the cost of a post depends on who wrote it, and that a skewed follower graph makes the choice between "do the work when someone posts" and "do the work when someone reads" a question about a distribution, not an average. Here you build the argument as a simulator. You generate a seeded power-law follower graph, run one stream of posts and feed loads through three timeline strategies (push, pull and a hybrid with a celebrity threshold), count every write, list read and heap operation, prove that the hybrid returns exactly the same feeds as the pure strategies, and then sweep the threshold and the read/write ratio to find the cheapest threshold that still meets a freshness and a latency target.

Why this lab matters

The fan-out question appears in disguise in notification systems, activity feeds, search-index updates and cache invalidation: do you precompute for every consumer, or compute for the consumer who asks? Interviews reward the answer "hybrid, with a threshold", and most candidates cannot say where the threshold comes from or how they would know it was right. A simulator turns the vague answer into three measurable facts: (1) under push, a handful of accounts account for a large share of all work, which is the problem; (2) under pull, every read pays for every followee, which is the other problem; (3) neither pure strategy meets both a freshness target and a read-latency target on a skewed graph, but a hybrid at the right threshold does, and the read:write ratio decides which end of the allowed range is best. You also practise the most important discipline of this kind of work: the optimisation is only worth anything if its output is provably the same as the naive version's, so correctness is checked first, post by post, against a brute-force reference. The numbers of chapter 9 are the model; the Twitter timelines case is the real-world version.

A thousand-times smaller network, the same shape

The graph has 2,000 users and 30,000 follow edges, not 200 million users. That is deliberate: what matters is the shape (mean 15 followers, median 5, a capped tail of 300) and the ratios, and a model that runs the whole check in about ten seconds can be run on twelve graphs and six workloads without hurting. Every random draw comes from random.Random(seed); the graph, the workload and every sweep are reproducible, and a failing check prints the seed (replay it with python3 check.py solution 4 7: milestone 4, seed 7). Python 3.8 or newer, standard library only. Counts are exact operations (inserts, list fetches, heap pushes and pops), not timings, so the results do not depend on your machine.

What you'll build

Five milestones:

  1. The graph: a seeded power-law follower graph (Zipf follower counts with a cap, a seeded shuffle for who is the star, random followers) with consistent followers and followees lists.
  2. Push: fan-out on write into per-user timeline lists capped at 50 ids; count the writes and the worst single fan-out.
  3. Pull: merge the recent lists of everyone a user follows at read time with a heap; count the lists fetched and the merge operations.
  4. Hybrid: push for ordinary authors, pull for accounts above a threshold; and a verifier that shows the feeds equal the pure models' and the uncapped reference's.
  5. The sweep: run the hybrid across a ladder of thresholds and read/write ratios, report the cost curve, and pick the cheapest threshold that meets a fan-out budget and a lists-per-load budget.

The cost model, stated up front

Every number in this lab comes from counting operations and weighting them with five constants at the top of the code. A timeline write (an insert into a follower's list, or an append to an author's own list) costs W_WRITE = 1. A list read at feed-load time costs W_LIST = 1, except for the list of a very popular author (at least 4x the mean number of followers), which is always cached and costs W_HOT = 0.25. A heap operation while merging costs W_MERGE = 0.1. Two service-level budgets bound the choice: no pushed post may need more than FANOUT_BUDGET = 100 inserts (freshness: a post must reach its followers quickly), and the 99th-percentile feed load may fetch at most LISTS_BUDGET = 20 lists (read latency). These are model choices, not facts about any real system; experiment 3 below shows how the best threshold moves when you change them.

Milestone 1: a seeded power-law follower graph

Follower counts in real social networks are heavy-tailed: many accounts with a handful of followers, a very few with millions (chapter 9 models this as Zipf: the account of rank i has about c·i−s followers). Chapter 9 also refuses to quote a measured exponent, and so should you: the exponent s is a knob, and the lab lets you turn it. Two further details make the graph usable. The counts are capped (nobody has more than a fixed fraction of all users as followers), and the constant c is found by bisection so the mean stays fixed at 15 whatever the exponent; otherwise raising s would change the total work as well as its distribution, and you could not tell the two effects apart.

Then wire it up: shuffle which user gets which rank (so user 0 is not always the star), give each author a random sample of the other users as followers, and build the inverse followees lists. The two lists are the two tables of chapter 9's data model: fan-out reads the first, a pull-time merge reads the second, and the check verifies they describe the same edges.

No self-follows, no duplicate edges, fully determined by the seed.

  • Who follows author a: rng.sample(range(n - 1), k), then map each pick x to x + (x >= a). That skips a itself without a rejection loop.
  • Shuffle the author order once with rng.shuffle, then pair it with the counts. Sort each list at the end so two graphs built from the same seed compare equal.

def zipf_counts(n, mean, s=1.0, cap_frac=CAP_FRAC):
    """Follower counts for n authors by rank: about c * rank**-s, capped at cap_frac * n.

    c is found by bisection so the MEAN stays `mean`; the counts are then rounded to integers
    (largest remainder) so the total is exactly round(mean * n). Returned largest first.
    """
    cap = max(1, int(cap_frac * n))
    target = mean * n
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1e12
    for _ in range(80):
        c = (lo + hi) / 2
        total = sum(min(cap, c * i ** -s) for i in range(1, n + 1))
        lo, hi = (c, hi) if total < target else (lo, c)
    raw = [min(cap, c * i ** -s) for i in range(1, n + 1)]
    counts = [int(x) for x in raw]
    short = int(round(target)) - sum(counts)
    order = sorted(range(n), key=lambda i: (-(raw[i] - counts[i]), i))
    for i in order[:max(0, short)]:
        counts[i] += 1
    return counts

def make_graph(n, mean_followers, seed, s=1.0, cap_frac=CAP_FRAC):
    """A directed follower graph with Zipf-distributed follower counts.

    Which user is the star is random (a seeded shuffle); each author's followers are a random
    sample of the other users. Nobody follows themselves; no duplicate edges.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    counts = zipf_counts(n, mean_followers, s, cap_frac)
    authors = list(range(n))
    rng.shuffle(authors)
    followers = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    followees = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    for a, k in zip(authors, counts):
        picks = rng.sample(range(n - 1), min(k, n - 1))
        fs = sorted(x + (x >= a) for x in picks)       # skip a itself
        followers[a] = fs
        for u in fs:
            followees[u].append(a)
    for u in range(n):
        followees[u].sort()
    return Graph(followers, followees)

Milestone 2: push (fan-out on write)

The push model keeps, for every user, a timeline cache: a list of the newest post ids (not bodies; chapter 9 explains why), capped at a few hundred (here 50). When an author posts, the fan-out worker walks their followers and inserts the id at the front of each list, trimming from the back. A feed load is then one list read. The work per post is exactly the author's follower count, and that is the entire problem:

Post ids are time-ordered (larger means newer, as with the Snowflake-style ids of chapter 7), so a timeline stays sorted if you always insert at the front, and "newest first" and "largest id first" are the same thing. The cap is also what makes the model testable: a pushed timeline holds the newest cap ids of the union of the user's followees' posts, so the first k of them (for k ≤ cap) equal the first k of the brute-force answer. The check compares every page, for every read, with reference_pages.

class PushModel:
    """Every post is inserted into each follower's timeline (a list of ids, newest first, <= cap)."""

    def __init__(self, graph, cap, counters):
        self.graph, self.cap, self.c = graph, cap, counters
        self.timeline = [deque() for _ in range(graph.n)]

    def post(self, author, post_id):
        """Insert post_id at the front of every follower's timeline; trim to cap; count the work."""
        c = self.c
        c.posts += 1
        fan = 0
        for f in self.graph.followers[author]:
            tl = self.timeline[f]
            tl.appendleft(post_id)
            if len(tl) > self.cap:
                tl.pop()
            fan += 1
        c.writes += fan
        c.max_fanout = max(c.max_fanout, fan)

    def read(self, user, k):
        """The k newest ids in the user's own timeline. One list fetched, no merging."""
        c = self.c
        c.loads += 1
        c.list_reads += 1
        c.lists_per_load.append(1)
        return list(self.timeline[user])[:k]

Milestone 3: pull (fan-out on read)

Pull moves the work to the reader. A post is a single append to the author's own recent list (capped the same way). A feed load fetches the recent list of every account the user follows and merges them into the newest k ids. Merging m already-sorted lists is a k-way merge with a heap: push the head of each list, repeatedly pop the largest, and push that list's next element. The helper merge_newest(lists, k, counters) is given and counts every heap push and pop in merge_ops; your job is the model that feeds it.

Count three things for each load: the lists fetched (list_reads, and lists_per_load for the percentile), how many of them belong to hot authors (hot_list_reads: a list read by that many followers stays in cache, so it is cheaper, as the cost model says), and the heap operations (inside merge_newest). Chapter 9's estimate was 3.7 million author lookups a second just to open feeds, against 230,000 timeline inserts under push; the same ratio, in miniature, is what you will measure.

class PullModel:
    """Posts are only appended to the author's own recent list; readers merge their followees' lists."""

    def __init__(self, graph, cap, counters, hot_cut):
        self.graph, self.cap, self.c, self.hot_cut = graph, cap, counters, hot_cut
        self.recent = [deque() for _ in range(graph.n)]

    def post(self, author, post_id):
        """Append to the author's recent list (trim to cap): one write."""
        c = self.c
        c.posts += 1
        r = self.recent[author]
        r.appendleft(post_id)
        if len(r) > self.cap:
            r.pop()
        c.writes += 1
        c.max_fanout = max(c.max_fanout, 1)

    def read(self, user, k):
        """Fetch every followee's recent list and merge: the k newest ids across all of them."""
        c, g = self.c, self.graph
        c.loads += 1
        lists = [self.recent[a] for a in g.followees[user]]
        c.list_reads += len(lists)
        c.hot_list_reads += sum(1 for a in g.followees[user] if g.follower_count(a) >= self.hot_cut)
        c.lists_per_load.append(len(lists))
        return merge_newest(lists, k, c)

def merge_newest(lists, k, counters):
    """K-way merge of lists of post ids (each newest first); return the k newest ids overall.

    A heap holds the head of every non-empty list. Each heap push and each pop adds 1 to
    counters.merge_ops. Stop after k results.
    """
    heap, its, out = [], [iter(l) for l in lists], []
    for i, it in enumerate(its):
        head = next(it, None)
        if head is not None:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (-head, i))
            counters.merge_ops += 1
    while heap and len(out) < k:
        neg, i = heapq.heappop(heap)
        counters.merge_ops += 1
        out.append(-neg)
        nxt = next(its[i], None)
        if nxt is not None:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (-nxt, i))
            counters.merge_ops += 1
    return out

Milestone 4: hybrid, and proving it correct

The hybrid keeps push for the many ordinary authors and pull for the few accounts whose follower count is above a threshold T. A celebrity's post is one append to their own list; an ordinary author's post is pushed to their followers as before. A feed load fetches the user's pushed timeline plus the recent list of each celebrity they follow, and merges. Both extremes are special cases: T = None (nobody is a celebrity) is push, and T = 0 (everybody with a follower is) is pull.

An optimisation that changes the answer is a bug, so correctness comes first. The argument is short and worth knowing: with time-ordered ids and a page size k at most the cap, a post in the true top k of everything the user follows is either by an ordinary author, in which case it was pushed into the user's timeline and is still within its newest cap entries, or by a celebrity, in which case it is within that celebrity's own newest cap. Either way the merge sees it. The check does not take the argument on trust; it tests it, on a dozen graphs, at every threshold on the ladder, with two different caps, against push, pull and the uncapped brute-force reference, read by read.

Your task

class HybridModel:
    """Push for authors with <= threshold followers; authors above it ("celebrities") are only
    appended to their own recent list and merged in at read time.

    threshold=None means nobody is a celebrity (pure push); threshold=0 means everybody with a
    follower is (pure pull).
    """

    def __init__(self, graph, cap, counters, threshold, hot_cut):
        self.graph, self.cap, self.c, self.hot_cut = graph, cap, counters, hot_cut
        self.threshold = math.inf if threshold is None else threshold
        self.timeline = [deque() for _ in range(graph.n)]
        self.recent = {}
        self.celeb_followees = [[a for a in graph.followees[u] if self.is_celebrity(a)] for u in range(graph.n)]

    def is_celebrity(self, author):
        return self.graph.follower_count(author) > self.threshold

    def post(self, author, post_id):
        c = self.c
        c.posts += 1
        if self.is_celebrity(author):
            r = self.recent.setdefault(author, deque())
            r.appendleft(post_id)
            if len(r) > self.cap:
                r.pop()
            c.writes += 1
            c.max_fanout = max(c.max_fanout, 1)
            return
        fan = 0
        for f in self.graph.followers[author]:
            tl = self.timeline[f]
            tl.appendleft(post_id)
            if len(tl) > self.cap:
                tl.pop()
            fan += 1
        c.writes += fan
        c.max_fanout = max(c.max_fanout, fan)

    def read(self, user, k):
        """Merge the user's pushed timeline with the recent lists of the celebrities they follow."""
        c, g = self.c, self.graph
        c.loads += 1
        celebs = self.celeb_followees[user]
        lists = [self.timeline[user]] + [self.recent.get(a, ()) for a in celebs]
        c.list_reads += len(lists)
        c.hot_list_reads += sum(1 for a in celebs if g.follower_count(a) >= self.hot_cut)
        c.lists_per_load.append(len(lists))
        return merge_newest(lists, k, c)

def mismatches(graph, workload, threshold, cap=CAP, k=PAGE):
    """Indexes of the feed loads where the hybrid page differs from the pure push page, the pure pull
    page or the uncapped reference. An empty list means the hybrid is correct."""
    hy, _ = run(graph, workload, "hybrid", cap, k, threshold)
    pu, _ = run(graph, workload, "push", cap, k)
    pl, _ = run(graph, workload, "pull", cap, k)
    ref = reference_pages(graph, workload, k)
    return [i for i in range(len(hy)) if not (hy[i] == pu[i] == pl[i] == ref[i])]

  • Build the workload once per call (make_workload(graph, seed, reads_per_post=...)) and reuse it for every threshold, so the thresholds are compared on identical events. That is the paired-comparison trick from Lab 4 of the reliability course.
  • best_threshold: filter, then min(..., key=cost). Break ties by the smaller worst fan-out so the result is deterministic.
  • If your best threshold at 20 reads per post is at the very top of the ladder, you are not applying the fan-out budget; if it is at 0 for 0.5 reads per post, you are not applying the lists budget.
Solution spoiler 
def candidate_thresholds(graph, ratio=1.5):
    """Thresholds to try: 0 (everyone pulled = pure pull), then a geometric ladder from a quarter of the
    mean follower count up to the largest account (ratio 1.5 per rung), then None (nobody pulled = pure push)."""
    top = max(graph.follower_count(a) for a in range(graph.n))
    t, ladder = max(1.0, graph.edges() / graph.n / 4), []
    while t < top:
        ladder.append(int(round(t)))
        t *= ratio
    return [0] + sorted(set(ladder)) + [None]

def sweep(graph, thresholds, reads_per_post, seed, cap=CAP, k=PAGE, hot_cut=None):
    """Run the hybrid model at every threshold on one workload. Returns a list of dicts:

    {threshold, cost, writes, list_reads, merge_ops, max_fanout, p99_lists}
    """
    wl = make_workload(graph, seed, reads_per_post=reads_per_post)
    out = []
    for t in thresholds:
        _, c = run(graph, wl, "hybrid", cap, k, t, hot_cut)
        out.append({"threshold": t, "cost": c.cost(), "writes": c.writes, "list_reads": c.list_reads,
                    "merge_ops": c.merge_ops, "max_fanout": c.max_fanout, "p99_lists": c.p99_lists()})
    return out

def best_threshold(points, fanout_budget=FANOUT_BUDGET, lists_budget=LISTS_BUDGET):
    """The cheapest point whose worst fan-out and p99 lists-per-load both fit their budgets.

    Returns the chosen dict, or None if no threshold satisfies both SLOs.
    """
    ok = [p for p in points if p["max_fanout"] <= fanout_budget and p["p99_lists"] <= lists_budget]
    return min(ok, key=lambda p: (p["cost"], p["max_fanout"])) if ok else None

Look under the hood

  • The mean hides the tail; the budgets expose it. The average post under push costs 15 inserts. The biggest account's costs 300, and a real network has accounts a million times larger than the mean. The fan-out budget is how a freshness requirement ("a post reaches its followers within a few seconds") becomes a bound on the threshold: if a worker can do X inserts a second and the target is S seconds, no pushed post may exceed X·S inserts.
  • The SLOs set the range; the workload picks the point. Two budgets turn "find the best threshold" into "find the cheapest point in an interval". The interval has a left end from the read-latency budget (pull too much and feed loads are slow) and a right end from the freshness budget (push too much and a post takes too long to land). Inside it the read:write ratio decides: write-heavy traffic wants the left end, read-heavy traffic the right end, and the hot-list discount creates an interior optimum in between.
  • All the numbers depend on the cost model. The weights are choices. Experiment 3 changes the hot-list discount and the best threshold at 2 reads per post moves from 64 to 96. A real team would measure its own weights and re-run the sweep on its own graph; the lab's contribution is the method, not the numbers.
  • Correctness before cost. A hybrid that is 20% cheaper and sometimes shows a stale feed is not an optimisation. Because the pure models and the brute-force reference are in the same harness, every cost number in this lab comes from a run whose output was verified.

Stretch goals

  • Preferential attachment. Replace the Zipf sampler with a growth model where each new user follows existing users with probability proportional to their followers. Compare the shapes (log-log slope, top shares) and rerun the sweep.
  • Inactive users. Make most users read rarely (a heavy-tailed read rate) and skip pushing to timelines that are cold. Does the pure-push cost fall enough to change the best threshold? This is the "cost that grows with useful work, not with idle accounts" requirement of chapter 9.
  • Ranking. Instead of "newest k", take the top k by a score that mixes recency and an author affinity. Which of the three models still returns exactly the same page, and what extra reads does ranking require?
  • Deletes and unfollows. Add a delete event and an unfollow event. Push must remove ids from many timelines (or filter at read time); pull gets it for free. Count the extra writes and decide whether to filter on read.
  • Per-author thresholds. Instead of one global threshold, decide per author by comparing their push cost (posts × followers) with the pull cost they induce (followers' reads × list cost). When does a per-author rule beat a single number?
  • A queue in front of fan-out. Model fan-out as asynchronous work with a capacity of X inserts a second and measure the post-to-timeline delay percentiles for the pure push model; reproduce the "a 50-million-follower post takes minutes" arithmetic of chapter 9 and show how the threshold bounds it.

Distributed systems connection

The fan-out is a distributed workload. Push is a write to many partitions (one timeline per user, spread by user id), and its worst case is a hot-key problem in reverse: one write that must touch thousands of shards. Pull is a scatter-gather read whose latency is the slowest of its lists, the tail-at-scale effect of tail latency, which is why lists per load (and its p99) is a budget and not just a cost. The threshold is a load-shedding style mechanism applied at design time: you bound the work of the biggest producers instead of discovering them in an incident, much as overload control bounds work per client. And the timeline cache is derived data: it can always be rebuilt from the graph and the posts, which is why it is a cache and not a database (caching), and why losing a shard of it costs latency, not data.

What you learned

  • Follower counts are a power law, so the cost of a write depends on its author: under push the top 1% of authors cause about a fifth of all timeline inserts although they write 1% of the posts. Generate a skewed graph with the mean held fixed and the tail capped, and measure shares, not averages.
  • Push does work per follower at write time and one list read at read time; pull does one write and work per followee at read time. Count writes, list reads and heap operations separately, because they are different resources.
  • A k-way merge of newest-first lists is a heap; its cost is counted in pushes and pops, and a page of k is exact only if the cap is at least k.
  • A hybrid is correct if its feeds equal the pure models' and a brute-force reference's, read by read; check it before costing it.
  • Neither pure strategy met both a freshness budget and a read-latency budget on a skewed graph. The two budgets carve out a range of thresholds; the read:write ratio picks the point inside it, higher when the workload is more read-heavy.
  • Cost numbers come from a model. State the weights, sweep them, and report the ordering that survives (more reads, higher threshold) rather than the digits that do not.

Under pure push on a power-law follower graph, which statement about the work per post is true?

  1. Every post costs about the mean number of followers, because posts are uniform
  2. A small number of authors cause a large share of all timeline inserts, even though they write a small share of the posts

Cost per post equals the author's follower count, and that count is heavy-tailed. In this lab the top 1% of authors write 1% of the posts but cause about 20% of the inserts (the cap keeps it from being worse), and one post by the largest account is 300 inserts against a median of 5. The mean (15) describes almost no actual post.

A pull-only feed load for a user who follows 24 accounts, with 50-entry recent lists and a page of 20, does what?

  1. One list read and no merge
  2. 24 list reads and a heap merge returning the newest 20 ids
  3. 24 inserts
  4. A scan of every post ever written
Pull fetches one recent list per followee and merges them with a heap (a k-way merge); the work per load is proportional to the number of followees, 24 here, and does not depend on who posted. That per-read cost is paid on every one of the (much more numerous) reads.

Why must the page size be at most the cap for the hybrid to match the brute-force feed?

  1. Because heaps can only return as many items as the smallest list
  2. Because a post in the true top k is guaranteed to be in the newest `cap` entries of its timeline or its author's recent list only if k <= cap
  3. Because celebrities must have exactly `cap` followers
  4. It does not matter; the models are equal for any page size
Each stored list keeps only the newest `cap` ids. A post in the global top k for a reader is within the newest k of its source (the pushed timeline or the author's own list), so it survives truncation only when k <= cap. Experiment 2 shows feeds going wrong as soon as the page exceeds the cap.

Both budgets (fan-out and lists per load) are met by a range of thresholds. Which end of the range does a read-heavy workload prefer, and why?

  1. The low end: pulling is cheaper when reads are common
  2. The high end: pushing more authors makes each of the many reads cheaper, until a single post would exceed the fan-out budget
  3. The middle, always
  4. Neither: read-heavy workloads should use pure pull
A feed load under push costs one list; under pull it costs one per followee. When reads far outnumber posts, doing the work once at write time wins, so the best threshold rises, up to the point where the largest pushed post would break the freshness budget. Write-heavy workloads prefer the opposite end, bounded by the read-latency budget.
