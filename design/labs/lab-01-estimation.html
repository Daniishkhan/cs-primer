Lab 1: Estimation Drills

Chapter 2 taught you to turn "10 million users" into queries per second, terabytes, gigabits and a number of machines, with round numbers and a straight face. Reading that is easy; doing it fast and being right within a factor of two is a skill, and skills come from reps. In this lab you build the small toolkit that chapter used (unit helpers, QPS, storage, bandwidth, cache and fleet sizing), and then a checker puts it to work on twenty scenario problems with known answers. Each answer has a tolerance band, and a wrong one prints the reasoning that gets to the right number, plus a guess at which classic mistake you made.

Why this lab matters

Every design in this course starts with numbers, and every interviewer watches for the same few slips: a factor of 8 between bits and bytes, a factor of 86,400 between "per day" and "per second", a replication factor applied twice or not at all, a peak that was never multiplied in. None of them is hard to avoid once you have been bitten, and cheap to be bitten here. The toolkit is deliberately tiny, because the point is not the code. It is that each function carries one unit conversion or one multiplication that you will otherwise do in your head under pressure, and that the scenarios force you to chain them in the order the pipeline of chapter 2 prescribes. Tolerances are part of the lesson: for most problems the check demands 10%, because the arithmetic is the arithmetic, but for loose prompts ("about a billion a month") it accepts anything within 2x, which is exactly the precision a real estimate has.

Pure arithmetic: no clocks, no randomness, no network

Nothing here sleeps or touches the machine you run it on. The scenario problems are fixed; the only randomness is in the property checks, which feed your functions sixty seeded random inputs (a failure prints the seed so you can replay it with python3 check.py solution 2 17, milestone 2, seed 17). The whole check takes a fraction of a second. Python 3.8 or newer, standard library only. Sizes are powers of ten (1 KB = 1,000 bytes), because the 2.4% gap to 1,024 is far inside the error bars of an estimate.

What you'll build

Five milestones, each adding a group of helpers and a group of scenarios:

  1. The toolkit: unit helpers (KB to PB, per day to per second, bytes to bits), order_of_magnitude, within_factor and human() formatting.
  2. QPS: from DAU, actions per user and a peak factor, then split into reads and writes.
  3. Storage: over N years, with replication and an index overhead, and how many nodes that is.
  4. Bandwidth and caches: bits on the wire, the origin's share behind a cache, the hit ratio you need, and 80/20 cache sizing.
  5. Machines: from per-box capacity with headroom, with zone loss, Little's law, and the latency cost of running hot (queueing theory).

Milestone 1: the toolkit

Start with the unglamorous part, because it is where the real mistakes live. There are four conversions you will use in every estimate:

  • Sizes. KB, MB, GB, TB, PB are 103 to 1015. Using decimal powers keeps every product readable ("1 M pastes of 10 KB is 10 GB").
  • Time. There are 86,400 seconds in a day, which is "roughly 105", so per_day_to_per_sec and its inverse are one division and one multiplication. A billion a day is about 11,600 per second; a million a day, about 12.
  • Bits. Storage is in bytes, bandwidth in bits: bytes_to_bits multiplies by 8. It is a one-liner that exists so you never skip it.
  • Magnitude. order_of_magnitude(x) is the floor of log10(x). It is how you say "that is five orders of magnitude over one machine", and it is easy to get wrong at exact powers of ten (math.log(1000, 10) gives 2.9999999999999996, and the floor of that is 2).

Then two helpers for the habit of the whole course. within_factor(estimate, truth, f) asks whether an estimate is inside the band [truth/f, truth×f]: the check uses it as the definition of "good enough", and so should you. human(n, unit) prints three significant digits and an SI suffix (54.8 TB, 231, 55.6 Mbit/s), the same format chapter 2's scripts use, so your outputs read like the chapters' outputs.

Your task

Write per_day_to_per_sec, per_sec_to_per_day, bytes_to_bits, order_of_magnitude (raise ValueError for x ≤ 0), within_factor and human. The constants KB..PB, SECONDS_PER_DAY and DAYS_PER_YEAR are given.

It checks the constants, hand values (86.4 M per day is 1,000 per second; 125 MB/s is 1 Gbit/s; human(54_750_000_000_000, "B") is '54.8 TB'; order_of_magnitude(999) is 2 and (1000) is 3, (0.02) is -2), and then sixty seeded random inputs: converting per-day to per-second and back must round-trip, 10**e ≤ x < 10**(e+1) must hold for the exponent you return, and within_factor must flip exactly at the boundary. A failure prints the seed.

Hints try without first
  • order_of_magnitude: math.floor(math.log10(x)) (not math.log(x, 10), which is less accurate), then correct it if 10 ** (e + 1) <= x. A floating-point log can land just below an exact power of ten.
  • within_factor: with r = estimate / truth, the answer is 1 / factor <= r <= factor. A zero or negative estimate is never within a factor of anything.
  • human: walk a list of (divisor, suffix) from peta down to kilo and use the first one that fits; format with f"{n / div:.3g} {suffix}{unit}". Below a thousand, f"{n:.3g} {unit}".strip() (so the unit is not left dangling after a bare number).
  • The suffixes are decimal: k 103, M 106, G 109, T 1012, P 1015.
Solution spoiler 
def per_day_to_per_sec(n_per_day):
    """Events per day -> events per second (the average rate)."""
    return n_per_day / SECONDS_PER_DAY

def per_sec_to_per_day(n_per_sec):
    """Events per second -> events per day."""
    return n_per_sec * SECONDS_PER_DAY

def bytes_to_bits(n_bytes):
    """Bytes -> bits (bandwidth is quoted in bits)."""
    return n_bytes * 8

def order_of_magnitude(x):
    """floor(log10(x)): 999 -> 2, 1000 -> 3, 0.02 -> -2. x must be positive."""
    if x <= 0:
        raise ValueError("order_of_magnitude needs a positive number")
    e = math.floor(math.log10(x))
    if 10 ** (e + 1) <= x:      # log10 can round just below an exact power of ten
        e += 1
    return e

def human(n, unit=""):
    """Format with three significant digits and an SI-style suffix.

    human(54_750_000_000_000, "B") -> '54.8 TB';  human(12) -> '12';  human(0.5) -> '0.5'
    """
    for div, suf in _SUFFIXES:
        if abs(n) >= div:
            return f"{n / div:.3g} {suf}{unit}"
    return f"{n:.3g} {unit}".strip()

def within_factor(estimate, truth, factor):
    """True if estimate is within `factor` of truth, in either direction."""
    if estimate <= 0 or truth <= 0:
        return False
    r = estimate / truth
    return 1 / factor <= r <= factor

Milestone 2: QPS from DAU, actions and a peak factor

The first real estimate. Chapter 2's recipe: DAU × actions per user per day ÷ 86,400 = average QPS, then multiply by a peak factor you state out loud (2 to 5 for a consumer app with a daily rhythm, 10 to 100 for spiky products). The average is almost never what matters; peak is what you size for. Then split into reads and writes, because the ratio decides whether a cache or write capacity is the problem (the workload-shape table of chapter 1).

Split a total with a read:write ratio of R : 1 and writes are 1/(R+1) of the total, not 1/R. That off-by-one is the classic slip: at 9 reads per write, writes are 10% of the traffic, not 11%, and the check has a scenario for exactly that.

Your task

Write avg_qps(dau, actions_per_user_per_day), peak_qps(avg, peak_factor=3.0), qps_from_dau(dau, actions, peak_factor=3.0) (returning the pair average, peak) and split_read_write(total_qps, reads_per_write) (returning reads, writes).

python3 check.py shows this milestone as ✔. Hand values first (86,400 DAU at 1,000 actions each is 1,000/s, 2,000/s at a 2x peak; a 9:1 split of 1,000/s is 900 and 100), then sixty seeded inputs: doubling DAU must double QPS, peak must equal average times the factor, and the split must add up to the total with exactly the requested ratio. Finally four scenarios, each within 10% (or 2x when the prompt is loose): a microblog at 100 M DAU, a photo app at 20 M, the write share of 50 M DAU at 9:1, and "about a billion page views a month".

Hints try without first
  • avg_qps is per_day_to_per_sec(dau * actions). Use the helper from milestone 1 rather than writing 86400 again; that is what the helper is for.
  • split_read_write: writes = total / (reads_per_write + 1) and reads are the rest, so the two always add up to the total.
  • For the monthly prompt, a month is 30 days; 1 B / 30 is 33 M a day, about 386/s on average. Any answer from 600 to 2,300 peak is inside the band.
Solution spoiler 
def avg_qps(dau, actions_per_user_per_day):
    """Average requests per second."""
    return per_day_to_per_sec(dau * actions_per_user_per_day)

def peak_qps(avg, peak_factor=3.0):
    """Peak requests per second: the average times a stated peak-to-average factor."""
    return avg * peak_factor

def qps_from_dau(dau, actions_per_user_per_day, peak_factor=3.0):
    """(average QPS, peak QPS) for a user population."""
    a = avg_qps(dau, actions_per_user_per_day)
    return a, peak_qps(a, peak_factor)

def split_read_write(total_qps, reads_per_write):
    """Split a total into (reads, writes) for a read:write ratio of `reads_per_write` : 1."""
    writes = total_qps / (reads_per_write + 1)
    return total_qps - writes, writes

Milestone 3: storage over N years

Storage is the estimate that grows without bound, so it is the one where a missing factor hurts most. The recipe, again from chapter 2: new bytes per day = writes per day × bytes per write; × 365 for a year; × the number of years you retain; × (1 + index overhead) if the data is structured (20 to 100% is typical; say which you assume); × the replication factor (3 copies is the default for durability, erasure coding gets it near 1.5, see object storage).

Keep two questions apart: raw size (what the application thinks it stores) and fleet size (what the disks hold). A single node holds one replica's share, not the total, which is why nodes_for_storage takes the fleet number and a max_fill (you never run disks at 100%; 70% leaves room for compaction, rebalancing and growth).

Your task

Write raw_bytes_per_year(writes_per_day, bytes_per_write), storage_bytes(writes_per_day, bytes_per_write, years, replication=3.0, index_overhead=0.0) and nodes_for_storage(total_bytes, bytes_per_node, max_fill=0.7) (round up).

python3 check.py shows this milestone as ✔. Hand values (1 M writes a day of 10 KB is 3.65 TB a year raw and 54.75 TB over 5 years at 3 replicas; replication 2 with 50% index overhead multiplies by exactly 3), sixty seeded inputs (storage is linear in years and in replication, and equals raw-per-year × years × replication × (1 + overhead); node counts are whole numbers), and five scenarios: the pastebin, a chat service with 30% index overhead (569 TB), photos with erasure coding at 1.5x (164 PB), 164 PB on 16 TB nodes filled to 70% (about 14,700), and a loose log-retention problem.

Hints try without first
  • Fractional years are fine (30 days is 30 / 365 years); do not round the years to an integer inside the function.
  • Replication may be fractional. 1.5 is erasure coding, not a typo.
  • index_overhead is a fraction (0.3 means +30%). The multiplier is (1 + index_overhead).
  • Count nodes with math.ceil(total / (bytes_per_node * max_fill)). Rounding down gives you a cluster that is full on day one.
Solution spoiler 
def raw_bytes_per_year(writes_per_day, bytes_per_write):
    """Unreplicated bytes written in one year."""
    return writes_per_day * DAYS_PER_YEAR * bytes_per_write

def storage_bytes(writes_per_day, bytes_per_write, years, replication=3.0, index_overhead=0.0):
    """Total fleet storage: raw bytes x (1 + index overhead) x replication factor.

    index_overhead is a fraction (0.3 means indexes and metadata add 30%).
    replication may be fractional (erasure coding at 1.5x).
    """
    raw = raw_bytes_per_year(writes_per_day, bytes_per_write) * years
    return raw * (1.0 + index_overhead) * replication

def nodes_for_storage(total_bytes, bytes_per_node, max_fill=0.7):
    """Machines needed if each may be filled to `max_fill` of its capacity."""
    return math.ceil(total_bytes / (bytes_per_node * max_fill))

Milestone 4: bandwidth and cache sizing

Two more conversions that go wrong in a characteristic way. Bandwidth is peak QPS × bytes per response × 8, in bits per second: 20,000 requests a second of 500 KB images is 10 GB/s of payload and 80 Gbit/s on the wire, eight 10 Gbit/s links before any headroom. Compare it with one NIC and you know whether a CDN is optional or the whole design.

Caches work on two numbers. The hit ratio h decides how much reaches the origin: origin_qps(qps, h) = qps × (1 - h), so going from 90% to 99% hits cuts origin load tenfold. And required_hit_ratio turns the question around: given an origin that can do 5,000 requests a second and 55,556 arriving, the cache must hit 91%. For how much memory, use the 80/20 rule: about 20% of the data serves about 80% of the reads, so hold hot_fraction of what was written in the window that is still read (for a pastebin, the last 30 days, not all history). It is a heuristic; real skew is often steeper, and chapter 6 measures it.

Your task

Hints try without first
  • Bandwidth: bytes_to_bits(qps * bytes_per_response). Use the helper.
  • required_hit_ratio: if qps <= origin_capacity return 0.0; otherwise 1 - origin_capacity / qps.
  • The cache size is new_bytes_per_day * window_days * hot_fraction. The window is the part people forget.
  • NICs: usable per card is nic_bps * max_utilisation; divide and round up.
Solution spoiler 
def bandwidth_bps(qps, bytes_per_response):
    """Bits per second for a request rate and a response size."""
    return bytes_to_bits(qps * bytes_per_response)

def origin_qps(qps, hit_ratio):
    """Requests that get past a cache or CDN with the given hit ratio."""
    return qps * (1.0 - hit_ratio)

def required_hit_ratio(qps, origin_capacity_qps):
    """The hit ratio the cache must reach so the origin sees at most its capacity (0 if none needed)."""
    if qps <= origin_capacity_qps:
        return 0.0
    return 1.0 - origin_capacity_qps / qps

def cache_bytes(new_bytes_per_day, window_days, hot_fraction=0.2):
    """80/20 cache sizing: hold the hot fraction of what was written in the window that is still read."""
    return new_bytes_per_day * window_days * hot_fraction

def nics_needed(bps, nic_bps=10e9, max_utilisation=0.5):
    """Network cards of `nic_bps` needed if each runs at most at `max_utilisation`."""
    return math.ceil(bps / (nic_bps * max_utilisation))

Milestone 5: machine counts with headroom

The last step turns a peak into a fleet: servers = peak QPS ÷ (per-box capacity × target utilisation) + spares. The utilisation factor is not padding. It is the price of latency: a single queue's mean time in system is about 1/(1 − ρ) times the bare service time, so a box at 90% takes ten times as long as an idle one, and at 95% twenty times (queueing theory, chapter 8). Run at 50 to 70% and the tail stays tame; run hot and a small burst becomes an outage (overload).

Two more rules. To survive losing one of k zones, the other k − 1 must carry the whole peak, so the fleet is multiplied by k/(k − 1): 1.5x for three zones, 2x for two (a good reason to prefer three). And Little's law, in-flight = rate × latency, tells you concurrency: 5,000 requests a second at 40 ms means 200 in flight, which is how you size a thread pool or a connection pool.

Your task

Write machines(peak, per_box, utilisation=0.6, zones=1, spares=0), in_flight(rate, latency_seconds), latency_inflation(utilisation) (raise ValueError unless 0 ≤ ρ < 1) and max_utilisation_for(inflation_budget) (the inverse; ValueError below 1).

Hints try without first

  • Apply the zone factor to the count, not to the capacity; and apply it once. (Three zones is ×1.5; do not also add a "spare zone".)

Solution spoiler 
def machines(peak, per_box, utilisation=0.6, zones=1, spares=0):
    """Machines for `peak` load when each box may run at `utilisation` of `per_box`.

    zones > 1 means: survive losing one whole zone, so the other (zones - 1) zones
    must carry the peak, which multiplies the count by zones / (zones - 1).
    Spares are added on top.
    """
    n = math.ceil(peak / (per_box * utilisation))
    if zones > 1:
        n = math.ceil(n * zones / (zones - 1))
    return n + spares

def in_flight(rate, latency_seconds):
    """Little's law: requests in flight = arrival rate x time in system."""
    return rate * latency_seconds

def latency_inflation(utilisation):
    """M/M/1 mean time in system as a multiple of the bare service time: 1 / (1 - rho)."""
    if not 0 <= utilisation < 1:
        raise ValueError("utilisation must be in [0, 1)")
    return 1.0 / (1.0 - utilisation)

def max_utilisation_for(inflation_budget):
    """The highest utilisation whose M/M/1 latency inflation stays within the budget."""
    if inflation_budget < 1:
        raise ValueError("a request cannot be faster than its service time")
    return 1.0 - 1.0 / inflation_budget

Look under the hood

  • Why two kinds of tolerance. Where the prompt gives exact inputs, the answer is arithmetic and a 10% band catches every conversion slip while forgiving rounding (86,400 versus 100,000). Where the prompt says "about", the inputs themselves are uncertain, and demanding more than 2x would reward false precision. Chapter 2's Monte Carlo showed that four inputs each uncertain by 2x give a middle-90% range of about 14x on the product, so "within 2x" is already generous to the estimator.
  • The toolkit is thin on purpose. There is no pastebin() function. An estimate is a chain of small steps whose order you choose, and each step's output should be a number you can sanity-check ("700 reads a second: one box with a cache"). The scenarios exercise the chain, not a memorised formula.
  • Property tests find the slips example tests miss. A function that returns the right answer at 100 inputs and the wrong one at 1013 (a float rounding at an exact power of ten, an integer division) is exactly what sixty random log-uniform inputs find; the failure prints the seed.

Distributed systems connection

Estimates are how a distributed design is justified. "55,556 feed loads a second" is why one node is not enough, which is the argument for partitioning; "10 GB/s of egress" is why a CDN exists; "18 boxes per zone, 27 across three" is the cost of surviving a zone failure that the blast radius chapter describes in the abstract. The failure arithmetic of availability maths and the headroom arithmetic here are the same discipline: every dependency and every utilisation target is a number you wrote down and can defend. The next two labs apply it: Lab 2 asks how many checks per second a rate limiter must absorb, and Lab 3 counts the writes and reads of a feed on a skewed graph.

What you learned

  • Estimation is a short chain of unit conversions and multiplications: per day to per second (divide by 86,400), average to peak (state a factor), bytes to bits (×8), raw to fleet storage (years, index overhead, replication).
  • Most wrong answers are a missing or doubled factor: 3x for replication or peak, 8x for bits, 365x for a year, 1.5x for the zone-loss rule. The size of the error identifies the slip.
  • A cache's value is logarithmic in its hit ratio: each nine cuts origin load tenfold, and the required ratio is 1 - origin_capacity / qps.
  • Utilisation is a latency decision: mean latency is about 1/(1 − ρ) times the service time, so 50 to 70% is the usual target, and surviving a zone loss multiplies the fleet by k/(k − 1).
  • Tolerance bands are the honest definition of "right": 10% for exact inputs, 2x when the prompt is loose. An estimate is judged by the decision it supports, not by its last digit.

Check your understanding

A product has about 1 billion page views a month. Roughly what peak QPS should you plan for, assuming a 3x peak factor?

  1. About 400 per second: that is the average, and peaks are covered by caching
  2. About 1,200 per second: 1 B / 30 days / 86,400 is about 390 on average, times 3
  3. About 12,000 per second: a billion a day is 12,000 per second
  4. About 35,000 per second: a month has 2.6 M seconds, so divide by 2.6 M and multiply by 100
A month is about 2.6 million seconds, so a billion views is about 385 per second on average; times 3 is about 1,150. The first option plans for the average, which is never what you size for; the third uses a billion per day, the classic factor-of-30 slip.

An origin serves 20,000 requests a second of 500 KB responses. What link capacity does that need, before any headroom?

  1. 10 Gbit/s
  2. 80 Gbit/s
  3. 1.25 Gbit/s
  4. 10 GB/s
20,000 × 500 KB = 10 GB/s of payload. Bandwidth is quoted in bits, so multiply by 8: 80 Gbit/s, which is eight 10 Gbit/s links before headroom, and at 50% utilisation sixteen. The first option forgets the 8; 10 GB/s is the right number in the wrong unit.

Boxes each handle 2,000 requests a second. The peak is 17,361/s, you run at 50% utilisation, and the fleet must survive losing one of three zones. About how many boxes?

  1. 9
  2. 18
  3. 27
  4. 54
At 50% each box does 1,000, so 17.4 → 18 boxes. With three zones the remaining two must carry the whole peak, so the fleet is 18 × 3/2 = 27 (9 per zone). Applying the zone factor to capacity and to the count, as 54 does, counts it twice.

A server's latency budget allows its mean latency to be at most 5 times the bare service time (single queue, M/M/1). The highest utilisation you can run it at is:

  1. 50%
  2. 80%
  3. 90%
  4. 95%
Mean time in system is 1/(1 − ρ) times the service time. Setting that to 5 gives ρ = 1 − 1/5 = 0.8. At 90% the inflation is 10x and at 95% 20x. Real systems have tail requirements stricter than the mean, which is why 50 to 70% is the usual target.
