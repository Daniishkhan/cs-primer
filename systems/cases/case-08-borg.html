Borg: Containers at Google Scale

Your laptop runs hundreds of processes and the kernel keeps them out of each other's way. Google needed the same trick across tens of thousands of machines at once: accept work from thousands of different applications, decide which machine runs what, and keep important services alive while squeezing in as much batch work as the hardware can take. Borg is the system that did it, and Kubernetes grew out of what its builders learned.

The pattern in one sentence

Isolation and bin-packing: if the kernel can reliably wall off each task's CPU and memory, then a scheduler can pack very different workloads onto the same machines, and the shared pool needs fewer machines than separate pools would.

The cast

The facts below come from the 2015 EuroSys paper "Large-scale cluster management at Google with Borg" (Verma, Pedrosa, Korupolu, Oppenheimer, Tune and Wilkes), and from the 2016 ACM Queue article "Borg, Omega, and Kubernetes" (Burns, Grant, Oppenheimer, Brewer and Wilkes). I use only the figures those two papers state. Where the paper gives a number, I say it is the paper's number.

Cell
A set of machines managed as a unit. A cluster usually hosts one large cell and maybe a few small test cells. The paper gives the median cell size as about 10 thousand machines, excluding test cells.
Job and task
Users submit jobs. A job has one or more tasks, all running the same program. Each task maps to a set of Linux processes running in a container on a machine, and a job runs in just one cell.
Alloc
A reserved set of resources on a machine in which one or more tasks can run, for example a web server and a log-copying helper that share a machine.
Borgmaster
The cell's logically central controller. It's logically one process but replicated five times, with state in a Paxos-based store; one elected master handles all operations that change the cell's state. The scheduler runs as a separate process.
Borglet
The agent on each machine. It starts and stops tasks, restarts them if they fail, manages local resources by changing OS kernel settings, and reports machine state.
Prod and non-prod
The paper calls higher-priority jobs "production" (prod) and the rest "non-production". Most long-running services are prod; most batch jobs are non-prod.

The two workloads that share a machine

Borg's cells run a mix of two kinds of work. The paper describes long-running services that should "never" go down and handle short, latency-sensitive requests (from a few microseconds to a few hundred milliseconds): Gmail, Google Docs, web search, and internal infrastructure like Bigtable. And batch jobs that take from a few seconds to a few days and are much less sensitive to short-term performance changes.

The ACM Queue article explains the history: Borg was built to manage both, which had previously been handled by two separate systems (Babysitter and the Global Work Queue). It says Borg shares machines between the two types "as a way of increasing resource utilization and thereby reducing costs", and that this was possible because container support in the Linux kernel was becoming available.

This is the opposite of the "one server, one job" mindset. It's the same insight as chapter 7's scheduler, one level up: just as the kernel time-slices a core among many processes, Borg bin-packs many tasks onto many machines.

What happens when you submit a job

Step through

From job to running task

  1. Submit. A user submits a job, usually described in a declarative configuration file. The Borgmaster records it persistently and adds its tasks to a pending queue. Each task has its resource needs (CPU, RAM, disk and so on) stated separately; the paper says Borg does not impose fixed-size buckets or slots.
  2. Admission control. Every job has a priority, a small positive integer, and users hold quota: a vector of resource quantities at a given priority for a period of time. The paper says quota-checking is part of admission control, not scheduling, so jobs without enough quota are rejected immediately on submission.
  3. Feasibility checking. The scheduler scans the pending queue from high to low priority, with round-robin within a priority for fairness. For each task it finds machines that meet the task's constraints and have enough "available" resources, which include resources held by lower-priority tasks that could be evicted. (Machine 2 is ruled out here because it is too full; the numbers on this diagram are illustrative, not from the paper.)
  4. Scoring. Among the feasible machines the scheduler picks one by a score. The paper says the score is mostly driven by built-in criteria: minimising the number and priority of preempted tasks, preferring machines that already have the task's packages, spreading tasks across power and failure domains, and packing quality, including putting a mix of high and low priority tasks on one machine so the high-priority ones can expand in a load spike.
  5. Start, preempting if needed. If the chosen machine still lacks room, Borg preempts (kills) lower-priority tasks, from lowest to highest priority, until the new one fits. The preempted tasks go back into the pending queue. The Borglet then starts the task, which runs inside a Linux cgroup-based container.

Two more numbers from the paper show how fast this has to be. A busy Borgmaster uses 10 to 14 CPU cores and up to 50 GiB of RAM, and several cells have arrival rates above 10 thousand tasks per minute. The paper says an online scheduling pass over the pending queue normally completes in less than half a second. It credits three techniques: caching scores, doing feasibility and scoring once per "equivalence class" of identical tasks, and examining machines in random order until "enough" feasible ones are found. With those techniques disabled, scheduling a cell's whole workload from scratch did not finish after more than three days; with them, it typically took a few hundred seconds.

Priorities, quota and preemption

When more work shows up than fits, Borg uses two tools. Priority says who wins now: a high-priority task can take resources from a lower-priority one, even if that means preempting (killing) it. The paper lists the non-overlapping priority bands in decreasing order as monitoring, production, batch, and best effort. To avoid cascades of preemptions, tasks in the production band are not allowed to preempt one another.

Quota says who is allowed to ask at all. It is expressed as a vector of resources at a priority for a period (the paper's example is "20 TiB of RAM at prod priority from now until the end of July in cell xx"), and higher-priority quota costs more. Production-priority quota is limited to the real resources in the cell, so a prod job that fits in a user's quota can expect to run. Lower priorities are over-sold: every user has infinite quota at priority zero, though the paper notes this is hard to use because resources are over-subscribed.

Preempted tasks get a SIGTERM before the SIGKILL, so they can save state and finish current requests (the paper says the notice is delivered about 80% of the time). That is the signal machinery from chapter 5, used as a protocol between cluster and application.

Isolation: what lets strangers share a machine

Sharing only works if tasks cannot easily hurt each other. The paper is candid that early Borg was weak here: the first Borglets did "post-hoc usage checking" and killed tasks that used too much memory or disk, and it "was still too easy for rogue tasks to affect the performance of other tasks". Some users inflated their resource requests, reducing utilisation, and in extreme cases petitioned for dedicated machines.

Today, the paper says, all Borg tasks run inside a Linux cgroup-based resource container, and the Borglet manipulates the container settings, "giving much improved control because the OS kernel is in the loop". A chroot jail is the primary security isolation mechanism between tasks on a machine. The paper also draws two distinctions that matter in practice:

Latency-sensitive vs batch

Tasks have an application class. Latency-sensitive (LS) tasks serve users and get the best treatment, including the ability to temporarily starve batch tasks for several seconds. Batch tasks may run on any core but are given tiny scheduler shares.

Compressible vs non-compressible

CPU cycles and disk bandwidth are compressible: they can be reclaimed by reducing quality of service, so the Borglet throttles. Memory and disk space are non-compressible: they generally cannot be reclaimed without killing the task, so if a machine runs out of memory the Borglet terminates tasks from lowest to highest priority.

That second distinction is chapter 6 in cluster form. CPU time can be taken back mid-flight; memory cannot be taken back without losing what is in it. The paper also says the standard Linux CPU scheduler required substantial tuning to support both low latency and high utilisation.

Common misconception: "containers are mini virtual machines"

The paper says most of Borg's workload does not run inside VMs, because it doesn't want to pay the cost of virtualisation. A Borg task is a set of ordinary Linux processes in a container, so it shares the host kernel (see chapter 19). The ACM Queue article is explicit about the limits: containers "cannot prevent interference in resources that the operating-system kernel doesn't manage, such as level 3 processor caches and memory bandwidth", and need an additional security layer, such as virtual machines, to protect against malicious actors in the cloud. Borg itself runs VMs for external software (such as Google Compute Engine users) inside a KVM process that is itself a Borg task.

Why bother? The utilisation numbers

The reason for all this is cost. The paper says increasing utilisation by a few percentage points can save millions of dollars. Its main experiment asks how many extra machines would be needed if prod and non-prod work were put in separate cells. Answer, for the median cell: 20 to 30 percent more. The paper explains why: prod jobs usually reserve resources to handle rare spikes but do not use them most of the time, and Borg reclaims the unused part (it calls this resource reclamation) to run much of the non-prod work. About 20% of the workload in a median cell runs in reclaimed resources. The paper also reports that nearly all machines run both: 98% of machines in shared cells, and 83% across the whole set of machines Borg manages.

It checked whether sharing hurt performance too. Measuring cycles per instruction (CPI) as a proxy for interference, it found mean CPU performance to be about 3% worse in shared cells than in dedicated ones, and concluded that "sharing doesn't drastically increase the cost of running programs". Even on the least favourable results, the paper says, the slowdown is outweighed by the decrease in machines required.

What these figures are, and aren't

The percentages come from the paper's own simulations and measurements on 15 sampled Google cells (its simulations used cell checkpoints taken on 1 October 2014), using a method it calls cell compaction. They support the claim that pooling saves machines at Google's scale and workload mix. They are not a prediction for your cluster.

The pattern: isolate, then pack

Stripped of Google's specifics, the general pattern has three parts:

  1. Declare needs and limits. Each task says how much CPU and memory it needs. The platform enforces limits with kernel features (cgroups) rather than trusting the task.
  2. Pack by feasibility and score. A scheduler filters to machines where the task fits, then ranks them. This is bin-packing, a hard problem, in practice handled with heuristics and scoring. The paper reports its hybrid scoring model gives about 3 to 5 percent better packing efficiency than "best fit" for its workloads.
  3. Recover from failure by rescheduling. Machines fail all the time. Applications are expected to tolerate it, and the system reschedules evicted tasks elsewhere. The paper says already-running tasks keep running even if the Borgmaster or a Borglet is down, and reports Borgmaster availability of 99.99% in practice.

Analogy: a shipping port

Containers revolutionised shipping not because the boxes are special but because they are standard: any crane can move any box and any ship can carry any box. A port scheduler can then pack ships tightly without caring what is inside. A cluster scheduler does the same with tasks, as long as each task's resource box has rigid walls.

Where it breaks down: shipping boxes don't interfere with each other on the ship. Tasks share caches and memory bandwidth, which container walls cannot divide, as the ACM Queue article says.

From Borg to Kubernetes

The ACM Queue article describes three systems: Borg, Omega (an offspring of Borg meant to improve the software engineering of the Borg ecosystem, storing cluster state in a Paxos-based store with optimistic concurrency control) and Kubernetes, which is open source. It says many of Omega's innovations, including multiple schedulers, have been folded into Borg. Both papers list what Kubernetes changed:

Pods instead of allocs

The Borg paper says the Kubernetes equivalent of an alloc is the pod, a resource envelope for one or more containers always scheduled onto the same machine. The ACM Queue article adds that Kubernetes always runs an application container inside a pod.

Labels instead of jobs

Borg's only grouping was the job, a vector of identical tasks indexed from zero. Both papers describe this as too rigid: people encoded roles like "canary" in job names. Kubernetes uses labels, key/value pairs that define overlapping sets with a query.

An IP address per pod

In Borg, all tasks on a machine share the host's IP address and port space, so Borg had to schedule ports as a resource. Kubernetes gives every pod and service its own IP address, so applications can use well-known ports.

A shared API and reconciliation loops

The Queue article says the idea of a reconciliation loop, which compares desired state to observed state and acts to converge them, is shared by Borg, Omega and Kubernetes, and makes systems robust: when a controller restarts "it simply picks up where it left off".

The Borg paper's own list of lessons names things that stood the test of time: allocs; the point that "cluster management is more than task management" (naming and load balancing matter too); the importance of surfacing debugging information to users ("introspection is vital"); and the master as "the kernel of a distributed system", a core surrounded by cooperating services. Among its cautionary lessons: jobs are too restrictive as the only grouping, one IP address per machine complicates things, and a very rich API (about 230 parameters in its configuration language) serves power users at the expense of casual ones.

Trade-offs and lessons

  • Utilisation costs isolation effort. The more tightly you pack, the more you depend on the kernel to keep tasks from interfering. The paper still reports occasional low-level interference even with cgroups.
  • Priorities mean someone gets killed. Preemption is the price of letting batch work soak up spare capacity. Applications must be built to tolerate eviction.
  • Centralisation scales further than expected, up to a point. The paper says it is not sure where the scalability limit of the centralised architecture will come from, because every time they approached one they eliminated it. It also says cells remain independent to avoid correlated failures, which, not scalability, is its main argument against larger cells.
  • Abstractions grow. The Queue article says Borg's surrounding tools grew into a "somewhat heterogeneous, ad-hoc collection of systems", which Kubernetes tried to avoid with consistent API conventions.

Try it yourself

A toy scheduler, and a look at your own limits

This script is feasibility checking plus a simple "best fit" score, nothing like Borg's real scoring. The machine sizes and task sizes are invented.

# A toy scheduler: feasibility checking, then scoring.
machines = {"m1": {"cpu": 8, "free": 8}, "m2": {"cpu": 8, "free": 3}, "m3": {"cpu": 8, "free": 5}}

def place(task_cpu):
    feasible = [m for m, s in machines.items() if s["free"] >= task_cpu]   # step 1
    if not feasible:
        return None                                                       # stays pending
    # step 2: score. "Best fit" = least space left over after placing.
    best = min(feasible, key=lambda m: machines[m]["free"] - task_cpu)
    machines[best]["free"] -= task_cpu
    return best

for cpu in (4, 4, 4, 4):
    print(f"task needing {cpu} cpu ->", place(cpu))
print({m: s["free"] for m, s in machines.items()})
task needing 4 cpu -> m3
task needing 4 cpu -> m1
task needing 4 cpu -> m1
task needing 4 cpu -> None
{'m1': 0, 'm2': 3, 'm3': 1}

The fourth task is "pending": 4 CPUs are free in total, but spread across machines so no single one has 4 free (the leftover 3 and 1 are stranded). That is the fragmentation problem the paper says its hybrid scoring tries to reduce, by reducing "stranded resources". Try changing the scoring function to max (worst fit, which spreads load) and compare the leftover.

Now look at the Linux side of isolation. These commands only read information. On macOS there are no cgroups, so run them in a Linux machine, WSL, or a container you already have:

$ cat /proc/self/cgroup               # Linux/WSL: which cgroup is this shell in?
$ cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.max           # cgroup v2: CPU quota ("max" means unlimited)
$ cat /sys/fs/cgroup/memory.max        # cgroup v2: memory limit in bytes, or "max"
$ nice                                  # macOS and Linux: this shell's scheduling niceness

Inside a container with limits set, cpu.max and memory.max show numbers instead of max. Those files are exactly the "container settings" the paper says the Borglet manipulates. Some systems still use cgroup v1, where the paths differ; if a file is missing, that is why.

Distributed systems connection

Borg is a distributed system managing other distributed systems. Its Borgmaster is replicated five times and uses Paxos to elect a master and keep state (the idea behind networking chapter 20), so the controller itself survives failures. Its design spreads tasks across failure domains (machines, racks, power domains) to reduce correlated failures, the lesson of networking case 6 and case 7. And the ACM Queue article says the reconciliation loop (compare desired state with observed state, then act to converge them) is shared by Borg, Omega and Kubernetes. General industry pattern, not from these sources: many other orchestrators use the same idea. Chapter 19 explains the kernel features underneath; this case is what you get when you put a scheduler on top.

Key takeaways

  • Borg runs jobs made of tasks inside cells of machines (median about 10 thousand), with a replicated Borgmaster and a Borglet on each machine.
  • Admission uses priorities and quota; scheduling is feasibility checking followed by scoring, with preemption of lower-priority work when needed.
  • Tasks run in Linux cgroup containers. CPU is compressible and can be throttled; memory is not and means killing tasks.
  • Mixing prod and batch work saves machines: the paper reports that segregating them would need 20 to 30 percent more in the median cell.
  • Kubernetes took Borg's lessons: pods, labels, an IP per pod, a uniform API and reconciliation loops.

What are the two phases of Borg's scheduling algorithm?

  1. Compile and link
  2. Feasibility checking to find machines where the task could run, then scoring to pick one
  3. Sorting and hashing
  4. Quota and billing
The scheduler first finds machines that meet the task's constraints and have enough available resources, then scores them. Quota is checked earlier, as part of admission control.

Why does mixing production and batch workloads on the same machines save hardware?

  1. Batch jobs are always smaller than services
  2. Containers make the CPU faster
  3. It lets prod tasks use more memory than they requested
  4. Prod jobs reserve resources for rare spikes they mostly don't use, and Borg reclaims the unused part to run batch work
Per the paper, segregating the two would need 20 to 30 percent more machines in the median cell, because the prod reservations would sit idle most of the time.

What is the practical difference between compressible and non-compressible resources?

  1. Compressible ones such as CPU can be throttled to reclaim them; non-compressible ones such as memory generally require killing the task
  2. Compressible ones are stored in ZIP files
  3. Non-compressible ones are free
  4. There is no difference
Taking CPU away just slows a task. Taking memory away would lose its data, so the Borglet terminates tasks from lowest to highest priority instead.

Which problem with Borg's design did Kubernetes address by giving every pod its own IP address?

  1. Slow scheduling
  2. Too few priority bands
  3. All tasks on a machine shared one IP address and port space, so ports had to be scheduled as a resource
  4. Cells that were too small
Both papers describe port management as a source of complexity: DNS, URLs and tooling all had to cope with IP:port pairs. A per-pod IP aligns network identity with application identity.
