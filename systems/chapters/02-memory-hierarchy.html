The Memory Hierarchy

A CPU can run several billion instructions a second, but fetching one number from main memory takes as long as hundreds of them. Computers hide this gap with a ladder of ever-bigger, ever-slower memories, and whether your program is fast or slow depends largely on how well it climbs that ladder. This is the most reusable idea in the course: it explains cache-friendly code, databases, CDNs and half of system design.

Why this matters

Two programs can do the same amount of "work" and differ by 10x in speed, just because one touches memory in a friendly order. Beyond single machines, the identical pattern appears everywhere: a small fast copy of something big and slow. CPU caches, the operating system's page cache, a database's buffer pool, Redis in front of a database, a CDN in front of an origin server: it's one idea at five scales. Once you see it, you'll ask the right question in every design discussion: where is the fast copy, and what happens when it's not there?

The gap between the CPU and memory

In chapter 1 a core ran at around 3 GHz: one cycle every 0.33 ns. Main memory (RAM, random-access memory) takes roughly 100 ns to deliver a value. That's about 300 cycles.

If the CPU simply waited for each load, it would spend almost all of its time idle. Why can't RAM just be faster? Partly physics: a signal needs time to travel to the memory chips and back, and the bigger the memory, the farther away part of it is. Partly cost: the memory cells that are fast enough to keep up with a CPU (SRAM, which needs about six transistors per bit) are large and expensive, while the cells in RAM (DRAM, one transistor and one tiny capacitor per bit) are small and cheap but slower. You can have a little fast memory or a lot of slow memory. You cannot have a lot of fast memory.

So computers use both, arranged as a hierarchy: a small amount of very fast memory close to the CPU, a bit more of somewhat slower memory behind it, and so on down to huge, slow storage. The trick is to arrange that most accesses are served from the fast levels. It works because programs are not random: they keep coming back to the same things, and to things nearby. More on that shortly.

Analogy: the kitchen, from hands to delivery truck

Things in the cook's hands (registers) can be used instantly. The cutting board (L1 cache) holds what's needed for this dish. The counter (L2 and L3) holds ingredients for the next few dishes. The pantry down the hall (RAM) has everything for today, but every trip takes a while. The walk-in freezer (SSD) is further still, the warehouse across town (a hard disk) further than that, and a delivery truck from another city (the network) takes a day. A good cook plans so that nearly everything needed is already within arm's reach.

Where it breaks down: a cook chooses what goes on the board. In a CPU the hardware decides by itself, using simple rules, with no knowledge of your recipe. You influence it only through how you lay out and visit your data.

Cache lines: memory moves in blocks

A subtle but important fact: when the CPU reads one byte that isn't in the cache, it doesn't fetch just that byte. It fetches a whole fixed-size block called a cache line. On most x86 and many ARM chips a line is 64 bytes; Apple's M-series chips use 128-byte lines. Memory is conceptually cut into line-sized pieces, and a line is always loaded and evicted as a unit.

Analogy: the egg carton

You can't buy one egg from the supermarket warehouse; the smallest unit they move is a carton. If your recipe uses six eggs, one trip brings all you need. If it uses one egg from each of six different cartons, you make six trips and carry five unused eggs home each time.

Where it breaks down: in a real shop you choose which carton to buy. Here the cutting points are fixed by address, so you can't pick which neighbours come along. You only control which data you place next to which.

Hits, misses and locality

When the CPU wants an address, the hardware first checks whether that line is in L1. If it is, that's a cache hit: the data arrives in about a nanosecond. If not, it's a cache miss: the hardware asks L2, then L3, then RAM, and the CPU may wait hundreds of cycles. The line is then copied into the cache, usually pushing out some older line (an eviction); a common rule is to evict the least recently used (LRU) line, on the guess that something untouched for a while is unlikely to be needed soon. (Real hardware uses cheap approximations of LRU.)

The fraction of accesses that hit is the hit rate, and it matters more than it looks. The average cost of an access is hit rate × hit time + miss rate × miss time. With a 1 ns hit and a 100 ns miss:

  • 99% hits: 0.99 × 1 + 0.01 × 100 ≈ 2 ns on average.
  • 90% hits: 0.90 × 1 + 0.10 × 100 ≈ 11 ns, more than five times slower.
  • 50% hits: ≈ 50 ns.

Going from 99% to 90% looks like a small drop. It makes everything over five times slower. Misses are so expensive that even a few of them dominate.

Caches pay off because real programs show locality of reference, in two flavours:

Temporal locality

If you used something recently, you'll probably use it again soon. A loop counter, a hot variable, a frequently called function. The line stays in the cache, so the next use is a hit.

Spatial locality

If you used an address, you'll probably use its neighbours next. Walking through an array, reading the fields of one object. Cache lines make this free: the neighbours arrived with the first access.

Hardware helps further with a prefetcher: circuitry that notices a regular access pattern, such as addresses going up by 64 bytes each time, and fetches the next lines before the program asks. Prefetching makes sequential scans spectacularly fast, and it can do nothing for access patterns it can't predict, such as following pointers wherever they happen to lead.

Things to try. Each experiment below uses 64 accesses (press Run 64 accesses, or watch it play):

  1. Sequential, 4 lines. Every fourth access misses, because each miss brings in a whole line of four cells and the next three are free. That's 48 hits out of 64, a 75% hit rate. The total is 1,648 ns.
  2. Stride by a line. Now each access lands in a new line, so every single access misses, whatever the cache size on offer (up to 8 lines). 0% hits, 6,400 ns: nearly four times slower, for the same number of accesses. The cache is full of useless neighbours.
  3. Repeat a small set, 4 lines. The program keeps returning to the same three lines. After three cold misses everything hits (61 of 64, about 95%). This is temporal locality.
  4. Same pattern, 2 lines. The working set (3 lines) no longer fits in the cache (2 lines), so lines keep being evicted just before they're needed again. The hit rate collapses to about 23%. This is called thrashing, and it is why "does my working set fit?" is a key question.
  5. Random, with 2, 4 and 8 lines. The widget's built-in random sequence gives hit rates of about 17%, 22% and 45%: in the neighbourhood of the fraction of memory the cache can hold (2, 4 or 8 of the 16 lines, i.e. 12%, 25% and 50%). With no pattern to exploit, a cache just holds a lottery ticket proportional to its size. (A different random sequence would give slightly different numbers.)

Common misconception

"Random-access memory means every address costs the same." RAM is "random access" only in the sense that you can jump to any address directly, unlike a tape. It is not uniformly fast in practice: the same address costs a nanosecond or a hundred depending on whether it's in the cache. The simulator just showed two sequences of exactly 64 reads whose costs differ by a factor of four.

Why arrays beat linked lists for scanning

The cache line idea has a famous consequence. An array stores its elements side by side in one block of memory. A linked list stores each element in its own small allocation, with a pointer to the next one, and those allocations can be anywhere. Both take time proportional to the number of elements to scan, but the machine treats them very differently.

With the array, one miss brings in the next several elements, and the prefetcher sees the pattern and loads ahead. With the linked list, each node is probably in a different cache line, and worse, the CPU can't even begin loading node 3 until node 2 has arrived, because node 2 contains node 3's address. This pointer chasing serialises the misses. In compiled languages such as C, C++ or Rust, scanning a big array is commonly several times to an order of magnitude faster than scanning a linked list of the same size, even though both are "O(n)". Big-O notation counts steps; it says nothing about how long each step waits.

An honest note about Python

A Python list is an array of pointers to objects, and each integer is its own small object on the heap (chapter 3). So even a "contiguous" Python list of numbers involves pointer-chasing, and the interpreter overhead from chapter 1 adds dozens of instructions to each step. Often those costs swamp the cache effect, which is why you can't reliably see these differences by timing plain Python loops. To get the compact layout you'd use array.array, bytes/bytearray, or NumPy. The principle still holds for any code that eventually runs as machine code, which includes databases, the OS and the libraries Python calls.

Row-major or column-major?

Here is the same idea with a grid. Memory is one-dimensional, so a 2D table has to be flattened. The common choice, called row-major order, stores row 0, then row 1, and so on (C and NumPy do this by default; Fortran and MATLAB store column by column). The figure below shows a 4×4 grid; the numbers are positions in memory.

Summing every cell of a table gives the same answer in either order, but the row-by-row walk visits memory in order (spatial locality: every cache line is fully used), while the column-by-column walk skips a whole row each step (each access lands in a different line, using one value out of 64 bytes). On large tables that doesn't fit in the cache, this is commonly the difference between "fast" and "several times slower".

Experiment: time it yourself

Save this as locality.py and run it with python3 locality.py. It uses only the standard library. The grid is 8,192 × 8,192 bytes (64 MiB), bigger than the CPU caches. Each function adds up all the bytes, one row at a time or one column at a time. The slicing and sum() calls run as compiled C code inside Python, so most of the interpreter overhead is out of the way:

import time

N = 8192                        # a grid of N x N bytes = 64 MiB
data = bytearray(N * N)         # stored row after row (row-major)

def by_rows():
    total = 0
    for r in range(N):
        total += sum(data[r * N:(r + 1) * N])   # one row: N contiguous bytes
    return total

def by_columns():
    total = 0
    for c in range(N):
        total += sum(data[c::N])                # one column: every N-th byte
    return total

for fn in (by_rows, by_columns):
    start = time.perf_counter()
    fn()
    print(f"{fn.__name__:11s} {time.perf_counter() - start:.2f} s")
$ python3 locality.py
by_rows     0.11 s
by_columns  0.29 s

On the machine used to write this chapter, walking down columns took about 2.5 to 3 times as long, for exactly the same sum. Your ratio will depend on your CPU's cache sizes and prefetcher; big modern chips with large caches can show a smaller gap than older ones. (Be fair to the experiment: the row slice is one plain block copy, while the column slice gathers bytes one at a time, so a little of the gap is the copying method rather than the cache. The main ingredient is still the memory pattern.)

Now do the "obvious" version in plain Python, which indexes element by element:

M = 3000
grid = bytearray(M * M)

def sum_rows():
    t = 0
    for r in range(M):
        for c in range(M):
            t += grid[r * M + c]      # neighbours in memory
    return t

def sum_cols():
    t = 0
    for c in range(M):
        for r in range(M):
            t += grid[r * M + c]      # a whole row apart each time
    return t

Timing these two on a laptop gave similar times, within roughly 1.3 times of each other (for example 0.32 s and 0.43 s), compared with the 2 to 3 times gap above. Each iteration costs dozens of instructions of interpreter work (chapter 1), and the memory access is a tiny share of that, so the effect is invisible. The cache effect didn't go away. It's just buried. The same two loops in C would show a clear gap on a large table, and the lesson transfers to every system component that does run as compiled code: databases, kernels, game engines, the libraries behind NumPy.

Caching as a universal principle

Now step back. The CPU cache is one instance of a pattern so common that you could almost define systems engineering as the management of it:

Keep a small, fast copy of the data you are likely to need again, in front of a big, slow source of truth.

You've met several of these already. The DNS resolver caches answers so it needn't ask the root servers. Browsers and CDNs keep copies of web pages and images close to users, which turns a 100 ms trip across an ocean into a few milliseconds. In upcoming chapters: the operating system keeps recently used file contents in RAM, the page cache (chapters 8 and 9), and uses RAM as a cache for the disk even for program memory (chapter 6). Databases run their own buffer pool of hot pages. And at the application level, systems like Redis keep results in memory so the database isn't asked twice.

Whatever the scale, every cache must answer the same four questions:

  1. What do we keep? Everything doesn't fit. Usually the most recently or most frequently used, so you need an eviction rule (LRU, as in the simulator, is the most common).
  2. How do we find it fast? A cache that takes as long to search as the original gains nothing. (Hash tables are the usual tool.)
  3. What if the original changes? The copy is now stale and wrong. Hardware uses protocols to keep CPU caches consistent; software caches use expiry times (TTLs), explicit invalidation, or just tolerate staleness. Deciding when a cached answer is no longer true is one of the genuinely hard problems in system design. We'll meet it again in replication and consistency.
  4. What happens on a miss? Someone must go to the original, wait, and fill the cache. If a cache that was serving 99% of traffic suddenly disappears (a restart, a deploy), a "cold" cache means the origin suddenly receives all the traffic at once. This cold start (or cache stampede) has taken down real systems.

Notice that the earlier arithmetic about hit rates applies unchanged: if a database takes 10 ms and a cache 1 ms, then a 99% hit rate gives a 1.1 ms average, and 90% gives about 1.9 ms. The average is easy to talk about, but the experience of users is in the tail (networking ch. 13): the 1% who miss still wait ten times as long.

Analogy: the library, the desk and the textbook shelf

Your desk holds a few books you're using now. The shelf in your room holds the ones you may need this term. The city library has far more, but it takes an afternoon. You keep the right books on the desk by remembering what you used recently and what's coming up, and you accept that the desk copy might be an older edition than the library's.

Where it breaks down: a library copy doesn't change when you scribble in the one on your desk. In computer caches, writes need rules (write the change through to the original at once, or write it later and risk losing it in a crash). That is the starting point for the durability story in chapter 9.

Designing for the hierarchy

You rarely tune cache lines by hand, but a few habits follow directly from this chapter:

  • Keep data that's used together next to each other. A compact array of records beats scattered objects joined by pointers.
  • Visit data in the order it's stored. Scan rows, not columns; process a file front to back. Storage systems love sequential access for the same reason CPUs do (an SSD or disk reading sequentially is far faster than random reads).
  • Mind the working set. If the data you touch repeatedly fits in a cache, you run at cache speed. If it's just a bit too big, performance can fall off a cliff, as the 2-line simulator showed.
  • Do all the work on a piece of data while it's hot. One pass that does three things is usually faster than three passes that each fetch everything again.
  • Treat every layer as a cache with a miss path. When you add one, ask what the miss costs, how it goes stale, and what happens when it's empty.

Common misconception

"If it's O(n), it's as fast as it can be, and constant factors don't matter." Constant factors here are factors of 10 to 100. An algorithm with a worse Big-O but good locality can beat one with a better Big-O and scattered access, for datasets of the sizes real systems handle. Big-O tells you how cost grows; the memory hierarchy tells you the price of each step.

Go deeper: false sharing, when cores fight over a line optional

Cache lines also matter when several cores are involved. Each core has its own L1 cache, so when two cores both hold the same line, the hardware must keep the copies consistent. If core A writes to a line, core B's copy is invalidated and B must fetch it again before it can read or write. This coordination, handled by a cache coherence protocol, is invisible to your code but not free.

False sharing is what happens when two threads write to different variables that merely sit on the same 64-byte line. Logically they're independent, but physically every write by one core steals the line from the other, and the line ping-pongs between the cores. The program is correct but slows down, sometimes dramatically, as you add threads: the opposite of what you wanted. A typical case is an array of per-thread counters stored next to each other; the fix is to pad each counter so it lives alone on its own line (or to keep per-thread data in separate allocations).

You'll mostly meet this in low-level code written in C, C++, Rust or Java, and it's very hard to see in Python, where the interpreter's own overhead and the global interpreter lock (chapter 10) dominate. But it's a good example of the principle that sharing is never free: even "unshared" data can interact through the hardware. We'll return to cores sharing state in chapters 10 to 12.

Distributed systems connection

Caching is the first tool anyone reaches for in a distributed system, and it comes with the same four questions at larger scale: what to keep, how to find it, when it goes stale, and what happens on a miss. The latency ladder explains why: a round trip to another machine in the same datacenter costs about 5,000 times more than a RAM access, so systems cache across the network boundary to avoid crossing it (CDNs, networking ch. 14). Techniques such as consistent hashing (networking ch. 19) exist partly so that the same key keeps landing on the same node and that node's cache stays warm. Redis, a hugely popular in-memory cache and store, is the subject of a case study. And the cold-start problem is why careful teams "warm up" new servers before sending them traffic.

Key takeaways

  • CPUs are far faster than RAM, so computers use a ladder of memories: registers, L1/L2/L3 caches, RAM, SSD, disk, network. Each rung is much bigger and much slower, often 10x to 100x per step.
  • If an L1 hit took one second, a RAM access would take under two minutes, an SSD read about a day, a disk seek about four months and an ocean-crossing network call about three years.
  • Memory moves in cache lines (64 bytes on most chips, 128 on Apple M-series). Reading one byte brings in its neighbours for free.
  • Caches work because of locality: temporal (reuse soon) and spatial (use neighbours). Data laid out contiguously and visited in order is fast; pointer-chasing and big jumps are slow.
  • Average cost is dominated by the miss rate: going from 99% to 90% hits makes memory access over 5x slower.
  • Caching is a universal pattern: CPU caches, the OS page cache, database buffer pools, Redis and CDNs all keep a small fast copy of a big slow source. Always ask what a miss costs, how the copy goes stale, and what happens when the cache is cold.
  • In pure Python, interpreter overhead hides much of the effect, but the principle governs everything that runs as machine code underneath.

Check your understanding

Roughly how much slower is a RAM access than an L1 cache hit?

  1. About 2 times
  2. About 100 times
  3. About 10,000 times
  4. About 1,000,000 times
About 1 ns for L1 versus about 100 ns for RAM. The million-fold figure is closer to the gap between L1 and a trip across the ocean, or a hard-disk seek compared with a register.

A program reads one byte from RAM and the byte isn't in any cache. What does the hardware load?

  1. Just that byte
  2. The whole program
  3. One 4 KiB page
  4. The entire cache line containing it (typically 64 bytes)
The cache line is the unit of transfer. That is what makes sequential access cheap: the following bytes were fetched with the first. (4 KiB pages matter too, but for virtual memory in chapter 6.)

Why is scanning an array usually much faster than scanning a linked list of the same length in a compiled language?

  1. Array elements share cache lines and the prefetcher can load ahead, while list nodes are scattered and each hop must wait for the previous node
  2. Arrays use fewer comparisons, so they have a better Big-O
  3. Linked lists are stored on disk
  4. Arrays are always kept in registers
Both scans do O(n) steps. The difference is the cost per step: contiguous data gives mostly cache hits, pointer chasing gives mostly misses that can't overlap.

In the cache simulator, "stride by a line" misses every time, even with a bigger cache. Why?

  1. The cache is broken for stride patterns
  2. The random number generator is unlucky
  3. Each access lands in a different line, so no access ever finds its line already loaded, and the 16 lines cycle through before any repeats
  4. Strided accesses can't be cached by definition
Spatial locality is what saves sequential access, and striding by a full line throws it away. With 16 lines visited in rotation, even an 8-line LRU cache has evicted each one before it comes round again.

A cache has a 1 ms hit time, the database behind it takes 10 ms, and the hit rate drops from 99% to 90%. Roughly what happens to the average response time?

  1. It barely changes, since 90% is still a high hit rate
  2. It rises from about 1.1 ms to about 1.9 ms, nearly doubling
  3. It falls, because the database is faster
  4. It becomes 10 ms for every request
0.99×1 + 0.01×10 ≈ 1.1 ms, and 0.90×1 + 0.10×10 = 1.9 ms. Misses are expensive enough that a small change in hit rate moves the average a lot, and the tail moves even more.

A production cache serving 99% of reads is restarted and comes back empty. What is the main risk?

  1. The data is permanently lost
  2. The cache will refuse to fill again
  3. CPU caches will become incoherent
  4. Nearly every request misses at once, flooding the slower origin (the database) that normally sees only 1% of traffic
A cache holds copies, so nothing is lost. The danger is the sudden 100x increase in load on whatever sits behind it. That's why caches are warmed up gradually, and why origin servers are protected with rate limits and request coalescing.
