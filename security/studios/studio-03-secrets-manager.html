Design a Secrets Manager

Every service needs passwords, API keys and certificates, and every one of those is a skeleton key if it leaks. A secrets manager is the system that holds them, hands them out to the right workload for the right time, and remembers who asked. Because it holds everything, it is also the most valuable thing to attack. This studio designs a Vault-style system and asks, at each step, what an attacker gets when something fails.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point. Numbers are assumptions to reason with. The design follows the ideas of HashiCorp Vault, but this is a general design exercise, not a description of any product's exact behaviour.

📐 The brief

You are building Keystone, an internal secrets manager for a company with about 3,000 services across two clouds and a Kubernetes platform. Requirements:

  • store static secrets (API keys, certificates, config) encrypted at rest under a master key that no single person holds;
  • after a restart the system starts sealed and needs several people to unseal it;
  • workloads authenticate with their platform identity (cloud workload identity, Kubernetes service accounts), not with a secret that has to be delivered first;
  • hand out dynamic database credentials that expire, with leases;
  • support rotation of static secrets and of the keys themselves;
  • be highly available (a three- or five-node cluster) with strong consistency;
  • keep a complete audit log, and offer break-glass access when normal paths are down;
  • serve about 15,000 secret reads per second at peak without becoming an outage source.

Draw on keys, secrets and PKI (envelope encryption, KMS and HSM), applied cryptography, cloud and container security and detection and response. Compare with the real incident in Storm-0558, where one stolen signing key had a very large blast radius.

1What are we defending, and who is the attacker?

List the assets (not only the secrets), the attackers and the first design constraints they imply. What is the one property the system must keep even if the storage backend is fully stolen?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Assets: the secrets themselves; the keys that protect them; the identity-verification rules that decide who gets what (policy); the audit log; and the system's availability, since if it is down every service that needs a secret at startup is down.
  • Attackers: a compromised workload (the most common: one service is breached); a stolen backup or storage snapshot; a malicious or phished administrator; an insider with cloud console access; and a network attacker between a workload and the manager.
  • The invariant: stealing the storage backend alone yields nothing readable. The data on disk is ciphertext, and the key that decrypts it is never written to the same place. That is why there is a seal (stage 3).
  • Secondary invariants: a workload can read only the secrets its identity is entitled to; every access is logged before it is served; no human has standing access to production secrets; and a compromised workload's access ends quickly because its credentials are short-lived.
  • Non-goals: protecting a secret from the workload that legitimately holds it (once your app has a database password in memory, an attacker with code execution in the app has it too), which is exactly why short-lived credentials matter (stage 5).

2Storage and the key hierarchy

How are secrets encrypted at rest? Design the key hierarchy so that rotation is cheap and so that the key which unlocks everything is as hard to obtain as possible.

Compare with a worked answer

Use envelope encryption (chapter 4) with three levels:

  • Why three levels and not one key. The root key protects one small object, the keyring key, so rotating the root key means re-wrapping a few hundred bytes, not re-encrypting every secret. The keyring key can be rotated too: new writes use the new version and old versions stay readable until a background job rewraps (a version number is stored with each ciphertext).
  • Per-secret data keys (or at least per-path) bound the damage of one key being exposed and let you delete a secret cryptographically.
  • Use authenticated encryption (AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305) and bind the secret's path as associated data. Then an attacker with write access to storage cannot move secret A's ciphertext into path B and have it decrypt (chapter 3). Never reuse a nonce; generate keys and nonces with a CSPRNG.
  • Where does the root key come from? Two options: split it with Shamir's scheme and make humans reassemble it (stage 3), or let a cloud KMS or HSM wrap it ("auto-unseal"), which trades the people problem for dependence on the KMS and its IAM policy. Many systems do the second day to day and keep Shamir shares as recovery keys.
  • Backups contain the same ciphertext and are therefore safe to store widely, but they are useless without the root key, so the unseal shares or KMS policy are part of your disaster-recovery plan, tested (reliability chapter 18).

3Sealing, unsealing and Shamir secret sharing

After a restart the server holds ciphertext and no key. You want any 3 of 5 trusted people to be able to unseal, but no 2 of them, and no single administrator. How can a secret be split so that exactly that is true? Try to find the mathematical trick before reading on.

Compare with a worked answer

Sealed means the process is running but cannot decrypt anything: the root key is not in memory. Unsealing rebuilds it. Splitting a key into pieces is easy: split into 5 parts and require all 5. Requiring only 3 of 5 without leaking information needs a better idea, Shamir's secret sharing (Adi Shamir, 1979).

The intuition. Two points determine a line. Three points determine a parabola. In general, k points determine a unique polynomial of degree k − 1, and fewer than k points leave it completely undetermined. So:

  1. To share a secret s among n people with threshold k, build a random polynomial of degree k − 1 whose constant term (its value at x = 0) is s: pick the other k − 1 coefficients at random.
  2. Give person i the point (i, f(i)).
  3. Any k people can interpolate the polynomial (Lagrange interpolation) and read off f(0) = s.
  4. With k − 1 points, there is exactly one polynomial through them for every possible value of the secret, so every secret is equally likely and the shares reveal nothing. This is information-theoretic security, not computational hardness.

The arithmetic is done modulo a prime p (a finite field), so that shares are the same size as the secret and the "equally likely" argument is exact. Real systems work byte by byte in GF(28); a large prime field is easier to read in a demo.

import random

P = 2**61 - 1            # a prime; all arithmetic is mod P

def split(secret, k, n, rng=random.SystemRandom()):
    """Hide `secret` as f(0) of a random degree k-1 polynomial; share i is (i, f(i))."""
    coeffs = [secret] + [rng.randrange(P) for _ in range(k - 1)]
    f = lambda x: sum(c * pow(x, i, P) for i, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % P
    return [(x, f(x)) for x in range(1, n + 1)]

def combine(shares):
    """Lagrange interpolation at x = 0."""
    total = 0
    for i, (xi, yi) in enumerate(shares):
        num, den = 1, 1
        for j, (xj, _) in enumerate(shares):
            if i != j:
                num = num * (-xj) % P
                den = den * (xi - xj) % P
        total = (total + yi * num * pow(den, -1, P)) % P
    return total

secret = 123456789012345
shares = split(secret, k=3, n=5)
print("shares:", len(shares))
print("any 3 recover it:", combine(shares[:3]) == secret, combine([shares[0], shares[2], shares[4]]) == secret)
print("2 shares give   :", combine(shares[:2]) == secret, "(a wrong value, and every secret is equally likely)")

Try it: change k to 2, then recover from shares 2 and 4 to see that any two points work. Print two shares of a degree-2 polynomial and ask yourself how you would find the third coefficient: you cannot.

Educational only

This demo uses a 61-bit secret and Python's ordinary integers, with no authentication of shares. Real implementations work on full-size keys, verify the reassembled key, and run in hardened code. Use a vetted implementation (the one inside your secrets manager), never your own.

Operational design around it:

  • Ceremony. Initialisation generates the root key in memory, splits it (say 3 of 5), encrypts each share to a different custodian's public key (or hands it over once, displayed and discarded), and records who got which. Custodians come from different teams and locations.
  • Unseal progress is memory-only. Each custodian submits a share; the server accumulates them in RAM; at the threshold it reconstructs the key, decrypts the keyring key, and wipes the shares. Submitted shares are never logged or stored.
  • Threshold choice. Too low and a few colluding or phished custodians suffice; too high and a holiday week or a lost laptop means you cannot restart. 3 of 5 tolerates two people being unreachable and needs three to collude.
  • Auto-unseal with a cloud KMS removes the on-call burden (a node reboot at 03:00 recovers alone), at the price of making the KMS key policy and its audit trail part of your root of trust. Keep the Shamir recovery shares for when the KMS is unavailable or untrusted, and still seal manually when you suspect compromise: sealing is the emergency stop.

4How a workload proves who it is

A new pod starts. It needs a database password. It has no password for the secrets manager (that would be the secret-zero problem). What does it present, how does the manager check it, and what policy does it then receive?

Compare with a worked answer

Solve secret zero by letting the platform vouch for the workload. The platform already knows which workload it started and can issue a short-lived, verifiable identity document (chapter 14).

  • Bind to claims, not to existence. An auth role says "the service account payments-api in namespace prod-payments of cluster eu-1 gets policy payments-db". A role that accepts "any service account in the cluster" turns every compromised pod into a secrets client (confused deputy, chapter 8). Check the token's audience so a token issued for another purpose cannot be replayed here.
  • Result: a Keystone token with a short TTL (an hour), bound to the policies of that role, renewable up to a maximum, and revocable (revoking a token revokes the leases created under it). Use it only to talk to Keystone.
  • Policy is path-based and deny-by-default: path "db/creds/payments-ro" { capabilities = ["read"] }. Policies live in version control, are reviewed like code, and are tested in CI.
  • Trust anchors are now your cluster and cloud. If an attacker controls the cluster's token issuer, they control who Keystone believes. Say so, and monitor those trust relationships.
  • Agent options. Applications can call Keystone themselves, or a sidecar or agent (stage 10) does it for them, so the app code never needs to know about auth.

5Dynamic database credentials and leases

A static database password shared by 40 pods is never rotated and cannot be traced to a pod. What is better? Design what happens when a pod asks for credentials, how they expire, what happens if the pod dies, and what can go wrong with timing.

Compare with a worked answer

Generate a unique, short-lived database account for each request. Keystone holds one privileged connection to the database (a "root" configuration the app never sees), and for each request it executes CREATE ROLE ... WITH PASSWORD ... VALID UNTIL ... plus the grants named by the role template, then returns the username and password with a lease.

  • Lease. A lease is a time-limited right with an id, a TTL (say 1 hour) and a maximum TTL (24 hours). The client renews before expiry; when the lease ends or is revoked, Keystone runs the revocation statement (DROP ROLE) and terminates the account's sessions. This is the same idea as the leases of distributed chapter 4: authority that expires by itself, so a crashed or partitioned holder cannot keep it forever.
  • What this buys. A leaked credential is valid for at most its remaining TTL; every database session maps to one pod and one request in the audit log; revoking a compromised workload means revoking its token (and so every lease under it); rotation is continuous and no one needs to coordinate a change window.
  • Timing hazards, in the style of that chapter. (1) The lease clock is Keystone's. A pod whose clock is slow, or that is paused (garbage-collection pause, VM freeze) can keep using a credential that has been revoked; the database enforcing VALID UNTIL and the revocation DROP ROLE is what actually stops it, so the database is the enforcement point and not the client's good behaviour. (2) Renewal must happen well before expiry (at about 2/3 of the TTL); a renewal that fails should fall back to requesting new credentials, and the app should drain connections made under the old ones. (3) A new account must be usable almost at once, but replication lag to read replicas can make it unknown there for a moment; retry once.
  • Load. Creating a role per request does not scale to 15,000 per second, and databases limit roles. Issue per pod start (about 3,000 services × maybe 10 pods, re-issued hourly, is roughly 30,000 / 3,600 ≈ 8 creations per second), not per query. Pool connections inside the pod. This number is an assumption; the point is to size by credentials issued, not by reads.
  • Same pattern elsewhere: short-lived cloud credentials, SSH certificates signed by a CA with a 10-minute validity, and TLS certificates issued on demand all follow it (chapter 4).
  • Failure of Keystone. Existing credentials keep working until their TTL ends. That makes the TTL a statement about how long you can tolerate Keystone being down versus how long a leak lasts. One hour with a 24 h maximum is a typical compromise; stage 7 reduces the dependence.

6Rotation of static secrets and keys

Some secrets cannot be dynamic: a third-party API key, a signing key. How do you rotate them without downtime and without a hero? What about rotating the encryption keys inside Keystone itself?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Two-phase rotation. The system on the other side must accept two secrets at once for a while: (1) create the new secret and register it as valid, (2) publish it as the current version in Keystone and let consumers fetch it over the next cycle, (3) wait for the maximum cache lifetime plus a margin, confirm from audit logs that no one still uses the old version, (4) revoke the old. The "two valid at once" overlap is what makes it free of downtime; for a key that only allows one active value, use a versioned name and switch consumers explicitly.
  • Versioned secrets. Each write creates a new version; reads default to the latest, and a consumer can pin one. Keep a limited history for rollback and delete old versions on a schedule.
  • Automate. A rotation job per secret type with an owner, a schedule (30 to 90 days for static API keys, shorter if cheap) and an alert if it fails. The most useful rotation is the one done under pressure, so rehearse it: a leaked key during an incident should be a routine operation (reliability chapter 16).
  • Signing keys need a longer overlap, because verifiers cache public keys: publish the new public key first, start signing with it later, retire the old one after the longest-lived signature has expired. Storm-0558 is a reminder of what an old, over-trusted signing key can do (case study).
  • Keyring key rotation is the cheap inner rotation of stage 2: add a new version, write with it, rewrap lazily. Root key rotation (a "rekey") means new shares for new custodians, for example after someone leaves; it re-wraps the keyring key only. Plan both and test them.
  • Detecting use of an old secret is as important as the rotation. Log which secret version each read served; a read of version N−2 after the cutover is a stale consumer or an attacker.

7High availability with a consensus-backed store

One Keystone node is a single point of failure for 3,000 services. Design the cluster: where is state kept, who handles writes, what happens on a node failure or a partition, and what do you do about the seal?

Compare with a worked answer

Secrets must be consistent: a revoked token must not come back, and a lease must not be issued twice. That calls for a replicated, linearizable store, which in practice means a consensus protocol, as in Raft in depth.

  • Topology. Five nodes (tolerates two failures) or three (one), spread across three availability zones so an AZ loss leaves a majority. A leader handles writes; the log is replicated to a majority before a write is acknowledged. Writes include: issuing a lease, revoking, writing a secret, and the audit entry for each.
  • Reads. Follower nodes can answer reads that tolerate slight staleness (for example a static secret fetched with a cache) by forwarding or by serving from the replicated state; operations that must be fresh (token revocation checks, lease renewals) go through the leader or use a read-index check. The trade-off is the usual one between latency and linearizability.
  • Failure. If the leader dies, the others elect a new one within a few seconds (election timeout in the hundreds of milliseconds to seconds), during which writes pause but cached reads continue. If a minority is partitioned, it cannot commit writes; the majority carries on. Only if a majority is lost does the whole service stop writing: it fails closed, which is what we want from a secrets system.
  • Seal state is per node. Each node must be unsealed. With auto-unseal, nodes join and recover alone. With Shamir shares, a node restart needs a quorum of custodians again, a strong reason to run auto-unseal and keep Shamir for recovery. Never let the unsealed state of all nodes depend on one custodian.
  • Disaster recovery. Within-region HA does not protect against losing the region, a bad write that replicates everywhere, or an operator error. Take regular snapshots of the replicated state (they are ciphertext) to another account, and keep a second cluster (warm standby) in another region replicating asynchronously, accepting a small data-loss window (RPO) you write down. Test a restore, including the unseal procedure (reliability chapter 18).
  • Bootstrap circularity. Keystone stores the credentials used by the platform that runs Keystone. Break the loop explicitly: the consensus nodes' own storage credentials come from the cloud's identity, not from Keystone itself, and there is a documented offline procedure for a cold start.
  • Upgrades. Rolling, one node at a time, followers first, the leader last, each waiting until the replica is caught up and healthy. Keep the version-skew window short.

8Audit: complete, tamper-resistant, and safe to read

Auditors will ask who read the production database password last Tuesday. What does every audit record contain, where is it written, what happens when the audit sink is down, and how do you avoid putting secrets in the audit log?

Compare with a worked answer
  • One record per request, written before the response is returned: timestamp, authenticated identity and the auth method and claims it was derived from, token id (not the token), operation, path, policy that allowed or denied it, source IP, request id, and the secret version or lease id involved. Denied requests are the most valuable records.
  • Never write secret values. Fields that could contain secrets are replaced by a keyed hash (HMAC with an audit-specific key), so equal values can be correlated without being readable. Review the log schema as carefully as the secret schema (reliability chapter 5).
  • Fail closed. If the manager cannot write an audit record to at least one configured sink, it refuses the request. A secrets system that serves without logging has made its attackers invisible. Run at least two sinks (a local file and a network sink) so one outage does not become a global outage, and alert on sink failure.
  • Ship it off the box. The audit stream goes to storage that Keystone's operators cannot edit (append-only, separate account) and to the SIEM, so a compromised administrator cannot erase their tracks. Consider a hash chain over records so deletion is detectable (chapter 18).
  • Detections worth writing. A workload reading a path it has never read; bursts of denied requests; reads outside the usual hours; use of the root token or a break-glass credential; a new auth role or policy change; a sealed-state transition; and a lease count spike.
  • Volume (assumed). 15,000 reads per second at peak, perhaps 3,000 per second on average × 500 bytes ≈ 1.5 MB per second ≈ 130 GB a day raw. That is a reason for caching agents (stage 10), which cut Keystone-visible reads, with the agents' own audit records forwarded in batches.

9Break-glass access

It is 3 a.m., the identity provider is down, the platform's auth path is broken and production secrets are needed to restore service. Design an emergency route that works then and is hard to abuse otherwise.

Compare with a worked answer

Break-glass is a controlled exception: it must work when other things fail, and it must be loud.

  • Eliminate standing root. The initial "root token" created at initialisation is revoked as soon as proper auth is configured. Day to day, nobody holds a token that can do everything.
  • Generate on demand with a quorum. Emergency root-level access requires the same kind of threshold as unseal: for example 3 of 5 recovery-share holders each submit their share to start a generate-root operation that creates a temporary, tightly time-boxed credential (30 minutes, single use, audited). Because the shares are already offline and spread across people, no one person can do it alone.
  • Independent of the normal path. It must not need the failed IdP or the platform you are trying to fix. Local, pre-registered break-glass identities with hardware security keys can serve as a second route for small, named operations.
  • Loud by design. Starting the procedure pages the security on-call and the channel, writes a prominent audit record and starts a timer. After use: rotate every secret that was read, because someone had a human-visible copy, and review the audit trail within 24 hours. A break-glass event without a written incident review is a finding.
  • Practice it. Run a quarterly drill in staging. A procedure that has never been run is the procedure that fails at 3 a.m. (reliability chapter 16).
  • What it is not. It is not a way around review for convenience, and a developer being impatient is not an emergency. Track the count; a rising number means the normal path is too hard.

10Blast radius and performance

Consider three compromises: one workload, one Keystone node's memory, and a Keystone administrator. What does each attacker get? Then handle 15,000 reads a second without making Keystone a bottleneck or a single point of failure.

Segment the blast radius. Separate namespaces per business unit or environment (production and non-production should be separate clusters, with separate roots), and consider a dedicated cluster for the highest-value material such as code-signing and payment keys. A single flat secret space is the Storm-0558 pattern: one key, a very large reach (case study).

Performance. The 15,000 reads per second are mostly the same few hundred secrets re-read by many pods. Placing Keystone in the hot path of every request would be wrong.

  • Read at startup and on rotation, not per request. An application should fetch its secrets at start and refresh on a timer, keeping them in memory.
  • Caching agents. A per-node agent or sidecar authenticates once, caches secrets and leases for their TTL, renews leases, writes the secret to a memory-backed file or a local socket for the app, and re-fetches after rotation. The agent absorbs fan-out: 3,000 services × 10 pods each = 30,000 clients, but with one agent per node (say 500 nodes) Keystone sees about 500 clients refreshing every few minutes: if each agent caches about 100 secrets and refreshes each every 5 minutes, that is 500 × 100 / 300 s ≈ 170 requests per second (an assumption) rather than 15,000.
  • The cost of caching. A revoked secret stays usable on cached copies for up to the refresh interval; this is the same staleness bound as everywhere else in the design, and it should be written next to the TTL. The agent's cache lives in memory with restrictive file permissions (it is a smaller version of the same high-value target).
  • Availability. If Keystone is down, agents keep serving cached values until the TTL ends, then fail. Choose TTLs knowing that number. Allow stale-while-unavailable for static secrets with an explicit maximum.
  • Limits and fairness. Per-client rate limits, so a crash-looping pod cannot flood the cluster; jittered refresh times so thousands of agents do not renew simultaneously after an outage (a retry storm).

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The invariant is that stealing the storage gets you ciphertext. So secrets are encrypted with per-secret data keys, wrapped by a keyring key, wrapped by a root key that is never stored whole: it's rebuilt at unseal from Shamir shares, where any three of five people interpolate a polynomial, or unwrapped by a KMS with the shares kept for recovery. Workloads authenticate with platform identity, such as a Kubernetes service-account token or a cloud identity, bound to specific claims, and get a short-lived token and policy. Databases get dynamic credentials per pod with leases, so a leak expires and every session is attributable. Static secrets rotate with an overlap. The cluster is five nodes on Raft across three zones, failing closed on loss of a majority, with snapshots to another region. Audit is written before the response and the system refuses to serve if it can't log. Break-glass needs a quorum and triggers a rotation and a review. Per-node agents cache secrets so Keystone sees hundreds of requests per second, not thousands, and the cache lifetime is the stated revocation delay. The worst case is a node's memory, so nodes are treated as crown jewels and environments are separated."

Notice the shape: invariant → key hierarchy → unseal → workload identity → leases → rotation → consensus HA → audit → break-glass → blast radius → performance.

Key takeaways

  • Design so that the stored data is useless without a key that is never stored with it.
  • Envelope encryption makes rotation cheap; bind ciphertext to its path.
  • Shamir sharing: k points determine a degree k−1 polynomial; fewer than k reveal nothing.
  • Authenticate workloads with the platform's identity and bind roles to precise claims.
  • Leases make access expire on their own; the database enforces them, not the client.
  • Use a consensus-backed store and fail closed; test restore and unseal.
  • Audit before serving, never log secret values, and ship the log somewhere admins cannot edit.
  • Break-glass needs a quorum, a timer and a review; practise it.
  • Cache at the edge to scale, and write down the staleness you accepted.
