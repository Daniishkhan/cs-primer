Secure Design Reviews

Everything earlier in this course becomes a habit only when it is applied before the code is written. This chapter turns the course into a practice: the security section of a design document, a concrete checklist to review against, the paved roads that make the secure choice the easy one, the places where security belongs in the development lifecycle, and a way to measure progress without fooling yourself. It ends with a worked review of a short, fictional design.

Why this matters

A design flaw (a missing tenant check, a fetch of attacker-chosen URLs, a link that never expires) costs a paragraph to fix in a design doc and weeks to fix after launch, because by then other systems depend on it. Reviews are also where senior engineers earn their title: spotting the class of problem from a one-page description, naming the chapter-sized idea behind it, and proposing a fix cheap enough that the team adopts it.

The security section of a design doc

Design documents and decision records, as architecture chapter 19 describes them, are where a team writes down what it intends to build and why. A security section belongs there as a first-class part, not an appendix added the night before launch. Its job is to make the author think, and the reviewer able to disagree with something specific. A useful template fits on a page:

## Security

**Assets and data classes.**  What is valuable here? Which fields are
restricted/confidential? (Ch. 17 tiers.)

**Data-flow diagram with trust boundaries.**  Who calls whom, what crosses
a boundary, where TLS terminates, what holds credentials.

**Actors and threats.**  Legitimate users, other tenants, insiders,
compromised dependencies, external attackers. Top 3-5 threats (STRIDE
per boundary crossing) and the mitigation for each. (Ch. 1.)

**Authentication and authorization.**  How is each caller identified?
Where is each permission decision made, and on which object? (Ch. 5-8.)

**Data protection.**  Encryption in transit/at rest, key ownership,
retention, deletion, residency, PII in logs. (Ch. 17.)

**Abuse and failure modes.**  What can a hostile or buggy client do at
scale? What happens when auth, the key service or a dependency is down?

**Detection.**  Which audit events are emitted? What would alert? (Ch. 18.)

**Residual risk and decisions.**  Risks accepted, who accepted them,
when to revisit. Open questions with owners.

Notice the last heading. A review that ends in "no risks found" is suspect; one that ends in a short list of explicit, owned, dated risk acceptances is honest.

Analogy: a building inspector reading the blueprints

An inspector who reads the plans can say "this stairwell has no second exit" before concrete is poured, and the fix is a line on a drawing. The same inspector at the finished building can only write it up, and the fix means knocking through walls. The inspector is not there to design the building, only to ask the questions the architect was too close to ask.

Where it breaks down: buildings are specified once and then stable, whereas software keeps changing after approval. A review is a snapshot, so the controls it relies on must be enforced by code and tests that keep running, not by the memory of the meeting.

Secure defaults and paved roads

You cannot review every change by hand, so the leverage comes from making the secure way the easy way. Secure by default means the zero-effort configuration is the safe one: the template engine escapes, the new bucket is private, the new service account has no permissions, and the new endpoint requires authentication unless someone writes an explicit exception (fail-safe defaults from chapter 2). A paved road (also called a golden path) is the supported route for common tasks: a shared authentication middleware, a data-access layer that parameterises and filters by tenant, a secrets client, a logging library with redaction (chapter 17). Teams that use it get security, monitoring and upgrades for free, and the platform team gets one place to fix a flaw for everyone.

Paved roads fail when they are slower or worse than the alternative; people route around them. Treat the platform as a product with users: measure adoption, collect complaints, and fix the road, instead of mandating it and hoping.

People: champions, bounties and disclosure

A small security team cannot be in every design discussion. A security champion is an engineer embedded in a product team who has extra training, a standing relationship with the security team, and the job of raising the security questions in their own team's reviews. It works when the role is recognised in their workload and promotion, and it fails when it is volunteered on top of a full job. Champions also scale review: the team asks the questions first, and security reviews the hard residue.

Coordinated disclosure is the agreed way for outsiders to report a vulnerability: privately to the vendor, with time to fix before details become public. Publish a clear route (a security contact page and a security.txt file, standardised in RFC 9116), acknowledge reports promptly, commit not to pursue good-faith researchers, and track each report to a fix. Public timelines vary; windows of around 90 days are a common convention (Google Project Zero is a well-known example), and are negotiated for complex fixes. A bug bounty program pays researchers for valid reports. It broadens the set of eyes on your system, but it is a supplement: it needs a defined scope, rules of engagement, a safe-harbour statement and enough triage capacity to respond, or it produces noise and resentment. Do not buy it before you can fix the findings you already have. And the rule of this course holds for everyone: only test what you own or are authorised to test, which a program's written scope is how you grant that authorisation.

Security in the lifecycle

Review is one checkpoint among several. A healthy lifecycle spreads cheap automated checks across the pipeline, and reserves human attention for design and for the risky changes machines cannot judge.

  • Design. The threat model of chapter 1 and the review above. This is the only stage that catches missing controls, as opposed to broken ones.
  • Code. Peer review with security in mind; SAST (static application security testing), which analyses source for risky patterns such as string-built SQL and flags them in the pull request; and secret scanning to catch committed keys before they spread (chapter 4).
  • Build and test. Dependency scanning for known-vulnerable libraries, plus SBOMs and signed builds (chapter 12); DAST (dynamic testing), which probes a running staging copy from the outside the way a scanner would; fuzzing for parsers. Wire these into the pipeline described in architecture chapter 21 as blocking checks for high-severity findings and non-blocking reports for the rest, so developers see them without being trained to click past them.
  • Release. Infrastructure-as-code checks for public buckets and wide roles, deploy credentials scoped to what the pipeline needs, gradual rollout so a bad change meets few users first.
  • Operate. Audit logging and alerting (chapter 18), patch deadlines by severity, periodic penetration tests, and the disclosure and bounty channels above.

Scanners only on what you own

DAST tools, port scanners and fuzzers send hostile-looking traffic. Run them against your own staging environment or an application you are explicitly authorised to test, never against third-party systems. The tools in this course's toolbox (ZAP, Semgrep, dependency scanners) are for your code and your lab.

A penetration test is a time-boxed, authorised attempt by skilled testers to break a defined scope of a system and report what worked. It is valuable for what automated tools cannot do: chaining several small weaknesses into a real compromise and testing the human and process side. It has limits: it samples, it is a snapshot, and its value falls if run on a system that was never reviewed (the testers will find the same easy design flaws a checklist would). Run it after the review and the automation, scope it to the scariest paths, and track every finding to closure.

Measuring posture without vanity

"We ran 4,000 scans" and "we found 700 vulnerabilities" are vanity metrics: they measure activity, they rise when you look harder, and they can be gamed. Better measures describe outcomes and exposure, and each one has a decision attached to it.

  • Time to remediate by severity, and the age of the oldest open critical and high findings. A long tail here is real exposure; a median alone hides it.
  • Coverage of the paved road and of reviews. What share of services use the shared authentication and data layer, and what share of significant designs had a security review? Measure from the inventory, not from self-reports.
  • Asset and dependency inventory completeness. You cannot patch what you do not know you run. Patch latency for vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild is more telling than for all findings.
  • Detection and response in exercises. Time to detect and to contain in a drill or red-team exercise, not the quiet months in which nothing happened.
  • Repeat classes. Do the same kinds of bug keep appearing (injection, missing object checks)? A recurring class means a paved-road gap, not careless developers.
  • Credential and key hygiene. Age of the oldest long-lived secret; the time it takes to rotate a representative one.

Be wary of targets. When a measure becomes a target, people optimise the number rather than the risk (the effect known as Goodhart's law): a "zero open criticals" goal can produce reclassification instead of fixes. Review trends and outliers together with a human who knows the context, and publish definitions so a change in a chart means a change in the world.

Worked example: reviewing a short design

Here is a fictional, deliberately flawed design. Read it as a reviewer would, with the checklist beside you, before you read the findings. (Everything about it is invented; the company, hostnames and fields are examples.)

# Scheduled report export (Acme Analytics, draft)

Users create a schedule ("every Monday, CSV of last week's events").
A worker runs the job, builds the CSV, uploads it to object storage and
emails the user a link. Optionally the user gives a webhook URL and the
worker POSTs the CSV to it.

- API: POST /schedules {tenant_id, query, webhook_url?}. The browser sends
  tenant_id from the page it is on.
- The worker runs as `report-worker`, which can read every tenant's event
  table so one worker pool serves everyone.
- Files are stored at https://reports.example.com/<random-uuid>.csv and
  the link is emailed. The bucket serves them publicly; the uuid is
  unguessable.
- CSV cells are written exactly as stored (event names, user-supplied
  labels, etc.).
- For debugging, the API logs the full request body and the worker logs
  each generated row count and a sample row.
- Libraries: a new CSV package (latest version, unpinned).
- Retention: files are kept so users can re-download them.

Now the findings. Severity here is a judgement of likelihood times impact for this system: High means a plausible path to cross-tenant exposure or server compromise, to be fixed before launch; Medium means real weakness with limiting factors, to be fixed in the launch window or tracked with an owner; Low means hardening. (Public vulnerability databases use the CVSS scoring system for products; design review is usually coarser and that is fine.)

#SeverityFindingFix
1HighCross-tenant read. tenant_id comes from the request body and the worker can read every tenant. A user can schedule a report for another tenant's id. This is a broken object-level authorization and confused-deputy shape (8, 15).Derive the tenant from the authenticated session, never the body. Have the worker act with a per-job, tenant-scoped credential or query through a data layer that enforces the tenant filter.
2HighSSRF via webhook_url. The worker will connect to any URL, including internal services and cloud metadata addresses (11).Egress through a proxy that blocks private and link-local ranges after DNS resolution, restrict schemes and ports, no redirects, and run delivery in a network zone with no internal access.
3HighPublic, permanent bearer links. Anyone who obtains the URL (forwarded email, browser history, referrer, logs) reads the report forever. "Unguessable" is not authorisation (6).Serve downloads through an authenticated endpoint, or issue short-lived signed URLs tied to the requester; make the bucket private; expire links.
4MediumSpreadsheet formula injection. Cells beginning with =, +, - or @ may be interpreted as formulas when the CSV is opened in a spreadsheet: another untrusted-data-to-interpreter case (9).Neutralise such cells on export (for example, prefix a single quote or tab), and document it.
5MediumPersonal data in logs. Full request bodies and sample rows land in logs that many people and vendors can read (17).Log ids and counts only; allowlist fields; redact as a safety net.
6MediumIndefinite retention, no deletion path. Reports hold customer data indefinitely, so account closure and erasure requests have no answer (17).Retention period per tenant plan, lifecycle expiry, deletion on tenant offboarding, per-tenant keys for shredding.
7MediumNo abuse limits. Unlimited schedules and unbounded queries per user invite resource exhaustion and email abuse (16).Per-tenant quotas, minimum schedule interval, row and runtime caps, email rate limits.
8MediumUnpinned new dependency. "Latest" gives a different build each time and no review of the supply chain (12).Pin and lock versions, review the package, scan in CI.
9LowNo audit trail. Nothing records who scheduled, ran or downloaded an export (18).Emit audit events for schedule changes, runs and downloads to the separate security store.

Look at how the findings group. Three are about who may touch which data (1 and 3, with 6 behind them), one is a classic server-side trap (2), and the rest are hygiene a paved road would remove: a shared data layer would have prevented finding 1, a redacting logger finding 5. That grouping is the review's real output: not a list of nine bugs, but a short message to the team, "we need to settle the tenancy model and the download path before anything else".

Try it yourself

Pick a design doc you wrote or can read (a lab project works). Spend 30 minutes with the checklist table and write at most seven findings, each with a severity, a one-line fix and a chapter reference. Then rank them by what you would fix first, and say what evidence would change your ranking. Only review and test systems you own or are asked to review.

Distributed systems connection

Almost every checklist row is a distributed-systems question in disguise: where is the authoritative check, what happens when a dependency is slow or down, which component trusts which. A review is the security counterpart of the failure-mode analysis in the reliability course, and the fail-open versus fail-closed choice in the last row is the same availability-versus-safety trade-off as in Byzantine fault tolerance: what do you do when you cannot be sure?

Key takeaways

  • Put a security section in every design doc: assets, trust boundaries, threats, authn and authz, data protection, abuse, detection, and explicit, owned residual risks.
  • Review with a checklist over ten areas: authentication, authorization, input handling, secrets, data protection, tenancy, abuse, logging, dependencies and failure modes. Ask "where is it enforced?" each time.
  • Secure defaults and paved roads multiply a review: concentrate human attention where a team leaves the road.
  • Champions scale the questions; coordinated disclosure and bounties scale the finding of bugs, but only if you can triage and fix.
  • Spread controls through the lifecycle: threat model at design, SAST and secret scanning in code, dependency scanning, DAST, SBOM and signing in build, scoped release, detection and pen tests in operation.
  • Measure outcomes (time to remediate, age of open criticals, coverage, repeat classes, drill results), not activity, and beware targets that invite gaming.
  • Report review findings as themes with severity and a fix, not just a list.

Check your understanding

A design says "the report link is a random UUID, so only the recipient can open it". What is the best review comment?

  1. Use a longer UUID
  2. An unguessable URL is a bearer credential that can leak and never expires; require authentication or issue short-lived signed links
  3. This is secure; random identifiers cannot be found
  4. Move the file to a different bucket name
Unguessable is not the same as authorised. Links get forwarded, logged and cached, and without expiry or identity checks, anyone holding one has access.

Which is a vanity metric of security posture?

  1. Age of the oldest open critical finding
  2. Share of services on the paved road
  3. Time to contain in a drill
  4. The number of scans run this quarter
Scan count measures activity, rises with effort rather than safety, and can be gamed. The others describe exposure or outcomes tied to decisions.

Why is a pen test a poor substitute for design review?

  1. It samples a snapshot and tends to rediscover design flaws that were cheaper to prevent; it complements review by finding chained, real-world weaknesses
  2. Pen tests are never allowed on production systems
  3. Design reviews find everything a pen test can
  4. Pen testers cannot read code
Both have a role. The review prevents classes of flaw early; the test checks the built system and how pieces combine.

What makes a paved road more effective than a mandatory policy document?

  1. It is cheaper to print
  2. It removes the need for monitoring
  3. The secure option is also the easiest, so teams adopt it and inherit fixes, instead of working around a rule
  4. It guarantees no vulnerabilities
People take the path of least resistance. If the supported path is easier and better, adoption is the default, and review effort goes to the exceptions.

A team wants to launch a bug bounty next week but has a backlog of unfixed known critical issues and no triage owner. What would you advise?

  1. Launch now; researchers will help with the backlog
  2. Fix the backlog and name a triage owner and safe-harbour terms first, since a bounty adds findings faster than they can be handled
  3. Cancel all testing
  4. Keep the program secret
A bounty multiplies input. Without capacity to respond and fix, reports sit unanswered, researchers lose trust, and known issues become public.
