Where Next: On to System Design

You started this course asking what an attacker wants and ended it reviewing a design for nine kinds of mistake. This short chapter draws the map of what you can now defend, says honestly where the course stops, points to the best next books and free courses, and hands you to the capstone with one rule: every design gets a security section.

Why this matters

Security knowledge decays into trivia unless it is attached to a way of working. The point of the last twenty chapters is not a list of attacks but a set of questions you can ask of any system: what is valuable, where does trust change, who is allowed to do what, what happens when a part is compromised, and how would we know?

What you can now defend

Follow a single request from a browser to a database and back, and notice how many chapters it touches. That path is the map below, and it is also the shape of a design review: walk it, box by box, and ask the checklist's question at each boundary.

In concrete terms, you can now:

  • Model threats. Draw a data-flow diagram, mark trust boundaries, enumerate threats with STRIDE and attack trees, and rank them (chapter 1), guided by the classic design principles (chapter 2).
  • Use cryptography without inventing it. Pick hashes, MACs, authenticated encryption and signatures for the job, manage keys and secrets, and understand certificates and mTLS (3, 4).
  • Handle identity and access. Store passwords properly, add multi-factor and passkeys, choose sessions or tokens, use OAuth and OpenID Connect, and model permissions with roles, attributes and relationships (5 to 8).
  • Write and review application code safely. Prevent injection, reason about the browser's security model, spot server-side request forgery, and treat dependencies as part of your attack surface (9 to 12).
  • Secure infrastructure. Drop the trusted-network assumption, scope cloud roles, isolate tenants and design for abusive load (13 to 16).
  • Protect data and respond. Choose the encryption layer that matches the threat, minimise and delete, log for detection, and run an incident with evidence intact (17, 18).

The case studies are the best check: for each, you should now be able to say which control was missing, which chapter explains it, and which question in a design review would have surfaced it.

Where this course stops

Security is wider than any single course, and it is worth knowing the edges. This course did not teach: designing new cryptographic primitives or protocols (do not do this; use vetted ones); reverse engineering and malware analysis; hardware, firmware and side-channel attacks; mobile and embedded platform security; the specific security properties of machine-learning systems; physical and social-engineering defences beyond mentioning them; formal verification; and the detail of compliance regimes such as SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI DSS or GDPR, where legal and audit expertise is needed. It also deliberately taught attacks only as far as defenders need to recognise them. If a topic here becomes your job, the sources below are where to go.

Reading list

Titles and years below were checked against publisher, author or course pages when this chapter was written. Editions and course numbers change, so look for the current one.

  • Ross Anderson, Security Engineering: A Guide to Building Dependable Distributed Systems, 3rd edition (Wiley, 2020). The broadest and most readable survey of why systems fail, from cryptography and access control to banking, medical and automotive systems. Anderson made the third edition available as free downloads on his Cambridge University page, which is the place to check for current availability.
  • Adam Shostack, Threat Modeling: Designing for Security (Wiley, 2014). The practical book behind chapter 1: the four-question framing ("What are we working on? What can go wrong? What are we going to do about it? Did we do a good job?"), STRIDE and attack trees applied to real designs. The best next step if threat modeling becomes part of your job.
  • Heather Adkins, Betsy Beyer, Paul Blankinship, Piotr Lewandowski, Ana Oprea and Adam Stubblefield, Building Secure and Reliable Systems (O'Reilly, 2020). Google's engineers on designing for security and reliability together, covering least privilege, recovery, incident response and culture. It speaks directly to the point of this course sitting beside the reliability one; O'Reilly lists it as a 2020 title and Google has made it available to read online, so check Google's SRE site for the current link.
  • OWASP Cheat Sheet Series and the Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS). Free, concise, regularly updated guidance by topic (password storage, SQL injection prevention, session management, logging and more) and a checklist-style standard for verifying web applications. ASVS version 5.0.0 was released in May 2025. Use the cheat sheets when implementing and the ASVS when reviewing.
  • Stanford CS 155, Computer and Network Security. A free course with lecture slides, readings and project handouts online at cs155.stanford.edu. It covers control hijacking, web security and network security in more depth than this course's treatment of those areas, and is the natural next step after chapters 9 to 12.
  • MIT 6.858 Computer Systems Security (now numbered 6.5660). MIT's graduate systems-security course, with lecture notes, papers and labs available through MIT OpenCourseWare and the course's own site. Older offerings appear under the 6.858 number; recent ones as 6.5660. Its readings are a tour of how real systems were broken and then redesigned.

Two older papers are short enough to read this week and are cited throughout: Saltzer and Schroeder, "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems" (1975), the source of the design principles in chapter 2; and Hardy's 1988 note on the "confused deputy" behind chapter 8's authorization discussion.

Practise on what you own

Both university courses include hands-on labs and capture-the-flag style exercises. Do them on the provided lab environments or your own machines. Never practise against a real third-party system, and when you find a flaw in someone else's, use coordinated disclosure as described in chapter 19.

The bridge to the capstone

Course 7 is the System Design Studio: you will design whole systems under real constraints, using everything from the first six courses. Security does not get its own station there. It gets a section in every design, the same way latency, consistency and cost do. Before you present a design, be able to answer, in a few lines each:

  1. What are the assets, and which data classes (17) will this system hold?
  2. Where are the trust boundaries, and what crosses each one?
  3. How is each caller authenticated and each action authorised, on which object?
  4. What does a tenant or user see of another's data, and what enforces it?
  5. Where are the keys and secrets, and how are they rotated and revoked?
  6. What happens when one component is compromised or an auth dependency is down?
  7. What will you log, and what would you alert on?
  8. What can a hostile client do at scale, and what stops it?

If you want practice first, the course's labs, case studies and studios exercise exactly this: threat-model a file-sharing service, design identity and access for a multi-tenant SaaS, and build envelope encryption and crypto-shredding.

Distributed systems connection

Security has been the same story as the rest of the path: components that fail, messages that can be forged or replayed, and clocks, keys and authority that do not agree. Where the earlier courses ask "what if this node crashes?", this one asks "what if this node lies?" (compare Byzantine faults). Carry both questions into the capstone.

Key takeaways

  • Walk any system along its request path, box by box, and ask at each boundary: who is this, what may they do, what if this part is compromised, and how would we know?
  • You can now threat-model, use cryptography correctly, build identity and authorization, prevent the common application bug classes, secure infrastructure and data, and run a review.
  • The course skipped inventing crypto, malware and hardware work, mobile and ML security, and compliance detail; those need further study and specialists.
  • For next steps: Anderson, Shostack, Google's Building Secure and Reliable Systems, the OWASP cheat sheets and ASVS, and the free Stanford CS 155 and MIT 6.858/6.5660 materials.
  • Every design in the capstone gets a security section. Do it as you go, not at the end.

Check your understanding

Which is the most accurate description of how security should appear in a system design?

  1. As a final audit after the design is finished
  2. As a separate team's problem
  3. As a section in the design itself, covering assets, trust boundaries, authn and authz, data protection, abuse, detection and accepted risks
  4. As a list of attacks to defend against, copied from a website
Design-time security catches missing controls, which are the most expensive to retrofit. It belongs alongside latency, consistency and cost.

Which resource is the best fit if your job is about to include running threat-modeling sessions?

  1. Shostack's Threat Modeling: Designing for Security
  2. A password-cracking tool manual
  3. A compliance checklist
  4. A cryptography research paper on a new cipher
Shostack's book is the practitioner's guide to the process taught in chapter 1, including how to run the sessions and evaluate the result.

You find a real vulnerability in a third party's web service while doing a course exercise. What should you do?

  1. Exploit it further to see how bad it is
  2. Post the details publicly right away
  3. Ignore it
  4. Stop, do not probe further, and report it privately through the owner's disclosure channel (such as a security.txt contact)
Testing without authorisation can be illegal and harmful. Coordinated disclosure gives the owner time to fix it before details spread.
