Abuse, Bots & Denial of Service

Some of your traffic is not trying to use your product; it is trying to use you: to log into stolen accounts, copy your catalogue, exploit a discount, send premium-rate text messages at your expense, or simply knock you offline. This chapter teaches a mindset (design for adversarial load, not average load), then walks through account takeover defences, bots, fraud signals, business-logic abuse and denial of service, always from the defender's side.

Why this matters

Every capacity plan in the earlier courses assumed that users behave like users. Adversaries do not: they make the one request your system handles worst, a million times, from ten thousand addresses, using valid-looking accounts. Abuse is also where security meets money: the harm is often not a data breach but a bill, a degraded service for real customers, or refunds you pay out. And the controls (limits, challenges, scoring) all trade security against friction for legitimate people, so they need design, not just a checkbox.

The adversarial-load mindset

Four habits turn ordinary engineering into adversarial engineering:

  • Ask "what is the cheapest request for the attacker and the most expensive for me?" The ratio is what an attacker exploits. A login that runs a deliberately slow password hash (chapter 5) is expensive by design, so unlimited login attempts are a CPU attack; a search with an unbounded wildcard, an export, a report, a password-reset email and an SMS code are all amplifiers: small input, large work or cost.
  • Assume attackers can scale. An attacker rents thousands of IP addresses, uses residential proxies that look like home users, rotates browsers and replays real people's credentials. A limit that only counts requests per IP will be defeated by distribution, so you need limits on several keys.
  • Treat every free thing as a resource. Free signups, free trials, coupons, referral credit, uploads, API keys and invitations are all things that can be farmed. Write abuse cases next to use cases (the "misuse cases" of chapter 1).
  • Plan for an arms race. Defences are measured, bypassed and adapted to. Instrument them, review the false-positive and false-negative rates, and keep the ability to change rules without a deploy.

The picture also explains where limits belong. You met the algorithms for rate limiting (token bucket, sliding window, distributed counters) in the rate-limiter design chapter, and edge limiting as a cross-cutting concern in architecture chapter 9. This chapter does not repeat them; it is about what to key them on and what to do when they trip.

Credential stuffing and account takeover

Credential stuffing is the automated replay of username and password pairs leaked from other sites, hoping that people reused them. It differs from classic brute force: the attacker does not guess, so each account sees only one or two attempts, from many addresses. Per-account lockouts barely notice it, and the attack works because password reuse is common. When a stuffed login succeeds, the result is account takeover (ATO): the attacker is the user, with all of their data and trust. The authentication side (hashing, MFA, passkeys) is chapter 5; here is the defensive stack around it.

  • Breached-password checks. At signup and password change (and ideally at login), reject passwords that appear in known breach corpora. NIST SP 800-63B recommends comparing against lists of compromised values, and advises against composition rules and forced periodic changes (the rule most people remember, "must contain a symbol", does not help). Services exist that let you check a password's hash prefix without revealing the password (a k-anonymity range query), or you can load a list locally.
  • Rate limits on several keys. Per account (limits the damage to one victim), per IP (stops one noisy source), and per network or autonomous system, ASN (the operator of a block of addresses, which catches a botnet spread over one hosting provider but can also penalise legitimate users behind a large carrier or shared office NAT). The simulation below shows why a single key is not enough.
  • Failure-aware limits. Count failed attempts and distinct accounts tried per source, not just total requests. One address touching 500 different accounts is the signature, even if each account saw one try. Do the limit check before the expensive hash, or the limiter itself is the vulnerability.
  • Device and context signals. A recognised device, an unusual country, a new browser and an impossible travel time are all inputs. A login from a new device with a correct password is a good moment to ask for more.
  • Step-up authentication. Demand a second factor (a passkey or authenticator code in preference to SMS) when risk is high or an action is sensitive (email change, payout details, large transfer). A password found in a breach is exactly the thing a second factor is for.
  • Do not help the attacker. Return the same message and timing for "wrong user" and "wrong password"; do not let the password-reset form tell you whether an email exists; notify the real owner of new-device logins and credential changes.
  • Plan for the aftermath. Provide session revocation ("sign out everywhere"), forced reset for accounts found in a corpus, and detection of mass-takeover patterns (chapter 18).

The listing is a deterministic simulation of the defender's view: 2,000 legitimate users each logging in once, and a stuffing run of 5,000 attempts at 5,000 random accounts from 40 addresses in one network, of which about 1% of attempts use a password that is correct. Nothing leaves the process. The three runs add one defence at a time.

"""Credential-stuffing defences on a simulated login endpoint. Seeded, in-process, no network.
Models what a DEFENDER sees: many accounts, each tried once or twice, from a few sources."""
import random
from collections import defaultdict

rng = random.Random(7)
BREACHED = {"hunter2", "password1", "qwerty123"}          # stand-in for a breached-password list

class Login:
    def __init__(self, per_account=5, per_ip=20, per_asn=300):
        self.lim = {"acct": per_account, "ip": per_ip, "asn": per_asn}
        self.count = {"acct": defaultdict(int), "ip": defaultdict(int), "asn": defaultdict(int)}
        self.blocked_by = defaultdict(int)
    def attempt(self, acct, ip, asn, ok):
        for k, key in (("acct", acct), ("ip", ip), ("asn", asn)):
            self.count[k][key] += 1
            if self.count[k][key] > self.lim[k]:
                self.blocked_by[k] += 1; return "blocked"
        return "success" if ok else "fail"

# 2,000 legitimate logins spread over many users/IPs, plus 5,000 stuffing tries over 5,000 accounts from 40 IPs in 1 ASN.
def run(lim):
    L, res = Login(**lim), defaultdict(int)
    for n in range(2000):
        res["legit_" + L.attempt(f"user{n}", f"10.{n%250}.{n//250}.1", f"AS{n%50}", True)] += 1
    for n in range(5000):
        ok = rng.random() < 0.01                               # ~1% of reused passwords happen to work
        out = L.attempt(f"user{rng.randrange(20000)}", f"198.51.100.{n%40}", "AS64500", ok)
        res["attack_" + out] += 1
    return dict(sorted(res.items())), dict(L.blocked_by)

print("only per-account limit :", run(dict(per_account=5, per_ip=10**9, per_asn=10**9)))
print("account + per-IP       :", run(dict(per_account=5, per_ip=20,    per_asn=10**9)))
print("account + IP + ASN     :", run(dict(per_account=5, per_ip=20,    per_asn=300)))

print()
def check_password(pw):                                   # breached-password check at signup/reset
    return "reject (appears in breached list)" if pw in BREACHED else "accept"
for pw in ("hunter2", "correct-horse-battery-staple"): print(f"{pw:30}->", check_password(pw))
only per-account limit : ({'attack_fail': 4952, 'attack_success': 48, 'legit_success': 2000}, {})
account + per-IP       : ({'attack_blocked': 4200, 'attack_fail': 796, 'attack_success': 4, 'legit_success': 2000}, {'ip': 4200})
account + IP + ASN     : ({'attack_blocked': 4700, 'attack_fail': 296, 'attack_success': 4, 'legit_success': 2000}, {'asn': 500, 'ip': 4200})

hunter2                       -> reject (appears in breached list)
correct-horse-battery-staple  -> accept

Read the three runs as an argument. With only a per-account limit, nothing is blocked (each account is tried once) and 48 accounts fall. Adding a per-IP limit blocks 4,200 attempts and cuts successes to 4; the per-ASN limit then blocks 500 more, leaving 296 attempts to fail normally. All 2,000 legitimate users were unaffected in this model, but note the assumption: they came from many addresses. If your real users share a corporate NAT or a mobile carrier, the same ASN or IP limit would hit them, which is why such limits should challenge (ask for more proof) rather than hard-block, and why the numbers must be tuned on your own traffic. The last stage of the funnel, the second factor, is what makes the 4 remaining reused-password successes harmless.

Scraping and bots

A bot is automated software acting as a client. Many are welcome (search crawlers, uptime monitors, partners' integrations); the problem ones scrape data, hoard scarce inventory, create fake accounts, post spam or stuff credentials. Scraping is bulk automated collection of content you serve. Whether it is harmful depends on the data, the volume and your business and legal position; the techniques below are neutral tools with costs.

  • Signals. Request rate and regularity; no cookies or JavaScript execution; unusual header combinations or TLS fingerprints compared with the browser that claims to be calling; sequential walking of ids; access patterns no human produces (never loading images or scripts, hitting only API endpoints); datacentre address ranges; many accounts sharing devices or addresses. Every signal can be imitated by a determined attacker, so combine them and treat the output as probability, not proof.
  • Structural defences. Require login for valuable data and rate-limit per account; offer an official API with keys and quotas for legitimate users so that scraping the HTML has no advantage; paginate with caps; avoid exposing sequential ids; remove data nobody needs to publish. robots.txt is a polite request that well-behaved crawlers honour and that offers no protection against anyone else.
  • CAPTCHAs. A CAPTCHA is a challenge that is meant to be easy for people and hard for software. Their costs are real: friction and lost conversions, accessibility barriers for people with disabilities, privacy implications of the third-party scripts and signals involved, and an economy of human solving services and increasingly capable automated solvers. Modern "invisible" variants shift the work to risk scoring and only challenge suspicious sessions. A fair summary: they raise the attacker's per-request cost and are a reasonable late-stage control when a session is already suspicious, but they are not a wall, and demanding them from everyone is a tax on legitimate users.
  • Proof of work. Instead of asking a question, ask the client to burn some CPU: the server sends a challenge and the client must find a nonce so that a hash of the two has a number of leading zero bits, which costs about 2bits hashes to find and one hash to verify. It makes bulk abuse cost something with no human interaction and no third party, but the cost falls on legitimate users too (phones, old laptops, battery), and attackers with large compute (or stolen cycles) pay far less per request than their victims. The experiment below shows the asymmetry.
"""Hashcash-style proof of work as a cost-shifting tool: the server verifies with ONE hash, the client pays by searching.
Standard library only. Counts attempts (deterministic), not seconds."""
import hashlib

def leading_zero_bits(digest: bytes) -> int:
    n = int.from_bytes(digest, "big")
    return len(digest) * 8 - n.bit_length()

def solve(challenge: bytes, bits: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    nonce = 0
    while True:
        if leading_zero_bits(hashlib.sha256(challenge + nonce.to_bytes(8, "big")).digest()) >= bits:
            return nonce, nonce + 1
        nonce += 1

def verify(challenge: bytes, nonce: int, bits: int) -> bool:        # the server's whole job: one hash
    return leading_zero_bits(hashlib.sha256(challenge + nonce.to_bytes(8, "big")).digest()) >= bits

print(f"{'bits':>4} {'client hashes (avg of 20)':>26} {'server hashes':>14}")
for bits in (8, 12, 16, 18):
    total = 0
    for i in range(20):
        ch = f"login-challenge-{i}".encode()
        nonce, tries = solve(ch, bits)
        assert verify(ch, nonce, bits)
        total += tries
    print(f"{bits:>4} {total/20:>26,.0f} {1:>14}")
bits  client hashes (avg of 20)  server hashes
   8                        241              1
  12                      3,602              1
  16                     66,469              1
  18                    178,318              1

Each four extra bits makes the client's work about sixteen times larger, while the server always does one hash. That is the design knob and also the trap: a difficulty that slows a bot noticeably slows a cheap phone as well. Proof of work is best seen as a way to adjust a price (raise it for suspicious sessions, keep it near zero for others), not as an identity check.

Fraud signals and risk scoring

When the question is not "is this password right?" but "is this person who they say, and will this transaction turn into a chargeback?", systems compute a risk score: a number or class (low, medium, high) combining many weak signals. Without any formula, the families of signals are:

  • Velocity: how many signups, logins, payments or password resets from this device, address, card or email in a time window.
  • Identity and device consistency: account age, whether the device has been seen with this account, mismatch between billing country, shipping country and network location, disposable email domains.
  • Behaviour: typing and navigation patterns, how quickly a form was completed, deviations from the account's own history.
  • Graph links: accounts that share devices, payment instruments or addresses with accounts already known to be abusive. Fraud rings show up as clusters.
  • Reputation and labels: past chargebacks, confirmed fraud, abuse reports and trusted-customer history.

The score maps to an action: allow, allow with extra friction (step-up, delay, hold for review), or deny. Design points that matter more than the model: both error types cost money (a false positive blocks a good customer, a false negative pays out), so measure both and set thresholds by the cost of each; keep a human review path and a way for legitimate users to appeal; log the signals and the decision so you can explain it (and so you can debug when a rule goes wrong); and train on labels that may be biased by what you already catch. Scores can also encode unfair or discriminatory proxies, so review inputs for legal and ethical issues (the privacy side is in chapter 17).

Business-logic abuse

Not all abuse breaks any technical rule. Business-logic abuse uses the product exactly as built, in a way the designers did not intend. Because there is no malformed input and nothing for a scanner to flag, it is found by thinking about incentives. Typical patterns and the defender's responses:

The common cure is to put cost and limits on every action that costs you something, and to monitor business metrics (SMS cost per verified user, signups per hour, promo redemptions per code) as security signals. A graph that suddenly bends is often the first and only alarm.

Denial of service

A denial of service (DoS) attack tries to make a service unavailable by exhausting a resource; when the traffic comes from many sources it is a distributed denial of service (DDoS). The useful classification is by the resource that runs out:

  • Volumetric attacks aim to fill the network link with more traffic than it can carry, so legitimate packets are dropped. Some use reflection and amplification: tricking third-party servers into sending large responses to the victim's address in reply to small spoofed requests. Defence is mostly capacity and placement.
  • Protocol attacks exploit the state that network protocols keep. The classic is the SYN flood: each TCP connection attempt (a SYN packet, see the handshake in networking chapter 8) makes the server reserve memory for a half-open connection, and a flood of SYNs with no follow-up fills the table. SYN cookies defeat this by encoding the needed state into the sequence number of the reply, so the server remembers nothing until a valid final ACK comes back.
  • Application-layer attacks send requests that look valid (HTTP GETs, logins, searches) to make the server do expensive work, or hold many slow connections open. They are the hardest to separate from a crowd of real users, and the expensive endpoints are the target: login (slow hashing), search, report generation, uncached pages, anything that calls a paid third party.

Absorption, filtering and caching

  • Anycast. Announce the same IP address from many data centres. Each client's traffic reaches the nearest site, so a distributed flood is spread over all of them instead of converging on one link. The mechanism and a real deployment are in the anycast case.
  • CDNs and scrubbing. A content delivery network answers cacheable requests at the edge without touching your origin, and large providers have enough bandwidth that most volumetric floods are a rounding error. Traffic can also be routed through "scrubbing" capacity that discards junk and forwards the remainder. Make sure the origin is only reachable through the edge (restrict by network, or require authenticated origin pulls); an attacker who finds the origin address skips the protection.
  • WAF. A web application firewall inspects HTTP requests against rules: blocked signatures of known attack patterns, geographic or reputation rules, and rate-based rules. It is a useful layer and a quick way to apply a virtual patch, but it works from patterns (so it can be evaded and generates false positives) and does not replace fixing the bug behind the pattern.
  • Cheap rejection. Put limits and authentication before expensive work; cache; make expensive endpoints asynchronous or queued with per-tenant limits (chapter 15); set timeouts, body-size and header limits, and connection limits so slow clients cannot hold sockets; shed load deliberately under pressure (reliability chapter 11) and degrade features rather than failing wholesale.

Autoscaling versus cost attacks

Autoscaling looks like the perfect answer: if the load grows, add servers. Against an application-layer flood it has a catch. The system stays up, and you pay for every extra instance, request, gigabyte of egress and downstream call. An attack whose goal is a huge bill instead of an outage is sometimes called a cost attack or "denial of wallet", and pay-per-use and serverless platforms are especially exposed. Combine scaling with: sensible maximum instance counts and concurrency limits; budgets and billing alarms that page a human; per-client and per-tenant quotas so a single key cannot consume unlimited capacity; and cached, rate-limited, authenticated paths for anything that is costly. Autoscaling absorbs surprise from good traffic; limits protect you from bad traffic. You need both (and capacity testing of your own system, as in reliability chapter 10).

Analogy: a nightclub door

Anycast and the CDN are the queue barriers on the street that stop a crowd spilling into the road. The WAF is the sign that says "no trainers, no weapons". Rate limits and bot checks are the doorperson asking some people for ID. The bar's own rules (one free drink each) are the business logic, and the risk score is the doorperson's experienced eye. Autoscaling is opening more rooms for the crowd, which is wonderful until you realise the crowd is not buying drinks and the rent is due.

Where it breaks down: a club sees the faces. Online, one person can be ten thousand arrivals, and a regular can be mistaken for an intruder, so every control must be measured for its effect on honest customers.

Only test what you own or are authorised to test

Load and abuse testing should be done against your own systems, in an environment you control, with the knowledge of your provider where their terms require it. Generating floods, stuffing logins or scraping against services you do not own is illegal in most places and can harm people other than your target. This chapter deliberately gives no recipes for attacks; the simulations above run in a single process and model the defender's view.

In pow_demo.py, estimate the cost on a device 20 times slower than yours and decide what difficulty you would choose for a login form. Distributed systems connection

Defences against abuse are themselves distributed systems with consistency choices. A per-account limit across many edge servers needs shared counters, and exactness is expensive, so most designs accept slightly loose limits for speed (the trade-offs are in the rate-limiter chapter). A fraud decision must be made in tens of milliseconds, so features are precomputed and the model is a replicated, cached service; its failure mode (fail open lets fraud through, fail closed blocks sales) is a deliberate policy. And the overload and retry-storm behaviours of the reliability course apply directly: a naive client that retries after a rejection turns your limiter into an amplifier, so tell clients when to back off.

Key takeaways

  • Design for adversarial load: find the requests that are cheap for the attacker and expensive for you, and assume attackers can distribute across addresses, accounts and devices.
  • Against credential stuffing, combine breached-password checks, limits on account, IP and ASN, failure-aware counting before the expensive hash, device signals and step-up MFA. No single key suffices.
  • Bot defences (behavioural signals, structural limits, CAPTCHAs, proof of work) each raise an attacker's price and carry a cost for real users; use them adaptively on suspicious sessions rather than on everyone.
  • Risk scoring combines weak signals into allow, step-up or deny; tune thresholds by the cost of both error types, keep appeals and explanations, and keep it qualitative until you have your own data.
  • Business-logic abuse (promo codes, signups, SMS pumping) follows incentives, not malformed input. Put limits and costs on every action that costs you, enforce single-use atomically, and monitor business metrics as security signals.
  • DoS is classified by exhausted resource: volumetric (bandwidth, answered by anycast and capacity), protocol (state, answered by SYN cookies and edge proxies) and application (work, answered by caching, early rejection, rate limits, WAF).
  • Autoscaling turns an outage into a bill; pair it with caps, quotas and budget alarms, and keep the origin reachable only through the edge.

A stuffing campaign tries each of 50,000 accounts once, from 2,000 different IP addresses. Why does a "lock the account after 5 failures" rule not stop it, and what helps?

  1. It does stop it; five failures is enough
  2. Lockouts are illegal
  3. No single account ever reaches the threshold; count failures and distinct accounts per source and network, check breached passwords, add device signals and step-up MFA
  4. Only longer passwords help
The attack is wide, not deep. Controls must be keyed on the attacker's footprint (IP, ASN, device, distinct accounts), and a second factor protects the accounts whose reused password was correct.

Why should the rate-limit check on a login endpoint run before the password hash, not after?

  1. Password hashing is deliberately expensive, so an attacker can exhaust CPU by sending many attempts; reject cheaply first
  2. Hashing is cheap, so order does not matter
  3. Because hashes are public
  4. Because limits only work on hashed values
The slow hash that protects stored passwords from offline cracking is an amplifier for denial of service if it runs on unauthenticated traffic before any cheap check.

Which statement about CAPTCHAs and proof of work is most accurate?

  1. Both completely stop bots
  2. Both are free for real users
  3. They only work against humans
  4. Both raise an attacker's cost per request but impose costs on legitimate users (friction, accessibility, device cost) and can be bypassed by well-resourced attackers, so they suit suspicious sessions better than everyone
They are price-setting tools, not identity checks. Adaptive use limits the harm to honest users.

Your SMS verification costs spike while the number of verified accounts stays flat. What is the likely abuse and the first response?

  1. A TLS downgrade; renew certificates
  2. SMS pumping, where sends are triggered toward numbers someone profits from; rate-limit and gate sends, restrict costly destinations, and monitor the send-to-verify ratio
  3. SQL injection; use prepared statements
  4. Session fixation; regenerate session ids
The attacker does not need the code; each message earns them a share of the fee. The tell is cost without conversions. Fix by limiting what unauthenticated users can trigger.

How do SYN cookies stop a SYN flood from filling the server's connection table?

  1. They encrypt the SYN packet
  2. They block every client from a new network
  3. The server encodes what it needs in the reply's sequence number and keeps no state until a valid final ACK arrives, so spoofed SYNs consume nothing
  4. They increase the size of the connection table
Statelessness is the defence: legitimate clients complete the handshake and prove the address is real; flooders never do.

Autoscaling keeps your service up under an application-layer flood. What risk remains?

  1. A very large bill; set maximum capacity, per-client quotas and budget alarms, and keep expensive paths rate-limited
  2. None; autoscaling is a complete defence
  3. Only that the servers become slower
  4. That anycast stops working
Scaling absorbs load but not cost. The attack succeeds if it makes you pay or if the scaled-up dependency (database, paid API) becomes the next bottleneck.
