Equifax, 2017

In 2017 attackers entered Equifax’s online dispute portal through an Apache Struts flaw that had a patch available, moved to databases the portal did not need, and sent data out through an encrypted channel that a certificate expiry had left unwatched. Two government reports describe the chain.

The pattern in one sentence

A patch you did not apply because you did not know where the software ran, and a monitor that quietly stopped working, are the same failure: nobody was checking the controls.

The cast

This page states what the two government reports say; general explanations are labelled as general. Related chapters: chapter 12 (patching and dependencies), chapter 18 (detection) and chapter 4 (certificate lifecycles).

Apache Struts
An open-source Java web application framework. CVE-2017-5638 is a flaw in its Jakarta Multipart parser that NVD says let remote attackers run commands via crafted request headers.
ACIS
Equifax’s Automated Consumer Interview System, the internet-facing portal that handled consumer credit-report disputes. The Oversight report says it was built in the 1970s.
Web shell
A script placed on a compromised server that gives the attacker a backdoor for running commands and moving files.
Traffic inspection device
A tool that decrypts and examines network traffic for malicious activity. To read encrypted traffic it needs a valid certificate installed where decryption happens (Oversight report).
Segmentation
Separating systems so that a compromise of one does not give access to unrelated ones (GAO footnote).
Exfiltration
Moving stolen data out of the victim’s network.

The attack path

GAO-18-559 reports that in July 2017 Equifax system administrators discovered that attackers had gained unauthorized access over the internet to an online dispute portal, and that the attackers accessed personal information of at least 145.5 million individuals. The House Oversight report puts the final total at approximately 148 million, a figure that rose after the September 2017 announcement of 143 million. The two reports date from different points in that revision, so I quote each with its source.

The entry point was a known flaw. The Struts project announced CVE-2017-5638 and released a patch on 7 March 2017; US-CERT alerted Equifax the next day. NVD records a CVSS v3.1 base score of 9.8 (Critical) for it and notes that it was exploited in the wild in March 2017. The Oversight report says the first evidence of exploitation of the flaw against servers on the Equifax network was on 10 March, and that attackers entered through the portal on 13 May.

From there the Oversight report describes a chain of steps, each enabled by a separate gap. The attackers uploaded web shells (the report says approximately 30 unique ones were used), found a mounted file share holding unencrypted application credentials, and used them to reach 48 databases outside the portal’s environment even though the portal needed only three. About 9,000 queries were run; the report says PII was located 265 times, and the data was transferred out without Equifax’s knowledge.

Why the patch was missed

The reports give several overlapping reasons rather than one. On 9 March Equifax’s Global Threat and Vulnerability Management team emailed the alert to over 400 people, instructing anyone running Struts to patch within 48 hours. GAO, citing Equifax officials, says the recipient list was out of date so the notice did not reach the individuals responsible for the patch. The Oversight report adds that a scan for vulnerable components, which did not detect the flaw on externally facing systems, was run on a root directory rather than the subdirectory where Struts was listed. In GAO’s terms the vulnerability was “not properly identified as being present” on the portal. In short, the failure was in finding the software, not in a refusal to patch it.

Why nobody saw the data leave

Equifax had a device to inspect network traffic. GAO reports that a misconfiguration let encrypted traffic pass uninspected, due to an expired digital certificate; the Oversight report says the certificate on the device that monitored ACIS traffic had expired 19 months before the discovery. Once it was renewed on 29 July 2017 the inspection resumed and, GAO says, the administrator recognised signs of an intrusion such as commands being executed in ways not part of normal operations. Equifax blocked addresses and, the following day, took the portal offline.

Common misconception: "the monitoring tool was in place, so we were covered"

A control that has silently stopped working is worse than a known gap, because everyone assumes it is on. Here a routine administrative item, a certificate renewal, turned a monitoring device into one that could not see the encrypted channel the data left through. The Oversight report adds that Equifax had allowed over 300 security certificates to expire, including 79 for monitoring business-critical domains. The general lesson is to monitor the monitors: alert on expiry and on a control that reports nothing.

Contributing factors

GAO lists four factors identified by Equifax’s own investigation. The Oversight report goes further into organisation and design.

  • Identification: the vulnerable component was not found on the portal; the alert list was out of date and a scan missed it (GAO, Oversight).
  • Detection: the expired certificate meant encrypted traffic was not inspected (GAO, Oversight). The Oversight report also says file integrity monitoring was not enabled on ACIS, though Mandiant told the committee it could have flagged the web shells.
  • Segmentation: the portal was not separated from unrelated databases (GAO). The Oversight report says it needed only three yet reached 48.
  • Data governance: credentials were stored unencrypted on a file share; the Oversight report says this was inconsistent with Equifax policy.
  • Accountability and complexity: the Oversight report cites unclear lines of authority between policy and operation, and a complex legacy environment built through growth.

What changed

GAO says that, according to Equifax’s public filings and officials, the company took remediation steps, and notes that GAO did not independently assess them. As reported: a new process to identify and patch software vulnerabilities and confirm the patches landed; new policies and tools for continuous monitoring of network traffic; additional controls on traffic between servers and at the network boundary; a new security controls framework and tighter access controls; an endpoint tool to detect misconfigurations and notify administrators; and a new governance structure under which a Chief Information Security Officer reports directly to the CEO (previously the security chief reported to the Chief Legal Officer). GAO also reports that the IRS, SSA and USPS, Equifax’s main federal identity-verification customers, assessed its controls and changed their contracts, including breach-notification requirements.

Patch and inventory lessons

  • You cannot patch what you cannot find. An accurate inventory of components, including those nested inside applications, is a precondition of any patch deadline (general lesson; chapter 12).
  • A deadline needs an owner and a delivery channel that is itself tested: a mailing list is only as good as its membership.
  • Verify, do not assume: scan from the right place, then check afterwards that the vulnerability is gone.
  • Treat certificates as inventory with expiry dates and owners.
  • Segment, so that one foothold does not open everything, and keep credentials out of readable files.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: the recall notice sent to a stale address list

A manufacturer announces a recall of a faulty part. The notice goes to a mailing list that has not been updated, and the checklist for finding affected units looks in the wrong folder. Meanwhile the alarm in the warehouse has been unplugged for months, though the panel still shows green.

Where it breaks down: software hides in nested folders and nobody sees the part; a physical recall can be walked and counted.

Key takeaways

  • Patch speed starts with inventory: know where every component runs.
  • Monitoring that depends on a certificate is monitoring that can expire.
  • Segmentation and credential hygiene limit what a single foothold can reach.
  • The breach needed several failures in a row; any one of the four controls working would have changed the outcome (an inference, not a claim from the reports).
