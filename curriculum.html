Curriculum Map

This page explains how the courses of both paths fit together: the eight courses of Zero to Systems and the nine of Zero to AI Systems. It shows how they line up with a university systems curriculum and what a gap review of the whole path found. It also lists what was added as a result, what is deliberately left out, and three ways through the material depending on how much time you have.

Why have a map at all?

A good systems education is built from layers that each assume the one below. When something feels hard, the cause is often a missing layer underneath it, not the topic itself. The map tells you which layer to go back to. If Raft feels like magic, the gap is usually in failure and timeouts or durability, not in Raft.

The gap analysis

Once the first six courses were drafted, the whole path was reviewed the way a department reviews a curriculum. It was checked against three things:

  1. A topic inventory. Every section heading of every chapter was listed and read as one outline, looking for missing steps between layers.
  2. A keyword sweep. Every page was searched for the vocabulary a senior engineer is expected to use fluently. A term that appears on zero or one page out of more than 200 is a strong signal.
  3. The university sequence above and the design problems in the capstone. If a capstone design needs an idea that no earlier course teaches, that is a gap.

Deliberately out of scope

A good curriculum also says what it leaves out, so you can find those topics elsewhere.

  • Data structures and algorithms. Big-O, hash tables, trees, graphs and dynamic programming are assumed knowledge. If they're rusty, refresh them alongside Course 1. Coding interviews test them separately from system design.
  • Frontend and mobile architecture. Rendering strategies, client state management and offline-first apps get only a light touch (in the collaborative editor studio). They deserve a course of their own.
  • Compilers, programming-language theory and hardware design. These are valuable, but they're a different path.
  • Machine-learning theory. This path covers ML as a system to design and run. Training and evaluating models is the subject of the second path, below.
  • Mathematical proofs. Correctness arguments are given intuitively, with pointers to the original papers. Specifying with TLA+ is the bridge to more formal work.

Suggested plans

📚 The full path

Everything, in order, at roughly 8–10 hours a week. Expect most of a year. Do every lab. Do one case study per week alongside the chapters, and the studios at the end of each course. This is the route to real understanding.

🎯 Senior design focus

If you already know the fundamentals, work through the Deep dive chapters of Courses 2–6. Then do all of Course 7, with every studio done out loud on a timer. Step back to an earlier chapter whenever a capstone design leans on something you can't explain.

⚡ Twelve-week sprint

Weeks 1–2: data systems deep dives. Weeks 3–4: distributed systems deep dives. Week 5: reliability. Week 6: security identity and access. Weeks 7–12: the capstone, two worked designs and one studio per week. Chapter 21 of the capstone has a more detailed practice plan.

The second path: Zero to AI Systems

The second path takes the same approach to AI systems engineering: building, evaluating and delivering systems that use learned models, from classical machine learning to agents. It starts from school mathematics and ends with take-home-style delivery studios. It is deliberately broad. It trains the engineer that applied AI teams, AI product companies, research labs' applied groups and forward-deployed teams all hire, rather than the candidate for one job advert.

How the two paths connect

The AI path assumes you can already reason about the engineering underneath: processes and memory, networks and retries, data stores, observability and security. Its chapters link back to the first path rather than repeating it. You do not need all eight first-path courses. A minimum set that covers what the AI path leans on is about 25 to 30 chapters:

  • How Programs Run: chapters 1, 2, 4 to 6, 10, 14 to 17 and 21 (parallelism and GPUs).
  • Networking: chapters 10 to 13 and 16 (failure, timeouts and retries).
  • Data Systems: chapters 1 to 3, 7, 14 and 17 (search and specialised stores).
  • Security: chapters 1, 7 and 9 (injection), which prompt injection extends.
  • Reliability: chapters 2, 4 to 6 and 9, which the evaluation and observability chapters build on.
  • System Design: Designing with ML & LLMs is a good bridge between the paths.

Teaching through a fast-moving field

Much of what is written about AI is out of date within a year. The second path handles that in three ways. The main text of every chapter teaches the durable mechanism: the loop, the objective, the trade-off, the measurement. Anything that changes monthly, such as which model leads a leaderboard, prices, library versions and protocol revisions, sits in boxes titled State of play with a date. And the labs implement each idea against a small interface with scripted stand-in models, so they keep working when providers and frameworks change, and a real model can be plugged in through one environment variable.

Out of scope for the second path

  • Mathematical depth beyond what systems need: measure-theoretic probability, convex-optimisation proofs and statistical learning theory are pointed to, not taught.
  • Generative image and audio models: diffusion and speech get at most a mention; vision appears where documents need it.
  • Robotics and model-based or offline reinforcement learning.
  • Writing GPU kernels beyond one Triton introduction, and multi-node pretraining operations beyond the concepts.
  • Building a vector database or an agent framework: you implement the core ideas by hand, then use existing tools.

🧬 The builder spine (recommended)

If your goal is to build evolutionary and self-improving systems yourself, rather than to deliver AI to customers, follow the builder spine. It is about 90 of the path's chapters, chosen because course A7 (Search, Evolution & Self-Improving Agents) depends on them. Every other chapter is marked elective. The Builder spine only button at the foot of any sidebar hides the electives, and its Next links then walk the spine from course to course.

  • A0: Python, NumPy, linear algebra, derivatives and autodiff, probability and statistics. It also covers the "programs as data" Python that the evolution labs need: closures, objects, ast, exec and running untrusted candidates safely.
  • A1: generalisation, metrics, baselines, linear models, nearest neighbours, validation and leakage.
  • A2: networks and backpropagation, embeddings, attention, a GPT by hand, tokenisation, decoding and compute.
  • A3: reinforcement learning, fine-tuning, preference optimisation, reward hacking, GRPO and evolutionary model merging.
  • A4: the agent loop, tools, context, workflows, coding agents, agent security and sandboxes.
  • A5: dense retrieval and nearest-neighbour search only.
  • A6: error analysis, harnesses, judges, eval statistics, benchmarks, contamination and agent evals.
  • A7: all of it, including the biology of evolution, genetic programming and CMA-ES, and self-referential improvers.

One problem runs through the spine: online bin packing, the task FunSearch used. You meet it in A0 as a tiny evolution engine, see it overfit in A1, sample mutations for it in A2, evolve it in A7, and finally make the evolver improve itself. Its evaluator is cheap and deterministic, and it can be checked on held-out instances, which is what an evolutionary loop needs from a domain. Roughly 350 to 400 hours with the spine labs.

📚 The full path

All nine courses in order, every lab and the studios: roughly 900 hours, plus about 80 to 100 hours of first-path chapters if you have not done them. At 10 hours a week that is about two years; full time, about six months.

🧭 Experienced engineer

Skim A0, take A1's validation part, then A2 to A6 in full, A7's integrity part and A8: about 600 hours. Start A6 alongside A4, so every agent you build is measured from the start.

⚡ Applied sprint

Already know ML? Do A4, A5, A6 and A8 (about 400 hours), reading A3 and A7 for context. Finish with the eight take-home studios on a timer and three portfolio projects from A5, A6 and A4.
