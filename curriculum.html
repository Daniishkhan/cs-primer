The Artifact Track

The path builds towards four public artifacts. Each one replicates a published result about automating AI research at small scale and adds one honest finding of its own. This page says what each artifact is, which chapters it needs and when, how long and how much it should take, and the standards it has to meet before it counts. If you have never trained a model or run a keep-or-revert loop, start with Leg 1: twenty steps that build both from five taxi rides, and end at Artifact 1.

Why build artifacts instead of finishing a curriculum?

Research labs hire on public evidence: code that runs, a write-up that survives a careful reader, and merged contributions to the repositories they maintain. A finished set of courses shows none of that. So the courses here are scheduled by the artifact that needs them. You read a chapter just before an artifact uses it, and you open others while you build. A chapter no artifact needs is kept as reading and never required.

The four artifacts

Each artifact is a replication plus one extension, and each one is the test bed for the next. The order follows the field's own ladder: a single-file research loop, then a harness that searches, then a harness that rewrites itself, then the measurement of whether any of it was real. Every artifact uses a cost-metered machine-learning experiment as its inner task.

Two constraints shape every choice: a total spend of roughly $1,000 to $3,000 over 18 months, and one rented machine at a time. Full runs of MLE-bench, PaperBench or the Darwin Gödel Machine are out of budget and are replaced by small "Lite" subsets. Costs and months are planning estimates for one person at 10 to 15 hours a week. If Artifact 1 takes longer, shift everything right rather than cutting its gate.

How to use the track

Open the settings panel (the sliders icon in the top bar) and pick an artifact under Artifact track. Sidebars and course roadmaps then show only the chapters that artifact uses, each chapter carries a badge saying whether to read it first or open it while building, and Next on each chapter walks the artifact's reading list across courses. Choose All to see everything again. "Mark complete" on a chapter ticks it off below.

Each artifact, chapter by chapter

For every artifact: what it replicates and extends, its gate (tick the boxes as you meet them), the chapters to read before starting in order, what to keep open while building, the labs, workbenches and case studies that build it, and the primary sources.

The 18-month roadmap

Each artifact ends at a gate that is a public result, not a private milestone, and the next artifact starts only when the gate is met. Pull requests to research repositories and visibility actions run in their own lanes, so a slow artifact never stalls the record of contributions.

The standards every artifact must meet

An artifact that fails any of these is not finished, because a reviewer at a lab checks exactly these points first.

The four Level-1 conditions

Whenever you claim that a loop improved something, it must pass the four conditions Weco set out for its own claim of self-improvement (taught in A12 chapter 1):

  1. A fair baseline. Your own hand-tuned version, given the same budget and the same number of attempts.
  2. A sustained trend. Improvement across several steps, not one lucky accept.
  3. Generalisation. Scored on tasks the loop never saw during search.
  4. A fixed physical budget. Dollars, GPU-hours and steps stated up front and never exceeded.

Evaluator hygiene

Carried from Artifact 3 into everything after it (taught in A10 chapter 3 and A11 chapter 3):

  • The evaluator lives outside the agent's write boundary, and checker code is never visible to the proposer.
  • Every task has public and private splits; only private scores decide what is kept.
  • Tool results are typed and attested, so an agent cannot write its own "tests passed" log.
  • Hacking rate is reported next to every score, measured on a held-out family of tasks.
  • Three seeds at least; report means with confidence intervals and a paired test against the baseline.

The write-up

Two to six pages, the same shape every time, so reviewers learn to read you fast (taught in A9 chapter 6):

  1. A one-sentence claim with its number and its budget.
  2. What was replicated, with the exact command, and the original paper's figure beside yours.
  3. The extension: hypothesis, design, result.
  4. Negative results, and what you would try next.
  5. Hacking rate, seeds, costs and hardware.
  6. Reproduction: one command, one configuration, a pinned environment.

Release it on GitHub with the write-up as the README, a results/ folder with the raw logs, and a short post that links both. Tag the maintainers of the repository you built on.
