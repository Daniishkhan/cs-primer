Run an Incident: A Tabletop Exercise

You are the on-call engineer when the page arrives. Over nine stages the situation changes, the obvious fix fails, and you are asked to hand over and to write it up. Nothing here is real; everything in it is the kind of thing that is. Write what you would do before you open each update, because the point of a tabletop is the decision, not the reading.

How this tabletop works

This is a staged scenario in the style of the game days of chapter 19. Each stage shows a time, an update (open it only when you are ready) and a Your turn question. Write what you, as incident commander (IC), would do and say in the notes box before opening the worked answer. The notes save in this browser. Opening an update counts as a reveal in the status line at the bottom, along with the worked answers. The scenario is built on the lifecycle and roles of chapter 16 and ends with the postmortem of chapter 17. Alerting background is in chapter 7.

Fictional scenario

Larkspur Market, its systems, people, numbers and timeline are invented for this exercise. They do not describe any real company or incident. All figures are illustrative, chosen to be internally consistent.

📐 The setting

Larkspur Market is a fictional online shop. You are the primary on-call engineer for checkout, and it is a Tuesday afternoon.

  • Two regions, eu-west (about 40% of traffic) and us-east (about 60%), each with three availability zones and its own database. The services are the six of studio 1: gateway, cart, pricing, inventory, payments and orders. The checkout flow reserves stock, authorises the card with the external PSP, and then writes the order.
  • Normal load is about 10 order submissions a second across both regions. The order SLO is 99.5% over 30 days, with the burn-rate alerts of the earlier studio (page at 14.4× over 1 hour and 5 minutes).
  • The deploy pipeline releases in waves: eu-west first, a 25-minute bake, then us-east automatically. Today the orders service is being released as v2.41.0, which also carries a database migration.
  • The incident policy says: anyone may declare; the IC does not debug; status updates every 30 minutes; severity 1 is "most customers cannot complete orders", severity 2 is "a large minority cannot", severity 3 is "a small group or a degraded experience".
  • Your role: you are the IC from the moment you decide to be. A colleague, Mara, is the secondary on-call, and an engineering manager, Idris, is reachable. Everyone is fictional.

1The page

[PAGE] CheckoutProbeFailing  region=eu-west
  The synthetic test order has failed 5 times in a row.
  Last error: HTTP 504 from gateway after 10.0 s
  Dashboard: https://grafana.example.internal/d/checkout-health
  Runbook:   https://runbooks.example.internal/checkout-probe

You are at your desk and have just acknowledged the page. List your first five minutes, in order. Do you open an incident now, or look first? What do you say, and to whom?

Compare with a worked answer

The answer to "declare or look first" is declare, and look at the same time (chapter 16: declare early; downgrading is cheap, a late page is not).

  1. Acknowledge so nobody else wonders whether the page was seen.
  2. Open the incident with the tooling: create the channel (for example #inc-checkout-eu-west), set a provisional severity of 2, and post one line: "Declaring an incident: checkout probe failing in eu-west since about 14:00; I am IC; investigating." Use the incident-channel template. A probe that fails five times with 10-second 504s is a real customer symptom, not a flaky check.
  3. Take the IC role explicitly and ask for the others: page Mara as operations lead, ask the support lead to be on the channel, and ask Idris to arrange a communications lead. You will not be debugging; if you find yourself in logs in the first ten minutes, ask someone else to take that.
  4. Glance, don't dive. Open the one dashboard the alert links to, with a single question: how big is this and where? Two minutes, not twenty.
  5. Start the clock on a scribe: "Mara, please start a timeline in the channel with the time of the page, 14:07."

A good IC's first message is short and says who is in charge. The classic trap is a quiet fifteen minutes of solo investigation, with nobody knowing it is an incident and the first status update already late.

2What the dashboards show

Reveal the next update: 14:10 
From the "Is checkout healthy right now?" dashboard:
  Order submission success (5 min):   global 87.9%   eu-west 70%   us-east 99.9%
  Burn rate, 5 min: 24x     Burn rate, 1 h: 7x      (fast-burn page has NOT fired yet)
  eu-west: gateway p99 10.0 s (timeout)   orders p99 8.4 s
  eu-west orders: DB connection-pool waiters 38 (pool size 20 per pod), db_ms normal
  Payments: normal in both regions. Inventory: normal.
  Support: 14 chats in the last 10 minutes: "my order failed", "my card was charged?"
  Errors started about 13:52.

You now have a first picture. What do you conclude, and what do you ask people to do? State two or three competing hypotheses, and say which action you will take before you know which is right.

Compare with a worked answer

What the data says. One region is failing, one is healthy: that is a strong hint of what changed or differs in eu-west, and it limits the blast radius to about 40% of traffic (global 87.9% = 0.4 × 70% + 0.6 × 99.9%). The orders service is slow in eu-west, waiting for database connections (pool waiters 38) while the database's own query time is normal, which looks like something is holding connections open. Payments and inventory are fine. The slow-burn arithmetic is a lesson in its own right: the 5-minute burn rate is 24× (12.1% errors divided by the 0.5% budget) while the 1-hour rate is only about 7× (18 bad minutes in a 60-minute window: 12.1% × 18/60 ≈ 3.6%), because the 1-hour window is still filling. The 14.4× page needs a 1-hour error ratio of 7.2%, which at 12.1% takes about 36 minutes of errors, so it would not fire until about 14:28, roughly another 18 minutes. The probe saved you time.

Severity. About 30% of eu-west submissions failing, 12% globally, and "my card was charged?" in support chats. Make it severity 2, and note it can rise.

Hypotheses (the IC lists, the operations lead tests, and the IC does not go and look):

  1. A change in eu-west. Ask: "What changed in eu-west in the last two hours? Deploys, config, flags, migrations?"
  2. The eu-west database is unhealthy or its connection limit is reached.
  3. A dependency or network problem specific to the region.

The action before you know: prepare the cheap, reversible mitigations in parallel: have Mara check the deploy log, the database dashboard and the last config change; ask the communications lead to draft the first customer message; and ask a third person to find out how long it would take to drain eu-west and whether us-east has room. The goal is that when the cause shows up, the lever is already within reach. Also record evidence cheaply before changing anything: a screenshot of the dashboard and five failing trace ids.

Post the first status update now, internally: "Impact: about a third of eu-west checkout attempts fail (about 12% globally) since about 13:52. Cause unknown. Next update 14:40." Declare a 30-minute cadence and keep to it even when there is nothing new.

3A deploy, and a clock

Reveal the next update: 14:11 
Mara, in the channel:
  "Deploy log: orders v2.41.0 went to eu-west at 13:47, finished 13:52 (rolling, 6 pods).
   It includes migration 0087 (changes the totals columns).
   The pipeline is in its 25-minute bake. Wave 2 (us-east) starts AUTOMATICALLY at 14:12.
   No dashboards flagged the bake as failing because the bake only checks pod readiness."
Time now: 14:11.

It is 14:11. What do you do in the next sixty seconds, before anything else, and why?

Compare with a worked answer

Stop the spread first. The same change is about to reach the other 60% of traffic, the only healthy region, in one minute. Pause the pipeline now (chapter 12: waves cap the blast radius, but only if something stops them). It is cheap, reversible and takes seconds. Say it in the channel: "Mara, pause the orders pipeline now. Nobody ships anything else until I say."

  • You are not yet certain the deploy is the cause. It does not matter: a pause costs nothing but a delayed release, and being wrong the other way costs the healthy region. This is the asymmetry that should drive early decisions.
  • Declare a change freeze in the channel as a side effect: other teams must not deploy checkout dependencies during the incident, so that you know what changed.
  • Then the real decision: roll back eu-west to v2.40.3. Correlation (errors from 13:52, just as the rollout finished, in the only region with the new version) is strong. Prefer rollback to an investigation (mitigate before you diagnose). Before telling anyone to roll back, ask the one question that matters, and ask it aloud: "Can this version roll back safely? Does the migration allow v2.40.3 to run?" That question is exactly what a release checklist should have answered, and today it may not have.

Mara confirms the pipeline is paused at 14:11, one minute before the next wave began. In the hindsight of the postmortem, that is luck, not design: write it down as such.

4The rollback fails

Reveal the next update: 14:19 
Mara, 14:19:
  "Rolled back eu-west orders to v2.40.3 at 14:15 (kubectl rollout undo).
   The new pods are not becoming Ready. Logs:
     ERROR column "legacy_total" of relation "orders" does not exist
   Migration 0087 dropped legacy_total as part of this release. v2.40.3 still reads it.
   Rollout is stalled: 2 old-version pods failing, 4 v2.41.0 pods still serving
   (maxUnavailable held it there). eu-west orders capacity is now 4 pods instead of 6.
   Success rate in eu-west: 66%."
  Idris: "Can't we just re-run the migration backwards?"

The rollback you counted on cannot work as it stands, and eu-west is slightly worse. What are your options? Which do you choose, and which questions do you ask before choosing?

Compare with a worked answer

The migration removed a column in the same release that stopped using it. The previous version still needs that column, so the standard recovery path is closed. This is the failure that the expand, migrate, contract discipline of chapter 12 exists to prevent: N and N−1 must interoperate. The lesson for now: when your plan A breaks, say so aloud and move to the next option calmly.

OptionTimeRiskVerdict
Stop the stalled rollback, return to six v2.41.0 podsminuteslow; restores capacityDo now: the broken pods contribute nothing
Reverse migration 0087 on the production database, liveunknown; has it a down script? has it been tested?high: untested schema change on the primary, during an incidentNot now; Idris's suggestion is natural but this is the riskiest lever
Write and ship a forward fix (re-add the column, or fix the connection hold)40+ minutes with review and a staged deploymediumStart it in parallel, owned by someone who is not the IC
Drain eu-west: shift its traffic to us-east at the global load balancerminutes plus DNS / connection drainmedium: us-east must absorb 1/0.6 = 1.67× its loadBest immediate mitigation, if headroom exists
Disable the new behaviour with a flagsecondsnoneNot available: the totals change shipped without a flag

Decide: (1) stop the rollback and return to the previous state, (2) drain eu-west gradually, and (3) in parallel, have a developer prepare the forward fix. Before draining, ask the capacity question aloud: "Operations lead, what are CPU and database load in us-east now, and will 1.67× fit?" Capacity planning for an N+1 or a lost-region case exists for this moment (chapter 15, chapter 10). Suppose us-east is at 48% CPU: 48% × 1.67 ≈ 80%, which is high but survivable, especially if non-essential features are switched off first.

To Idris: "Reversing the migration live is a last resort: nobody has tested it, and it can lose data. Let's get customers out of the broken region first." The IC protects the group from the second bad idea. Say what you are not doing and why, so the channel does not think it was forgotten.

5Mitigation works, then something else breaks

Reveal the next update: 14:38 
14:38. You started draining eu-west at 14:24; weight reached 10% of traffic by 14:34.
  Global order success: 97.3%   (eu-west users now served from us-east)
  us-east:   CPU 88%  gateway p99 1.9 s (SLO 2 s)  pricing DB 92% busy  cache hit rate 71% (normally 95%)
             order success 98.4% and falling
  Mobile app: failed submissions are retried up to 3 times automatically
  An internal update went out at 14:10. No customer-facing update has gone out yet; the first is due at 14:40.
  Support: 140 chats open. Several say "charged but no order".

The worst is over in eu-west, but us-east is now running hot and the next update is due. What are the two or three things you do now, and what do you write in the status update?

Compare with a worked answer

Name what has happened: the mitigation worked (global success up from 87.9% to 97.3%), and it has transferred pressure to us-east, whose headroom was less than hoped (pricing cache hit rate 71% because eu-west's customers' items were not warm there; this is the cold-cache effect of chapter 11). Customers' retrying mobile app multiplies the load. Do not let a recovered region collapse under a recovery.

  1. Protect us-east. Switch off the nonessential: recommendations and "recently viewed" off, extended price caching, and ask the app team to cut the client retry count to 1 or to apply backoff via remote config. Hold the drain at its current level; do not drain the last 10% yet.
  2. Assign the new problem to an owner: a second operations lead for us-east, so that Mara can stay on eu-west. The IC keeps the whole picture.
  3. The status update, on time: the channel's cadence is a promise. Write for readers who do not know your systems:
14:40 UPDATE - Checkout
Impact: From about 13:52 to 14:34 around 30% of checkout attempts from European customers
  failed. Since 14:34 European customers are served from our US region and checkout works
  for about 98% of attempts. Some customers may see a pending charge for an order that
  did not complete; these are authorisation holds and will be released. No action is needed.
What we are doing: keeping European traffic on the US region while we prepare a fix;
  we have turned off some non-essential features to keep checkout fast.
Next update: 15:10, or sooner if anything changes.
  • Impact in customer terms, no internal names, no blame, no speculation about cause, no promises of a time to fix. Say what you know, what you are doing and when the next update is.
  • This is the first customer-facing update, 30 minutes after declaration, which is late. That is a small failure to note; the cause is that no communications lead was assigned. Fix it now: appoint one.
  • Send it through a channel that does not depend on the broken system (a status page on separate infrastructure, not the shop's own domain).

Notice also the line "charged but no order", which shows up in support: you will need to learn more about it. Hand that to someone as a named task now (see the next update).

6Customers charged, orders missing

Reveal the next update: 14:55 
14:55. The support lead and a payments engineer have looked:
  Between 13:52 and 14:34 the payments service authorised 212 cards in eu-west whose
  order never got written (the orders pod timed out after the authorisation).
  The authorisations are HOLDS on the cardholder's account, not captures. They expire on their
  own (up to 7 days, depending on the bank), but until then customers see reduced available funds.
  There is no job today that voids an authorisation when its order fails.
  Mara: "The forward fix is ~40 min away, tests running."
  Support: asking whether to refund customers.

This is a new kind of harm, separate from availability. How does it change severity, roles and priorities? What do you tell support, and who does the work?

Compare with a worked answer

Raise the severity (or at least say the severity is under review) and say why aloud: customers are experiencing financial effects. Money and data problems are worse than slow checkouts, whatever the percentage of affected requests. In a hospital this is the patient who deteriorates quietly while the loud one gets the attention.

  1. Create a second work stream with its own owner, and keep it away from the mitigation: a payments engineer owns "the 212". They list the affected authorisations from the payments logs (the wide events carry order_id and psp_status), check with the PSP whether holds can be voided in bulk by the API, and plan a reconciliation job. The IC does not do this; the IC asks for an estimate and a time for the next report.
  2. Support gets one honest sentence to use: "Some customers saw a charge that is a temporary hold for an order that did not complete; it is being released; no money has been taken." Do not refund yet: a refund on a hold that never captured can create a second problem. Only say it if the payments engineer has confirmed that these were authorisations, not captures. Keep the facts separate from guesses.
  3. Add it to the next public update: "A number of customers may see a temporary hold; we are releasing them."
  4. Don't forget what is still burning. The mitigation on the main stream remains in place and the forward fix is in progress. Check back on both at fixed times (say, 15:10 and 15:30).
  5. Note the gap for later: authorisation before the order is written, with no compensation, is a design weakness. Not now. Write it on the scribe's "to discuss after" list so that it does not distract and is not lost.

15:32. You have been IC since 14:07: one hour twenty-five minutes. Before that you were on
  call from 09:00 and have had no break. State of play:

  Idris: "Diane (secondary IC, just started her shift) can take over at 16:00."

You are tired, and the most delicate part of the incident, shipping the fix, is coming. Do you hand over at 16:00, or now? Write the handoff you would give: what must the next person know?

Compare with a worked answer

Hand over before the fix deploys and while you can still answer questions, not after it. Tired people make worse decisions about riskier steps; swapping the IC during a quiet stretch is better than during a crisis (chapter 16: plan handoffs and rest). Waiting until 16:00 makes the swap coincide with the deploy. Ask Diane to take over at about 15:40, after the 15:40 update, and stay on the call for ten minutes.

Handoff structure (spoken, then written in the channel so everyone sees the same thing):

HANDOFF - checkout incident, from IC1 to Diane (IC), 15:38
1. Impact now: eu-west mostly drained, us-east order success 99.3% (global about 98% while the last 10% of eu-west traffic still fails). Customers in
   Europe are served from us-east. 212 authorisation holds to release.
2. What happened (one line): orders v2.41.0 (migration 0087) exhausted DB connections
   in eu-west; rollback failed because the migration dropped a column v2.40.3 needs.
3. Mitigations in place (and how to undo each):
   - eu-west weight 10%, undo by raising the weight in the load balancer config (step 10 -> 25 -> 50 -> 100).
   - recommendations off, extended price cache, mobile retries cut to 1 (flag set in remote config).
   - orders pipeline paused, change freeze announced in #eng-all.
4. In flight: forward fix. Owner: Mara. Plan: re-add column, then rollout v2.40.3 in one zone,
   bake 10 min, then the rest. Deploy start about 15:45. Abort if pool waiters or errors rise.
5. In flight: reconciliation of 212 holds. Owner: Sam (payments). Needs review before run.
6. Decisions pending: when to restore eu-west traffic (suggest 10 -> 25 -> 50 -> 100 with
   10 min bakes, watching us-east cache hit rate); who tells the PSP.
7. Roles: Mara ops (eu-west), Jun ops (us-east), Priya comms, Lee scribe, Sam payments.
8. Clocks: next public update 15:40 (Priya has the draft), then every 30 min.
9. Watch-outs: the pricing cache is cold in us-east; mobile retries; we got lucky that the
   pipeline paused one minute before wave 2.
10. I am staying on until 15:50 for questions, then off.
  • Say aloud in the channel "Diane is now IC" and have Diane acknowledge. Ambiguity about who is in charge is dangerous, especially with many people on the call.
  • Hand over the state and the open decisions, not a story. Put the clocks (what is due when) in the handoff.
  • Everyone else with a role also needs rest and a replacement plan: ask the managers whether anyone else has been on since the morning. Fatigue is a contributing factor in many incidents, and tired-at-hour-six is the norm, not the exception.

8Recovery and declaring the end

Reveal the next update: 16:48 
Timeline since the handoff (Diane is IC):
  15:46  Column re-added in eu-west (migration 0088, expand-only). No errors.
  15:51  Rollout of orders v2.40.3 in eu-west zone a: pods Ready, success 100% on probe.
  16:03  Remaining zones rolled back. eu-west orders pool waiters back to 0.
  16:05  Reconciliation job voided 212 holds; PSP confirms 212 voids, 0 errors.
  16:08  Traffic to eu-west raised 10% -> 25% (bake 10 min) -> 50% -> 100% at 16:38.
  16:45  Order success global 99.9% for 7 minutes; eu-west gateway p99 0.7 s;
         us-east pricing cache hit rate 95%. Recommendations back on at 16:40.
  Idris: "Can we call it resolved and go home?"

Diane is the IC now; imagine you are still on the channel as the previous IC and are asked what you think. When is an incident over? What must be true, what do you announce, and what stays open?

Compare with a worked answer

An incident ends when impact has stayed gone, not when the first graph turns green (chapter 16). Reasonable end criteria:

  • The SLI has been back at target for a window that exceeds the quickest recurrence (here, 30 minutes at the full restored load, not 7), including at peak load, since eu-west just returned to 100% at 16:38.
  • Every mitigation has been undone or recorded with an owner: traffic weights restored (yes), recommendations on (yes), mobile retries back to normal (not mentioned: ask), pipeline pause lifted (not yet: the orders pipeline stays paused until v2.41 is re-released with a compatible migration), change freeze lifted.
  • The harm has been repaired or has an owner: the 212 holds were voided and confirmed. Check that no other kind of orphaned state exists (stock reservations without orders, for example): ask for a quick query.
  • Evidence is saved (dashboards, trace ids, deploy log, logs of the failing window) and the scribe's timeline is complete.

So the right answer for Idris is "almost": hold the incident open until about 17:10 (30 minutes of clean full-load operation), then announce:

17:10 RESOLVED - Checkout
Between 13:52 and 14:34 about 30% of checkout attempts from European customers failed.
Service was restored to normal for all customers by 16:38. A temporary hold appeared on
some customers' cards for orders that did not complete; all of them have been released.
We will publish a review within 5 working days.
  • Close the channel only after the follow-ups are written down: the postmortem owner and date, the open actions (the orders release stays blocked), and a note to the on-call who is next ("orders pipeline is paused; do not resume it").
  • Thank people by name in the channel. Incidents are costly to the people in them.
  • Schedule the review for the next few days while memory is fresh, and tell people to write down their own recollections beforehand, separately.

9Write the postmortem skeleton

Without opening the worked answer, write the skeleton of the postmortem using the sections you know: summary, impact, timeline, contributing factors, what went well, where we got lucky, action items. Draft the first two or three contributing factors in blameless wording. Which sentences from your own timeline would you rewrite so they do not blame anyone?

Compare with a worked answer

The structure from chapter 17, with this scenario filled in (fictional, and deliberately brief; use the postmortem template for the full text):

TITLE   Checkout failures in eu-west after orders v2.41.0 release, 3 March (fictional)
STATUS  Draft for review. Owner: IC1. Review meeting: Friday 11:00.

SUMMARY (3 sentences)
  A release of the orders service with a database migration caused database connection
  exhaustion in eu-west, failing about 30% of European checkouts for 42 minutes. Rolling back
  was not possible because the migration had removed a column the previous version needs.
  Traffic was moved to the US region, and the fix was a forward migration plus a rollback.

IMPACT
  Duration 13:52-14:34 full impact; full restoration 16:38. About 3,000 failed submissions
  (illustrative: 1.2 per second for 42 minutes). 212 customers saw a temporary card hold for an order that did not complete.
  Error budget: 30 days is about 25.9 million submissions, so the budget
  (0.5%) is about 130,000 bad ones; this incident used about 2% of it.

TIMELINE (UTC+0, from the scribe; facts only)
  13:47 v2.41.0 begins in eu-west   13:52 errors begin   14:07 probe alert pages
  14:11 pipeline paused (one minute before wave 2)   14:15 rollback started
  14:19 rollback found blocked by migration 0087     14:24 drain of eu-west begins
  14:34 drain at 90%   14:55 holds discovered   15:46 forward migration   16:38 restored

CONTRIBUTING FACTORS (no single root cause)
  1. The migration removed a column in the same release that stopped reading it, so the
     previous version could not run against the new schema (expand/contract not followed
     for this change; our checklist asks about it but does not enforce it).
  2. The new code held a database connection open during an external call, exhausting
     the pool at regional peak; the load test used a smaller pool size and lower traffic.
  3. The wave pipeline's bake step checked pod readiness only, not user-facing SLIs,
     so it was set to continue automatically after 25 minutes while the alert needed longer.
  4. No feature flag or kill switch for the new totals behaviour.
  5. The payment authorisation happens before the order write, with no compensating void,
     so a failure between the two leaves a hold on the customer's card.
  6. Drain capacity: us-east had less headroom than assumed, with a cold pricing cache,
     and mobile clients retried failed submissions three times.

WHAT WENT WELL
  Probe caught it before the burn-rate alert; the IC declared early and assigned roles;
  the pipeline pause protected the other region; the team did not run an untested reverse
  migration under pressure; status updates were sent (the first external one late).

WHERE WE GOT LUCKY
  The pipeline paused one minute before the us-east wave. The incident began at mid-afternoon
  traffic, not at a peak. Authorisations are holds, not captures, which made repair easy.

ACTION ITEMS  (type: detect / mitigate / prevent;  owner = role;  due;  tracked in the backlog)
  P1 prevent  Enforce expand/contract in CI: block releases whose migration drops or renames
              a column read by the previous version. Owner: platform. Due: 2 weeks.
  P1 detect   Bake step evaluates the order SLI of the new wave and halts automatically
              (see automated canary analysis, chapter 12). Owner: release eng. Due: 4 weeks.
  P1 mitigate Void authorisation when order creation fails (compensation + reconciler job).
              Owner: payments. Due: 4 weeks.
  P2 prevent  Add connection-pool wait time to the standard dashboards and an alert.
  P2 mitigate Flag for new totals; practise rollback in the load test with the migration.
  P2 mitigate Client retry budget in mobile app; a game day to rehearse region drain.

QUESTIONS FOR THE REVIEW
  What did the engineer who approved the migration know at the time? How does our checklist read
  to someone shipping on a busy day? Where else could a migration make rollback impossible?

Rewriting for blamelessness. The timeline is facts and times. Compare:

Blameful or hindsight wordingBlameless rewording
"The developer dropped the column without checking rollback.""The migration dropped the column in the same release. Our release checklist asks about rollback safety but nothing enforces it."
"The on-call should have noticed the deploy sooner.""The alert fired 15 minutes after impact began; the deploy was not shown on the alert's dashboard."
"Human error in not testing.""The load test used a smaller connection pool than production, so it did not reproduce the exhaustion. The test environment's configuration differs from production in these ways: ..."
"The IC took too long to update status.""The first customer-facing update went out at 14:40 because no communications lead had been assigned at declaration; we now assign one at declaration."

The test, from chapter 17: for each sentence ask "what did the people involved know at that moment, and why did their action make sense?" and "what in the system let this cause harm?" Action items should change the system (an enforcement, an automation, a guard rail) instead of reminding people to be careful.

Self-check rubric

Tick what your own responses (before reading the answers) covered. An experienced engineer would hit most of these.

  • Declared early, in a dedicated channel with a provisional severity, rather than investigating alone first.
  • Took the IC role and assigned the others (operations, communications, scribe) and did not debug personally.
  • Used the region split to bound the blast radius and asked what changed.
  • Stopped the spread first by pausing the pipeline before the next wave, even before the cause was certain.
  • Preferred mitigation over diagnosis (rollback, then drain) and kept diagnosis in parallel with someone else.
  • Recognised that the rollback failure was a plan A failure, listed alternatives with risks and avoided the untested reverse migration.
  • Checked capacity before draining, and noticed that a mitigation can move the problem (cold cache, retries).
  • Sent status updates on the promised cadence, in customer terms, without blame or speculation, through an independent channel.
  • Re-assessed severity when a new kind of harm appeared and gave it its own owner.
  • Planned a handoff before the risky step, with state, mitigations and how to undo them, pending decisions, clocks and roles.
  • Defined end criteria (impact gone for a window, mitigations undone or owned, harm repaired, evidence saved).
  • Wrote a blameless postmortem with contributing factors, what went well, luck, and system-changing action items.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"When a customer-facing alert fires I declare the incident straight away, take the commander role and get an operations lead, a communications lead and a scribe, because the commander's job is decisions, not debugging. I look for what bounds the damage: one region failing and one healthy points to a change, so the first act is to pause the pipeline before it reaches the healthy region. Then I mitigate before I diagnose: roll back, and when the rollback fails because the migration was not backwards compatible, move to the next lever, which is draining the region, after asking whether the other region has the headroom. I say out loud what I am not doing, like running a migration backwards live. I keep to the update cadence in customer terms, give a new kind of harm, here card holds, its own owner, and hand over before the risky deploy rather than during it. I'd declare the end only after impact has stayed gone, and I'd write the review in terms of contributing factors and fixes that change the system."

Notice the shape: declare → roles → bound the blast radius → mitigate, with a second option ready → communicate on a clock → hand off → declare the end on evidence → learn blamelessly.

Key takeaways

  • Declare early. Taking the commander role and assigning the others is the first mitigation.
  • Stop the change from spreading before you know the cause; pausing is cheap.
  • Mitigate first (rollback, drain, shed), diagnose in parallel with someone else, and have a second option for when the first fails.
  • A mitigation moves load: check headroom, caches and retries before you use it.
  • Communicate on a clock, in customer terms, over a channel that does not depend on the broken system.
  • New harm, especially money or data, changes priorities and deserves its own owner.
  • Hand off at a calm moment with state, undo steps, open decisions and clocks.
  • End the incident on evidence, and write the review as contributing factors and system fixes without blame.
