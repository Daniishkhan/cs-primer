Prepare for a Flash Sale

A flash sale is the rare outage you can schedule: you know the day, the hour and roughly the size. That is a gift, and teams still waste it. This studio plans one: forecast it, find the bottleneck, test honestly, add headroom, decide who is let in and what you will switch off, and agree in advance when to call it off.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is not the only right design; it is one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point. All numbers on this page are assumptions to reason with, not measurements.

📐 The brief

The shop from the observability studio has announced a flash sale: a limited stock of a few popular items, starting at a fixed time on a fixed day, announced by email and push notification.

  • Normal peak is 300 requests per second at the gateway, about 10 order submissions per second. The sale is expected to bring 20× normal traffic for about one hour: 6,000 requests per second.
  • The checkout path is gateway, cart, pricing, inventory, payments (an external PSP with a contractual limit of 300 authorisations per second), orders (a relational primary) and an email queue. Services run on Kubernetes with a horizontal autoscaler; today each service has 6 pods.
  • Two earlier sales are in the history: they peaked at 9× and 12× the normal peak.
  • Three weeks remain. The sale is a marketing commitment; it cannot be moved because you are not ready.

Draw on queueing theory, load testing and capacity, overload and load shedding, orchestration and incident response. The load generators in the toolbox let you rehearse the test plan on a laptop.

Your notes

Keep decisions and numbers here.

1Forecast from previous events

The business says "20×". How do you check that number against history, and what do you actually size for? Which inputs are uncertain, and who can reduce the uncertainty?

Compare with a worked answer

Treat the 20× as a hypothesis from marketing and test it against your own data (chapter 10: plan inorganic launches separately from organic growth).

  • Find what drives the peak. A sale peak is set by audience size and how sharply it arrives, not by last month's traffic. Take the two past events and the size of the audience that was mailed or pushed. Suppose they were 9× and 12× normal, and the audience for this sale is about 1.7× the last. A first estimate is 12 × 1.7 ≈ 20×, which agrees with marketing: good, two independent routes landed together.
  • Shape matters more than the average. Pull the per-second request rate from the last sale's gateway logs. Typically the rate reaches its maximum within a minute of the start, stays high for 10 to 15 minutes, then decays. Plan for the peak second, not the hourly mean, and note how few seconds it takes to arrive: 300 to 6,000 requests per second in under a minute.
  • Mix changes. In the last sale the share of requests that were order submissions rose from 3.3% to about 4%, and a few SKUs took most orders. At 20× that means about 6,000 × 4% = 240 orders per second (against about 10 normally, a 24× jump in the part of the path that writes), concentrated on a handful of rows.
  • Uncertainty. Treat 20× as the median, and ask the 90th percentile: audience reach, a social media post going viral, a bot swarm. Size and test to 25× (7,500 requests per second, 300 orders per second) and have a plan, stages 6 and 7, for beyond it.
  • Who can narrow it. Marketing can say how many people receive the push, at what second and whether a social post is coming. Finance can give the stock count, which caps the number of successful orders (a sale of 5,000 items cannot sustain 240 orders per second for an hour; the interesting load is the first minutes, then "sold out" polling).

That last point is a design lever: the stock count bounds the useful work, and the long tail of the hour is mostly people refreshing a sold-out page, which can be served from a cache or a static page.

2Per-dependency capacity and the bottleneck

For each dependency in the checkout path, estimate what it can do at its SLO and compare it with the load at 20× and 25×. Which one is the real ceiling? Which has the longest lead time to fix?

Compare with a worked answer

Capacity is not peak throughput; it is the rate at which the SLO still holds (chapter 10). The figures below are assumed outputs of earlier tests, the kind of table you build by load-testing each dependency alone.

  • The bottleneck is the PSP, the dependency where the contract, not your fleet, sets the cap, and where the lead time is longest. Open the conversation now: ask for a temporary limit of 500 per second for the sale window, ask what the provider does beyond the limit (reject with 429, or queue), and ask for a sandbox that behaves like production.
  • The second bottleneck is serialisation on one row, which no amount of pods fixes. It is a design problem: stage 3 puts numbers on it.
  • Little's law on the connection pools (chapter 8): in-flight calls = arrival rate × time in system. Payments at 240 per second with a 0.4 s PSP call has 240 × 0.4 ≈ 96 calls in flight; a pool of 150 works. If the PSP slows to 2 s, in-flight demand becomes 480, the pool saturates, requests queue inside your process, and a provider slowdown has become your outage. Size the pool, set a per-call timeout (shorter than the user's patience) and let the breaker open.
  • Re-test the bottleneck after fixing it, because it moves (chapter 10). With the PSP raised and inventory split, the orders database is next, and then the gateway's connection count.

3Queueing maths for the checkout path

Take the inventory decrement: each transaction holds the row for about 4 ms. Compute its service rate, its utilisation at 240 orders per second and the time in system. Then do the same after splitting the stock into four buckets. What does bursty arrival do to the answer?

Compare with a worked answer

The service rate is μ = 1 / 0.004 s = 250 per second, and the arrival rate is λ = 240 per second (chapter 8).

Designλρ = λ / μTime in system W = 1 / (μ − λ)
Normal day, one row10 /s0.044.2 ms
Sale, one row200 /s0.8020 ms

Sale, one row260 /s1.04no steady state: the queue grows without bound
Sale, stock split in four buckets60 /s per bucket0.241 / (250 − 60) = 5.3 ms
  • Beyond the mean. Orders do not arrive evenly. In the first seconds they arrive in bursts, and Kingman's formula says waiting scales with (ca² + cs²) / 2. A Poisson stream has ca² = 1; if the burst makes it 4 and service stays at 1, the factor is 2.5. The queueing delay at ρ = 0.8, which M/M/1 puts at ρ/(1 − ρ) × 4 ms = 16 ms, becomes roughly 40 ms; at ρ = 0.96 it grows from 96 ms to about 240 ms. The sale start is the worst case for the average-based estimate.
  • Why the split works: the stock of an item is divided into, say, four counters; an order picks one at random and decrements it; if that bucket is empty it tries another. Four independent rows each see a quarter of the load. The price is complexity at the very end (the last few items may be stranded in the wrong buckets, so you rebalance when any bucket empties) and a check that the total never goes negative, which is a correctness property, not a performance one.
  • The path as a whole. Latency adds along the path and each hop has its own knee. If every service runs at ρ = 0.9 the response takes about 10× its unloaded time at each hop (chapter 8: 10× at 90%), and a 40 ms path becomes roughly 400 ms before the first retry. So the pod count in stage 4 targets about 50% to 55% utilisation, not 90%, and the inventory fix targets 24%.
  • Utilisation and failure. Plan for a lost zone: with three zones, losing one lifts the others by 3/2 = 1.5×. Staying at or below 80% after a zone loss means running at about 53% before (chapter 8, chapter 10).

4Pre-scaling versus autoscaling lag

Your autoscaler can add pods. Why not let it? Add up the reaction time, compare it with how fast the load arrives, and decide how many pods to have running at the start. What do you keep the autoscaler for?

Compare with a worked answer

Autoscaling is a control loop with delay (chapter 14). Add the delays on the path to a new, ready pod (typical values, which you should measure on your cluster):

StepDelay
Metric scraped and aggregated15 to 30 s
Autoscaler evaluation periodabout 15 s
Pod scheduled, image pulled, process started10 to 60 s
Readiness probe passes5 to 15 s
If no node has room: provision a node first2 to 5 minutes
Scale-up policy limits (for example, at most double every minute)6 → 12 → 24 → 48 pods takes 3 steps, 3+ minutes

That is one to six minutes before the first extra pod serves traffic, against a load that goes from 300 to 6,000 requests per second in less than one minute. In that time requests queue, time out, retry and make it worse (stage 7 and chapter 11).

  • Size for the peak and start there. At 200 requests per second per pod (the load-tested capacity) and a target of 80% or less after losing a zone: remaining pods needed = 6,000 / (200 × 0.8) = 37.5, so 38 after the loss; with three zones that is 19 per zone, 57 pods in total. Normal utilisation then is 6,000 / (57 × 200) ≈ 53%. For 25×, 7,500 requests per second: 47 remaining, so 24 per zone and 72 pods in total. Pre-scale to the 25× size for the first hour and shrink afterwards.
  • When: scale up hours before the start (stage 8 puts it at T minus 3 h), check that pods are Ready and spread across zones, and freeze deploys. Reserve the nodes (or confirm the node group's max and your cloud quota) well before: a quota error is a discovery you want to make three weeks ahead.
  • Connections warm up too. New pods need their database and PSP connection pools filled, JIT warm-up and caches populated. Send synthetic traffic to them before the sale.
  • The autoscaler stays on as a safety net above the pre-scaled floor: minimum replicas at 57 and a maximum well above, so an unexpected 30× still adds capacity (late, but it helps for the long tail of the hour).
  • The database does not autoscale. Pre-scaling the stateless tier moves the bottleneck down to the primary, which is why stage 2 matters more than the pod count.

5Load-test plan and pre-warmed caches

Design the load test: shape, generator, data, environment, and the questions it must answer. Which mistakes produce a comforting but false result? Then design the cache pre-warm: what happens to the database if every cache is cold at the first second?

Compare with a worked answer

Plan. The test must answer: at what rate does each tier stop meeting its SLO, which dependency is the first to fail, and does the system recover when the load falls?

  • Open-loop generation. Real customers do not wait for a slow reply before arriving. Use a generator that sends at a fixed arrival rate (k6's constant-arrival-rate or ramping-arrival-rate executors, wrk2 with -R, or vegeta) and measures from the intended send time; a closed-loop tool slows down when the server does and hides the worst latencies (coordinated omission, chapter 10). Check the generator's own health: dropped iterations in k6 mean it could not keep up.
  • The shape: a staircase of 1×, 5×, 10×, 15×, 20× and 25×, ten minutes at each so queues and caches settle, then a spike test (300 to 6,000 in 60 seconds, to match the real arrival), then a 60-minute soak at 20× to find leaks and the effect of a full hour, then ramp down and watch recovery.
  • Production-like: the request mix (94% browse, 4% order submissions, the rest cart updates), the skew (80% of orders on three SKUs, the thing that creates the hot row), the data size (a full catalogue and an orders table of production size, because index sizes change plans), the same instance types and the same configuration. Use real PSP behaviour in the sandbox if it models latency and rate limits; otherwise a stub with a recorded latency distribution and a 300 per second limit that returns 429 beyond it.
  • Never load-test the real PSP, or any third party, without a written agreement.
  • When: twice. Three weeks before, to find the bottleneck with time to fix it; and a week before, after the code freeze, to confirm the fixes. Test the fixes by repeating the failing step.
  • Pass criteria written beforehand: order SLI at or above 99.5%, p99 order latency under 2 s, no pod restarts, queue age under limit, and after the load ends, latency returns to baseline within five minutes.
  • The lies: a test environment smaller than production; empty tables; uniform SKUs; a warm cache that production will not have; the generator on the same machine as the server; checking only averages.

Caches pre-warmed. Pricing normally serves 95% of 4,000 lookups per second from cache, so its database sees about 200 reads per second. With a cold cache every lookup misses: 4,000 reads per second against a 400 per second database, a tenfold overload at the worst moment, and the retries make it worse (a cold-cache version of the metastable failure, chapter 11).

  • Fill the caches before the start: replay the top few thousand SKUs and the sale items at least an hour ahead and top up in the last 30 minutes, from a script that reads the same keys the application reads.
  • Use request coalescing (one in-flight load per key; others wait for it) so a miss on a hot item produces one database read, not 2,000.
  • Stagger TTLs with random jitter so all entries do not expire at the same second; for the sale window extend them, or serve stale entries while refreshing in the background.
  • Restarting pods or a cache node during the sale removes the warm state: no deploys, no restarts. If a cache node dies, the system must tolerate its share of misses (a test case).

6Admission: a queue-based waiting room

Even at 25× the PSP is the ceiling. What do you do with demand beyond what the path can serve? Design a waiting room: who gets in, in what order, at what rate, and how does the number of people waiting relate to how long each waits?

Compare with a worked answer

If demand exceeds capacity, something will wait or fail. The choice is whether the waiting happens in an orderly queue that you control, or as timeouts and retries inside your system (chapter 11: shed early and choose what to shed). In the hospital analogy this is triage at the door instead of in the corridor.

  • Where it sits: in front of checkout (and only checkout). Browsing and the sold-out page are cheap and cacheable; order submission is the expensive, scarce part. The waiting room is served from a static, edge-cached page that polls (or holds a WebSocket) for a place in line, so it costs almost nothing per waiting user.
  • The admission rate comes from the bottleneck, not the demand: PSP limit 300 per second, keep 30% in reserve for retries, so admit load that produces about 210 orders per second. If 60% of admitted users complete an order and stay about 3 minutes, that is 350 users admitted per second, and Little's law gives 350 × 180 ≈ 63,000 users in checkout at once, at roughly one request every ten seconds each, near the 6,000 requests per second we sized for. The numbers are assumptions; the point is the chain from the bottleneck to an admit rate.
  • The wait: if 150,000 people arrive in the first two minutes (1,250 per second) and 350 per second get in, the queue grows by about 900 per second and the queue is about 108,000 people when the surge ends (900 × 120 s), so the last arrival waits 108,000 / 350 ≈ 310 seconds, about five minutes (draining all 150,000 takes about 430 seconds from the start). Little's law again: people waiting = arrival rate × wait. Show an honest estimate ("about 5 minutes") and a position; a progress bar that lies destroys trust.
  • Fairness: admit first-come, first-served by arrival time of the first request, with a signed token so a refresh does not lose the place and a reload does not cut the line. Tokens expire in a few minutes, so idle holders free their slots.
  • Abuse: bots are the largest user group in many sales. Rate-limit by device and account, require login before the queue, and give each person one token. This is a security design problem (course 7) as well as a capacity one.
  • The rate is a dial, turned by humans or by an automatic controller on the SLI: slow the admit rate when order latency or PSP errors rise, speed up when they fall (the same AIMD idea as an adaptive concurrency limit). A rate of zero is the abort switch of stage 8.
  • Failure of the waiting room itself must fail closed to a static page, not open. If the checkout is unprotected when the room dies, you have built a trap.

7Degradation plan, feature switches and retry budgets

List what you can turn off or simplify, in order, with what the customer sees, who flips it and what triggers it. Then work out how retries could turn a 20% PSP slowdown into an outage, and what budget prevents it.

Compare with a worked answer

Decide the degradation ladder now, build the switches now and rehearse them, because nobody designs well during an outage (chapter 11, architecture chapter 18 for flags). Each rung gives up something nonessential to protect something essential.

  • Every switch is a feature flag read at request time, defaulting to the safe side, flippable by the war room without a deploy, and listed on the runbook. Test each in the load test: turning it on must reduce load, not just change behaviour.
  • Shed by priority: order submission and payment confirmation outrank browsing. Drop requests whose client deadline has already passed (chapter 11).

Retry budgets. The PSP allows 300 per second and the sale drives 240. Suppose the PSP slows by 20% and some calls time out. If each failed call is retried up to three times by the payments service and again by the orders service and the client, the worst case multiplies, as in chapter 11: 4 attempts at each of three layers can reach 4³ = 64 attempts per request in the worst case. Even a modest fraction retried pushes 240 per second well past 300: the PSP answers 429, which triggers more retries, and the system is in a retry storm that persists after the PSP recovers (a metastable failure).

  • Retry in one layer only, the one closest to the call that failed, with exponential backoff and jitter.
  • A budget of about 10%: retries may add at most 10% to the request rate. At 240 per second that is 264 at most, under the 300 limit; over three layers the budget composes to about 1.13 ≈ 1.33 rather than 64.
  • Idempotency keys on every order and authorisation request, so a retry cannot charge twice; this is also what makes the pending-payment mode safe.
  • Deadlines propagate. Each call passes its remaining time; a service that cannot finish in it returns early rather than doing work no one will read.

8War room, roles and abort criteria

Who is in the room, and what is each person watching? Write the go/no-go checklist for the hours before, and the numeric criteria at which you stop the sale. Who has the authority to press the button?

Compare with a worked answer

A planned peak is an incident you hope not to have; run it with the incident roles from chapter 16 from the start, so that if something goes wrong nobody is deciding who is in charge.

  • Roles: an incident commander (not debugging; owns decisions and the abort), an operations lead for each hot area (platform, checkout services, data), a communications lead (status page, marketing, support), a scribe, and a named contact at the PSP on a live channel. Include a business owner who can authorise "pause the sale". Dashboards: "Is checkout healthy right now?" (studio 1), the PSP dashboard and the waiting room.
  • Timeline: T minus 24 h: freeze deploys; T minus 3 h: pre-scale, warm caches; T minus 60 min: go/no-go; T minus 15 min: join the call, announce who is in charge, test the flip of one switch; T zero: eyes on the first minute; T plus 60 min and decay: scale down gradually, keep the room until the queue is empty and orders are reconciled.
  • Go/no-go checklist: pod count and zone spread correct; caches warm (hit rate above 95% on the sale SKUs); PSP limit raised and a contact online; stock buckets seeded and totals match; every switch tested in the last 48 hours; alert routing tested with a test page; rollback of the last release verified; on-call people rested and a replacement named.
  • Abort criteria, agreed before and written in the plan so nobody argues during it: any oversell (stock goes negative, a correctness failure, stop at once); order SLI under 97% for 5 minutes (a burn rate of 6× against a 99.5% budget is 3%, so 97% is 6× and 5 minutes catches the fast case); PSP errors above 10% for 3 minutes, or a PSP rate-limit response sustained; p99 order latency over 5 s for 5 minutes; database replication lag growing for 10 minutes; or the commander's judgment.
  • What aborting means: admit rate to zero, static pause page for the sale items, drain the checkout in flight, keep the rest of the shop up. Aborting a sale is a business embarrassment; a half-working sale that double-charges customers is far worse. Decide beforehand that the commander may press it without asking.
  • After: reconcile orders against payments and stock, write down surprises while fresh, and run a short review (chapter 17) even if all went well: what went well, and where were you lucky?

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"I'd forecast from the last two sales scaled by audience, get about 20 times, and size and test for 25 because the peak arrives within a minute. Then I'd find the ceiling by testing each dependency alone: the payment provider is capped at 300 a second by contract, so I'd ask for more today because the lead time is theirs, and the hot inventory row serialises at about 250 a second, so I'd split the stock into buckets. The queueing maths shows why: at 96% utilisation that row takes 100 milliseconds, 25 times a normal day. I'd pre-scale the stateless tier an hour ahead to survive the loss of a zone, because an autoscaler needs minutes and the load needs seconds, warm the caches, and run an open-loop load test shaped like the real arrival. Beyond capacity I'd put a waiting room at the front of checkout with the admit rate set from the payment limit, and prepare an ordered ladder of switches with a retry budget. And I'd write the abort criteria beforehand, including that any oversell stops the sale."

Notice the shape: forecast → bottleneck → queueing maths → headroom → honest test → admission → degradation → abort criteria.

Key takeaways

  • Check marketing's multiplier against history, and plan for the peak second and a higher percentile.
  • The ceiling is often outside your fleet or in one serialised row; find it dependency by dependency and start the long-lead conversations first.
  • Time in system is 1 / (μ − λ): the last 10% of utilisation costs most of the latency. Burstiness multiplies it.
  • Autoscaling reacts in minutes; a sale arrives in seconds. Pre-scale, and keep the autoscaler as a safety net.
  • Test open-loop, with production-like data and skew, early enough to fix what you find.
  • A cold cache is an overload event. Warm, coalesce and jitter.
  • Put the queue where you control it: a waiting room with an admit rate derived from the bottleneck.
  • Cap retries with a budget, build and rehearse the degradation ladder, and write the abort criteria before the day.
