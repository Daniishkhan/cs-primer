Postmortems & Learning from Incidents

The incident is over, the status page says resolved, and everyone is tired. What happens in the next week decides whether this was a one-off or the first of a series. This chapter is about turning an outage into understanding: why blameless reviews work, why "root cause" is a misleading phrase, how to write a postmortem people actually read, and how to make sure its action items do not die in a backlog.

Why this matters

An incident is an expensive experiment you did not choose to run. You have already paid for it in user pain and lost sleep; the postmortem is how you collect the return. Organisations that skip it, or that treat it as a hunt for the guilty, pay again: the same class of failure comes back, and the people who know the most learn to say the least. Chapter 16 gave you the scribe's timeline; this chapter shows what to build with it.

Blameless does not mean accountability-free

A postmortem (also "incident review" or "retrospective") is a written analysis of an incident: what happened, why it made sense to the people involved at the time, and what will change. A blameless postmortem is one that deliberately does not name an individual as the cause. The idea was popularised in software by John Allspaw, then at Etsy, in a 2012 essay on blameless postmortems and a just culture, drawing on decades of safety practice in aviation and medicine.

Analogy: airline safety reporting

Aviation learned long ago that pilots will report near misses only if reporting does not end their careers. Accident investigators ask what the crew saw, what they believed, and what the cockpit and procedures made easy or hard. The aim is a safer system for the next crew, not a verdict on this one. Software teams copy the stance: assume everyone acted reasonably given what they knew, and ask how the system allowed a reasonable action to produce this outcome.

Where it breaks down: aviation has mandatory investigators and a regulator. Your postmortem depends on the goodwill of your own management; if leaders react to a review by punishing the engineer it names, the whole practice quietly collapses.

Why blame destroys information

Blame is not just unkind; it is bad engineering. If people expect punishment, three things happen. They hide details ("I just ran the normal script" omits that the script's confirmation prompt had been disabled for months). They stop reporting near misses, which are the cheapest lessons you will ever get. And the review stops at the human: "operator error" feels like an answer, so nobody asks why the system let one wrong command reach production at once. A person who pressed the wrong key was given a keyboard in which that key was possible.

Blameless is not the same as consequence-free. You still expect people to be honest and to fix what they own. It does mean separating two questions: how did this happen (a systems question, answered openly) and is someone persistently careless (a management question, rarely the answer, handled elsewhere).

Contributing factors, not a single root cause

The phrase root cause promises that if you dig deep enough you find one thing which, had it been different, would have prevented the incident. Sometimes the story is that simple. For real production systems it usually is not, because the defences are many and each is imperfect. Richard Cook's short paper "How Complex Systems Fail" (1998) makes the point forcefully: catastrophe in a complex system needs multiple faults to line up, each necessary but only jointly sufficient, and the system is always running with latent flaws. He also argues that post-hoc attribution to a single root cause is fundamentally mistaken, because the "cause" is chosen by the people looking back, and many of the contributing conditions exist in every normal day.

The better vocabulary is contributing factors: the conditions that, together, let the failure happen and made it harder to detect or fix. A good review lists them all and does not rank them by blame.

The Swiss-cheese model

James Reason's model of accident causation, widely used in aviation and healthcare safety, pictures each defence as a slice of Swiss cheese. Every slice has holes (a missing test, a rushed review, an alert with a bad threshold), but the holes are in different places, so a hazard usually hits a solid part of some slice and stops. An accident happens on the rare occasion that holes in all the slices line up.

Two lessons follow. First, "which hole was the root cause?" is the wrong question; each aligned hole was necessary. Second, the model reframes safety as maintaining many cheap layers rather than demanding that one layer be perfect. That is the same logic as redundancy in chapter 1, now applied to defences instead of components.

The five whys can mislead

Asking "why?" five times is a useful prompt, but it produces a single chain, and chains hide branches. Each "why" has several true answers; picking one is a judgment, and the temptation is to stop at a satisfying human error. Use whys to generate factors, then draw the web, not the chain.

First stories and second stories

When an incident happens, the first account is usually short and satisfying: someone made a mistake, a component broke. In the language of resilience engineering (Cook, Woods, Dekker and others) this is the first story. It is easy to tell, and it asks nothing of the organisation. The second story asks what the people involved knew, what the tools showed them, what pressures they faced and why their action seemed sensible at that moment. It is longer, less comfortable, and it is where the learning is.

Two thinking errors push you back to the first story:

  • Hindsight bias: once you know the outcome, the signals look obvious and the right action looks inevitable. At the time, the log line was one of ten thousand and the dashboard looked ordinary.
  • Counterfactuals: statements about what did not happen. "The engineer should have checked the unit" is a counterfactual; it states an action that was not taken and implies it was easy. Replace it with a question: what made not checking reasonable? (Was the unit shown anywhere? Does everyone skim config diffs? Did the tooling make checking easy?)

A practical test for wording: if a sentence contains "should have", "failed to", "carelessly" or "forgot", rewrite it as a description of the situation: "The diff view displayed ttl: 10 without a unit."

A template that works

A postmortem should be short enough to read and complete enough to teach someone who was not there. This structure covers what most teams settle on:

SectionContents
SummaryThree sentences: what happened, how bad, how it ended. Readable by an executive.
ImpactWho was affected, for how long, in numbers: failed requests, users, money if known, SLO budget consumed.
TimelineTimestamped events in one time zone, from the scribe's log. Include detection and each mitigation decision, and mark where the time went.
Contributing factorsThe web, not a chain. Each factor stated as a condition of the system, no names.
What went wellDefences that worked. These are as important to preserve as holes are to fix.
Where we got luckyThings that could have made it worse but did not by chance. Luck is a hole that nobody has noticed yet.
Action itemsEach with an owner, a due date, a ticket link and a type: prevent, detect or mitigate.
Lessons and open questionsWhat surprised us; what we still do not understand.

An example postmortem (fictional)

This incident is fictional

The company, service names, people and numbers below were invented for teaching. Any resemblance to a real postmortem is coincidence. Watch how the wording describes the system and never names a culprit, and how "where we got lucky" is used.

Postmortem: checkout slowness after a cache setting change

Fictional company: Acme Shop. Date: a Tuesday in March, 10:31 to 11:09 UTC. Severity: SEV2. Status: reviewed; action items in progress.

Summary

A configuration change intended to shorten a product-price cache lifetime from 10 minutes to 10 seconds in staging was applied to production as a value of 10 meaning seconds, rather than 600. The cache hit rate collapsed, the primary database saturated, and checkout latency and errors rose for 38 minutes until the change was reverted. No data was lost.

Impact

About 11% of checkout requests failed for 22 minutes at the peak, then about 2% for 16 minutes more: roughly 7,700 failed requests out of about 2 million per day. The checkout SLO is 99.9% over 30 days, a budget of about 60,000 failed requests. This incident used about 13% of that month's budget.

Timeline (UTC)

  • 10:31 Config change pricing.cache.ttl from 600 to 10 is merged and pushed to all three regions.
  • 10:34 Database CPU begins to climb. No alert fires: the CPU alert threshold is 90% for 10 minutes.
  • 10:41 Fast-burn SLO alert pages the on-call engineer (time to detect: 10 minutes).
  • 10:45 On-call declares SEV2 and takes the IC role; roles assigned by 10:48.
  • 10:52 Ops lead notes the recent config push; the cache dashboard shows hit rate fell from about 95% to under 20%.
  • 10:55 IC decides to revert the config rather than investigate further. Revert is pushed, region by region.
  • 11:02 Revert complete. Errors fall but remain elevated: retries and cold cache are sustaining load. Batch jobs are paused.
  • 11:09 SLI back within SLO. Status moved to monitoring; resolved at 11:40 after a stable half hour.

Contributing factors

  • The configuration key had no unit in its name, and the review diff displayed ttl: 10. Reviewers reasonably read it as 10 minutes because the previous value, 600, had never been seen with its unit either.
  • Configuration was applied to all regions at once. The deploy pipeline stages code through a canary, but configuration changes bypass it.
  • The pre-merge test suite contained no check on cache hit rate or on load against the database.
  • The database alert fired on sustained CPU, not on saturation relative to its SLO, so the first signal was user-facing.
  • Clients retry failed price lookups three times without a retry budget, which amplified load on the database during the incident.
  • The runbook for "checkout slow" named the cache but not the config system, so the first ten minutes were spent on the wrong layer.

What went well

  • The SLO burn-rate alert paged the right person within ten minutes of the change.
  • The incident was declared with roles within seven minutes, and the status page cadence was kept.
  • The IC chose to revert before the cause was confirmed, which shortened the incident.

Where we got lucky

  • It happened on a Tuesday morning, not during a promotion; the same change at peak would have been a SEV1.
  • The database did not fail over automatically. A failover under this load would have added several minutes of write unavailability.
  • The engineer who wrote the cache layer happened to be online and recognised the hit-rate drop at once.

Action items

#ActionTypeOwnerDue
1Rename duration keys to include units (ttl_seconds) and reject unit-less values in the config schema (CHK-412)PreventMei, platform+3 weeks
2Roll configuration out through the same staged waves as code, with automatic halt on SLI divergence (CHK-413)Prevent, mitigateTomas, deploy tooling+8 weeks
3Add a database saturation alert tied to the checkout SLO; page on it (CHK-414)DetectPriya, SRE+2 weeks
4Introduce a client retry budget on price lookups (CHK-415)MitigateSam, pricing team+6 weeks
5Update the checkout-slow runbook to include recent config changes as step one (CHK-416)DetectPriya, SRE+1 week

Notice what the example does not say: nobody "mistyped", nobody "failed to check". Notice also the shape of the actions: the biggest (staged config rollout) closes a layer, the cheapest (rename the key) closes a hole, and none of them is "be more careful".

Making action items stick

Most postmortems end with a list of actions, and a depressing fraction of those are never done. A few habits help:

  • Every item has one named owner and a date, and lives in the team's normal ticket system, not only in the document.
  • Prefer fewer, stronger actions. Removing a hole (a type system that rejects unit-less durations) beats adding a warning (a reminder in a wiki). Reminders and training decay; automation does not.
  • Prioritise explicitly against feature work, using the error budget from chapter 2 as the argument: "this incident burned 13% of the month's budget and has five more ways to happen."
  • Review completion at a regular meeting, and report the percentage of priority items closed by their due date. If it is low, the problem is the process, not the engineers.

Near misses and resilience

The cheapest lessons are the ones where nothing broke. A near miss is an event that could have caused an incident but did not: a failed deploy caught by a canary, a restore test that took twice as long as planned, an alert that fired on the wrong host and was dismissed. Treat the important ones like small incidents, with a short review, because the same holes that nearly aligned today will align one day.

A closely related idea comes from resilience engineering (Erik Hollnagel, Sidney Dekker, David Woods, among others): study not only how things fail but how they usually succeed. Real work differs from the procedures written for it. Work-as-imagined is what the manual says people do; work-as-done is what they actually do, adapting to missing tools, ambiguous instructions and time pressure. Most of the time those adaptations are why systems work at all. Postmortems that compare the two, and ask why the procedure was not followed instead of who ignored it, find the workarounds your system silently depends on.

Go deeper: reading Cook's "How Complex Systems Fail" optional

Cook's paper is only a few pages and worth reading in full (see chapter 21). Several of its eighteen short points bear directly on this chapter: complex systems contain many latent failures at any given time; they run in a degraded mode as normal; catastrophe requires multiple failures; hindsight biases post-accident assessments of human performance; and safety is a property of the whole system, produced continuously by people adapting, not a component you install. Each is easy to nod along to and harder to put into practice when someone asks, on Monday morning, whose fault it was.

Try it yourself

Take an incident you were part of, or the one in chapter 16's timeline. Write only the contributing factors section, with these rules: no names, no "should have", at least five factors, and at least one that exists in your system today and that you have not fixed. Then ask a colleague who was not involved whether the factors would let them explain the incident to someone else. If any factor can only be stated by naming a person, ask what in the system made that person's action reasonable.

Distributed systems connection

In a distributed system the interactions are the failures. Retries multiplying load (retry storms), a config change spreading to every node (chapter 13), and a missing blast-radius boundary (chapter 15) are all contributing factors whose individual parts look harmless. The Swiss-cheese picture is not a metaphor for this; it is nearly a description.

Key takeaways

  • Blameless reviews assume reasonable action and ask how the system let it cause harm. Blame hides details, suppresses reporting and ends the inquiry at "human error".
  • Prefer contributing factors to a single root cause. Reason's Swiss-cheese model and Cook's "How Complex Systems Fail" both say accidents need several defences to fail together.
  • Tell the second story: what the people knew and why their actions made sense. Watch for hindsight bias and counterfactual wording such as "should have".
  • Use a consistent template: summary, impact, timeline, contributing factors, what went well, where we got lucky, action items with owners and dates.
  • Prefer actions that remove holes (automation, guard rails) over reminders. Track completion and prioritise using the error budget.
  • Study near misses and the gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done; they are the cheapest lessons.

Check your understanding

A draft postmortem concludes: "Root cause: engineer mistyped a config value." What is the strongest objection?

  1. Engineers never make typing mistakes
  2. The conclusion is too long
  3. It stops at the person and omits the system conditions (no unit, no staging, no test) that let one typo reach all users
  4. Root causes should be written in the passive voice
People will always make slips; the question is why a slip had such a large effect. Every layer that failed to catch it is a contributing factor with a possible fix, whereas "be more careful" fixes nothing.

In Reason's Swiss-cheese model, what does an accident correspond to?

  1. Holes in several independent defensive layers lining up, so a hazard passes through all of them
  2. One slice with an especially large hole
  3. A slice that was never installed
  4. The cheese being too old
Each layer is imperfect but the holes are in different places, so most hazards are stopped. Accidents are the rare alignment. This is why strengthening several layers is more robust than perfecting one.

Which sentence from a postmortem best reflects a blameless, second-story style?

  1. "The on-call engineer should have noticed the alert sooner."
  2. "Carelessness in review let the change through."
  3. "Ops failed to escalate in time."
  4. "The alert fired at 10:41 and was one of eleven pages that hour; its text did not mention the config push."
The last option describes the situation the responder was in, which is what you need to improve. The others are counterfactual or judgmental, and imply the right action was obvious (hindsight bias).

What is the purpose of a "Where we got lucky" section?

  1. To thank people who responded well
  2. To record conditions that could have made the incident worse but did not by chance, since those are undiscovered holes
  3. To argue that the incident was not serious
  4. To record which engineer was on call
Luck is a defence you cannot rely on. Naming it turns a near-worse outcome into an action item before it happens for real.

A team's postmortems consistently end with actions like "improve monitoring" and "be more careful with configs", and few are ever completed. What change would help most?

  1. Write longer postmortems
  2. Skip postmortems for small incidents
  3. Make each action specific, owned, dated and tracked, preferring changes that remove a hole (automation, validation) over reminders
  4. Assign all actions to the on-call engineer
Vague actions have no completion criterion and no owner. Automated guard rails keep working after attention fades; reminders do not. Tracking completion shows whether the process itself is healthy.
