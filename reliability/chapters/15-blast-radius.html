Blast Radius: Cells, Shuffle Sharding & Static Stability

You cannot prevent every failure, but you can decide in advance how many customers a failure is allowed to reach. This chapter is about designing that number: partitioning a service into cells, assigning customers to shards so that a poison customer hurts almost nobody else, keeping the data plane alive when the control plane is not, and finding the hidden dependencies that quietly undo all of it.

Why this matters

The arithmetic of chapter 1 had a sting in its tail: redundancy helps only against independent failures, and the failures that hurt are the correlated ones: the bad deploy, the poison request, the shared dependency. Replicas share a blast radius. The only reliable defence is to make the blast radius itself small and known by construction. This is the structural counterpart to the procedural safety of chapter 12: waves limit how much of the fleet a change can reach; cells and shuffle shards limit how much of the customer base a fault can reach.

Blast radius as a design goal

The blast radius of a failure is the set of users, requests or components it can affect. Designers rarely ask for "no failures"; they ask a question with a number in it: if the worst plausible thing goes wrong in this component, what fraction of customers notice? A service that can fail only for 1/20th of its customers at a time has a far better worst case than one that can fail for all of them, even if the two have the same overall failure rate, and it is far easier to operate: smaller incidents, smaller diagnosis space, smaller apologies.

You met the idea in the S3 2017 case: a mistyped command removed more capacity than intended from subsystems that had grown into very large, shared fleets, and recovery was slow partly because those subsystems had not been restarted at that scale for a long time. The lasting engineering response, as the public post-incident summary described, was to partition such services into smaller independent units. This chapter goes from that intuition to the mechanisms.

Analogy: isolation wards and watertight compartments

A hospital does not run one open ward. It has isolation rooms, separate wings, independent ventilation zones and fire doors. An infection or a fire is not prevented from starting, but its reach is bounded by design. A ship is divided into watertight compartments for the same reason: a hull breach floods one compartment, not the ship. Nobody believes the compartments make holes less likely; they make holes survivable.

Where it breaks down: a ship's bulkheads are passive. Software compartments need active routing (something must decide who goes where) and the router itself is a shared component that can sink the whole ship.

Cell-based architecture

A cell is a complete, independent instance of the service: its own load balancers, application servers, caches, queues and databases, serving a fixed subset of customers. Nothing in a cell talks to another cell's internals. A thin cell router in front maps each request to the one cell that owns the customer (usually by looking up or hashing a customer identifier) and forwards it.

What makes cells work

  • Fixed maximum size. This is the key design decision. A cell has a known maximum capacity (users, requests per second, data volume) that has been load-tested and operated at that size. To grow the service you add cells, you do not enlarge them. The consequence is that no cell is ever larger than something you have already proved works, so growth never takes you into untested territory (the S3 lesson), and the blast radius of any cell-level failure is capped at 1/N of customers (more precisely, the fraction a cell holds), and shrinks as N grows. Contrast a single scaled-up cluster whose failure modes change as it grows.
  • Share nothing at runtime. Cells must not call each other, share databases or share caches. If cell 3 can saturate something that cell 4 depends on, you have a cell in name only. Shared infrastructure still exists (the network, the control plane, the identity system); each such item is a new, bigger blast radius and should be listed, not forgotten.
  • A thin, boring router. The router is the one component every request touches, so it sits in series with everything (chapter 1) and must be more available than any cell. Make it as simple as possible: a stateless lookup from customer to cell, with the mapping cached at the edge so that routing continues even if the place that stores the mapping is down. Avoid putting business logic in it. Test it hardest.
  • Cells are the unit of deployment. The waves of chapter 12 map naturally onto cells: deploy to one cell, bake, judge, then the next. A bad release is stopped at one cell, so cells make progressive delivery nearly free.
  • Cells are the unit of recovery. You can drain a sick cell, rebuild it, or move its customers to a spare cell. The operational drill is small and repeatable.

Cell size is a trade-off

Smaller cells give a smaller blast radius but cost efficiency: each cell has fixed overhead (minimum replicas, a database, a warm standby), spare capacity is spread thinly, and a very large customer might not fit in one cell. Larger cells are cheaper but each incident is bigger. There is no universal number. Choose the cell size from a blast radius you can accept ("no single failure may affect more than 5% of revenue"), then check that the overhead is affordable. Three practical complications:

  • Placement. New customers must be assigned to cells, and the assignment should balance load (a few huge customers in one cell make it hot). Keep the mapping explicit and movable, not a pure hash, so you can rebalance.
  • Cross-cell features. Anything that must see across customers (global search, billing roll-ups, admin consoles) needs a separate aggregation path, ideally asynchronous and read-only, and it becomes a shared dependency of its own.
  • The control plane above the cells. Something provisions cells, assigns customers and deploys software. That control plane is exactly what must not sit on the request path of the data plane, as the section on static stability explains.

Common misconception

"Cells are just sharding." Sharding splits data for scale; the shards often share the application tier, the cache, the deployment and the operators' mistakes. A cell is a split of the whole stack, designed explicitly so that a failure of anything inside it stays inside. You can shard without cells; if you do, a bad deploy of the application tier still reaches every shard.

Shuffle sharding

Cells bound the damage to one cell's worth of customers. But what if a single customer's behaviour is the fault, a "poison" customer whose requests crash whatever worker handles them? Inside a cell, with ordinary sharding, that customer takes down its shard and everyone assigned to the same shard with it. Shuffle sharding, described in the AWS Builders' Library article "Workload isolation using shuffle-sharding", is a way to make that overlap almost vanish using combinatorics.

Start with ordinary sharding. Eight workers are split into four shards of two. Each customer is assigned to one shard. If a poison customer takes down both workers of its shard, every other customer in that shard is fully down too. With customers spread evenly, that is one quarter of everyone: 25%.

Now do something different. Give each customer its own pair of workers, chosen from all eight. Customer A gets workers (1, 4), customer B gets (2, 7), customer C gets (3, 6), and so on. The number of different pairs you can choose from eight workers is "8 choose 2":

C(8, 2) = 8! / (2! × 6!) = (8 × 7) / 2 = 28

So there are 28 distinct two-worker shards. A poison customer takes down its own two workers. Another customer is fully down only if it has exactly the same pair, which, with assignments spread over 28 possibilities, happens to 1/28 of customers, about 3.6%, instead of 25%. The AWS article makes precisely this toy comparison (eight workers, shards of two, 28 possibilities, "1/28th" of the impact, seven times better than four ordinary shards at 25%). Most other customers are not hurt at all, and a good fraction share one worker with the poison customer and merely lose half their capacity.

The real power appears at scale, because C(n, k) grows explosively. With 100 workers and shards of 5, there are 75,287,520 possible shards. In Route 53, the AWS article describes 2,048 virtual name servers with each customer domain assigned four of them, which the article quotes as about 730 billion possible shuffle shards. (We compute the exact figure below: 730,862,190,080.) With that many shards, giving each customer a unique one is easy, and the probability that two customers share a whole shard is negligible.

Now the exact calculation of overlaps. If a poison customer has a shard of k workers out of n, the probability that another random shard shares exactly j workers with it is hypergeometric: C(k, j) × C(n−k, k−j) / C(n, k). The script computes this, checks the sums, and verifies it against a seeded simulation of 1,000 customers.

"""Shuffle-shard combinatorics: how likely is it that another customer shares workers with a poison customer?"""
import random
from math import comb


def p_overlap(n: int, k: int, j: int) -> float:
    """P(two random k-subsets of n workers share exactly j workers) - hypergeometric."""
    return comb(k, j) * comb(n - k, k - j) / comb(n, k)

Read the numbers. For 100 workers and 5 per customer, about 77% of other customers share no worker at all with the poison customer, 21% share one (and lose a fifth of their capacity), under 2% share two, and the chance of a complete overlap is 1 in 75 million. The simulation for the toy fleet gives 36 fully-affected customers out of 999 against an expectation of 35.7, and 413 partially affected, close to 12/28 of 999 ≈ 428 (sampling noise). Note the line "classic sharding: 255 of 999 share the poison customer's shard", the 25% the article warns about.

What shuffle sharding needs, and what it cannot do

  • Fault-tolerant clients. The benefit assumes that a customer whose shard is half dead still gets service. That requires clients (or a routing layer) to retry on another worker in their shard, with timeouts and backoff from architecture chapter 17. Without it, one dead worker out of two is a partial outage for everyone who has it.
  • Enough workers and a good assignment. With few workers the combinations are few. Assignment should be deterministic from the customer id (so every router agrees), and for the best isolation constrained so that no two customers' shards overlap by more than a chosen number of workers; Amazon's open-source Route 53 Infima library, mentioned in the article, includes such implementations.
  • A customer-attributable fault. It protects against faults that follow a customer (a poison request, a noisy neighbour, an abusive client, a DoS aimed at one domain). It does nothing against a fault that hits all workers equally, like a bad deploy to the whole fleet. That one needs cells and waves. Combine them: shuffle-shard within a cell, deploy cell by cell.
  • The poison can move. If a poison request is retried, the client will walk through every worker in its shard, killing all of them. Shuffle sharding limits where it can go, but a retry policy that spreads a poison request across the shard is how you lose the whole shard fast. Pair it with request-level protections (timeouts, concurrency limits per tenant, the admission control of chapter 11).

Control plane, data plane and static stability

Almost every large system has two halves with different jobs. The data plane does the work customers pay for: serving requests, forwarding packets, running the instances. The control plane manages the system: creating and deleting resources, changing configuration, scaling, assigning customers to cells, and distributing the changes. The two have different characteristics. The data plane handles far more traffic, is usually simpler, and is where availability matters most. The control plane has more moving parts, more complex operations (create an instance, wire a network, update a database), and is far more likely to be impaired at any moment. It is also used more heavily in the middle of an incident, when everyone is trying to launch capacity at once.

That leads to the design rule of static stability, in the AWS Builders' Library article "Static stability using Availability Zones": a system is statically stable if it continues to operate correctly when a dependency, typically the control plane, is impaired, without needing to make changes. The system has already done in advance everything it needs to survive the failure. The static stability case goes into the public details.

Concrete examples of the discipline:

  • Pre-provision, do not react. If you must survive the loss of an availability zone, the plan "when a zone fails, launch replacement capacity in the surviving zones" depends on the control plane being healthy in exactly the moment everyone else is also trying to launch capacity. The statically stable plan keeps enough spare capacity already running in the other zones. The cost is arithmetic: to survive the loss of one of n equal zones, each zone must be able to take its share of the failed zone's load, so total capacity is n/(n−1) of peak: 200% with two zones, 150% with three, 133% with four. That extra capacity is the premium you pay, and it is why three zones is a common choice.
  • Cache what the data plane needs. A router that needs the customer-to-cell mapping should hold a local copy and keep serving from it if the database that stores the mapping is down. A client that needs a feature flag should hold the last known good value (the fail-static behaviour of chapter 13). A service that needs credentials or certificates should cache them well beyond a short expiry with proactive renewal.
  • No control-plane calls on the request path. If serving a request requires calling a service that "creates" or "configures" something, you have glued the planes together.
  • Test it. Block the control plane in a staging or game-day exercise (chapter 19) and see what breaks. Statically stable on paper and in production are different claims.

Bimodal behaviour and constant work

A system is bimodal if it has two operating modes, a normal one and a fallback one, with different code paths and different capacity needs. Fallbacks feel safe ("if the cache is down, read from the database"), but they are dangerous because the fallback path is rarely exercised, so it is the least tested code in the system, and it is triggered at the worst moment: when something has already failed and load is rising. The AWS Builders' Library article "Avoiding fallback in distributed systems" makes this argument at length. A fallback to the database may need ten times the capacity the database was sized for, so it converts a cache failure into a database failure, the exact amplification of a metastable failure. Prefer designs with one mode that is always the one running; if you do keep a fallback, exercise it continuously.

The positive form of that idea is constant work, from the Builders' Library article "Reliability, constant work, and a good cup of coffee". A system doing constant work does the same amount of work regardless of load or of whether anything changed. The article's analogy is a coffee urn that keeps a large amount of coffee hot, rather than brewing each cup to order: it does the same work whether one person or a crowd turns up, so a rush does not change its behaviour. Applied to software, as I read the article: instead of sending only changes to a fleet (and having the sending rate surge exactly when a lot is changing, or fail to resync after a missed update), send the entire current state on a fixed schedule. The sender and the consumers then do the same amount of work all the time, a missed update heals itself on the next tick, and there is no "mode" that only runs in a crisis. The cost is that you pay for the work even when it is not needed, and you must be able to do the maximum amount of work comfortably. It is the same level-triggered principle as the Kubernetes controllers of the previous chapter: reconcile from state, not from events.

Dependencies: hard, soft and circular

Everything above can be undone by a dependency you forgot. A hard dependency is one whose failure fails your request. A soft dependency is one whose failure you can survive with reduced quality (recommendations missing, a stale price, an unavailable avatar). From chapter 1: hard dependencies multiply, soft ones do not. The design work is to make as many as possible soft, to know the list, and to ask of each one: what is its blast radius, and is it bigger than mine? If your cell depends on a shared service that is not itself cellular, your cells are only as isolated as that service.

The nastiest dependencies are the ones nobody drew. Circular dependencies are the clearest case, because they stay hidden while everything is up and only bite during a cold start, when everything has to come up from nothing:

Real incidents have this flavour. In the Facebook 2021 outage, recovering the network was slowed because the internal tools and even physical access depended on the very network that was down. The AWS us-east-1 December 2021 case is about internal dependencies, including monitoring, that nobody expected to be coupled to the congested network. Practical defences:

  • Draw the dependency graph and look for cycles and for shared dependencies of supposedly independent components. Generate it from traces and service configs (chapter 6), not from memory.
  • Rank tiers. Foundational services (identity, config, DNS, network, secrets) must not depend on anything built on top of them. Lower tiers never call higher tiers.
  • Practise cold starts. Rebuild a cell, or a whole non-production environment, from nothing, regularly. The first time you discover a boot cycle should not be during an incident.
  • Keep the tools that fix things independent. The monitoring, paging, runbook and access systems used during an incident should not share failure domains with what they monitor. Keep a break-glass path (out-of-band access) that you have tested.
  • Timeouts and caches in every hard dependency, and a documented answer to "what happens when this is down for an hour?"

Zones and evacuation

The same logic applies to the physical layout. A cloud region is made of several availability zones (AZs): data centres, or groups of them, with separate power, cooling and networking, close enough for low-latency links. An AZ is a built-in blast radius boundary, at a larger scale than a cell. Zonal isolation means each zone's stack mostly serves requests from within the zone: an instance in zone a talks to a database replica and cache in zone a where possible, rather than a random one in zone b. Failures then stay local, and cross-zone latency and charges shrink. The cost is that a zone must be able to cope alone for a time, so its dependencies need to be present in each zone too.

When a zone is impaired (a power problem, a network fault, a bad dependency that only affects it) the most reliable fast response is an AZ evacuation: stop sending traffic to the sick zone. Load balancers and DNS-based traffic managers can be told to drain a zone (cloud providers offer features for this; the generic idea is a "zonal shift"), and the other zones absorb its load. Evacuation depends on three things already discussed:

  • Capacity. The surviving zones must already hold the spare capacity (the n/(n−1) rule above), because evacuating into a full zone just moves the outage.
  • A data-plane action. Draining should be a fast, simple routing change that works even when the control plane is impaired, which is static stability applied to operations.
  • Practice. A mechanism that is only used in disasters is the least tested one. Evacuate a zone deliberately, in a controlled game day, and measure the time and the effect on latency and error rate. Compare the decision to region failover in multi-region designs: evacuating a zone is far cheaper and faster, which is why you want a design where you rarely need to evacuate a region.

Bringing the pieces together, each layer has its own blast-radius boundary, its own routing component, and its own drill:

Distributed systems connection

Blast-radius design is failure-domain reasoning, the same discipline as placing replicas for a quorum in multi-region designs. Cells are the partitioning of data chapter 16 applied to the whole stack, shuffle sharding trades a little complexity for combinatorial isolation, and static stability is the operational twin of the question "what happens when the coordination service is unreachable?" from the coordination chapter. The common instruction in each: keep the thing that must stay up independent of the thing that changes.

Key takeaways

  • Design the blast radius: decide in advance what fraction of customers any one failure may reach. Replicas share fate; partitions do not.
  • A cell is a complete, independent copy of the stack with a fixed maximum size; capacity grows by adding cells. A thin router maps customers to cells. Cells give a failure cap of about 1/N, make waves natural, and keep every cell at a size you have already operated.
  • Shuffle sharding gives each customer a random subset of workers. With 8 workers and shards of 2 there are C(8,2) = 28 shards, so a poison customer fully disables only about 1/28 (3.6%) of others instead of 25%; at scale (2,048 workers, shards of 4) there are about 730 billion shards. It needs fault-tolerant clients and only helps against customer-attributable faults.
  • Keep the data plane independent of the control plane. A statically stable system survives an impaired control plane by having done the necessary work in advance: pre-provisioned capacity (n/(n−1) of peak to survive one of n zones), cached state and no control-plane calls on the request path.
  • Avoid bimodal designs and rarely used fallbacks; prefer constant work, which behaves the same under every load and heals missed updates on the next tick.
  • Know your hard dependencies and hunt for cycles and shared fate; practise cold starts and keep recovery tools independent of what they repair.
  • Isolate by zone, keep enough spare capacity to evacuate one, and rehearse the evacuation.

Check your understanding

A service has 8 workers and gives each customer a shuffle shard of 2 workers. Roughly what fraction of other customers share the poison customer's complete shard?

  1. 25%
  2. 12.5%
  3. 1/28, about 3.6%
  4. 50%
There are C(8,2) = 28 possible pairs, so a random customer has the identical pair with probability 1/28. Ordinary sharding into four shards of two would put 25% of customers in the poison shard.

Why do cells have a fixed maximum size rather than growing as demand grows?

  1. So that every cell is never larger than a size you have already tested and operated, and so the blast radius of a cell failure is capped; growth is by adding cells
  2. Because larger cells cost more per customer
  3. Because routers cannot handle large cells
  4. Because the cloud provider limits instance counts
A scaled-up shared cluster enters untested regimes as it grows and can fail for everyone. Fixed-size cells keep behaviour known and bound the damage of any single failure.

Which fault does shuffle sharding NOT protect against?

  1. One customer sending requests that crash workers
  2. A bad release deployed to every worker in the fleet
  3. One tenant saturating its workers with traffic
  4. A DoS attack against one customer's domain
Shuffle sharding limits the overlap between customers, so it helps when the fault follows a customer. A bad deploy hits all workers equally. For that you need cells and progressive delivery (chapter 12).

You want a three-zone service to keep serving at peak load if one zone is lost, and to do so without launching anything. How much total capacity must be running, as a multiple of peak?

  1. 1.0
  2. 1.33
  3. 2.0
  4. 1.5
After losing one of three zones, the remaining two must carry the whole peak, so each needs 50% of peak, and the total is 3 zones × 50% = 150% of peak, i.e. n/(n−1) with n = 3. Four zones would need 133%.

Why is a rarely used fallback path often dangerous?

  1. Because fallbacks are slower than normal paths by design
  2. Because it uses extra memory
  3. Because it is the least tested code, and it is triggered during failure when load is rising, often needing capacity the fallback target was never sized for
  4. Because fallbacks cannot be monitored
A fallback creates a bimodal system whose second mode runs rarely. For example, a cache failure that sends all reads to the database can overload the database and turn a partial failure into a total one. Prefer one always-running mode, or exercise the fallback regularly.

What is the main danger of a circular dependency between three infrastructure services?

  1. It makes requests slower in normal operation
  2. It is invisible while all are running, but after a full outage none can start first, so recovery is blocked
  3. It doubles the cost of the services
  4. It breaks TLS
Cycles surface during cold starts, the worst time to learn about them. Defences are tiering foundational services, drawing the real dependency graph, practising cold starts, and keeping recovery tools independent.
