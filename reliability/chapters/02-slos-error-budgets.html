SLIs, SLOs & Error Budgets

Chapter 1 gave you the arithmetic of availability. This chapter answers the harder question: what exactly should you measure, how reliable should you promise to be, and what do you do when you are more or less reliable than that? The answer is a small, powerful vocabulary (indicator, objective, budget, burn rate) that turns arguments about "stability versus features" into a shared number.

Why this matters

Without explicit targets, reliability is decided by whoever shouts loudest after the last outage. Product wants to ship; operations wants to freeze; neither has a principled way to say "enough". SLOs give a team a measurable definition of "working well enough that users do not leave", and error budgets turn the remaining slack into a resource that can be spent deliberately on launches, migrations and experiments. This is the central idea of the SRE approach, and every later chapter on alerting, deploys and incidents refers back to it.

The vocabulary

Three terms get mixed up constantly, so fix them now. They come from Google's Site Reliability Engineering book (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy, 2016) and its sequel The Site Reliability Workbook (2018), and the industry has largely adopted them.

SLI (service level indicator)
A measurement of how well the service is doing, expressed as the proportion of events that were good: good events / valid events. Always a number between 0% and 100%.
SLO (service level objective)
A target for an SLI over a time window: "99.9% of valid requests succeed over a rolling 30 days". An internal goal that guides decisions.
SLA (service level agreement)
A contract with a customer that names consequences (usually refunds or credits) if a target is missed. A business and legal document, not an engineering one.

Analogy: vital signs and treatment goals

In the emergency department, a patient's heart rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation are indicators: numbers that say how the patient is doing. A treatment goal such as "oxygen saturation above 94%" is the objective; it exists to guide decisions (give oxygen, escalate). The hospital's published promise that "most patients are seen within four hours" is closer to an agreement with consequences if it is repeatedly missed. Clinicians do not stare at one vital sign; they pick a few that predict how the patient will fare, and they know which are cheap to read and which are not.

Where it breaks down: a patient's vital signs are checked individually, whereas an SLI aggregates millions of requests, so a single sick request vanishes into the percentage. SLIs tell you about the population, not about any one user.

An SLI is a ratio of good events to valid events

The definition is deliberately mechanical so that two people given the same logs compute the same number. You decide three things: what counts as an event (usually a request), which events are valid (the denominator), and which of those are good (the numerator).

An availability SLI, concretely

A well-formed specification names the events, the filter, the success criterion and the measurement point:

Availability SLI for the public API
  Events:      HTTP requests received by the load balancer for /api/*
  Valid:       all of them, except health checks and requests from the
               partner load-test range
  Good:        response status is not 5xx (4xx counts as good: the client erred)
  Measured at: the load balancer access log, per-minute counts

Each line is a decision. Excluding health checks stops a flood of trivially passing probes from inflating the number. Counting 4xx as good says "the service behaved correctly when it rejected a bad request", which is the usual choice, with exceptions: if your own bug turns valid requests into 400 or 401 responses, or if you are shedding load with 429 and users are being refused, those requests are not good in any sense a user would accept, and the SLI should count them as bad.

A latency SLI is also a ratio

Latency feels like it needs a different treatment: "p99 under 300 ms". But recall from networking chapter 13 that percentiles are awkward to aggregate (chapter 4 shows exactly why). The SRE Workbook recommends the same shape as before. Pick a threshold and count the fraction of requests that met it:

Latency SLI for the public API
  Events:      the same valid requests as the availability SLI
  Good:        response status is not 5xx AND the time from the load balancer
               receiving the request to sending the last byte is <= 300 ms
  Measured at: the load balancer, from a histogram with a bucket boundary at 300 ms

Then an SLO such as "99% of valid requests are good, over 30 days" is the percentile statement in disguise ("the 99th percentile is under 300 ms"), but it is built from counters, which are cheap to add up across servers and windows. It also forces you to make failed requests count: a request that returned an error after 5 ms is not a "fast" success. Many teams use two thresholds, for example 99% under 300 ms and 99.9% under 1 s, because a single threshold ignores how bad the tail is.

Other kinds of SLIs

Request/response services are the easy case. Choose SLIs that reflect what each kind of system owes its users:

SystemTypical SLIsExample good event
Interactive API or web pageAvailability, latency, qualityNon-5xx within 300 ms; full (non-degraded) response
Data pipeline / batchFreshness, coverage, correctnessA dashboard row refreshed within the last 15 minutes
StorageAvailability, durability, latencyA read returns the bytes that were written
Message queueDelivery latency, lossA message consumed within 10 s of being enqueued

"Quality" deserves a note: a service that returns an empty recommendations list instead of an error is "available" and also broken. If degraded answers matter to users, either count them as bad in the SLI or give them their own indicator.

Where to measure

The same request can be observed at several points, and each point is blind to different failures. This is a design choice with real consequences, because an SLI that cannot see an outage cannot protect you from it.

  • Server-side metrics are the cheapest and easiest. Their blind spot is large: if the process crashes, if requests wait in the kernel's accept queue, or if they never arrive because of a routing or load balancer fault, the server counts nothing, and the SLI looks perfect during an outage. "Perfect SLI, angry customers" almost always means the SLI was measured too far inside.
  • Load balancer (or API gateway) logs see every request that reaches your front door, including time spent queueing and every backend failure the user would have seen (networking chapter 14). They are a strong default: one place, consistent for all services behind it, and independent of your application code. They still miss failures before the balancer, and a down balancer produces no logs at all.
  • Client-side measurements (real-user monitoring in browsers and apps, plus synthetic probes from outside your network) see what users see, including DNS, TLS handshakes and device-specific problems. They are also noisy (users' networks, ad blockers, old devices), harder to ship securely, and delayed. A few synthetic probes running the critical journey from several outside locations are cheap insurance against the "front door is down" blind spot.

A practical recipe is to take the load balancer as the primary SLI source, add black-box probes for the few journeys that matter most, and use server and client metrics for diagnosis rather than as the headline number. The point is that the choice is made deliberately and written down, and that you know which failures your SLI cannot see.

Choosing the target: from users, not from history

The tempting way to set an SLO is to look at last quarter's graph, see 99.97%, and declare "our SLO is 99.95%". Resist it. Current performance is an accident of your architecture and your luck, not an expression of what users need. If you copy it you lock in whatever mediocrity you happen to have, you give up the ability to notice when you are over-investing, and you will defend a number nobody asked for.

Work from the other direction:

  1. List critical user journeys. "Search for a product", "check out", "see my order status". An SLO is attached to a journey, not to a server.
  2. Ask what users notice and what they tolerate. Does a failed request get retried silently by the app? Does a 2-second page load make users leave? How does failure compare with the alternatives they have? Product and support have data here; use it.
  3. Check what your dependencies allow. By chapter 1's arithmetic, you cannot exceed the product of your hard dependencies without redundancy or fallbacks.
  4. Pick a number you can defend, then iterate. The SRE book's advice is to start simple and revise it. Few SLOs, loosely held, refined as you learn.
JourneyWhy users careIllustrative SLO
Checkout submitRevenue; a failure may mean a lost customer99.95% non-5xx; 99% under 800 ms
Product searchFrequent, but a retry is cheap99.9% non-5xx; 99% under 400 ms
Recommendation carouselNice to have; the page works without it99% returns something within 1 s
Nightly report exportWanted by 8 a.m.; not interactive99% of reports ready by 8 a.m.

These numbers are illustrations, not recommendations. The important thing is their spread: different journeys deserve different targets, and the SLO on a dependency-heavy journey should be looser than the SLO on its tightest dependency.

100% is the wrong target

It is tempting to aim for perfect reliability, but it is almost never right. Users cannot distinguish 100% from 99.99%, because their own phone, network and browser fail more often. The cost of each extra nine rises steeply (chapter 1), and a target of 100% means no change is ever acceptable, because every deploy carries risk. A team that cannot change the system cannot fix it either. Choose a number deliberately below 100% and treat the gap as something to use.

Windows: rolling or calendar

An SLO is meaningless without a window: "99.9%" of what period? A bad hour matters differently inside a day, a month or a year. There are two common designs.

Rolling (for example 28 or 30 days)Calendar (month or quarter)
Behaviour after an incidentBudget recovers gradually as the incident ages outBudget resets at the boundary
IncentivesSteady: there is no special day to dump risk onPerverse: ship recklessly right after the reset, freeze at the end
Fit with businessNeeds explanationMatches reporting and SLAs, which are usually per month
Operational feelAlways answers "how are we doing over the last month?""3 days left, 80% spent" is very easy to act on

Many teams use a rolling window of around four weeks for their internal SLO (a whole number of weeks keeps weekly traffic patterns balanced) and a calendar window for the contractual SLA. Too short a window and a single incident dominates and the number is noisy; too long and it forgets slowly, so the team keeps paying for a fixed bug for months.

The error budget

If the SLO is 99.9%, then 0.1% of valid events are allowed to be bad. That allowance is the error budget:

error budget = 1 − SLO

It can be expressed in whichever unit is convenient. For a 30-day window and a 99.9% SLO:

  • In time: 43.2 minutes of complete unavailability (chapter 1's table).
  • In requests: if the window sees 10,000,000 valid requests, 10,000 may fail.
  • As a fraction: "we have used 35% of this month's budget".

The shift in mindset is that the budget is not only a limit but a resource. A team with budget left can deploy more often, run risky migrations, do a load test in production, try a new dependency, or run a chaos experiment (chapter 19). A team that has burned its budget should invest in reliability instead. A service that consistently ends the window with 95% of its budget unspent is probably too cautious: it could ship faster or be cheaper. (It might also have an SLO that is too loose to bind anything. Check that first.)

Try it below. Switch incidents on and off and add your own; watch the line fall, and notice how a few short, severe incidents can outspend a whole month of steady background errors.

Error budget policy

A budget is only useful if something happens when it runs low. The error budget policy is a short document, agreed in advance by engineering, product and whoever owns the business outcome, that says what the team will do at which level. An example in the spirit of the SRE Workbook:

Budget remaining (over the window)Agreed action
More than 50%Business as usual. Ship features; consider planned risk (migrations, experiments).
25% to 50%Review recent burn. Slow rollouts; extra review on risky changes.
Under 25%Pause risky launches. Reliability work (the causes of recent burn) takes priority.
ExhaustedFreeze feature releases. Only fixes for reliability and security ship until the window recovers. Any single incident that used a large share (say 20% or more) gets a postmortem.

The policy's power comes from being agreed before it is needed. When the budget is gone and a launch deadline looms, the argument is not "do you care about stability?" but "we all agreed what happens now", and an exception requires a named senior person to sign it. It also needs an escape hatch (a severe security fix is not blocked by a freeze), and it needs to be about the system, not about blaming a team: the budget measures what the service did to users, whoever caused it. Budgets that are never enforced decay into decoration within a quarter.

SLO, SLA and the cushion between

SLISLOSLA
What it isA measurementAn internal targetA contract

Missing it meansNothing by itselfThe error budget policy appliesCredits, refunds, penalties, reputation
Typical leveln/aTighterLooser than the SLO

An SLA is deliberately looser than the SLO behind it. If you promise customers 99.9% and your internal target is also 99.9%, then every time you miss internally you are already paying. With an internal SLO of 99.95% against an external SLA of 99.9%, you notice and react while the contract is still safe. Notice also what a customer's SLA with you and your SLA with your provider imply together: you cannot promise 99.99% on the strength of a provider's 99.9% unless you have a fallback (chapter 1 again). Many SLAs are also narrower than the SLO in what they measure: monthly rather than rolling, and typically excluding scheduled maintenance, which is one reason a vendor can hit its SLA while your users had a bad month.

Burn rate

The budget tells you how much you have spent. The burn rate tells you how fast. It is the ratio of the observed error rate to the error rate the SLO allows:

burn rate = (observed bad fraction) / (1 − SLO)

A burn rate of 1 means you are consuming the budget exactly as fast as the window allows: spend steadily at 1× and you arrive at the end of the window with nothing left. A burn rate of 10 would exhaust a 30-day budget in 3 days, 100 in about 7 hours, and 720 in an hour (the window length in hours divided by the burn rate gives the time to exhaustion). For a 99.9% SLO, a 1% error rate is a burn rate of 10; a complete outage is a burn rate of 1,000.

A preview of burn-rate alerting

Why does this matter before we have even discussed alerts? Because it gives you a principled way to decide when to wake someone up. A naive alert ("page if the error rate is above 1% for five minutes") ignores the objective: it pages for blips that cost nothing and misses slow burns that quietly consume the whole budget. Burn-rate alerts instead ask: "at this pace, how much of the budget will be gone, and how soon?" The SRE Workbook proposes several windows at once; a commonly quoted set for a 30-day window:

Burn rateLong windowBudget consumed in that windowAction
14.41 hour2%Page now
66 hours5%Page
13 days10%Open a ticket

The third column is just arithmetic: burn rate times window length divided by the SLO window. 14.4 × 1 hour / 720 hours = 2%; 6 × 6 / 720 = 5%; 1 × 72 / 720 = 10%. Fast, severe burns page quickly; slow, mild burns become tickets. Each alert pairs the long window with a short one so that it stops firing when the problem is over. Chapter 7 develops this fully, and Lab 1 has you build and test the alerts yourself. For now, keep the single idea: alert on the rate at which you are spending the budget, not on a raw metric. Computing it from a request log

Everything above is arithmetic on counts, so let us do it on data. The script builds a synthetic 30-day log (one sampled request every 30 seconds, with a seeded random generator), bakes in three incidents (a 30-minute bad deploy, a 10-minute dependency blip, and 2 hours of slowness with no errors), and then computes both SLIs, the budget, and a few burn rates. It deliberately includes health checks and bots, which are excluded from the valid set.

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Req:
    t: int          # second since start of the 30-day window
    status: int     # HTTP status
    ms: float       # latency as seen at the load balancer
    kind: str       # "api" or "health" or "bot"

DAY = 86_400
DAYS = 30

def make_log(seed: int = 5) -> list[Req]:
    """One sampled request every 30 s for 30 days, with three incidents baked in."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    log = []
    for t in range(0, DAYS * DAY, 30):             # one request per 30 s, sampled
        day = t // DAY
        bad = 0.0005                                # background error rate
        slow = 0.02                                 # background fraction > 300 ms
        if day == 6 and 3600 * 10 <= t % DAY < 3600 * 10 + 1800:  # 30 min bad deploy
            bad = 0.40
        if day == 17 and 3600 * 2 <= t % DAY < 3600 * 2 + 600:    # 10 min dependency blip
            bad = 0.90
        if day == 22 and 3600 * 14 <= t % DAY < 3600 * 14 + 7200: # 2 h slow DB (no errors)
            slow = 0.35
        kind = rng.choices(["api", "health", "bot"], [0.90, 0.07, 0.03])[0]
        status = 500 if rng.random() < bad else (400 if rng.random() < 0.01 else 200)
        ms = rng.lognormvariate(4.3, 0.45) * (4 if rng.random() < slow else 1)
        log.append(Req(t, status, ms, kind))
    return log

log = make_log()

def is_valid(r: Req) -> bool:
    # Valid events: real user API calls. Health checks and bots are excluded;
    # 4xx are the client's fault, so they are valid but count as "good".
    return r.kind == "api"

def avail_good(r: Req) -> bool:
    return r.status < 500

def latency_good(r: Req, threshold_ms: float = 300) -> bool:
    return r.status < 500 and r.ms <= threshold_ms

def sli(reqs, good) -> float:
    valid = [r for r in reqs if is_valid(r)]
    return sum(1 for r in valid if good(r)) / len(valid)

valid = [r for r in log if is_valid(r)]
print(f"total requests {len(log)}, valid {len(valid)}")
print(f"availability SLI : {sli(log, avail_good):.5f}")
print(f"latency SLI      : {sli(log, latency_good):.5f}   (good = non-5xx and <= 300 ms)")
naive = sum(1 for r in log if r.status < 500) / len(log)
print(f"naive (all traffic) availability: {naive:.5f}")

SLO = 0.999
budget_frac = 1 - SLO
allowed_bad = budget_frac * len(valid)
bad_now = sum(1 for r in valid if not avail_good(r))
print(f"\nSLO {SLO:.3%}: budget = {allowed_bad:.1f} bad events of {len(valid)}; used {bad_now} ({bad_now/allowed_bad:.0%})")

def burn_rate(reqs, t0, t1, good, slo=SLO) -> float:
    w = [r for r in reqs if t0 <= r.t < t1 and is_valid(r)]
    if not w:
        return 0.0
    err = sum(1 for r in w if not good(r)) / len(w)
    return err / (1 - slo)

print("\nburn rate (1.0 = spending exactly at the sustainable pace)")
for label, day, start_h, hours in (("quiet day 3", 3, 10, 1),
                                   ("bad deploy, 1h around it", 6, 10, 1),
                                   ("blip, 1h around it", 17, 2, 1)):
    t0 = day * DAY + start_h * 3600
    print(f"  {label:<26} {burn_rate(log, t0, t0 + hours * 3600, avail_good):6.1f}")

# running budget consumed, sampled daily
print("\nday  cumulative budget used")
used = 0
for d in range(DAYS):
    day_reqs = [r for r in valid if r.t // DAY == d]
    used += sum(1 for r in day_reqs if not avail_good(r))
    if d in (5, 6, 7, 16, 17, 29):
        print(f"{d+1:>3}  {used/allowed_bad:6.0%}")

# the same latency data under two thresholds
for th in (200, 300, 500):
    print(f"latency SLI at {th} ms: {sli(log, lambda r, th=th: latency_good(r, th)):.4f}")

Read it the way you would read a real one:

  • The availability SLI is 99.877%, just under the 99.9% target, and the budget accounting says the same thing in another unit: the SLO allows 77.8 bad events out of 77,832, and 96 happened, so 123% of the budget is used: the SLO was missed for this window.
  • The budget shows where it went. The 30-minute bad deploy on day 7 took the cumulative figure from 17% to 50% (a burn rate over 200 for that hour), and the 10-minute dependency blip on day 18 took another 22 points. Together those two short events cost more than half the budget. The steady 0.05% background error rate (a burn rate of about 0.5) quietly used up roughly half by itself. Both matter: the background sets where you start, and the incidents decide whether you finish over. It is also why MTTR matters so much (chapter 1): a deploy that is rolled back in 5 minutes instead of 30 would have cost a sixth as much.
  • The latency SLI of 98.76% for a 300 ms threshold is a different story. It misses a hypothetical 99% target, mostly because the script bakes in a 2% background of slow requests (without the slow-database incident it would still be 98.81%), but the incident is the part the availability SLI could never see: two hours of slowness with no errors at all. Two SLIs catch two kinds of failure.
  • The final three lines show how sensitive a latency SLI is to the threshold you pick: 96.8% at 200 ms, 98.8% at 300 ms, 99.6% at 500 ms. Choose the threshold from user needs and a measured histogram, not by trying values until the SLO is green.
  • The "naive" availability, which includes health checks and bots, is almost identical here because the synthetic probes fail at the same rate. In production they usually do not: health checks that always pass dilute the denominator and flatter the result.
Go deeper: counting events versus counting time optional

An SLI built on request counts weights busy periods more heavily than quiet ones. That is usually what you want, but it has a quirk: at 3 a.m., a total outage barely moves the SLI, while the same outage at lunchtime wrecks it. A service that must be equally dependable at all hours (a safety alarm, say) may prefer a time-sliced SLI: divide time into one-minute slices, call a slice good if its success rate is above a threshold, and take the fraction of good slices. The Workbook discusses such "windowed" indicators. They resemble the time-based availability of chapter 1 but are computed from real requests.

Another subtlety is low traffic. A service seeing 10 requests a day cannot support a 99.99% SLO: one failure is a 10% error rate for the day, and the budget in events is a tiny fraction of one request. Synthetic probes are one remedy, and so are longer windows or aggregating several services into one SLO.

Try it yourself

Save the script as slo_from_log.py and run it with Python 3.9 or newer; the output should match exactly. Then experiment, predicting before you run:

  • Change SLO to 0.995. How much of the budget is used? Which incident now looks small?
  • Delete the "bad deploy" incident. Does the SLO pass? What is the day-by-day budget curve now?
  • Change the definition of good in avail_good so that 400 responses count as bad. How far does the SLI move? Is that the SLI you would want?

Distributed systems connection

In a distributed system the SLO of a service is a chain of promises: its own, plus those of everything it depends on. Retries and timeouts (architecture chapter 17) can raise a request's chance of success above any single attempt's, but only up to the point where they overload the dependency. Error budgets give the whole organisation a shared language for that trade: the SLO of each service becomes a parameter in the design of its callers, and the budget decides how much new risk each team may take on.

Key takeaways

  • An SLI is good events / valid events, defined precisely: which events, which are valid, what is good, where measured. Latency SLIs use a threshold and count the fraction under it.
  • Measure as close to the user as is practical. Load balancer logs are a strong default; server-side metrics miss requests that never arrive; add black-box probes for the critical journeys.
  • Choose SLO targets from user needs and dependency limits, per journey, not from current performance. 100% is not a goal.
  • Rolling windows (about 28 to 30 days) give steady incentives; calendar windows match reporting but encourage end-of-period games.
  • Error budget = 1 − SLO. It is a resource to spend on change and risk as well as a limit. A policy agreed in advance (slow down, then freeze) is what makes it bite.
  • SLAs are looser contracts built on top of tighter internal SLOs; breaches of an SLO trigger engineering policy, breaches of an SLA trigger money.
  • Burn rate = observed bad fraction / (1 − SLO). Time to exhaust the budget is the window divided by the burn rate. Alert on burn rate, over multiple windows (chapter 7).

Check your understanding

Which of these is a well-formed SLI?

  1. Average response time of the service
  2. CPU utilisation of the API servers
  3. The proportion of valid API requests, measured at the load balancer, that return a non-5xx response within 300 ms
  4. The number of errors per day
An SLI is a ratio of good to valid events with a clear measurement point. Averages hide the tail; CPU is a cause or a resource, not what users feel; a raw error count has no denominator, so it means something different at different traffic levels.

An SLO of 99.9% over 30 days applies to a service that sees 10,000,000 valid requests in the window. How many failed requests does the error budget allow?

  1. 10,000
  2. 1,000
  3. 100,000
  4. 99,900
The budget is 1 − 0.999 = 0.1% of valid events: 0.001 × 10,000,000 = 10,000. In time terms, the same 0.1% of a 30-day window is 43.2 minutes of full outage.

A team sets its SLO to 99.95% because "that is what we achieved last quarter". What is the main flaw?

  1. 99.95% is too high for any service
  2. SLOs should always be a round number such as 99%
  3. They should have set it to 100%
  4. Current performance reflects luck and past architecture, not what users need, so the target may be too costly, too lax, or just unexplained
Targets should come from critical user journeys, user tolerance and dependency limits. Copying history locks in accidents of the past and gives you no principled reason to defend or revise the number. 100% is never a sensible target.

For a 99.9% SLO over 30 days (720 hours), the service runs at a burn rate of 6 for six hours. What fraction of the budget was consumed?

  1. About 0.5%
  2. About 5%
  3. About 36%
  4. About 60%
Budget consumed = burn rate × time / window = 6 × 6 / 720 = 5%. A burn rate of 1 for the whole window would use 100%, so 6× for a fraction 6/720 of it uses 6 × 6/720.

A failed DNS configuration makes your service unreachable for ten minutes. Your SLI is computed from application server metrics and stayed at 100% throughout. What does this illustrate?

  1. The SLO was too strict
  2. Servers should log more detail
  3. Server-side measurement is blind to requests that never arrive; measuring at the edge or from outside would have seen the outage
  4. DNS problems do not count as downtime
Where you measure determines which failures you can see. Requests that fail before reaching the server leave no trace in server metrics. Load balancer logs help for failures behind the balancer, and external probes catch faults in front of it.

Why is an SLA usually looser than the internal SLO for the same service?

  1. So that engineers notice and react to problems while the contract, and its financial penalties, are still safe
  2. Because customers prefer lower numbers
  3. Because SLAs are measured over shorter periods
  4. Because an SLA cannot be broken
The gap between the SLO and the SLA is a cushion: when you miss the SLO the error budget policy kicks in and you act, long before a breach costs money. If both were equal, every internal miss would be a contractual one.
