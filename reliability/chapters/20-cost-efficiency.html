Cost & Efficiency

Every nine and every spare region has a price. This short chapter gives you the vocabulary of cloud bills, the one number that makes cost discussions concrete (cost per unit of work), and a way to see why efficiency and reliability are partners that sometimes pull in opposite directions.

Why this matters

Chapters 1 and 18 showed that reliability costs money, and chapter 8 showed that running hot costs latency. Somebody eventually asks what all this is worth. If you can express cost per request, explain why headroom is not waste, and point at the cheapest safe way to buy capacity, you move from "ops spends too much" to a conversation about trade-offs with numbers on both sides.

How cloud capacity is priced

Most providers sell the same compute in three main ways. The names differ, but the shapes are stable.

ModelYou payYou give upGood for
On-demandThe list price, by the second or hour, for as long as you run itNothing; you can stop any timeSpiky, new or unpredictable workloads
Reserved capacity / savings plansA discounted rate in exchange for committing to a level of usage for a term (commonly one or three years), whether you use it or notFlexibility: you pay for the commitment even if demand fallsThe steady baseline you are sure you will always run
Spot / preemptibleA deep discount on spare capacityGuarantees: the provider can reclaim the machine at short noticeFault-tolerant work: batch jobs, stateless workers, anything restartable

The usual strategy is a layered one: cover the always-on baseline with commitments, handle the daily and weekly rise with on-demand or autoscaling, and put interruptible work on spot. Spot is a reliability decision as much as a cost one: using it means designing for sudden loss of nodes, exactly the failures chaos experiments (chapter 19) rehearse.

Egress and other hidden meters

Capacity is not the only line on the bill. Egress is data leaving a provider's network (to the internet, to another provider, and often between regions or even availability zones), and it is typically charged per gigabyte, while data coming in is usually free. A chatty design that moves large payloads across zones, or a multi-region replication stream, can quietly cost more than the servers. Other meters include per-request charges for object stores, managed-service minimums, and log ingestion (the cost of keeping everything, as chapter 5 warned). Prices change and vary by provider, so the habit to build is checking the meter before you design around a data flow, not memorising numbers.

Unit economics: cost per request

A monthly total ($47,000) tells you nothing about whether you are efficient. Divide by the unit of work and you can compare across time, teams and designs. Unit economics means expressing cost per useful thing: per thousand requests, per active user, per order, per gigabyte processed. If traffic doubles and the bill doubles, the unit cost is steady; if the bill rises faster than traffic, something is getting worse. It also lets you compare against revenue, which is the business's real question. Choose a unit that reflects value, since cost per request can look good while each request is wastefully heavy.

Utilisation versus headroom

The cheapest fleet is the one running at 100% utilisation; it is also the one that falls over. Chapter 8 showed why: as utilisation approaches 1, queueing delay grows without bound. In the simplest model (M/M/1) the mean time in the system scales like 1 / (1 - utilisation), so going from 50% to 90% busy is a five-fold rise in that factor. Cost per request, meanwhile, falls like 1 / utilisation: the more you cram onto a machine, the cheaper each request. Plotting both shows the tension.

This is why headroom is not waste. Spare capacity buys latency, buys protection from bursts, and buys survival when a part fails: with four instances at 75% each, losing one pushes the others to 100%. Chapter 10 turns this into capacity planning. What you want to remove is unplanned idle capacity (forgotten test clusters, oversized instances), not deliberate margin.

Right-sizing, autoscaling and scale-to-zero

  • Right-sizing means matching instance size and count to measured load, based on real utilisation of CPU, memory and I/O over weeks, not the guess made at launch. Over-sized instances are the most common saving. But size against the peak and a failure scenario, not the average.
  • Autoscaling adds and removes capacity as demand changes. It saves money on spiky workloads, but scaling takes time (instances boot, caches warm, connections build), so it reacts rather than predicts; sudden surges can outrun it, and it depends on a control plane that may itself be impaired in an outage. Keep a floor you can survive on without it.
  • Scale to zero (serverless functions, idle environments shutting down) removes cost when there is no traffic. The price is a cold start: the first request after idleness waits while the service starts. Fine for internal tools and queues, risky for latency-sensitive user paths.

Storage tiers and data lifecycle

Data has a life: hot when new and read often, then rarely touched, and eventually deletable. Storage is sold in tiers: fast and expensive for hot data, slower and cheaper for infrequent access, and archive tiers that are very cheap to hold but slow and costly to retrieve (often minutes to hours, plus a retrieval fee). A lifecycle policy moves objects between tiers by age and deletes them at the end of retention. Remember the tensions: backups (chapter 18) need retention and quick restores; privacy rules may require deletion; and a tier that is cheap to store but slow to read is a hazard if it holds the data you need during an incident.

FinOps basics: tagging and showback

FinOps is the practice of bringing engineering, finance and the business together to manage cloud spend. Its first step is visibility. Tagging attaches labels (team, service, environment, cost centre) to every resource so that a bill can be split by owner. Showback reports each team's spend back to them without charging; chargeback actually bills it internally. Showback alone changes behaviour surprisingly often, because people who see their costs for the first time tend to find waste themselves. Untagged spend is the first thing to hunt down.

A worked example

This script prices a small fleet. It uses made-up round prices (not any provider's) and a few assumptions you can change: a 2,400 requests per second peak, 900 average, and 100 requests per second per instance. It shows both the effect of the target utilisation and the savings of mixing purchase models.

"""Unit economics and utilisation vs headroom. All prices are made-up round numbers, not any provider's."""
ON_DEMAND = 0.10          # $ per instance-hour
RESERVED_DISCOUNT = 0.40  # commit 1 year: pay 60% of on-demand for every hour, used or not
SPOT_DISCOUNT = 0.70      # interruptible: pay 30% of on-demand
HOURS = 730               # hours per month

def instances_needed(peak_rps: float, per_instance_rps: float, target_util: float) -> int:
    """Instances so that peak load sits at target_util of capacity (ceil)."""
    cap = per_instance_rps * target_util
    n = int(peak_rps // cap)
    return n if n * cap >= peak_rps else n + 1

peak, avg, per_inst = 2400.0, 900.0, 100.0
requests_per_month = avg * 3600 * HOURS

print(f"{'target util at peak':>20} {'instances':>10} {'$/month':>9} {'$ per 1M req':>13}")
for util in (0.90, 0.80, 0.65, 0.50):
    n = instances_needed(peak, per_inst, util)
    monthly = n * ON_DEMAND * HOURS
    print(f"{util:>20.0%} {n:>10d} {monthly:>9.0f} {monthly / requests_per_month * 1e6:>13.2f}")

print()
n = instances_needed(peak, per_inst, 0.65)
base = int(avg / (per_inst * 0.65)) + 1        # steady floor that is always running
burst = n - base
BURST_FRACTION = 0.25   # assumption: burst capacity is needed 25% of the hours
od = ON_DEMAND * HOURS
res = od * (1 - RESERVED_DISCOUNT)
spot = od * (1 - SPOT_DISCOUNT)
mix = {
    "static fleet, all on-demand":        n * od,
    "autoscaled, all on-demand":          base * od + burst * od * BURST_FRACTION,
    "base reserved, burst on-demand":     base * res + burst * od * BURST_FRACTION,
    "base reserved, burst on spot":       base * res + burst * spot * BURST_FRACTION,
}
print(f"fleet at 65% peak utilisation = {n} instances; always-on floor = {base}; burst = {burst}")
for k, v in mix.items():
    print(f"  {k:34} ${v:8.0f}/month")

Two readings. First, moving from a target of 90% to 50% peak utilisation raised cost per million requests by about 80%, a concrete price tag for headroom that you can weigh against what an outage costs. Second, how you buy matters as much as how much: reserving the baseline and putting the burst on spot cut the same capacity's cost by roughly three quarters, at the price of tolerating interruption in the burst tier.

Efficiency and reliability

Efficiency helps reliability in one way: less to break. Fewer services, fewer instance types, smaller data and simpler topologies mean fewer failure modes, fewer pages and a smaller bill. Removing a dependency or deleting an unused service is among the most effective reliability work there is.

It hurts reliability in another: margin is what absorbs surprise. Squeezing utilisation, dropping redundant regions, putting everything on spot and scaling to zero each trade resilience for money. None of these is wrong; they are choices that should be explicit, tied to the SLO and the error budget (chapter 2), and revisited as the business changes. A useful rule: cut cost where the failure is cheap and recoverable, and keep margin where it is expensive.

Distributed systems connection

Many classic distributed designs are really cost trade-offs in disguise: replication factor multiplies storage, multi-region multiplies compute and egress, a cache trades memory for fewer database reads. When a design review asks "what does this cost per request at 10 times the scale?", it is asking the unit-economics question of this chapter.

Key takeaways

  • Compute is bought on-demand, by commitment (reserved or savings plans) or as interruptible spot. Cover the baseline with commitments, burst with on-demand, and fault-tolerant work with spot.
  • Egress and per-request meters can outweigh the servers; check them when designing data flows.
  • Use unit economics (cost per request, user or order), not the monthly total.
  • Utilisation lowers cost per request but raises queueing delay steeply near saturation; headroom buys latency and failure tolerance.
  • Right-size to peak and failure cases, autoscale with a floor, and use scale-to-zero only where cold starts are acceptable.
  • Use storage tiers and lifecycle rules, tag everything, and use showback to make owners see their spend.
  • Efficiency reduces what can break, but also trades away margin; make those trades explicit.

Check your understanding

A nightly data-processing job can be restarted from checkpoints and runs for a few hours. Which purchase model is the best fit?

  1. On-demand, because it needs guarantees
  2. A three-year reservation
  3. Spot or preemptible capacity, because interruptions are tolerable and the discount is deep
  4. None; it should run on a laptop
Spot capacity is cheap because it can be reclaimed. A restartable, checkpointed batch job tolerates that. Reserving capacity for a few hours a night wastes the commitment the rest of the day.

Why is running a service at 95% peak utilisation usually a poor choice, even though it minimises cost per request?

  1. Queueing delay grows steeply near saturation, and there is no capacity to absorb a burst or the loss of one node
  2. Cost per request rises at high utilisation
  3. Cloud providers forbid it
  4. Utilisation above 90% is impossible
Utilisation lowers unit cost but pushes the system up the queueing knee. Headroom is what pays for tail latency and failure tolerance.

Monthly cloud spend doubled while traffic grew 20%. Which measure best shows whether this is a problem?

  1. The total bill alone
  2. The number of instances
  3. The size of the engineering team
  4. Cost per unit of work (for example per thousand requests), tracked over time
The total mixes growth with efficiency. A unit cost that jumps by about 67% (2.0 divided by 1.2) while the product has not changed says efficiency worsened and needs investigation.

What is the main downside of scale-to-zero for a user-facing API?

  1. It costs more when idle
  2. The first request after idleness may wait for a cold start, hurting latency
  3. It cannot handle any traffic
  4. It requires reserved instances
Scaling to zero removes idle cost but means starting a service on demand. That is acceptable for background or internal workloads and risky where users feel the delay.
