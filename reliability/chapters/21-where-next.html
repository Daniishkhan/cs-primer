Where Next: On to Security

You started this course knowing how to build systems. You can now also run them: define how reliable they must be, see inside them, keep them upright under load, change them safely, handle them when they break and learn from it. This last chapter gathers that into one map, points to the best things to read next, and bridges to the next course, which asks what changes when some of the users are adversaries.

Why this matters

A course on operations can leave you with a heap of techniques. What turns them into judgment is seeing how they connect: the SLO justifies the alert, the alert starts the incident, the incident feeds the postmortem, the postmortem becomes a chaos experiment, and the experiment tests the SLO's assumptions. Stand back once and see the loop.

What you can now run and reason about

Concretely, you can now:

  • Compute what a set of components can promise (chapter 1) and turn it into SLIs, SLOs and a budget to spend (chapter 2).
  • Instrument a service so a stranger can ask new questions of it: merge histograms correctly (chapter 4), structure events (chapter 5) and follow a request across services (chapter 6).
  • Predict where a service will hit its latency knee (chapter 8), why the tail dominates fan-out (chapter 9) and what to shed when demand exceeds supply (chapter 11).
  • Ship change in small waves with a way back (chapter 12), contain the damage by design (chapter 15), and recover from the worst case (chapter 18).
  • Run an incident, write a blameless review and rehearse failure before it rehearses you (chapters 16 to 19), and say what all of it costs (chapter 20).

Honest limits belong here too. Operations is judgment under uncertainty. This course gave you the models (nines, queues, budgets) and the habits (mitigate first, blameless review, restore tests), but no model replaces knowing your own system. The next step is to practise on a real one, ideally by taking a turn on a rota with good colleagues who remember their last incident.

A reading list

These are the sources this course leans on, roughly in the order I would read them.

SourceWhy read it
Site Reliability Engineering, Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy (eds.), O'Reilly, 2016The origin of SLOs, error budgets, toil and the SRE role. Free to read online. Chapters on monitoring, incident management and postmortem culture map directly to this course.
The Site Reliability Workbook, Beyer, Murphy, Rensin, Kawahara and Thorne (eds.), O'Reilly, 2018The practical companion: implementing SLOs, multi-window burn-rate alerting, and worked examples.
Observability Engineering, Majors, Fong-Jones and Miranda, O'Reilly, 2022The case for wide events and asking new questions of production, with a candid look at tooling and culture.
Release It!, Michael Nygard (2nd ed., 2018)Stability patterns and anti-patterns told through production war stories: timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads, and how outages cascade.
"How Complex Systems Fail", Richard Cook, 1998Eighteen short observations that reshape how you read any postmortem. A few pages; read it more than once.
The Amazon Builders' Library (aws.amazon.com/builders-library)Practical articles from AWS engineers on timeouts, retries and backoff, load shedding, static stability, shuffle sharding and cells.
"The Tail at Scale", Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso, Communications of the ACM, 2013Why rare slowness dominates fan-out systems, and the hedged and tied requests that tame it (chapter 9).
"Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems", Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu, HotOS 2021A framework for failures that sustain themselves after the trigger is gone (chapter 11).

Beyond these, read the postmortems that companies publish after big outages. This course's case studies (for instance Roblox and AWS) are written to be read that way: look for contributing factors, not a villain.

The bridge to Course 6: Security

Everything in this course assumed that the opponent is entropy: hardware wearing out, software with bugs, traffic arriving at the wrong time. The next course, Security Engineering, changes the assumption: some of the load is hostile, some of the credentials are stolen, and some of the "failures" are chosen by someone who studied your system. You will meet threat modelling, cryptography, authentication and OAuth, authorization, web and supply-chain security, zero trust, multi-tenancy and privacy.

You are better prepared than you think, because the habits transfer. Defence in depth is the Swiss-cheese model with an adversary choosing where to push. Blast radius and cells limit a compromise as they limit a bad deploy. Immutable backups (chapter 18) are your answer to ransomware. Postmortems and blameless culture are how security incidents become learning. And rate limits and load shedding reappear as defences against abuse.

After that comes the capstone, the System Design Studio, where you design whole systems end to end and defend your trade-offs using all seven courses: networks and storage, consistency and consensus, reliability and operations, and security. Come to it with a habit worth keeping: for every design, ask how it fails, how you would know, how you would stop the bleeding and what you would change afterwards.

Try it yourself

Pick a system you work on, or one you know well, and fill in the six boxes of the loop in one page: its SLO, the three signals you would look at first, its capacity knee, its riskiest kind of change, the last restore you tested, and the last thing you learned from a failure. The empty boxes are your next work.

Distributed systems connection

The earlier courses taught what distributed systems are: replication, consensus, partitions, clocks. This one taught how they behave in the world: they partially fail, overload, change under you and surprise their owners. Together they are the working knowledge of someone who can design a system and also be responsible for it at 3 a.m.

Key takeaways

  • Operations is a loop: define reliability, observe, size and protect, change safely, respond and recover, learn, and back again.
  • The habits matter as much as the maths: mitigate first, measure what users feel, make failure rehearsed and boring.
  • Keep reading: the SRE book and Workbook, Observability Engineering, Release It!, Cook, the Builders' Library, Dean and Barroso, and Bronson et al.
  • Next comes Security Engineering, which assumes some actors are adversaries, and then the System Design Studio capstone.
