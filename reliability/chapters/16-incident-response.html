Incident Response

At some point the pager goes off for real. This chapter is about what a calm team does in the first hour: how an incident is declared and graded, who does which job, why you stop the bleeding before you understand the wound, how to talk to the people who are waiting, and how to know when it is over.

Why this matters

Chapters 1 and 2 told you how much failure you can afford; chapter 7 tells you how a human gets woken up. What happens next decides whether a bad twenty minutes becomes a bad four hours. Most of the extra time in long incidents is not spent on hard debugging. It is spent on confusion: three people changing three things, nobody talking to customers, the same hypothesis tried twice, and a tired engineer who has been awake for fourteen hours making the call. Incident response is the craft of removing that confusion, and it is learnable.

The lifecycle, from detection to resolution

An incident is an unplanned event that degrades or threatens a service enough that people must coordinate to fix it. Not every alert is an incident (a single failed disk that the system tolerates is a ticket), but the moment the user-visible SLO is burning and nobody is sure why, you are in one. Every incident, large or small, passes through roughly the same stages.

Chapter 1 argued that recovery speed often beats prevention. The lifecycle shows why: only the middle interval, time to mitigate, is what the SLO sees. A bad deploy that is rolled back in six minutes costs the same budget whether the root fault takes six hours or six days to understand.

Analogy: the emergency department

An ambulance arrives with a patient who is bleeding. The team does not first establish why the patient fell; they stabilise (airway, breathing, circulation), then diagnose. The charge nurse, who is not the person holding the scalpel, keeps track of beds, staff and who has been told what. Incident response is the same shape: triage grades severity, a charge nurse coordinates, and stabilisation comes before the full workup.

Where it breaks down: a patient is a single body with one chart. A distributed system has thousands of interacting parts, and your "vital signs" (metrics) can lie or go missing in the middle of the emergency, as the AWS December 2021 case shows.

Severity levels

A severity level is a shared label for how bad an incident is, chosen so that the response is proportional: a minor degradation should not wake the CTO, and a total outage should not be handled by one sleepy engineer. The labels (SEV1, SEV2, ... or P0, P1, ...) vary by company; what matters is that the criteria are written down in terms of user impact, not of internal causes.

LevelTypical criterion (illustrative)Response
SEV1Core user journey down or badly degraded for most users; data loss or security exposurePage immediately, any hour. Full roles, executive and customer communication, updates every 15-30 minutes
SEV2Significant degradation or a major feature down for a subset of users; SLO burning fastPage on-call, declare in the incident channel, IC plus ops and comms leads, hourly updates
SEV3Minor degradation, workaround exists, budget burning slowlyHandle in working hours by the owning team; no formal roles
SEV4Cosmetic, or a problem caught before users saw itA ticket

Two habits make severity work. First, declare early and downgrade freely: the cost of calling a SEV2 that turns out to be a SEV3 is a few people's attention for twenty minutes, while the cost of under-calling one is lost time before help arrives. Second, tie severity to the SLO from chapter 2. "Burning budget at 14.4 times the sustainable rate" is a number, not an opinion; it reduces arguments about whether this is really a SEV1.

Roles: the Incident Command System

The structure most software teams use was borrowed from emergency services. The Incident Command System (ICS) was developed in the 1970s for coordinating wildfire response in California and later adopted widely in emergency management. Its core ideas are qualitative but powerful: one person is clearly in charge, responsibilities are divided into a few standard roles, and the structure scales up or down with the size of the emergency. Technology companies adopted a light version of it because the failure mode is the same: many capable people arrive at once, each acting independently.

Incident commander (IC)
Owns the response, not the keyboard. The IC decides what to try next, assigns people, and keeps a running summary of what is known, what is being tried and what is next. An IC who starts debugging has stopped commanding.
Operations lead
Directs the engineers who actually change the system and ensures that only one person changes a given thing at a time. Often the subject-matter expert for the failing service.
Communications lead
Writes the status page, answers internal and customer-facing questions and shields the responders from them. Needs enough technical judgment to translate but not to debug.
Scribe
Keeps a live, timestamped record: what was observed, what was decided and by whom, what was changed. This becomes the raw material for the postmortem in chapter 17, and it saves a surprising number of arguments ("did we roll back before or after the cache flush?").

Analogy: the charge nurse

The charge nurse does not insert the IV. They know which patients are stable, which beds are free, who is on break and who must be called. When the IC says "I need someone who knows the billing database", they are doing the charge nurse's job. Handing the commander role to the most senior debugger is the classic mistake: they are also the person you most need hands-on.

Where it breaks down: a charge nurse has a permanent role and authority. An IC is often just the person on call that week, so the role has to be understood in advance and the authority has to be explicit: during the incident the IC outranks the VP in the channel for decisions about the response.

Mitigate first, diagnose later

The single most valuable rule is to mitigate (reduce or remove user impact) before you diagnose (understand why). Understanding a failure in a live distributed system can take hours; many mitigations take minutes and do not require understanding. If a deploy started the trouble, you do not need to know which line was wrong to know that going back to the last good version should help.

MitigationUse whenWatch out for
Roll back a deploy or config changeTrouble began soon after a change (chapter 12, chapter 13)Rolled-back code meeting migrated data; some failures outlast the cause
Fail over to a replica or standbyOne component or zone is sick (data chapter 15)Replication lag means lost writes; a failover that itself needs a broken control plane
Shed load: reject low-priority work, disable heavy featuresDemand exceeds capacity (chapter 11)Decide who loses service before the incident, not during it
Drain a region or cell: move traffic awayA failure is contained to one place (chapter 15)The remaining capacity must absorb the traffic; draining everyone into one cell can start a second incident
Turn off a feature flagA new feature is implicated (architecture chapter 18)Flags that interact; flag systems that depend on the broken service
Add capacityYou are simply shortOften the last resort: it can worsen an overload, and the control plane needed to scale may be impaired

Mitigation is not a licence to destroy evidence

Restarting everything is the most tempting mitigation and the one that most often erases the clues. Before a restart, take the cheap snapshots: thread dumps, a heap summary, the relevant logs, the exact versions and config. A rule of thumb is that capturing evidence should add minutes, not hours, and the IC makes the trade-off explicitly rather than by default.

Mitigations are not always clean. After a rollback the error rate may fall only partway, because retries and cold caches keep the system overloaded long after the original cause is gone. You met this shape in the retry-storm case and it is the subject of chapter 11. Treat "rolled back, still broken" as new information, not as failure of the plan: it points to a sustaining effect, and the next mitigation is usually load shedding.

A worked timeline

Here is a fictional but realistic SEV2, walked through step by step. The service is a checkout API; the change is an ordinary deploy that went through a normal canary. Watch the error rate (red line), the numbered events along the bottom, and the status page wording at each stage.

Step through

One incident, 14:00 to 15:20 (fictional)

  1. 14:02, deploy. A new build of the checkout API starts rolling out after a normal canary. Nothing is visibly wrong yet.
  2. 14:09, detection. The wave reaches most hosts and the error rate climbs to 30%. A fast-window burn-rate alert (chapter 7) pages the on-call engineer. Seven minutes of user pain have already happened: that is the time to detect.
  3. 14:12, declare. The on-call engineer acknowledges, opens the incident channel and declares a SEV2, taking the IC role. The first status page post goes up with a promised next-update time, even though nobody knows the cause.
  4. 14:15, roles. The IC names an operations lead (the service owner), a communications lead and a scribe. From here the IC stops touching dashboards and asks short, specific questions.
  5. 14:18, mitigate before diagnosing. The ops lead notes that errors began with the deploy. The IC decides to roll back now rather than find the bug. At 14:24 the rollback completes, but errors only fall to 12%: retries and cold caches are keeping the service overloaded.
  6. 14:28, shed load. The IC approves disabling a heavy recommendations widget and rejecting batch traffic. The error rate drops towards 1% within minutes. Two mitigations were needed; the second one was not in anyone's plan.
  7. 14:40, recovered. The SLI is back inside the SLO. The status page moves to "monitoring". The incident is not over: the team watches for a recurrence.
  8. 15:10, resolved. After thirty stable minutes the IC declares the incident over, thanks the responders and schedules a postmortem. The scribe's timeline goes into the review document.

Notice what is absent: no one found the bug during the incident. That investigation happens afterwards, calmly, with the scribe's timeline and the preserved logs, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

Communication

Silence is read as incompetence, and a flood of speculation is read as chaos. The remedy is a fixed rhythm. The communications lead publishes updates on a promised cadence (for a SEV1, perhaps every 15 to 30 minutes), and each update says three things: what we know, what we are doing, and when the next update will arrive. "No change" is a valid update; a missed one is not.

  • The public status page uses plain words about user-visible effect, not internal component names. Typical states are investigating, identified, monitoring and resolved. Do not guess at causes you have not confirmed.
  • The internal channel has one thread for the response and a separate stakeholder channel for executives and support, so that people who only need an update do not interrupt the people fixing things.
  • Customer-facing teams need a short script they can paste. Giving support the same sentence the status page uses prevents contradictory answers.

One further trap: your status page and your incident tooling should not depend on the thing that is broken. In the AWS December 2021 case the monitoring itself went dark during the event, and the Roblox case shows how long recovery can run when the shared infrastructure everything depends on is the thing that is broken. Keep a fallback channel (a phone bridge, an out-of-band chat) that you have actually tried.

Handoffs, fatigue and declaring the end

Long incidents are endurance events. Decision quality drops after many hours awake, and tired engineers make the kind of mistakes that turn one incident into two. Build in relief:

  • Hand off the IC role explicitly, in the channel, with a summary: current state, active hypotheses, what has been tried, pending decisions, who is doing what. The outgoing IC stays for a few minutes to answer questions. "I'm handing over to Priya" must be said and acknowledged.
  • Rotate people at roughly the pace of a working shift. Send responders home to sleep; do not reward heroics. An incident that is still going after a day needs a plan for staffing, not more caffeine.
  • Declare the end deliberately. An incident is resolved when user impact has ended and stayed ended through a watch period, not when the first graph turns green. Write down what remains (cleanup, a temporary mitigation still in place, follow-up tickets) and who owns each, then close the channel.
Go deeper: where the "mitigate first" instinct comes from optional

Complex systems rarely have a single clean cause (Richard Cook's "How Complex Systems Fail", discussed in the next chapter), which means that during an incident you are usually forming hypotheses from incomplete data. Mitigation is attractive precisely because it works on the effect and is therefore robust to being wrong about the cause. A rollback to a known-good state is a better bet than a clever fix to a bug you have not yet fully understood. The risk is the opposite: a team that always mitigates and never investigates repeats the incident. The follow-through is the postmortem's job.

Try it yourself

Write a one-page incident checklist for a service you know. Start from this skeleton, and test it by asking a colleague to follow it cold.

1. DECLARE   Open #inc-YYYYMMDD-short-name. Post: what users see, since when, severity.
2. ROLES     IC: ____  Ops lead: ____  Comms lead: ____  Scribe: ____
3. FRAME     What changed in the last 2 hours? (deploys, config, flags, traffic)
4. MITIGATE  Can we roll back / fail over / shed load / drain a cell? Who is doing it?
5. COMMS     Status page posted? Next update due at: ____
6. EVIDENCE  Before any restart: capture logs, dumps, versions.
7. HANDOFF   Every 2-3 hours: summary + explicit "you are IC now".
8. CLOSE     User impact over and stable for ____ min. Owners for follow-ups.

Distributed systems connection

Distributed systems fail in ways that look alike from outside: slow, partial, intermittent. The mitigations in this chapter are the operational face of what you learned earlier: timeouts and breakers (architecture chapter 17), failover and replication lag (distributed chapter 18), and containment by cell (chapter 15). The better the design, the more mitigations are one command away.

Key takeaways

  • An incident passes through detect, declare, mitigate, resolve and learn. The interval users feel is time to mitigate.
  • Define severity by user impact and SLO burn. Declare early; downgrading is cheap, a late page is not.
  • Separate the jobs: incident commander (decides), operations lead (acts), communications lead (talks outwards), scribe (remembers). The IC does not debug.
  • Mitigate before you diagnose: roll back, fail over, shed load, drain a cell. Preserve evidence cheaply first, and expect that one mitigation may not be enough.
  • Communicate on a promised cadence, in user terms, through channels that do not depend on the broken system.
  • Plan handoffs and rest. Declare an end only after impact has stayed gone, and record what is left.

Check your understanding

Soon after a deploy the error rate rises sharply. Nobody knows why. What should the incident commander usually do first?

  1. Wait until the root cause is understood so that the fix is correct
  2. Roll back the deploy while the investigation continues in parallel
  3. Restart every server to clear any bad state
  4. Add capacity so the errors average out
Mitigation works on the effect and does not require knowing the cause. A change closely correlated with the trouble is the best-supported lever, and rolling back is quick and reversible. Restarting everything destroys evidence; adding capacity can worsen overload.

Why should the incident commander avoid typing debugging commands?

  1. Commanders are not allowed production access
  2. It is faster to ask someone else every time
  3. Debugging is not important during an incident
  4. A commander who is deep in a terminal stops tracking the overall picture, assignments and communication
The IC's value is coordination: who is doing what, what has been tried, what the next decision is. Absorbed in one hypothesis, the IC loses that overview. In a very small incident one person may do both, but they should know they are switching hats.

An update on the status page says only "We are still investigating. Next update at 15:30." Is this a good update?

  1. Yes: it admits what is unknown and sets a time for the next message, which builds trust
  2. No: updates should be sent only when there is news
  3. No: it should name the suspected component
  4. Yes, but only for internal channels
A predictable cadence matters more than content. An honest "no change, next update at 15:30" is better than silence, and better than a guessed cause that may be wrong. Internal component names usually mean nothing to customers.

After a rollback, errors fall from 30% to 12% but not to zero. What is the most useful reading?

  1. The rollback was a mistake and should be undone
  2. The incident is over; the remaining errors are noise
  3. Something is sustaining the overload (retries, cold caches); try the next mitigation, such as load shedding
  4. The monitoring is broken
Failures can outlast their cause, especially when retries multiply load (chapter 11). A partial improvement confirms the deploy was a contributor and points to a sustaining effect that needs its own mitigation.

The incident has run for eleven hours and the same engineer is still the commander. What is the best action?

  1. Keep going; changing leaders loses context
  2. Do an explicit handoff to a rested commander with a written summary, and send the tired responders to rest
  3. Close the incident so everyone can go home
  4. Move the commander role to the most senior engineer regardless of rest
Fatigue degrades judgment, and mistakes late in an incident are common. A structured handoff (state, hypotheses, tried actions, pending decisions) preserves context better than a tired person pressing on. Closing an unresolved incident only hides it.
