Start Here

The first four courses taught you to build systems: send bytes, run programs, store data, structure software, coordinate machines. This course is about what happens next. The system has users, the users have expectations, something breaks at 3 a.m., and someone has to understand why. That is operations, and it turns out to be a discipline with its own mathematics, tools and judgment.

A note from your mentor

Most engineers meet operations by accident: a page arrives, a graph goes red, and nobody has taught them what to look at first. The skill is learnable, and it is less about heroics than about preparation: measuring the right things, limiting what a mistake can break, and rehearsing recovery before you need it. You do not need any operational experience for this course. You need the four courses behind you, a little patience with arithmetic, and a willingness to think about failure calmly.

Why this matters

A system is only useful while it is running, and most of its life is spent running, not being written. The decisions that determine whether it stays up (what it promises, how it is measured, how changes reach it, how it behaves under overload, how people respond when it fails) are made by engineers, often long before any outage. Senior engineers are judged less on whether their designs work on a good day than on how they behave on a bad one. This course gives you the language and the numbers for the bad day.

From building to running

Building software is a project: it has a goal, a deadline and an end. Operations is the ongoing work of keeping a deployed system useful: making sure it is up, fast and correct for its users, that changes reach it safely, that it has capacity for the load it will see, that failures are noticed and repaired, and that what is learned is fed back into the design. It never ends, because users, load, dependencies and code keep changing.

Two things have shaped how the industry does this. DevOps is a cultural movement of the late 2000s that said the people who write software and the people who run it should work together, rather than throwing code over a wall. SRE (site reliability engineering) is one concrete way to do that, described in Google's book Site Reliability Engineering (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy, 2016): staff the operation with engineers who write software, and manage reliability with explicit numbers (targets, budgets) rather than slogans. You will learn that vocabulary properly in chapters 1 to 3. This course uses SRE ideas throughout, but you do not need to work at a company that has an SRE team to use them.

The shape of the course

Operations is a loop, not a line. You decide what "working" means, you build the means of seeing it, you protect the system from demand and from your own changes, you respond when things go wrong, and you carry what you learned back into the first step.

Part by part, the questions are:

An emergency department

We will lean on a few analogies throughout the course. The first is an emergency department, a place that has spent a century learning to run reliably under uncertain demand, limited staff and high stakes.

Analogy: the hospital emergency department

Patients arrive at random, in bursts, with problems of very different severity. Triage sorts them so that the sickest are seen first and the walking wounded wait: that is load shedding and priority queues. Vital signs (pulse, pressure, oxygen) are cheap to read and predict a lot: those are SLIs. A crowded waiting room is a queue: when beds are nearly all full, wait times shoot up non-linearly, which is exactly the utilisation curve of chapter 8. When a serious case arrives, the charge nurse coordinates, assigns roles and keeps one voice in charge: the incident commander of chapter 16. And there are checklists, handover protocols and a culture of reviewing what went wrong without blaming people, which brings us to the second analogy.

Where it breaks down: an ED's patients cannot be copied, retried or redirected to an identical hospital with a click. Software can clone its work, which makes redundancy cheap and also makes some failures (retry storms) possible that hospitals never see.

The second is aviation, which got safe by treating accidents as system failures: checklists, mandatory reporting without punishment, and James Reason's Swiss-cheese model, in which an accident happens only when holes in several independent layers of defence line up. You will meet it again in chapters 15 and 17.

Common misconception

"Operations is somebody else's job: the ops team's, or the cloud provider's." The decisions that matter most (what happens when a dependency is slow, whether a deploy can be undone, whether a retry can double a charge) are made in design and code review. Providers run the hardware; they do not run your service's behaviour under failure. Reliability is a property of the whole system, including its authors.

Labs, studios and cases

The course has the same shape as the others: chapters, labs, case studies and design studios. Three things are worth knowing.

  • Labs are simulations. You will not need a cloud account. The labs run small Python programs on your laptop that use simulated time and seeded random numbers: a fake service with a queue, a stream of synthetic requests, a pretend deploy. That makes outages reproducible, lets you run a 30-day window in a second, and means you can break things without consequences. The price is that they are models; each lab says what it leaves out.
  • Case studies are real incidents, told with the public sources and an analysis of the pattern. Chapters themselves mention real companies only qualitatively and point to the case.
  • Studios are design exercises: given a scenario (a checkout flow, a flash sale, an incident tabletop), produce your own plan before comparing it with a worked answer.

There is also an optional route for readers who want the real thing: rent a small cloud virtual machine, or use your laptop, and run kind (Kubernetes in Docker) to practise deploys and probes against an actual cluster. Everything required to follow the course works without it; everything in it is easier to believe if you have tried it. Setup notes are on the lab machine page, and the "Tools to try" boxes that appear on chapters name the relevant tools (Prometheus, Grafana, OpenTelemetry, k6, kubectl and others) and mark which need Linux.

Distributed systems connection

The Distributed Systems course gave you the ways a collection of machines can disagree, stall and partially fail, and the algorithms that tolerate some of it. Operations is the practice of living with the rest. Consensus can keep a database correct through a leader failure; it cannot tell you whether the new leader is fast enough for your users, whether the failover was exercised last quarter, or whether the change that triggered it could have been staged. Those are this course's questions.

Key takeaways

  • Operations is the continuing work of keeping a deployed system useful; most of a system's life is spent in operation, so design decisions about failure matter more than demos suggest.
  • SRE treats operations as an engineering problem managed with explicit numbers: indicators, objectives and error budgets (chapters 1 and 2).
  • The course is a loop: define targets, observe, protect from load, change safely, respond and learn, then revise the targets.
  • You already know the building blocks (timeouts, queues, failover, canaries); the course adds the quantitative and organisational layer on top.
  • Analogies to keep: an emergency department (triage, vital signs, incident command) and aviation (checklists, blameless reporting, Swiss cheese).
  • Labs are deterministic simulations that run offline; the cloud VM and kind route is optional but rewarding.

Check your understanding

Which statement best describes operations?

  1. Fixing servers when they break
  2. The continuing work of keeping a deployed system useful: reliable, fast, changeable and recoverable, with lessons fed back into the design
  3. The final phase of a project after development ends
  4. Work done only by a dedicated ops team
Operations is ongoing and covers prevention, measurement, safe change and response as well as repair. Dedicated teams may carry parts of it, but the design choices that decide reliability are made by everyone who builds the system.

In the emergency department analogy, what corresponds to load shedding?

  1. The charge nurse
  2. Vital signs
  3. The waiting room
  4. Triage: seeing the sickest first and deferring or redirecting the least urgent
Triage decides who gets scarce capacity when demand exceeds it, which is what load shedding and priority queues do. The charge nurse maps to the incident commander, vital signs to SLIs and the waiting room to a queue.

Why are the labs in this course simulations rather than work on real cloud infrastructure?

  1. Simulated time and seeded randomness make failures reproducible, fast and free to cause, at the cost of being models
  2. Real infrastructure cannot fail
  3. Simulations are always more accurate than reality
  4. Cloud providers forbid fault injection
A deterministic simulation lets you run a month in a second and repeat an outage exactly. It is a model, so each lab says what it leaves out. The optional kind route lets you check the ideas on a real cluster.

Where does the course say the biggest reliability decisions are made?

  1. In the data centre
  2. During incidents
  3. In design and code review, long before any outage
  4. By the cloud provider
What happens when a dependency is slow, whether a change can be undone, and whether a retry is safe are properties of the design. Incident response is vital, but it works within what the design allows.
