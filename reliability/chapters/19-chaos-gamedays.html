Chaos Engineering & Game Days

Resilience features (retries, failover, backups, runbooks) are claims about the future. Chaos engineering turns each claim into an experiment you run on purpose, at a time and scale you choose, so that production does not run it for you at 3 a.m. This chapter covers the principles, how to design a safe experiment, the human exercises that go with it, and a small harness you can run.

Why this matters

Everything in Part 4 and the first half of Part 5 is untested until a failure arrives: the circuit breaker that should open, the replica that should be promoted, the on-call who should know what to do. Untested resilience mechanisms have a way of containing their own bugs; a failover path used once a year is the most poorly exercised code in your system. Rehearsing failure is the only way to find out, cheaply, whether your confidence is justified.

What chaos engineering is

Chaos engineering is the discipline of experimenting on a system in order to build confidence in its ability to withstand turbulent conditions. It grew out of Netflix's move to the cloud, where instances were expected to disappear at any time, and the practice of deliberately terminating them (the tool called Chaos Monkey) so that engineers built services that tolerated it. The principles were later written down at principlesofchaos.org. They describe a scientific method, not a licence to break things.

You met the idea briefly in distributed chapter 19, where fault injection helped find bugs in protocols. Here the target is different: the operational resilience of a whole running service, including its people.

The principles

The site lists five advanced principles. In their own words, and with what each means in practice:

  1. Build a hypothesis around steady-state behaviour. Define steady state as a measurable output that indicates normal operation (orders per minute, the success-rate SLI) and not a list of internal metrics. The hypothesis is that it persists in both a control group and an experimental group. You are trying to refute it.
  2. Vary real-world events. Inject what actually happens: servers crash, disks fill, networks slow, a dependency returns errors, a clock jumps, traffic spikes. Prioritise by likelihood and by impact.
  3. Run experiments in production. Systems behave differently under real traffic and configuration; a staging environment cannot reproduce them. This is the principle most often misunderstood and is balanced by the fifth.
  4. Automate experiments to run continuously. Manual experiments rot. A system changes daily, so yesterday's confirmation expires.
  5. Minimise blast radius. Experiments in production carry risk, so contain the harm: a small fraction of traffic, one cell, a short duration, a ready abort. This ties directly to chapter 15.

Chaos is not "break things and see"

An experiment without a hypothesis and a measurable steady state is just an outage. The craft lies in choosing a failure you expect to survive, being specific about what "survive" means, and stopping the moment you are wrong. If you already know the system will fail, fix it first; do not demonstrate it to your customers.

Designing an experiment

Write the experiment down before you run it:

Steady-state metric
The user-facing indicator (usually an SLI from chapter 2) and its normal range.
Hypothesis
"When one of four instances is killed, the checkout success rate stays at or above 99%." Specific and falsifiable.
Fault and scope
What is injected, where, for how long, and what fraction of traffic is exposed.
Abort conditions
Automatic stop rules: the SLI below threshold for N seconds, an on-call page for a different problem, a customer complaint, or any human pressing the stop button. Include a check that the rollback itself works.
Observers and communication
Who is watching, and who has been told. On-call and support should know an experiment is running, so that a real incident is not mistaken for one (or vice versa).

Prerequisites matter. You need working observability (chapter 4), an error budget you are willing to spend (an experiment that burns budget is an expense, and chapter 2 says how to account for it), and a mitigation path. Never start chaos in a system that cannot be observed or rolled back.

Fault-injection layers

Faults can be injected at several levels. Start at the bottom (simple, common) and work upward as confidence grows.

Concrete mechanisms (illustrative; run them only in an environment you own, and the Linux command needs root):

# add 200 ms delay with 20 ms jitter to all egress on eth0
sudo tc qdisc add dev eth0 root netem delay 200ms 20ms
# ... observe the steady-state metric ...
sudo tc qdisc del dev eth0 root       # always remove it: this is your rollback
apiVersion: chaos-mesh.org/v1alpha1
kind: NetworkChaos
metadata:
  name: checkout-latency
  namespace: chaos-testing
spec:
  action: delay
  mode: one                  # affect a single matching pod
  selector:
    namespaces: ["shop"]
    labelSelectors:
      app: cart-service
  delay:
    latency: "200ms"
    jitter: "20ms"
  duration: "5m"             # the experiment ends by itself

Both examples have an explicit end. That is a design rule: faults should expire on their own, so that a forgotten experiment, or a crashed experimenter, does not leave production broken.

A tiny harness you can run

The program below is a miniature chaos platform against a simulated service. The service has four instances (40 requests per second each) serving 100 requests per second, and each request calls a dependency. A fallback flag switches on a timeout and cached response. The harness injects a fault at second 10, checks the steady-state hypothesis every second, aborts after three consecutive violating seconds, and always rolls the fault back. Time and randomness are simulated and seeded, so the output is deterministic.

"""A tiny chaos experiment harness against a simulated service (seeded, simulated time)."""
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Callable

class Service:
    """4 instances, each handles 40 req/s. Every request calls a recommendations dependency.
    With fallback=True a failed or slow dependency call degrades to a cached answer."""
    def __init__(self, fallback: bool, seed: int = 1):
        self.rng = random.Random(seed)
        self.fallback = fallback
        self.instances_up = 4
        self.dep_error_rate = 0.0
        self.dep_extra_ms = 0.0

    def tick(self, load: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
        """Simulate one second. Returns (success rate, p99 latency ms)."""
        capacity = self.instances_up * 40
        served = min(load, capacity)
        lat, ok = [], 0
        for _ in range(served):
            ms = self.rng.lognormvariate(3.4, 0.35)              # ~30 ms median own work
            dep_ms = self.rng.lognormvariate(2.5, 0.3) + self.dep_extra_ms
            failed = self.rng.random() < self.dep_error_rate
            if self.fallback and (failed or dep_ms > 250):        # timeout 250 ms, serve cache
                failed, dep_ms = False, min(dep_ms, 250) if not failed else 5
            elif dep_ms > 2000:
                failed = True
            ms += dep_ms
            lat.append(ms)
            ok += not failed
        shed = load - served                                      # no capacity: rejected
        total = served + shed
        lat.sort()
        p99 = lat[int(0.99 * len(lat)) - 1] if lat else 0.0
        if shed:
            p99 = max(p99, 1500.0)                                # queueing/rejects look slow
        return ok / total, p99

@dataclass
class Experiment:
    name: str
    hypothesis: str
    inject: Callable[[Service], None]
    rollback: Callable[[Service], None]
    min_success: float = 0.99      # steady-state: success rate...
    max_p99_ms: float = 400.0      # ...and p99
    abort_after_bad_ticks: int = 3 # abort condition: consecutive violating seconds
    duration: int = 30
    log: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

def run(exp: Experiment, svc: Service, load: int = 100, inject_at: int = 10) -> str:
    bad_run, t = 0, 0
    print(f"== {exp.name}\n   hypothesis: {exp.hypothesis}")
    for t in range(exp.duration):
        if t == inject_at:
            exp.inject(svc); print(f"   t={t:2d}s  INJECT")
        sr, p99 = svc.tick(load)
        violated = sr < exp.min_success or p99 > exp.max_p99_ms
        bad_run = bad_run + 1 if violated else 0
        if t >= inject_at and (t - inject_at) % 5 == 0 or violated and bad_run == 1:
            print(f"   t={t:2d}s  success={sr:6.2%}  p99={p99:6.0f}ms  {'VIOLATED' if violated else 'steady'}")
        if bad_run >= exp.abort_after_bad_ticks:
            exp.rollback(svc)
            print(f"   t={t:2d}s  ABORT: steady state broken for {bad_run}s -> rolled back")
            return "refuted (aborted)"
    exp.rollback(svc)
    print(f"   t={t:2d}s  end: steady state held -> rolled back")
    return "held"

def kill_one(s): s.instances_up -= 1
def kill_two(s): s.instances_up -= 2
def restore_all(s): s.instances_up = 4
def dep_slow(s): s.dep_extra_ms = 900
def dep_errors(s): s.dep_error_rate = 0.4
def dep_heal(s): s.dep_error_rate, s.dep_extra_ms = 0.0, 0.0

if __name__ == "__main__":
    results = []
    for fallback in (False, True):
        print(f"\n######## service fallback={'on' if fallback else 'off'}")
        for exp in (
            Experiment("kill 1 of 4 instances", "losing one instance keeps success >= 99%", kill_one, restore_all),
            Experiment("kill 3 of 4 instances", "losing three instances keeps success >= 99%",
                       lambda s: (kill_two(s), kill_one(s)), restore_all),
            Experiment("dependency 40% errors", "dependency errors do not reach users", dep_errors, dep_heal),
            Experiment("dependency +900 ms", "a slow dependency does not break the latency SLO", dep_slow, dep_heal),
        ):
            results.append((fallback, exp.name, run(exp, Service(fallback, seed=7))))
    print("\nsummary")
    for fb, name, r in results:
        print(f"  fallback={'on ' if fb else 'off'}  {name:24} {r}")

Read the summary at the bottom:

  • Killing one of four instances held in both configurations: with 3 x 40 = 120 requests per second of capacity against a 100 request load, there was headroom. This is the capacity lesson of chapter 8 in action: the hypothesis holds only because utilisation was below 100% after the loss. With a load of 130 requests per second the same experiment would have been refuted.
  • Killing three of four refuted the hypothesis everywhere, and was aborted after three seconds. The system cannot survive that; the experiment found its limit safely.
  • The dependency faults were refuted without a fallback and held with one. That is the circuit-breaker and fallback pattern from architecture chapter 17, verified rather than assumed. Netflix's Hystrix is the famous library built on that idea, and chaos experiments were how teams checked it worked.

The same shape scales up: in a real platform the "service" is production, tick reads your SLI from the metrics backend, and the injector calls a tool like those above. The skeleton (hypothesis, injection, check, abort, rollback) is identical.

Game days and tabletop exercises

Chaos experiments test the machine. A game day tests the machine and the people together: a scheduled exercise in which a team simulates or causes a failure and responds as if it were real, following chapter 16's roles. A good game day has a goal ("can we evacuate region B in under an hour?"), a facilitator who injects events and keeps the time, observers who take notes, and a debrief that produces action items in the same format as a postmortem (chapter 17).

A tabletop exercise is the cheap version: a discussion around a table or video call. A facilitator describes a scenario ("at 10:40 checkout errors double; here is the dashboard") and the participants say what they would do, step by step, without touching a system. It finds missing runbooks, unclear ownership and unrealistic assumptions in an hour at almost no risk, which makes it the right place to start.

Wheel of misfortune

The wheel of misfortune, a practice popularised by Google's SRE teams, is a tabletop training format for on-call engineers. A facilitator plays the system, drawing a scenario from a past incident or a plausible one ("the primary database is at 100% CPU, and the last deploy was an hour ago"). The trainee plays the on-call engineer: they ask questions, and the facilitator answers with the output the real tool would show. Others can play the IC, communications and a confused customer. It builds the habit of mitigating first and communicating, and it is a gentle way to bring new people onto the rota.

Analogy: the fire drill

Nobody believes a fire drill is a fire. They run it so that when the alarm is real, people already know where the exits are and the doors are not locked. A tabletop is walking the evacuation route on paper; a game day is the real alarm in a safe building; a production chaos experiment is a controlled burn.

Where it breaks down: a drill proves the building can be evacuated, but systems keep changing, so a drill's result expires. Hence the principle of automating and repeating them.

Go deeper: choosing what to try first optional

A useful ordering is by the product of likelihood and impact: instance loss and a slow dependency first (common), zone loss next, region loss and data corruption last (rare but severe, and better handled with game days than automated chaos). Draw candidates from your postmortems: every action item that says "we should verify that X works" is a ready-made experiment. And keep a catalogue of experiment results: a refuted hypothesis that was fixed becomes a regression test that runs forever.

Try it yourself

Run chaos.py, then predict before changing anything:

  • Change the load from 100 to 120 requests per second. Does killing one instance still hold? Why or why not?
  • Add a fifth instance. How many can you now kill and still hold?
  • Change abort_after_bad_ticks to 1 and then to 10. Which setting would you pick for production, and what does a longer window cost?
  • Add an experiment for a dependency timeout of 3 seconds with no fallback. What does the p99 do?

Then pick one claim your own team makes ("the service survives losing a node") and write it as a hypothesis with a steady-state metric and an abort condition. Do not run it yet: first read it aloud in a tabletop.

Distributed systems connection

In distributed systems the interesting failures are partial: a node that is slow rather than dead, a link that drops one direction, a dependency that works for some tenants. Chaos tooling is built to produce exactly those, which is why it exposes bugs that clean crashes never do. The principle of minimising blast radius is the experimenter's version of the cells and shuffle sharding of chapter 15: design so that being wrong is cheap.

Key takeaways

  • Chaos engineering is experimentation: define a measurable steady state, hypothesise it holds, inject a real-world fault, try to refute it.
  • The principles: hypothesis around steady state, vary real-world events, run in production, automate continuously, minimise blast radius.
  • Define abort conditions and rollback before you start; faults should expire by themselves.
  • Inject at the process, network, dependency and region layers, starting small and widening as confidence grows.
  • Game days and tabletop exercises test the people and the runbooks; the wheel of misfortune trains on-call responders.
  • A refuted hypothesis is a win: fix it, keep the experiment, and run it again.

Check your understanding

What should the steady-state metric in a chaos experiment usually be?

  1. CPU utilisation of the targeted host
  2. The number of faults injected
  3. A user-facing measure of normal operation, such as the request success rate or orders per minute
  4. Whether the on-call person is awake
Steady state is defined by system output that users and the business care about. Internal metrics can change without harm (CPU moves when an instance dies) and can mislead. The experiment asks whether the user-facing output survives.

An experiment injects a dependency fault and, within seconds, the success rate drops below the threshold. The harness rolls back. What is the right conclusion?

  1. The hypothesis was refuted, which is a useful finding: a weakness to fix, discovered safely
  2. The experiment failed and should be deleted
  3. The abort condition was wrong
  4. Chaos engineering does not work for that service
The goal is to find where the hypothesis breaks while the blast radius is small. A refuted hypothesis plus a quick rollback is the design working as intended.

Why does the principle "run experiments in production" come paired with "minimise blast radius"?

  1. Production is always safer than staging
  2. Blast radius is irrelevant outside staging
  3. Because staging has no users
  4. Only production shows real behaviour, but it also carries real risk, so exposure should be a small fraction of traffic for a short time with a ready abort
The two principles balance one another. Production has real traffic, configuration and dependencies, which staging cannot fully reproduce; containment lets you learn from it without making the experiment the outage.

What is the main difference between a tabletop exercise and a game day?

  1. A tabletop is for managers only
  2. A tabletop is a discussion of a scenario without touching systems; a game day actually exercises systems, tools and responders
  3. A game day has no facilitator
  4. A tabletop requires a production outage
Tabletops are cheap and safe and find gaps in plans and ownership; game days find gaps in tools, access and timing. Many teams do tabletops first and graduate to game days.

In the harness, killing one of four instances held but killing three did not, with 100 requests per second of load and 40 per second per instance. What explains it?

  1. Random bad luck
  2. The dependency was slow
  3. After losing one instance, capacity (120) still exceeded load (100); after losing three, capacity (40) did not
  4. Instances cannot be killed in simulation
Headroom decides whether failure of a part is invisible. Three instances give 120 requests per second against a 100 request load; one gives 40. This is utilisation and headroom, as in the queueing chapter.
