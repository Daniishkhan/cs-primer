Tail Latency at Scale

A server that is slow once in a hundred calls sounds healthy. Put a hundred of them behind one user request and most users now see the slow call. This chapter does the fan-out arithmetic, catalogues where the rare slowness comes from, and walks through the techniques Dean and Barroso collected in "The Tail at Scale": hedged and tied requests, micro-partitions, selective replication, probation, good-enough answers and canaries. You will simulate hedging yourself and see what it costs in extra load.

Why this matters

Modern products are assembled from many services, and a single page view or search can touch dozens or thousands of machines. At that scale the rare event is the normal experience: the 99th percentile of a component is the typical latency of the whole. If you only watch averages, or only the percentiles of a single service, you will ship a system that looks fine on every dashboard and feels slow to every user. Tail-tolerant design is also one of the clearest cases where an engineering technique (sending a request twice) is justified by arithmetic, and where the arithmetic also warns you when it will backfire. That combination, a clever trick plus a quantitative reason to be careful with it, is what interviewers and incident reviews both want to see.

Where we left off

You met the idea in networking chapter 13: percentiles describe the slow end of a latency distribution, the median hides it, and fan-out amplifies it. Chapter 8 added one cause of the slow end, waiting in queues, and showed that variability multiplies waiting. This chapter takes the next step: quantify the amplification exactly, list the many causes that are not queueing, and learn the techniques that make a system fast even though its parts are not. The source for most of it is the paper "The Tail at Scale" by Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso (Communications of the ACM, 2013); the case study in this course reads it in more detail, and this chapter does not repeat that discussion.

Fan-out: the arithmetic of the tail

A fan-out request is one that the front end splits into N sub-requests sent to N servers (often called leaves), then waits to combine all the answers. Think of a search engine sending the query to every index shard, or a feed service that fetches from dozens of backends in parallel. The user's response cannot be assembled until the slowest leaf answers.

Suppose each leaf is "slow" (above its own 99th percentile, say over a second, or over 100 ms) in 1 call out of 100, independently of the others. The probability that a single call is not slow is 0.99. All N calls must be not slow for the request to avoid the tail, and independent probabilities multiply:

P(at least one slow call) = 1 − 0.99N

For N = 1 that is 1%. For N = 10 it is 9.6%. For N = 100, the paper's example, it is 1 − 0.99100 ≈ 63.4%: almost two thirds of user requests experience at least one slow leaf, even though only 1% of leaf calls are slow. At N = 1,000 it is 99.996%, so effectively every request hits the tail.

A second way to say the same thing is more disturbing. At fan-out 100, the median user request (the one that is slower than half of all requests) is slower than all its hundred leaf calls only half the time. That happens when each leaf call is faster than its q-th quantile where q100 = 0.5, i.e. q ≈ 0.9931. So the median request at fan-out 100 experiences about the 99.3rd-percentile latency of a leaf. The leaf's "p99" has become the user's "p50". The same logic gives the leaf quality you need to meet a target on the whole request: for the user-facing p99 to be fast, the share of slow leaf calls must be tiny.

The same math applies to sequences and sessions

Fan-out is not the only way to multiply chances. A page that makes 40 backend calls (in series or in parallel) has the same exposure to the slowest one. A user session of 100 page views meets a 1% slow page at least once with probability 63%. And a request that passes through 8 services in series meets an 8-times-larger chance of a bad hop. Whenever one user-visible outcome depends on many independent draws, ask what the chance is that any of them is slow, not whether a typical one is.

Where does the rare slowness come from?

If every slow call had one cause, you would fix that cause. The sobering fact from the paper is that there are many causes, each rare, each plausible, and together they set the tail. Some live in the machine, some in the shared environment, some in the software.

Two properties matter for design. First, many of these are independent across replicas: a GC pause or a noisy neighbour on server A says nothing about server B. That is what makes "ask another replica" an effective cure. Second, you cannot eliminate them all. The authors argue the right attitude is the one fault-tolerant computing already took: just as we build a reliable system out of unreliable components (chapter 1), we should aim for a predictable whole out of less-predictable parts. The paper calls this being tail-tolerant.

That said, reduce variability where it is cheap. The paper lists measures such as giving interactive requests priority over batch work, breaking long requests into smaller pieces so short ones do not wait behind them (reducing head-of-line blocking), and throttling or staggering background activity so that it does not run on every machine at once. Variability that you remove at the source is variability you do not have to hedge.

Techniques: making the whole fast when parts are slow

The paper divides the techniques into two groups, by the time scale at which they act. Within-request techniques react in tens of milliseconds, inside the life of a single request. Cross-request techniques act over seconds to minutes and shape the system so that fewer requests meet slowness at all.

Hedged requests

A hedged request is the simplest and most widely used trick. Send the request to one replica. If it has not answered within a short delay, send the same request to a second replica, and use whichever answer arrives first. The key design choice is the delay. The paper's suggestion is to wait until the 95th percentile of the expected latency for this type of request. Then only the slowest 5% of requests ever generate a second copy, so the extra load is about 5%, yet because the slow 5% are exactly where the long tail lives, the improvement is large.

The paper reports a Google benchmark in which a read of 1,000 key-value pairs spread over 100 servers had its 99.9th-percentile latency fall from 1,800 ms to 74 ms by hedging after a 10 ms delay, at the price of sending only about 2% more requests. Treat the exact figures as the paper's, for its system and its workload. The shape of the result, a large tail improvement for a small cost, is what the simulation below reproduces.

Hedging has conditions. The request must be safe to execute twice: reads and idempotent operations qualify, while "charge this card" does not unless it carries an idempotency key (networking chapter 16). The slowness must be independent of the replica: if every replica is slow because the request itself is expensive, or the shared database behind them is struggling, the second copy is slow too and you have only added load. And there must be spare capacity, which is the subject of the cost section below.

Tied requests

Hedging has a weakness: during the delay, and after the second copy goes out, both servers might do the same work. Tied requests shrink that window. The client sends the request to two replicas at (almost) the same time, and each copy carries the identity of the other. As soon as one server starts executing its copy, it tells the other to cancel. If the request was waiting in a queue, the loser is removed before it consumes any CPU. Both servers can still dequeue at nearly the same instant and both do the work. The paper mitigates this by delaying the second send by roughly twice the average network delay. Tied requests suit systems where queueing delay dominates, because the whole point is to pick the shorter queue after the fact rather than guess which is shorter before. It is like standing in two checkout lines and leaving one as soon as the other starts moving.

Micro-partitions

If you give each machine exactly one big partition, then a slow or overloaded machine slows exactly one big share of the data, and moving it is slow and lumpy. Micro-partitioning splits the data into many more partitions than machines, for example 20 per machine. Load balancing becomes fine-grained (shift a few small partitions away from a hot machine), and recovery becomes parallel (a failed machine's partitions are rebuilt by many others at once, so recovery is quick). Dynamic reassignment of micro-partitions is how the system keeps any one machine's share of the work close to even. You have seen the same idea as virtual nodes in networking chapter 19 and data chapter 16.

Selective replication

Some items are far hotter than others: a popular document, a trending product, a celebrity account. Selective replication adds extra replicas of those items only, either predicted in advance (popular content) or detected as hotspots emerge. The extra copies spread the load across more servers, which keeps queues short on the hot item's machines. It is cheaper than replicating everything, and the load balancer then has real choice, which is also what makes hedging possible for those items.

Latency-induced probation

When a server is slow because it is sick (a failing disk, a runaway neighbour, a memory leak), sending it a fair share of the traffic guarantees a fair share of slow answers. Latency-induced probation temporarily takes a slow server out of rotation and keeps probing it with shadow requests so that it can be reinstated when its latency recovers. In the paper's formulation the exclusion is temporary and the system keeps sending probes. Service meshes and load balancers expose the same idea as outlier detection or ejection. A guard rail is essential: only eject up to a limited fraction of the fleet, because if the whole fleet is slow for a shared reason, ejecting everyone leaves nobody, and the load balancer must fall back to using them all.

Good-enough results

Sometimes the best fix is to stop waiting. In a search over many shards, the answer from the last 1% of shards rarely changes the result the user sees. A good-enough response returns after a deadline, or after most shards answered, with what it has, and may flag the result as partial. This trades completeness for latency, and it only works where an approximate answer is acceptable (ranking, recommendations, a dashboard tile, but not an account balance). It is the read-path version of the degradation strategies in architecture chapter 17.

Canary requests

Fan-out amplifies not only latency but also damage. A query that triggers a bug can crash thousands of leaf servers at once, all because the front end faithfully sent it everywhere. A canary request sends the query first to one or two leaves. Only if they answer normally does the front end fan out to the rest. The cost is one extra round-trip of latency in exchange for protection against "queries of death". It is the same logic as a canary deploy (chapter 12) applied to an individual request.

Simulating hedging

The program below builds the model from the first section. A leaf call usually takes about 10 ms (a log-normal, which is a common shape for service latency), but 1% of calls hit a "hiccup" of 50 to 300 ms. The request fans out to N leaves and its latency is the maximum over them. With hedging on, a call still unanswered after the leaf's 95th-percentile latency (measured from a large sample at the start) is sent again to another replica, and the first answer wins. The simulation is seeded, so you will get exactly this output. It counts the second copies it sends, so you can see the cost.

"""Fan-out to N leaf servers, with and without hedged requests. Seeded and deterministic.

Leaf latency model (ms): 99% of calls are lognormal around 10 ms; 1% hit a 'hiccup'
(GC pause, compaction, noisy neighbour) and take 50-300 ms. Leaves are independent."""
import random

SLOW_P = 0.01


def leaf_latency(rng: random.Random) -> float:
    if rng.random() < SLOW_P:
        return rng.uniform(50, 300)
    return rng.lognormvariate(2.3, 0.25)       # median about 10 ms


def percentile(sorted_xs, p):
    return sorted_xs[min(len(sorted_xs) - 1, int(p / 100 * len(sorted_xs)))]


# The hedge delay is the leaf's own 95th percentile, measured from a big sample.
rng0 = random.Random(1)
sample = sorted(leaf_latency(rng0) for _ in range(200_000))
P50, P95, P99 = (percentile(sample, p) for p in (50, 95, 99))


def request(rng, n, hedge_delay=None):
    """End-to-end latency = slowest of n leaf calls. Returns (latency, extra_calls_sent)."""
    worst, extra = 0.0, 0
    for _ in range(n):
        first = leaf_latency(rng)
        if hedge_delay is not None and first > hedge_delay:
            extra += 1                               # still waiting at the delay: send a second copy
            second = hedge_delay + leaf_latency(rng) # it starts hedge_delay later, on another replica
            first = min(first, second)               # whichever answers first wins
        worst = max(worst, first)
    return worst, extra


def run(n, hedge_delay, trials, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    lats, extra = [], 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        lat, e = request(rng, n, hedge_delay)
        lats.append(lat)
        extra += e
    lats.sort()
    return percentile(lats, 50), percentile(lats, 99), extra / (trials * n) * 100


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"one leaf: p50 = {P50:.1f} ms, p95 = {P95:.1f} ms, p99 = {P99:.1f} ms")
    print(f"hedge delay = leaf p95 = {P95:.1f} ms\n")
    print("P(at least one of N leaves is slow) = 1 - 0.99^N, vs simulated")
    rng = random.Random(3)
    for n in (1, 10, 100, 1000):
        sim = sum(any(rng.random() < SLOW_P for _ in range(n)) for _ in range(5_000)) / 5_000
        print(f"  N = {n:4d}: formula {1 - 0.99 ** n:6.3f}   simulated {sim:6.3f}")

    print(f"\n{'N':>5} {'hedging':<8} {'p50 ms':>7} {'p99 ms':>7} {'extra load':>11}")
    for n in (1, 10, 100):
        for label, d in (("off", None), ("on", P95)):
            p50, p99, extra = run(n, d, trials=20_000, seed=10 + n)
            print(f"{n:5d} {label:<8} {p50:7.1f} {p99:7.1f} {extra:10.1f}%")

    print("\nChoosing the hedge delay (N = 100)")
    print(f"{'delay':<14} {'p99 ms':>7} {'extra load':>11}")
    for label, d in (("none", None), ("leaf p50", P50), ("leaf p90", percentile(sample, 90)),
                     ("leaf p95", P95), ("leaf p99", P99), ("leaf p99.5", percentile(sample, 99.5))):
        _, p99, extra = run(100, d, trials=20_000, seed=500)
        print(f"{label:<14} {p99:7.1f} {extra:10.1f}%")

Read it in four steps. The first block confirms the formula: the simulated chance of at least one slow call matches 1 − 0.99N. The second block shows the damage and the repair. At N = 100 without hedging, the median request already takes 129 ms (the 63% of requests that hit a hiccup dominate) and the p99 is nearly 300 ms. With hedging at the leaf p95 (15.4 ms), the median becomes 24 ms and the p99 becomes 39 ms, for 5.0% extra calls. Even at N = 1 hedging helps the leaf's own p99 (57 to 23 ms), but the gain grows with fan-out because there are more chances for a hiccup to be rescued.

The third block is the design lesson. The hedge delay is a dial. Hedge after the median and you get a good p99 (33 ms) but 50% more load. Hedge after the leaf's p99 and the extra load is only 1%, but the hedge fires after the hiccup has already begun, so the rescue is later and the p99 is 78 ms. The leaf's p95 is a sensible middle: 5% extra load, p99 of 40 ms. Past the p99.5 the hedge barely fires and you are back where you started (194 ms). The paper's recommendation of the 95th percentile is a rule of thumb that lands near the knee of this trade-off, not a law.

What the simulation leaves out

The leaves are independent and have no queues, so the second copy never makes the first slower. Cancellation is not modelled, so the duplicate's cost is counted as a full extra call rather than partial work. Real slowness is often correlated: a shared dependency, a bad deploy, a network problem hitting a whole rack. When the cause is correlated, the second copy is also slow and the gain shrinks. Treat the result as an upper bound on what hedging can buy.

The cost of hedging: extra load meets the queueing knee

"5% extra load" sounds cheap. Chapter 8 tells you when it is not. Extra requests raise utilisation, and utilisation maps onto latency through the knee. Take a service at ρ = 0.80 whose time in system is 1/(1 − ρ) = 5 service times. Add 5% hedged load and ρ becomes 0.84: time in system is 1/0.16 = 6.25 service times, so the average request is 25% slower in return for fixing the tail. At ρ = 0.90 the same 5% takes you to 0.945 and the time in system from 10 to about 18 service times. In other words, hedging is a technique for systems with headroom. A system that is already near its knee cannot afford the discount.

That leads to three practical rules.

  • Budget the hedges. Cap hedges at a fixed fraction of traffic (a few percent). If the cap is reached, stop hedging. A budget like this is exactly the retry-budget idea from chapter 11, and for the same reason: unbounded extra work under stress turns a latency problem into an outage.
  • Hedge reads, not unsafe writes, and prefer hedging against a different replica or zone. Hedging against the same overloaded server only adds to its queue.
  • Make the delay adaptive. Measure the latency distribution continuously and set the delay to a rolling p95 or so, rather than a constant that goes stale when the service changes or the load rises (and with it all latencies, so a fixed delay starts firing for everything).

Hedging is not a substitute for capacity

If tail latency rises because the system is overloaded, sending more requests makes it worse: more load, longer queues, a larger tail, more hedges. That is a positive feedback loop of exactly the kind chapter 11 studies under the name metastability. Hedging belongs to a system that is healthy but unlucky, not to one that is saturated. A good implementation turns hedging off when the budget is exhausted, when the system is shedding load, or when the error rate rises.

A note on measuring the tail honestly

All of this assumes you can measure the tail, and measuring it is surprisingly easy to get wrong. A load generator that waits for each response before sending the next one stops sending exactly when the server stalls, and so silently omits the slow samples. The effect is called coordinated omission (a term from Gil Tene), and it makes a tool report a clean p99 for a system that has a terrible one. Percentiles also cannot be averaged across hosts (chapter 4). We will demonstrate coordinated omission with a runnable example in chapter 10; for now, remember that a number you have not measured honestly is worse than no number, because people will design against it.

Designing for the 99.9th percentile

Put the pieces together as a checklist for a fan-out service.

  1. Set the objective on the user-facing request (an SLO as in chapter 2), then work backwards using the table above to see which leaf percentile you actually need.
  2. Reduce fan-out where you can. A cache, an index that avoids asking every shard, or a coarser partitioning shrinks N and gives you "nines" for free. Adding an intermediate aggregation tier does not reduce the number of leaves, but it can move the timeout and hedging decisions closer to where they can be made cheaply.
  3. Remove variability at the source (GC tuning, background work scheduling, priorities, request-size limits).
  4. Add tail tolerance with a budget: deadlines, hedged or tied requests against different replicas, good-enough answers behind a deadline.
  5. Watch the tail: record latency histograms per leaf and for the whole request (chapter 4), so that you can see when a leaf's p99.9 starts moving, because it becomes your users' p50 at scale.

Distributed systems connection

Hedging, tied requests and canaries are all answers to the same fact: in a distributed system you cannot tell whether a slow server is slow or dead (networking chapter 15), so you hedge your bet by using redundancy that you already pay for, namely replicas. The reason replicas serve both availability and latency is that independent failures and independent slowness are both defeated by the same trick: ask more than one. The price is extra load, and load is what the next chapter is about: what to do when there is more of it than the system can serve.

Key takeaways

  • With fan-out N and a fraction p of slow calls, P(at least one slow) = 1 − (1 − p)N. At p = 1% and N = 100 that is about 63%: the leaf's p99 becomes the request's typical latency.
  • Each tenfold increase in fan-out moves the leaf percentile that matters up by one "nine". Reducing N is as valuable as speeding up the leaves.
  • The tail has many causes: GC, queueing, background tasks, shared resources, power management, contention, cache misses and packet loss. You cannot remove them all, so design to tolerate them.
  • Hedged requests resend after about the p95 delay and take the first answer; the extra load is roughly the fraction of requests that exceed the delay (about 5%). Tied requests have the servers cancel each other; both need safe-to-repeat requests and independent replicas.
  • Micro-partitions, selective replication and latency-induced probation shape the system over seconds to minutes; good-enough results and canary requests act inside a request.
  • Extra load is not free: it pushes you up the queueing knee. Budget hedges, adapt the delay, and turn hedging off when the system is overloaded.
  • Measure the tail honestly. Closed-loop tools omit the slow samples (coordinated omission), and percentiles cannot be averaged.

Check your understanding

Each of 100 leaf servers independently has a 1% chance of a slow response. A request waits for all 100. About what fraction of requests see at least one slow response?

  1. 1%
  2. 10%
  3. 63%
  4. 99%
The chance all 100 are fast is 0.99100 ≈ 0.366, so the chance at least one is slow is about 0.634. The answers 1% and 10% treat the slowness as if it did not accumulate, and 99% would need a fan-out of about 460.

At fan-out 100, which leaf percentile does the median user request roughly experience?

  1. About the 99.3rd percentile of a single leaf
  2. The leaf's median
  3. The leaf's 90th percentile
  4. The leaf's 99.99th percentile
The median request is one where the chance all 100 calls are faster than some leaf quantile q is 50%: q100 = 0.5 gives q ≈ 0.9931. The 99.99th percentile is what governs the request's own p99 at this fan-out, not its median.

You hedge requests by resending after the leaf's 95th-percentile latency. Roughly how much extra load does this add, and what could make it backfire?

  1. About 95% extra; it backfires when requests are idempotent
  2. About 50% extra; it backfires when the leaf latency is low
  3. No extra load, because the first copy is cancelled
  4. About 5% extra; it backfires when the system is near its queueing knee or when the slowness is correlated across replicas
Only requests slower than the p95 trigger a second copy, so roughly 5% extra. Near the knee even a few percent of extra load multiplies latency for everyone; and if all replicas are slow for the same reason, the second copy is slow too, so you pay load and gain nothing. Cancellation reduces the waste but does not eliminate the second copy.

What distinguishes a tied request from a hedged request?

  1. A tied request is sent to a replica in another region
  2. Both copies are sent at (nearly) the same time and each can cancel the other as soon as one starts executing
  3. A tied request is only sent after the p99 delay
  4. A tied request waits for both replies and compares them
Tied requests enqueue on two servers up front and cancel the sibling when one starts work, so the duplicate usually never runs. A hedge waits for a delay first and relies on the first copy still being slow. Neither compares replies.

A query crashes any leaf that processes it, and the front end fans each query out to 2,000 leaves. Which technique specifically protects against this?

  1. Hedged requests
  2. Micro-partitions
  3. Canary requests: send to one or two leaves first, fan out only if they survive
  4. Selective replication
The danger of fan-out is that a bad query is multiplied by N. A canary request limits the first exposure to one or two leaves. Hedging would only send the killer query to even more servers.

Why can't you rescue a tail-latency problem with hedging when the service is overloaded?

  1. Because overloaded servers ignore duplicate requests
  2. Because hedges are always slower than the original request
  3. Because tail latency under overload has no randomness to exploit
  4. Because the extra requests add load, deepening queues and making all replicas slower, which triggers still more hedges
Overload is a feedback problem: more load means longer queues and longer latency. Hedging adds load exactly when the system cannot afford it, so it can start a feedback loop. Hedge budgets, adaptive delays and load shedding exist to stop that.
