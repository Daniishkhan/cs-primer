Queueing Theory for Engineers

Why does a server that is "only 85% busy" feel slow, and why does adding one more machine to a pool help far more than one more machine to a fleet of separate pools? Queueing theory answers both with a handful of formulas you can carry in your head. This chapter derives them intuitively, checks them against a simulation you can run, and ends with the practical rules: what utilisation to aim for, how much headroom failover needs, and why variability is the quiet multiplier behind most latency problems.

Why this matters

Almost every performance incident in a healthy-looking system has the same shape: traffic rose a little, latency rose a lot, and someone said "but CPU was only at 80%". The relationship between load and latency is not linear, and the shape of the curve is not a quirk of one product. It follows from arithmetic about randomness and waiting, which means you can predict it before you ship and explain it afterwards. Senior engineers are expected to know where the knee is, which levers move it (more servers behind one queue, less variability, shorter jobs), and how much capacity to leave unused on purpose. The rest of this course leans on it: tail latency, capacity planning and overload are all queueing in different clothes.

Three numbers: λ, μ and ρ

A queue exists wherever work arrives faster, for a moment, than it can be served: requests waiting for a worker thread, queries waiting for a database connection, packets waiting for a link, patients waiting for a bed. Queueing theory describes such a system with a few numbers. You already met the most important relationship in systems chapter 14: Little's law, L = λW, says the average number of items in a stable system equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends there. That chapter also sketched the "hockey stick" of utilisation. This chapter explains where the hockey stick comes from and what bends it.

Arrival rate λ ("lambda")
Average number of requests arriving per second. For a web service this is simply requests per second.
Service time and service rate μ ("mu")
One server takes on average 1/μ seconds to do one request when nothing is in its way, so it completes μ requests per second when it never idles. A server with a 20 ms average service time has μ = 50 per second.
Utilisation ρ ("rho")
The fraction of time a server is busy. For one server, ρ = λ/μ. If 40 requests/s arrive at a server that can do 50/s, ρ = 0.8, meaning 80%.
Servers c
Several identical workers (threads, cores, instances) taking from the same queue. With c servers, ρ = λ/(cμ).
Waiting time Wq and time in system W
Wq is the time spent in the queue before service starts. W = Wq + 1/μ is the whole time from arrival to departure, which is what a user experiences.

Queueing theorists describe a system with Kendall notation, "arrivals / service / servers". M/M/1 means: M for arrivals that are random and "memoryless" (the technical name is a Poisson process: each arrival is independent of the others, and the gaps between them are exponentially distributed), M again for service times that are memoryless in the same way, and 1 server. M/M/c has c servers sharing one queue. G/G/1 is the general case, "G" for general distribution, which Kingman's formula approximates later in this chapter. Nobody's real traffic is exactly Poisson, but the models are close enough to build correct intuition, and where they are not, the later sections show which direction the error goes.

Analogy: the emergency department

Picture a hospital emergency department. Patients arrive at random (λ), each bed and team can treat a patient in some average time (1/μ), and the waiting room is the queue. Utilisation is the fraction of beds occupied. When the department is at 50% occupancy, a new arrival nearly always finds a free bed. At 90% the waiting room is permanently populated, and one bad hour (a bus crash, three chest-pain cases at once) pushes waits from minutes to hours. Hospitals know that a department running near 100% occupancy has no room to absorb the unpredictable, which is exactly the lesson of this chapter. We will return to the emergency department when we discuss triage as load shedding in chapter 11.

Where it breaks down: patients can be turned away, die or leave, and a hospital can borrow staff from other wards. A basic M/M/1 queue has none of that: nobody leaves, and the staff is fixed. Real services need those escape hatches too, which is what overload control is about.

Why a queue forms at all when ρ is below 1

If the server can do 50 requests per second and only 40 arrive, why is there ever a queue? Because the 40 do not arrive on a metronome. Imagine a perfectly regular world where a request arrives every 25 ms and each takes exactly 20 ms to serve. Nobody ever waits, at any utilisation below 100%. Now keep the same averages but let the arrivals be random. Sometimes two arrive 3 ms apart, and the second waits 17 ms. After a quiet stretch the server sits idle, and that idle time is gone forever: it cannot be "banked" to serve a later burst faster. Random gaps in the arrivals therefore cost waiting, and so does randomness in the service times. Waiting comes from variability, not from the average load alone.

This is the central insight, and it is worth saying twice. The average load decides whether the queue is stable (ρ < 1) or grows without bound (ρ ≥ 1). The variability decides how long the waits are on the way. The formulas below are what you get when you work out the arithmetic of that randomness.

M/M/1: the formulas, derived intuitively

Let N be the number of requests in the system (waiting plus being served) at a random moment. We want the average of N, and from it the average time. The derivation below is not a full proof, but each step is a statement you can check.

Step 1: balance. Think of N as a counter. It goes up by one when a request arrives (at rate λ, whenever the system is in any state) and down by one when a request finishes (at rate μ, but only if the server is busy, that is when N ≥ 1). In the long run, the rate at which the system goes from "n" to "n+1" must equal the rate at which it goes from "n+1" back to "n", otherwise the counter would drift. Writing P(n) for the fraction of time the system holds n requests:

λ · P(n) = μ · P(n+1), so P(n+1) = ρ · P(n)

Step 2: a geometric distribution. Each extra request in the system is ρ times as likely as the one before. So P(n) = (1 − ρ) ρⁿ, where the factor (1 − ρ) makes the probabilities add up to 1. Two consequences are worth checking by feel. The server is idle for a fraction P(0) = 1 − ρ of the time (at ρ = 0.8, idle 20%, which matches the definition of utilisation). And the chance of having at least n requests in the system is ρⁿ: at ρ = 0.9, the chance of ten or more requests in the system is 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 35%.

Step 3: the average length. Averaging n over that distribution gives

L = ρ / (1 − ρ)

At ρ = 0.5, L = 1. At 0.8, L = 4. At 0.9, L = 9. At 0.99, L = 99. (The sum is the standard geometric series; the formula is simply the average of the distribution above.)

Step 4: Little's law gives time. The average time in the system is W = L / λ. Substituting L and ρ = λ/μ:

Plug in numbers. One server that can do 200 requests per second (5 ms each). Each row raises the load; watch W stay flat, then explode in the last three rows.

The idea

  1. mu ← 200 requests/s, so one request alone takes 1/mu = 5 ms
  2. for each arrival rate lambda: rho ← lambda ÷ mu; spare ← mu − lambda
  3. W ← 1 ÷ spare and also (1/mu) ÷ (1 − rho): the two forms must agree
  4. L ← rho ÷ (1 − rho); check Little's law: lambda × W = L
"""M/M/1 queue, by hand: time in system W = 1 / (mu - lambda).
One server that can do 200 requests/s (5 ms each). Raise the load."""

# ── 1. The server ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MU = 200.0                                # service rate: requests per second
SERVICE_MS = 1000 / MU                    # 1/mu: 5 ms per request, alone

# ── 2. Walk the arrival rate up towards the service rate ─────────────────
print("lambda/s   rho   spare/s  W=1/(mu-lambda)  (1/mu)/(1-rho)   L")
for lam in (20, 100, 160, 180, 190, 198, 199.5):
    rho = lam / MU                        # utilisation
    spare = MU - lam                      # step 1: spare capacity
    w1 = 1000 / spare                     # step 2: W in ms, first form
    w2 = SERVICE_MS / (1 - rho)           # step 3: W in ms, second form
    length = rho / (1 - rho)              # requests in system (Little)

# ── 3. Little's law as a cross-check: L = lambda x W ─────────────────────
lam = 190
print()

A second way to see it. An arriving request finds, on average, L requests ahead of it. Each of them, because service times are memoryless, still needs an average of 1/μ to finish (even the one already in service: "memoryless" means that the remaining time does not depend on how long it has been running). Then the request needs its own 1/μ. So W = (L + 1)/μ = (ρ/(1−ρ) + 1)/μ = (1/μ)/(1 − ρ). Same answer.

Read the result as service time divided by the fraction of the server that is free. If the server is 90% busy, a request effectively sees a server that is 10% free, so everything takes ten times as long as it would on an empty machine. That one sentence is the whole latency knee.

The whole distribution, not just the mean

In M/M/1 with first-come-first-served service, the time in system is exponentially distributed with rate μ − λ. The mean is 1/(μ − λ), and the 99th percentile is ln(100) ≈ 4.6 times the mean, the 99.9th about 6.9 times. At 90% utilisation with a 20 ms service time, the mean is 200 ms, the p99 is about 920 ms, and the p99.9 is about 1.4 s. Percentiles move with the mean, so the knee in the average is a knee in the tail too, just steeper in absolute terms.

The latency knee

Plot W against ρ and the "hockey stick" appears. Everything is measured in units of the service time, so the picture is the same for a 2 ms cache lookup and a 2 s report.

The last column is the real lesson. Because W = (1/μ)/(1 − ρ), one more percentage point of utilisation multiplies the latency by (1 − ρ)/(1 − ρ − 0.01). At ρ = 0.5 that is a 2% increase. At ρ = 0.9 it is 11%, at 0.95 it is 25%, and going from 98% to 99% doubles it. The curve is not just high near 100%, it is steep, which means small, unnoticeable changes in traffic or capacity produce large changes in latency. A deploy that makes each request 10% slower also raises ρ by 10% (from 0.8 to 0.88), and the time in system goes from 5 service times to 1.1/0.12 ≈ 9.2: nearly double, from a change that looked small.

"But CPU is only at 70%"

Utilisation averaged over a minute hides bursts. A server that is 100% busy for 7 seconds and idle for 3 seconds is also "70% busy" over a 10-second window, and during those 7 seconds the queue grows without limit. Also remember which resource you are measuring. The queue forms at the bottleneck: a database connection pool at 100% can sit behind a CPU at 30%. Chapter 10 returns to finding the bottleneck.

Formula against simulation

Formulas deserve suspicion until you have watched them hold. A discrete-event simulation models a system by jumping from one event (an arrival, a departure) to the next rather than ticking in fixed steps. For a FIFO queue with c servers there is a pleasingly short trick: keep the times at which each server will next be free; an arriving request starts at the later of its arrival time and the earliest of those free times. The program below simulates 400,000 requests per row, with a fixed random seed, and compares the average time in system with the formula. The M/M/c rows use the Erlang C formula, which the next section explains. Run it yourself: it takes about a second.

"""Discrete-event simulation of FIFO queues with c servers, checked against the formulas.
Arrivals: exponential gaps (Poisson). Service: a pluggable distribution. Seeded, so reproducible."""
import heapq
import random
from erlang import mmc


def simulate(lam, mean_service, c, n, service_draw, seed, warmup=5_000):
    """One shared FIFO queue, c servers. Returns (mean wait in queue, mean time in system)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    free = [0.0] * c                      # min-heap of the times each server becomes free
    t = 0.0
    wq_sum = w_sum = 0.0
    counted = 0
    for i in range(n):
        t += rng.expovariate(lam)         # next arrival
        server_free = heapq.heappop(free) # the server that frees up first
        start = max(t, server_free)       # wait if every server is busy
        s = service_draw(rng, mean_service)
        heapq.heappush(free, start + s)
        if i >= warmup:
            wq_sum += start - t
            w_sum += start + s - t
            counted += 1
    return wq_sum / counted, w_sum / counted


def expo(rng, mean):
    return rng.expovariate(1 / mean)


def kingman_wq(rho, ca2, cs2, mean_service):
    """Kingman: E[Wq] ~ rho/(1-rho) * (ca2+cs2)/2 * mean_service"""
    return rho / (1 - rho) * (ca2 + cs2) / 2 * mean_service


def gamma_service(cs2):
    """Service times with a chosen squared coefficient of variation (shape k = 1/cs2)."""
    k = 1 / cs2
    return lambda rng, mean: rng.gammavariate(k, mean / k)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    N = 400_000
    print(f"M/M/1 and M/M/c: formula vs simulation ({N:,} requests each, mean service = 1)")
    print(f"{'model':<8} {'rho':>5} {'W formula':>10} {'W simulated':>12} {'error':>7}")
    for c in (1, 4):
        for rho in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9):
            lam = rho * c
            _, _, w_formula, _ = mmc(lam, 1.0, c)
            _, w_sim = simulate(lam, 1.0, c, N, expo, seed=42 + c)
            err = (w_sim / w_formula - 1) * 100
            print(f"M/M/{c:<4} {rho:5.2f} {w_formula:10.3f} {w_sim:12.3f} {err:+6.1f}%")

    print("\nKingman, G/G/1 at rho = 0.9 (Poisson arrivals, c_a^2 = 1; mean service = 1)")
    print(f"{'c_s^2':>6} {'Kingman Wq':>11} {'simulated Wq':>13}")
    for cs2 in (0.25, 1.0, 4.0):
        wq_sim, _ = simulate(0.9, 1.0, 1, N, gamma_service(cs2), seed=7)
        print(f"{cs2:6.2f} {kingman_wq(0.9, 1.0, cs2, 1.0):11.2f} {wq_sim:13.2f}")

Many servers, one queue: M/M/c and the pooling benefit

Now give the system c servers. There are two ways to arrange them, and the difference is one of the most useful ideas in the chapter.

Analogy: the supermarket versus the bank

A supermarket gives each till its own line, and you spend the afternoon choosing the wrong one. A bank gives everyone one line that feeds whichever teller is free. The bank's design means no teller is idle while anybody waits. Both have four servers and the same customers; the bank's average wait is shorter, and the worst waits are far shorter.

Where it breaks down: a single line only helps if every server can serve every customer. If one till is "express, 10 items or fewer", or one teller is the only one who can do mortgages, you are back to separate queues for those jobs. In software this is why sharding and sticky routing quietly give back the pooling benefit.

The numbers. For the same utilisation ρ, an M/M/c system with a shared queue waits much less than c independent M/M/1 queues. The key quantity is the Erlang C probability, named for the Danish engineer A. K. Erlang who worked out telephone-exchange capacity: C(c, a), the probability that an arriving request finds all c servers busy and therefore has to wait. Here a = λ/μ is the offered load, the amount of work arriving, measured in "servers' worth" (a = 3.2 means on average 3.2 servers are kept busy). The formula is

C(c, a) = [ aᶜ/c! · c/(c − a) ] / [ Σₖ₌₀..c₋₁ aᵏ/k! + aᶜ/c! · c/(c − a) ]

and the mean wait in the queue is Wq = C(c, a) / (cμ − λ), the probability of waiting multiplied by the average length of a wait once you are waiting. The intuition for the formula: the terms aᵏ/k! are the relative likelihoods of k servers being busy when no one is queueing (they come from the same balance argument as before). The last term is the weight of "all c busy", stretched by c/(c − a) to account for all the states with a queue behind them. Divide that by everything and you get the share of time all servers are busy.

Here is the whole thing in about a dozen lines of Python, with the comparison the diagram describes. Four separate M/M/1 queues are modelled by one M/M/1 at utilisation ρ, since they are identical and independent.

"""Erlang C and the M/M/1, M/M/c formulas (no simulation yet)."""
from math import factorial


def erlang_c(c: int, a: float) -> float:
    """P(an arriving request must wait) in M/M/c.
    c = servers, a = offered load = lambda/mu (in 'servers worth of work'); needs a < c."""
    if a >= c:
        return 1.0
    top = (a ** c / factorial(c)) * (c / (c - a))
    bottom = sum(a ** k / factorial(k) for k in range(c)) + top
    return top / bottom


def mmc(lam: float, mu: float, c: int):
    """Return (P_wait, Wq, W, Lq) for M/M/c with arrival rate lam, per-server rate mu."""
    a = lam / mu
    pw = erlang_c(c, a)
    wq = pw / (c * mu - lam)          # mean wait in queue
    return pw, wq, wq + 1 / mu, lam * wq


def max_utilisation(c: int, target_wq: float, mu: float = 1.0) -> float:
    """Highest utilisation at which the mean wait in queue stays <= target_wq (bisection)."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, 0.9999
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if mmc(mid * c * mu, mu, c)[1] <= target_wq:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return lo

Read the first table. At ρ = 0.8, four separate queues give a mean time in system of 5.0 service times; one shared queue gives 1.75, nearly three times better, with the same servers and the same total traffic. The second table explains why: with 1 server at ρ = 0.8, 80% of arrivals wait; with 16 servers, only 30% do, and the mean wait is under a tenth of a service time. The third table is the practical one. To keep the mean wait under half a service time, a single server must stay below 33% utilisation, a pool of 8 can run at 85%, and a pool of 64 at 98%.

Why pooling works. The aggregate load on a big pool fluctuates less, in relative terms, than the load on a small one. If each server is independently busy 80% of the time, the number of busy servers in a pool of 4 swings widely (two, three or all four busy are each common), while in a pool of 64 it hovers near 51 with swings of only a handful. Relative fluctuation shrinks roughly as 1/√c, an effect called statistical multiplexing. A queue only forms when demand momentarily exceeds all c servers, and for a big pool that is a rare, large coincidence. So large pools can safely run at high utilisation; small ones cannot. This is a tool for architecture decisions: one large shared connection pool beats many small ones, a single worker pool beats a pool per request type (unless you want isolation, see the bulkheads in architecture chapter 17), and a load balancer that sends each request to the least-loaded server approximates the single shared queue far better than one that picks at random (networking chapter 14).

Pooling is not free

One shared queue is one shared thing to synchronise: a lock, a dispatcher, a cache line, a network hop. It also loses locality (a request that would have hit a warm per-server cache goes to a random server) and it concentrates risk, because one poisoned request or slow dependency now affects the shared pool. The pooling benefit is a statement about waiting, and it competes with the costs of coordination. Where requests must go to the server that owns their data, as in a sharded database (data chapter 16), you cannot pool, and each shard is its own small queue that must be provisioned for its own peak.

Go deeper: square-root staffing and the other direction optional

The third table above has a famous practical form. For a call centre or a thread pool facing offered load a, a good rule of thumb is to staff c ≈ a + β√a servers for some small constant β (around 1 to 2 for decent service). The spare capacity grows only like the square root of the load, so the larger the system, the smaller the fraction you must keep idle. A load of 4 servers' worth needs about 2 to 4 spare (50 to 100%); a load of 64 servers' worth needs about 8 to 16 spare (12 to 25%). It is known as square-root staffing and is one reason hyperscale services can run at higher utilisation than a small start-up fleet can.

The opposite limit matters too. With a huge number of servers, the formula says nobody ever waits, but real systems still suffer from service-time variability: a single slow request does not queue, it is just slow. Pooling fixes waiting caused by randomness in who arrives when; it cannot fix a request that is slow once it is being served. That is the territory of tail latency.

Variability is the multiplier: Kingman's formula

M/M/1 assumed Poisson arrivals and exponential service times. Real traffic is neither. Requests come in bursts (a mobile app wakes up and sends six calls at once; a cron job fires at :00 on every host), and service times differ wildly (a cache hit takes 1 ms, a miss 40 ms, a garbage-collection pause 300 ms). We need a way to say "how variable" and to see what it costs.

The coefficient of variation of a quantity is its standard deviation divided by its mean, usually written c. Its square, c², is what appears in formulas. A perfectly regular sequence has c² = 0. An exponential distribution (the random gaps in a Poisson process, the service times in M/M/1) has c² = 1. A workload with occasional huge values, say requests that usually take 5 ms but sometimes 500 ms, can have c² = 10 or more. We write c_a² for the variability of inter-arrival times and c_s² for the variability of service times.

Kingman's formula (J. F. C. Kingman, 1961) is an approximation for the mean wait in a G/G/1 queue, any arrival pattern and any service-time distribution, that works best when the queue is busy:

E[Wq] ≈ ( ρ / (1 − ρ) ) × ( (c_a² + c_s²) / 2 ) × τ

where τ is the mean service time. Read it as three factors multiplied together: a utilisation factor (the knee, ρ/(1 − ρ)), a variability factor (the average of the two c² values), and a time factor (how long one job takes). When arrivals and service are both exponential, the variability factor is exactly 1 and the formula reduces to the M/M/1 waiting time you derived above, ρτ/(1 − ρ). When arrivals are Poisson and service is general (M/G/1) the formula is exact and is known as the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula.

Three conclusions follow, and each is a design principle.

  1. Variability multiplies the wait at every utilisation. A system with c_s² = 4 (some jobs far longer than others) waits 2.5 times as long as the M/M/1 baseline, and 5 times as long as one with perfectly steady service. Halving the variability halves the wait without touching capacity.
  2. Reduce service variability, not just the mean. Cap work per request (pagination limits, query timeouts, maximum payload size). Split big and small jobs into separate pools so a fat request does not hold up thin ones. Fix the cache so hits and misses do not differ by 40×. Use the fast path where you can. These all lower c_s².
  3. Smooth the arrivals. Bursts raise c_a², sometimes far above 1. Add jitter to scheduled jobs and client retry timers, batch fairly, and rate-limit abusive callers. The client side of this is why well-written SDKs add random jitter (see networking chapter 16).

The simulation earlier confirms the formula. The last table in its output sets arrivals to Poisson and varies only the service-time spread at ρ = 0.9: Kingman predicts waits of 5.6, 9.0 and 22.5 service times for c_s² of 0.25, 1 and 4, and the simulator measures 5.5, 8.9 and 21.5. The simulator comes out a little low, for the same slow-convergence reason as the M/M/1 row above: with Poisson arrivals the formula is exact (the Pollaczek-Khinchine result), so the gap is sampling noise in a finite run, not approximation error.

For several servers, the same variability factor is commonly applied on top of the M/M/c wait. That is the Allen–Cunneen approximation, and it is what the explorer above uses when c > 1: Wq(G/G/c) ≈ Wq(M/M/c) × (c_a² + c_s²)/2. Take it as a rule of thumb that gets direction and rough size right, and not a guarantee.

Open and closed systems

Everything so far assumes an open system: arrivals are independent of what the system is doing. A public web service is the textbook case. Millions of people decide independently when to load a page, and they do not know or care that your queue is long. If λ exceeds capacity the queue grows without bound. The decisive feature is that offered load is fixed by the world, not by your response time.

A closed system has a fixed population of N clients, each of which sends a request, waits for the reply, "thinks" for a while (time Z), and then sends the next. Think of a batch job with 32 worker threads, a database with a pool of 20 connections feeding it, or a load-testing tool configured with 100 virtual users. Here a slow server slows the arrivals down: while clients are stuck waiting, they are not sending more. The system cannot be overloaded without limit, because at most N requests exist. Little's law gives the interactive response time law for it: R = N / X − Z, where X is throughput and R the response time. Rearranged, X = N/(R + Z).

Open systemClosed system
Who sets the load?The outside world (arrival rate λ)The population (N clients, think time Z)

Real systems are mixtures. A single user is closed (they wait for the page before clicking), but a population of millions behaves like an open source at the scale of your service. This distinction becomes urgent when you measure: a load generator built as a closed system hides the very behaviour you are looking for, because it slows down exactly when the server does. That is chapter 10's coordinated-omission problem, and the reason the tools it recommends send at a fixed arrival rate.

Scalability is not free: the Universal Scalability Law

The formulas so far say how latency behaves at a given capacity. A related question is what happens to throughput when you add servers, threads or users. Neil Gunther's Universal Scalability Law (USL) models relative throughput C(N) at concurrency N as

C(N) = N / ( 1 + α(N − 1) + β·N(N − 1) )

Two overheads separate real systems from the ideal line C(N) = N. The contention term α is the fraction of work that is serialised (a lock, a single leader, a shared log): with α alone you recover Amdahl's law, and throughput levels off at a ceiling of 1/α. The coherency term β is the cost of keeping the participants consistent with each other (cache-line ping-pong, distributed consensus chatter, cross-node coordination), and it grows with the number of pairs, N². With β > 0 throughput does not just level off: it reaches a peak at about N* = √((1 − α)/β) and then falls.

You use it by measuring throughput at several concurrency levels in a load test (chapter 10), fitting α and β, and reading off both the peak and which overhead dominates. It is a model fitted to data, not a law of physics, and it is useful mainly inside the range you have measured: extrapolating far beyond it is guesswork. Its practical message is queueing's message from another angle. Throughput gains from adding servers diminish, and past some point adding concurrency makes a system slower. That is one reason a thread pool of 2,000 threads can perform worse than one of 50 on the same hardware, and it reappears in overload when a saturated service's goodput falls as load rises.

Practical rules

Turning the mathematics into numbers you can put in a design doc takes three decisions: a target utilisation, a failover margin and a way to notice you are wrong.

1. Choose a target utilisation from the latency goal, not from habit. Work backwards: if your latency objective is a p99 of five times the service time, M/M/1 says that is hopeless for a single queue: the p99 is about 4.6 times the mean W, so p99 ≤ 5 service times needs W ≤ about 1.1 service times, i.e. ρ below roughly 0.08. (A mean of five service times is what ρ = 0.8 gives.) If you have a pool of 16 workers sharing a queue, you can run much hotter. The table from the Python output gives a starting point: for the mean wait to stay under half a service time, 33% (c = 1), 75% (c = 4), 85% (c = 8), 92% (c = 16). Variability pushes all of these down, since the wait is scaled up by the factor (c_a² + c_s²)/2. Many teams settle on a peak utilisation of 50 to 70% for latency-sensitive tiers and let batch work run hotter, but these are conventions, not laws: do the calculation for your service.

2. Leave headroom for failure, not just for traffic. Utilisation at the current load is not the number that matters. What matters is utilisation after the failure you have promised to survive. If you run N identical servers and must survive losing one, you need N − 1 to carry the peak, an N+1 design; surviving two failures is N+2. The same arithmetic applies to zones and regions: losing one of Z equal zones leaves (Z − 1)/Z of the capacity, so the maximum pre-failure utilisation is (Z − 1)/Z of whatever you can tolerate afterwards. The sharper your tolerance, the more that costs.

Take a stateless service at peak 1,800 requests/s, with instances that can each do 100/s at your 70% target utilisation (70 requests/s each). Without failover you need 1,800/70 ≈ 26 instances. Spread over 3 zones and surviving the loss of one zone, the two remaining zones must carry the whole load at 70%, so you need 26 instances in two zones, i.e. 13 each, and therefore 39 across three: 50% more than the bare minimum, bought for the right to survive a zone outage. Chapter 10 works through regional capacity in more detail, and distributed chapter 18 covers why failover only works if the survivors really have the room.

3. Alert on saturation and queue depth, not on average utilisation. Utilisation hides bursts and is a poor leading indicator at the knee. Queue length (requests waiting for a thread, run-queue length, pending connections) and the time spent waiting are direct measurements of the thing queueing theory warns about. This is the "saturation" in the USE method and the golden signals (chapter 4).

4. Autoscale with lead time in mind. A scaling target of 60% means the system adds capacity when it is far from the knee, because new capacity takes minutes to arrive (instance boot, cache warm-up) while traffic can double in seconds. A scaling policy tuned to hold 85% looks efficient on a dashboard and is a few bad seconds from the steep part of the curve.

Distributed systems connection

Every distributed system is a network of queues. A request through five services is five waits in a row, and a delay at the busiest one sets the pace for the rest. The pooling lesson explains why work stealing schedulers, shared worker pools and least-loaded load balancing work, and why a hot shard (one queue at ρ close to 1 while its neighbours idle) is such a stubborn problem. Variability explains why fan-out hurts and why retries, which add bursts, make things worse. The next chapter takes the distribution beyond the mean and asks what happens when a request must wait for many queues at once.

Key takeaways

  • Queues form because of variability, not just load. Average utilisation decides whether the queue is stable; variability decides how long the waits are.
  • In M/M/1, time in system is W = (1/μ)/(1 − ρ) and the average number in the system is ρ/(1 − ρ). At 50% busy a request takes 2× the unloaded time, at 80% 5×, at 90% 10×, at 95% 20×.
  • The curve is steep, not just high: near ρ = 0.9, one more point of utilisation costs about 10% more latency. "80% busy is already busy."
  • One shared queue feeding c servers (M/M/c) beats c separate queues, and the more servers share the queue, the higher the utilisation you can run at the same latency. Erlang C gives the probability an arrival has to wait.
  • Kingman: wait ≈ [ρ/(1 − ρ)] × [(c_a² + c_s²)/2] × τ. Variability multiplies waiting at every load, so reducing it (smaller jobs, jittered arrivals) is as good as adding capacity.
  • Open systems (independent arrivals) can be overloaded without limit; closed systems (fixed clients) slow themselves down. Load generators that are closed lie about open services.
  • Plan utilisation for the failure you intend to survive: an N+1 or one-of-Z-zones design caps normal utilisation at (N − 1)/N or (Z − 1)/Z of what you can tolerate afterwards. Watch queue depth and saturation, not average utilisation.

Check your understanding

A single server with a 50 ms average service time behaves as an M/M/1 queue. What is the average time in the system at 80% utilisation, and at 90%?

  1. 100 ms at 80%, 200 ms at 90%
  2. 250 ms at 80%, 500 ms at 90%
  3. 250 ms at 80%, 2.5 s at 90%
  4. 62 ms at 80%, 69 ms at 90%
W = service time / (1 − ρ). At 80% that is 50 ms / 0.2 = 250 ms; at 90% it is 50 ms / 0.1 = 500 ms. The option with 2.5 s mistakes a 10× multiple for the wait when 90% is only 2× worse than 80% here. The 62 ms and 69 ms answers treat the queue as a small additive delay instead of a divisor.

You have 4 identical servers and the same total traffic, running at 80% utilisation. Which arrangement gives the lower average wait, and why?

  1. Four separate queues, because each server then has less work to coordinate
  2. They are the same, because the total capacity and total traffic are the same
  3. Four separate queues, because requests in a short line are served faster
  4. One shared queue, because no server idles while any request is waiting
With separate queues a server can sit idle while another has a line, and that idle capacity is wasted forever. With one shared queue the work-conserving effect removes that waste. At ρ = 0.8 the shared queue's time in system is about 1.75 service times against 5 for a separate M/M/1, nearly three times better. Coordination cost is a real counterweight, but it is a different cost, not a lower wait.

Arrivals are Poisson (ca² = 1) and you reduce service-time variability from cs² = 4 to cs² = 1 at the same utilisation. By Kingman's formula, the mean wait in the queue changes to about:

  1. 40% of what it was, since (1 + 1)/2 = 1 against (1 + 4)/2 = 2.5
  2. Half of what it was
  3. The same, because utilisation did not change
  4. A quarter of what it was
The variability factor is (ca² + cs²)/2. It was 2.5 and is now 1, so the wait falls to 1/2.5 = 40%. Utilisation sets the knee's position, but variability scales the whole curve, so it changes waiting even when ρ is unchanged. It does not fall by 4, because the arrival term is untouched.

A load-testing tool uses 50 virtual users, each of which sends a request, waits for the reply and then sends the next. The server freezes for 5 seconds. What does the tool most likely report?

  1. Thousands of slow requests, accurately reflecting what real users would see
  2. It speeds up to compensate for the missed requests, so the average is correct
  3. Very few slow samples, because the 50 users are stuck waiting and send almost nothing during the freeze
  4. No effect, because closed systems are immune to stalls
This is a closed system: arrivals slow down exactly when the server does. Real users of a public service are an open source and keep arriving during the freeze, so thousands of them would see the stall. The closed tool records only 50 stuck requests. The effect is called coordinated omission and is the subject of the next chapters.

Your service must still meet its latency target at 70% utilisation after losing any one of 3 equal zones. What is the highest utilisation you can run at when all zones are healthy?

  1. 70%
  2. About 47%
  3. About 35%
  4. About 23%
Losing one of three zones leaves 2/3 of the capacity, so the same load is 1.5 times as high afterwards. To land at 70% after the failure you must be at 70% × 2/3 ≈ 47% before it. Running at 70% in normal times would put the survivors at 105%, which is overload.

In the Universal Scalability Law, what does a non-zero coherency term β mean for adding more concurrency or nodes?

  1. Throughput levels off at the ceiling 1/α
  2. Throughput keeps growing, just more slowly
  3. It makes no difference once α is known
  4. Throughput peaks and then declines, so past a point more capacity makes things worse
Contention (α) alone gives an Amdahl ceiling. Coherency cost grows with the number of pairs of participants, N², and eventually outweighs the benefit of an extra participant: throughput peaks near √((1 − α)/β) and falls after that.
