Alerting & On-Call

An alert is a request to interrupt a human. Interrupt too rarely and outages go unnoticed; interrupt too often and people stop listening, burn out and quit. This chapter is about getting that trade-off right: alerting on symptoms users feel, deciding what deserves a page, the multi-window, multi-burn-rate method from the Site Reliability Workbook (with its numbers checked and a program that shows which windows fire on which kinds of incident), and the human system around the pager: runbooks, hygiene and the health of the on-call rotation.

Why this matters

You now have an SLO and an error budget (chapter 2) and the metrics to measure them (chapter 4). Alerting is where measurement meets people. A bad alert design has two failure modes with very different costs: the alert that never fires when users are suffering, and the alert that fires at 3 a.m. for something that fixes itself. The second is not harmless: it teaches responders to ignore alerts, and it is a leading reason good engineers leave on-call rotations. The same emphasis that SRE places on error budgets applies to alerting: it is an engineering problem with numbers, not a matter of taste.

Alert on symptoms, investigate causes

The central rule, found in Google's SRE book chapter on monitoring (and in Rob Ewaschuk's widely read essay on alerting philosophy that fed into it) is: page on symptoms, not causes. A symptom is something a user or the business experiences: requests failing, pages loading slowly, orders not being placed. A cause is a mechanism that might produce a symptom: a CPU at 95%, a full disk, a dead replica, a restart loop.

Why prefer symptoms? Two reasons, one about recall and one about precision.

  • Recall (you miss nothing). You cannot enumerate all causes in advance. A symptom alert ("checkout success rate below SLO") fires no matter which of a thousand causes is responsible, including ones you have never seen. A cause alert fires only for the causes you anticipated.
  • Precision (you are not woken for nothing). Many causes are not symptoms: CPU at 95% on one of 200 machines may harm no user at all, because the load balancer, retries and spare capacity absorb it. A cause alert can fire for hours with no user impact; the symptom alert fires only when someone is affected.

Cause information is not useless; it belongs on dashboards and in runbooks, where the on-call engineer reads it after being told there is a symptom. Some cause alerts are legitimate when they predict a symptom with enough lead time: "disk will be full in 4 hours" is a good ticket, because it is a symptom-in-waiting that humans can fix during the day.

Analogy: the ICU alarm

Intensive care units learned this the hard way. A bedside monitor that beeps for every transient dip in a reading produces so many false alarms that nurses silence them or stop hearing them, a documented hazard called alarm fatigue. Good alarm design alarms on a clinically meaningful condition that needs action, not on every wobble of a sensor. The patient's deteriorating condition is the symptom; a single noisy sensor is a possible cause worth glancing at.

Where it breaks down: a nurse can see the patient. An on-call engineer sees only the instruments, so the quality of the instruments (your SLIs) matters even more.

Page or ticket?

Not every alert deserves to interrupt a person at night. A useful split:

Three questions guard the pager. Is it urgent? (Will users or the budget suffer if no one acts for hours?) Is it actionable? (Is there something a human can do, not just "be informed"?) Does it need a human? (If a restart script is the fix, automate it and alert only when the automation fails.) An alert that fails any of these should not wake anyone. This is the same philosophy as the SRE concept of toil from chapter 3: a page that always leads to the same manual fix is toil to be removed.

Burn-rate alerting

How do you turn an SLO into an alert? Recall the vocabulary from chapter 2. An SLO of 99.9% over a 30-day window leaves an error budget of 0.1%: of every 1,000 requests, one may fail. The burn rate is how fast you are consuming that budget relative to the rate that would use it exactly up by the end of the window:

burn rate = observed error ratio / (1 - SLO)

A burn rate of 1 means you will finish the window with exactly zero budget left. A burn rate of 14.4 means you are failing 14.4 times the allowed fraction, and the whole month's budget will be gone in 30 days / 14.4, about 2 days. The fraction of the monthly budget consumed in a stretch of time is simply burn rate x (alert window / SLO period).

The Site Reliability Workbook (2018) walks through a progression of alerting strategies for this, each fixing a flaw of the previous, in its chapter "Alerting on SLOs": a plain error-ratio threshold over a short window, then a longer window, then alerting on burn rate, then several burn rates at once, and finally multiple burn rates with multiple windows. To compare strategies it names four attributes, which are worth memorising because they are the vocabulary for any alerting design:

Precision
The proportion of alerts that correspond to significant events. Low precision means false alarms.
Recall
The proportion of significant events that raise an alert. Low recall means missed incidents.
Detection time
How long after an event starts the alert fires. Long detection time burns budget before anyone looks.
Reset time
How long the alert keeps firing after the problem is over. Long reset time causes alerts that stay red after the fix and confuse responders.

The tension: a short window detects fast but flaps on brief blips (low precision); a long window is precise but slow to detect and slow to reset. A single threshold forces a compromise. The standard answer is two ideas combined.

  • Multiple burn rates. A fast burn (a hard outage) should page within minutes; a slow burn (a leak that would exhaust the budget in a week) should be noticed within hours or days but need not wake anyone. Use a high burn-rate threshold over a short window to catch the first, and a lower threshold over a long window to catch the second.
  • Multiple windows. Require the burn to be high over a long window (so the event is significant and not a blip) and also over a short window, typically one-twelfth as long (so the problem is still happening now). The long window gives precision; the short window gives a fast reset: when the incident ends, the short window recovers in minutes, so the alert clears, instead of lingering until the long window drains.

The Workbook's recommended starting parameters for a 99.9% SLO (its Table 5-8), which I checked against the published page:

You can re-derive the budget column with the formula above, which is a good habit: 14.4 x (1 h / 720 h) = 2%; 6 x (6 h / 720 h) = 5%; 1 x (72 h / 720 h) = 10%. The 720 hours is a 30-day period. The numbers are chosen so that each rule fires after a meaningful share of the budget is gone: the fast rule after 2% in an hour (the budget would last about two days at that pace), the medium rule after 5% in six hours (five days at that pace), and the ticket rule after 10% in three days (a month at that pace, i.e. a slow leak). Note that these are the Workbook's starting points, and you should tune them to your service and your tolerance.

How quickly does a given rule notice a given incident? If the error ratio during the incident is r and the rule's threshold ratio is T (burn rate x budget fraction), the long window's average first exceeds T after about T x window / r of the incident (as long as r is above T and the window starts out clean). Check against the table with a 99.9% SLO. A complete outage (r = 100%) trips rule 1 after 0.0144 x 60 min = 52 seconds. A 5% error ratio trips rule 1 after 0.0144 / 0.05 x 60 = 17 minutes. A 1% error ratio is below rule 1's 1.44% threshold, so it can never fire rule 1; rule 2 notices it after 0.006 / 0.01 x 6 h = 3.6 hours, which is 216 minutes. Keep those three numbers in mind for the program below, which reproduces them.

Evaluating alert rules on synthetic incidents

Rather than trust the arithmetic, simulate. This program builds a minute-by-minute error series for four incidents (with three healthy days first, so every window is full), implements the Workbook's three rules with both windows, and reports which rule fires, how long it takes, and how much of the 30-day budget has been spent by then. It ends by measuring reset time with and without the short window.

from dataclasses import dataclass

SLO = 0.999
BUDGET = 1 - SLO                     # 0.1% of requests may fail
PERIOD_MIN = 30 * 24 * 60            # 30-day SLO window
RPM = 1000                           # requests per minute (constant, for clarity)

@dataclass
class Rule:
    name: str; severity: str; long_m: int; short_m: int; burn: float

RULES = [
    Rule("2% in 1h",  "page",   60,        5,   14.4),
    Rule("5% in 6h",  "page",   6 * 60,    30,  6.0),
    Rule("10% in 3d", "ticket", 3 * 24 * 60, 6 * 60, 1.0),
]

def window_error_ratio(errs, t, w):
    lo = max(0, t - w + 1)
    return sum(errs[lo:t + 1]) / (RPM * (t + 1 - lo))

def evaluate(errs):
    """Return {rule name: first minute at which long AND short windows exceed burn*budget}."""
    first = {}
    for t in range(len(errs)):
        for r in RULES:
            if r.name in first: continue
            if (window_error_ratio(errs, t, r.long_m) > r.burn * BUDGET and
                window_error_ratio(errs, t, r.short_m) > r.burn * BUDGET):
                first[r.name] = t
    return first

def series(minutes, segments):
    """segments: (start_min, end_min, error_ratio)"""
    e = [0.0] * minutes
    for a, b, p in segments:
        for t in range(a, min(b, minutes)): e[t] = RPM * p
    return e

DAY = 24 * 60
LEAD = 3 * DAY                         # 3 healthy days first so every window is full
N = LEAD + 4 * DAY
T0 = LEAD + 600                        # incidents begin at 10:00 on day 4
scenarios = {
    "A: hard outage, 100% errors for 30 min":    series(N, [(T0, T0 + 30, 1.0)]),
    "B: blip, 20% errors for 3 min":             series(N, [(T0, T0 + 3, 0.20)]),
    "C: bad deploy, 1% errors for 8 h":          series(N, [(T0, T0 + 480, 0.01)]),
    "D: slow leak, 0.2% errors for 3 days":      series(N, [(T0, T0 + 3 * DAY, 0.002)]),
}
for title, errs in scenarios.items():
    fired = evaluate(errs)
    print(title)
    if not fired: print("   no rule fired")
    for r in RULES:
        if r.name in fired:
            t = fired[r.name]
            spent = sum(errs[:t + 1]) / (RPM * PERIOD_MIN) / BUDGET * 100
            print(f"   {r.severity:6s} {r.name:10s} fired {t - T0:5d} min after start; "
                  f"{spent:5.1f}% of the 30-day budget spent by then")
    print()

# Reset time: how long does the 1h page rule keep firing after scenario A ends?
errs = scenarios["A: hard outage, 100% errors for 30 min"]
r = RULES[0]
thr = r.burn * BUDGET
end = T0 + 30
def still_firing(use_short):
    n = 0
    for t in range(end, N):
        long_hot = window_error_ratio(errs, t, r.long_m) > thr
        short_hot = window_error_ratio(errs, t, r.short_m) > thr
        if long_hot and (short_hot or not use_short): n += 1
        elif n: break
    return n
print("After the 30-minute outage ends, the 1h page rule keeps firing for:")
print(f"   long window only        : {still_firing(False):3d} min")
print(f"   long AND 5-minute window: {still_firing(True):3d} min")

   page   5% in 6h   fired     2 min after start;   6.9% of the 30-day budget spent by then

Reading the output as an alert designer:

  • A, a hard outage. The 1-hour page rule fires in the first minute (at one-minute granularity), when 2.3% of the monthly budget is already gone; the 6-hour page rule joins two minutes in. The ticket rule fires too, since the burn is enormous. A human is paged almost at once, as intended.
  • B, a brief blip (20% errors for three minutes) fires nothing. It spent a sliver of budget, short windows were hot, but the long windows never crossed their thresholds. This is the precision the long window buys: the pager stays silent for events that do not matter.
  • C, a bad deploy at 1% errors. That is 10x the allowed rate but below rule 1's 1.44% threshold, so rule 1 stays quiet. Rule 2 pages after 216 minutes, exactly as predicted, with 5.0% of the budget gone. Without rule 2 this incident would burn 8 hours unnoticed until the ticket rule at 432 minutes. (Lesson: rule 2 exists for the incidents too small for rule 1.)
  • D, a slow leak at 0.2%. Only the ticket rule fires, after a day and a half, at 10% of the budget. It is a ticket by design: the budget would last 15 days at this pace.
  • Reset time. After the 30-minute outage ends, a rule using only the 1-hour window keeps paging for another 59 minutes because the bad half-hour is still inside the window. With the 5-minute window required as well, the alert clears after 4 minutes. That difference is the second reason for multi-window rules.

In a real Prometheus setup the page rule 1 would look like this (illustrative, not run here; recording rules for the ratios are the usual practice at scale to keep the expression readable and cheap):

groups:
  - name: checkout-slo
    rules:
      - alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
        expr: |
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout", code=~"5.."}[1h]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))
            > (14.4 * 0.001)
          )
          and
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout", code=~"5.."}[5m]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[5m]))
            > (14.4 * 0.001)
          )
        labels:
          severity: page
        annotations:
          summary: "Checkout burning 30-day error budget at 14.4x"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/checkout/error-budget-burn"

The number 0.001 is the error budget for a 99.9% SLO. Because both sides of each division are sums of rates (chapter 4's rule: aggregate then divide), a handful of failing requests on a low-traffic host cannot dominate. For genuinely low-traffic services there is still a problem: one failed request out of ten is a 10% error ratio. The Workbook discusses options such as combining services into a single SLO, generating synthetic traffic, or accepting longer windows; the honest conclusion is that burn-rate alerting works best when there is enough traffic for ratios to be stable.

Common misconception: "just alert when the error rate goes above X% for 5 minutes"

That single threshold is the first, naive strategy, and the Workbook explains why it disappoints: if X is low it flaps on blips (poor precision); if X is high it misses slow burns that still exhaust the budget (poor recall); and it says nothing about how much budget is at stake. Burn rate ties the alert to the SLO, so the pager fires in proportion to the damage.

Runbooks and alert hygiene

A page at 3 a.m. reaches a person who may be asleep, new to the service, or unfamiliar with this component. A runbook is the written guide attached to the alert: what the alert means, what the user impact is, how to confirm, the first mitigations to try (roll back the latest deploy, shift traffic away from the failing region, scale up, disable a feature flag), whom to escalate to, and which dashboard to open. Principles that make runbooks useful:

  • Mitigate first, diagnose second. The first steps should reduce user impact (roll back, fail over, shed load) even if the cause is not yet understood. Chapter 16 develops this.
  • Link it from the alert (see runbook_url above) and keep it next to the code so it is reviewed with changes.
  • Prefer automation to prose. If a runbook step is "run this command", that step is a candidate for a button or for removal of the need.
  • Test them in game days (chapter 19) and update after every incident. A stale runbook is worse than none because it is trusted.

Hygiene is the continuing work that keeps the alert set healthy. A short checklist worth enforcing:

  1. Every alert has an owner and a purpose. No orphaned alerts. If nobody knows why it exists, nobody dares delete it.
  2. Every page requires an action. Review the pages of the past week in the on-call handoff: which were acted on, which were not? Alerts that were not acted on are deleted, downgraded to tickets, or fixed.
  3. Group and de-duplicate. One incident should create one page, not two hundred. Alertmanager-style grouping, and inhibition (when the "region down" alert fires, mute the per-service alerts it explains), are how.
  4. Alerts carry context. Impact, a link to the dashboard and the runbook, and the relevant labels, so the responder is not starting from blank.
  5. Track the pager load as a metric (pages per shift, percent actionable, time to acknowledge) and treat regressions as bugs.

The human side: on-call health

The on-call rotation is part of your reliability system, and it is made of people who need sleep. Treat its health as a first-class measure.

  • Load. The SRE book recommends keeping operational work, including on-call, well under half of an engineer's time, and notes that an on-call shift should have few enough incidents that each can be handled properly, including follow-up (write the postmortem, file the fix). A rule of thumb to adopt: if the average shift has more pages than you can investigate, you need to reduce noise or add people, and no amount of heroics substitutes.
  • Rotation size and shape. Small rotations mean frequent shifts and no recovery time. Typical designs have a primary and a secondary (a backup who is paged if the primary does not acknowledge, and who helps in a big incident). Escalation policies ensure a missed page is not a lost page.
  • Follow-the-sun. Teams in multiple time zones hand the pager across so that each person is on-call only during their daytime. It eliminates night pages but costs coordination: the handoff, shared context, and a team size and spread that not every company has. An alternative is a single-site rotation with a strict policy for rest after a night page.
  • Handoffs. At each shift change, the outgoing engineer briefs the incoming one: open incidents, noisy alerts, risky changes in flight, and anything that was "weird". A written handoff note plus a short meeting prevents the same page being relearned from scratch.
  • Compensation and recognition. Being interruptible is a cost on a person's evenings, weekends and sleep, and organisations handle it differently: extra pay, time off in lieu, or both; some give none, and then morale suffers. There is no single standard, and local labour law matters; the principle that holds is that on-call burden should be acknowledged and bounded, not simply absorbed. Opting in, swapping shifts easily, and protecting recovery time after a bad night are as important as money.
  • Psychological safety. People who fear blame for a wrong decision at 3 a.m. hesitate or hide mistakes. Blameless review (chapter 17) is not only fairness; it is how you get honest reports, and honest reports are how you improve.

Alert fatigue is what happens when pages are too frequent, too often false or not actionable: people start acknowledging reflexively, muting channels, and delaying response. It is the pager version of the ICU problem described earlier. The cure is the discipline above: symptoms not causes, burn-rate thresholds that match user harm, deletion of unactionable alerts, and a rule that any alert that fires without producing an action produces an action item.

Systems connection

Alerting is a control loop whose controller is a person, and it inherits the tradeoffs of any control loop: sample too slowly and you react late; react to noise and you oscillate. The multi-window trick is a standard noise filter: require agreement between a slow, stable estimate and a fast, responsive one. The slow-burn ticket rule echoes systems chapter 14's point about queues: a trend that is invisible at any instant is obvious over time. And when many alerts fire together after one underlying failure, the dependency structure from the architecture course (a shared database or network) is why: group them by cause when you can.

Key takeaways

  • Page on symptoms users feel (good for recall and precision); keep cause information on dashboards and in runbooks. Use tickets for problems that can wait for daylight, and automate anything a script can fix.
  • Burn rate = error ratio / (1 - SLO); the budget fraction consumed is burn rate x (window / SLO period).
  • The Workbook's starting parameters for a 99.9% SLO: page at 14.4x over 1 h with a 5 m window (2% of budget), page at 6x over 6 h with a 30 m window (5%), ticket at 1x over 3 d with a 6 h window (10%). The short window is 1/12 of the long.
  • Judge any alert by precision, recall, detection time and reset time. Multiple burn rates cover fast and slow failures; multiple windows give precision and fast reset.
  • Detection time is about threshold x window / error ratio; very small incidents can be below every page threshold by design.
  • Alerts need runbooks that lead with mitigation, an owner, and a regular pruning habit; track pager load as a metric.
  • On-call is a people system: bounded load, a primary and secondary, good handoffs, a plan for nights, fair compensation and blameless culture. Alert fatigue is a reliability risk, not a morale footnote.

Which of these is the best candidate for a page?

  1. CPU on one of 300 web servers is above 90%
  2. The checkout error budget is burning at 14x for both the last hour and the last five minutes
  3. A nightly batch job took 5 minutes longer than usual
  4. A single node's disk is 70% full
The burn-rate condition is a user-visible symptom that is both significant (an hour) and ongoing (five minutes). The others are causes or minor conditions that rarely affect users and, if they matter at all, are tickets or dashboard items.

An SLO is 99.95% over 30 days. The service is failing 0.5% of requests. What is the burn rate, and how long until the month's budget is gone at that pace?

  1. 0.5; 60 days
  2. 5; 6 days
  3. 100; 7 hours
  4. 10; 3 days
The budget is 0.05%, so 0.5% / 0.05% = 10. At burn rate 10 the 30-day budget lasts 30 / 10 = 3 days.

Why does the multi-window rule require a short window (e.g. 5 minutes) in addition to the 1-hour window?

  1. So the alert clears soon after the problem ends, instead of staying red until the long window drains, and so it only fires while the problem is still happening
  2. To make the alert more likely to fire on tiny blips
  3. Because Prometheus cannot compute 1-hour rates alone
  4. To halve the number of alerts by always ignoring the long window
The long window gives significance (precision); the short window adds "still burning right now" and gives a fast reset. In the simulation, the page rule stayed on for 59 minutes after the outage ended without the short window, and 4 minutes with it.

An incident causes 1% errors on a 99.9% SLO. Which of the Workbook's three rules fires first, and roughly when?

  1. Rule 1 (14.4x, 1 h), after about 52 seconds
  2. Rule 3 (1x, 3 days), after about 7 hours
  3. Rule 2 (6x, 6 h), after about 3.6 hours
  4. None, the error rate is too low to be significant
1% is 10x burn, below rule 1's 14.4x threshold (1.44%) so it never fires, but above rule 2's 6x (0.6%). Time to fire for rule 2 is 0.006 / 0.01 x 6 h = 3.6 h. Rule 3 fires later (432 minutes in the simulation). Ten times the budget rate is hardly insignificant: it would exhaust the month in 3 days.

A team's pager fires 40 times a week and half of the pages need no action. What is the best first step?

  1. Tell the on-call to acknowledge faster
  2. Review the pages: delete or downgrade alerts that required no action, fix noisy causes, and group duplicates, treating each unactionable page as a bug
  3. Lower all thresholds so real problems are caught earlier
  4. Add a second rotation that also ignores them
Unactionable pages are the cause of alert fatigue and are fixable by engineering. Faster acknowledgement and more thresholds do nothing for precision, and lowering thresholds makes it worse.
