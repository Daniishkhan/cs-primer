Backups & Disaster Recovery

Replication protects you from a machine dying. It does not protect you from a deleted table, a corrupted write or a vanished region. This chapter is about the last line of defence: how much data you can afford to lose, how long you can afford to be down, what you build to meet those numbers, and why a backup you have never restored is only a hope.

Why this matters

Availability maths (chapter 1) assumes failures are independent and repairable. Disasters are neither: the whole region, the whole account, or the data itself is gone, and the fault is faithfully copied to every replica. Recovery plans are rarely used, so they quietly rot, and the first real use is on the worst day of the year. The antidote is to define the targets precisely, choose a strategy that can hit them, and rehearse it until it is boring.

RPO and RTO, precisely

Two numbers define a disaster recovery objective. They are easy to confuse with each other and with SLOs, so define them carefully.

RPO (recovery point objective)
The maximum amount of data you are willing to lose, expressed as time: the age of the newest data you can still recover, measured backwards from the moment of the disaster. An RPO of 15 minutes means that after a disaster you may lose up to the last 15 minutes of writes.
RTO (recovery time objective)
The maximum time from the disaster until the service is working again at an agreed level. An RTO of 2 hours means users should be served again within 2 hours.

Both are objectives. What you actually achieve in a drill or a real event are the actual recovery point and recovery time, and the gap between the two is the honest measure of your plan. Notice also that RPO says nothing about speed and RTO says nothing about data: a system can restore in five minutes from a week-old backup (great RTO, terrible RPO) or lose nothing but take two days.

Analogy: the hospital's emergency plan

A hospital plans for losing power or a wing. RTO is how long before surgery can resume somewhere else; RPO is how far back the patient records may be missing when the backup system takes over. A hospital that keeps paper charts in a second building is cheap but slow to resume; one with a mirrored twin hospital is nearly instant and enormously expensive. The question is never "can we recover" but "how fast, how completely, and at what price".

Where it breaks down: a hospital rarely needs to restore a hundred terabytes in a fixed order while a thousand dependent departments retry. Software recovery has to survive its own thundering herd.

The cost curve

Smaller RPO and RTO cost more, and the cost rises steeply because each step forces you to keep more things running, more things replicated and more things tested. There is no free lunch; the right target is where the marginal cost of the next improvement exceeds what an outage of that length would cost you.

Disaster recovery strategies

The AWS whitepaper Disaster Recovery of Workloads on AWS names four strategies, and the vocabulary is now common across the industry. They differ in how much of the recovery environment is already running when the disaster strikes. (The names below are AWS's; the ideas apply on any platform.)

Active/active deserves a caution. It removes failover, but you must still decide where writes go: all writes to one region (and promote another if it dies), writes to the nearest region with conflict resolution, or writes partitioned by key. Those are the multi-region trade-offs of distributed chapter 18. Note also that active/active does not protect against logical disasters: a bad delete replicates to every region. You still need backups.

Backups: types and the 3-2-1 rule

A full backup copies all the data. An incremental backup copies only what changed since the previous backup of any kind: small and fast, but restoring needs the last full plus every incremental in order. A differential copies what changed since the last full, so restores need just two pieces but grow as the week goes on. Point-in-time recovery (PITR) combines a base backup with a continuous stream of the database's write-ahead log: you restore the base and replay the log up to any chosen moment, such as the second before someone ran a bad query. That is the crash-recovery machinery of data chapter 13 reused as a time machine, and it is what gives you an RPO of minutes instead of a day.

The classic rule of thumb is 3-2-1: keep three copies of your data, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy offsite. Modern practice adds a fourth idea: at least one copy must be immutable (write-once, cannot be altered or deleted for a retention period) and logically separated (a different account or credential domain), so an attacker or a buggy script holding your production credentials cannot erase it.

Replication is not backup

A replica faithfully copies DROP TABLE within milliseconds. Replication protects against hardware loss; backups, versioning and immutability protect against mistakes and malice. Ransomware and operator error are both "valid writes" as far as a replica is concerned. If your only second copy is a replica or a synced folder, you have availability, not recoverability.

Restoring at the right granularity

Most plans imagine one kind of disaster: restore everything. Real incidents are often small and surgical: one customer's data deleted, one table dropped, a few hundred rows changed. If your backups can only be restored whole, recovering one tenant means restoring the world into a side system and extracting what you need, which can take days. The Atlassian case is the cautionary tale: a maintenance script deleted a number of customer sites, and recovering a small set of customers from backups designed for large-scale restores took far longer than anyone wanted. Ask of every backup: can I restore one tenant, one table, one row, without touching the rest? And separately, have you timed it?

The other classic failure is the backup nobody tested. The GitLab 2017 case covers it; the lesson here is the policy it implies, below.

Restore testing is the only proof

A backup has two states, usable and unusable, and you cannot tell which by looking at it. Backup jobs can succeed while writing empty files, compress corrupted pages, skip a schema, or depend on a key that nobody has. The only evidence that a backup works is a successful restore, and the only evidence of your RTO is the time that restore took. So make it routine:

  1. Restore automatically and regularly into an isolated environment (daily for critical data, at least weekly otherwise).
  2. Verify: checksums, row counts compared with the source, and an application-level query that exercises the data ("can we load the newest order?").
  3. Record the duration and alert if it drifts upward, since data growth silently erodes your RTO.
  4. Practise the human path too: can someone who did not write the procedure follow it at 3 a.m. with the usual systems down?

Control-plane dependency during recovery

The most painful discovery in a DR drill is that the recovery plan needs the thing that failed. Spinning up replacement servers, changing DNS, promoting a database, fetching credentials and even logging into the console are all control-plane operations (chapter 15), and during a regional event the control plane is often what is impaired or overloaded by everyone else doing the same thing. The AWS whitepaper itself recommends that failover use only data-plane operations where possible, because data planes are designed for higher availability than control planes. Practical consequences:

  • Prefer to pre-provision capacity (warm or hot standby) rather than depend on auto-scaling or API calls to create it during the disaster. This is the static stability idea of case study 8.
  • Keep the runbook, the credentials and the tooling outside the blast radius: not in a wiki hosted in the region that is down, not behind a login that depends on the failed identity provider.
  • Make failover a pre-built, rehearsed action (a single command or switch that uses data-plane mechanisms) rather than a chain of improvised steps.
  • Beware the thundering herd: when a region is evacuated, every dependent system retries and every customer reconnects at once. Capacity in the recovery region must absorb this, and clients need backoff (architecture chapter 17).

Region evacuation drills

A region evacuation drill deliberately shifts all production traffic out of one region and runs without it for a while. It is the only way to learn whether the other regions have the capacity, the quota, the data and the configuration to cope. Start small: drain a cell, then a zone, then a region during low traffic with everyone watching, and a clear abort button. Chapter 19 covers the general practice of rehearsed failure. Treat each drill's findings as you would a postmortem's ( chapter 17): contributing factors, owners, dates.

Calculating what a plan really delivers

Plans are usually described by their schedule ("nightly full backup, WAL shipped every five minutes"), while objectives are described as RPO and RTO. This script bridges the two. It models a backup schedule and a log-shipping stream, tries every failure minute over a day, and reports the worst-case data loss, then estimates the restore time from data size, network throughput and log replay. Time is simulated in minutes, so the output is deterministic.

"""RPO / RTO calculator over a backup schedule plus log shipping (simulated time, minutes)."""
from dataclasses import dataclass

DAY = 24 * 60

@dataclass
class Plan:
    name: str
    full_every: int          # minutes between full backups (they complete instantly in this model)
    incr_every: int | None   # minutes between incrementals, or None
    ship_every: int | None   # WAL/log segment closed every N minutes, or None
    ship_lag: int = 0        # minutes for a closed segment to arrive offsite

def recovery_points(plan: Plan, horizon: int) -> list[int]:
    """Times (minutes) at which each recoverable point *becomes available offsite*,
    paired with the data time it covers. Returns sorted (available_at, covers_until)."""
    pts = []
    for t in range(0, horizon + 1, plan.full_every):
        pts.append((t, t))
    if plan.incr_every:
        for t in range(0, horizon + 1, plan.incr_every):
            pts.append((t, t))
    if plan.ship_every:
        for t in range(plan.ship_every, horizon + 1, plan.ship_every):
            pts.append((t + plan.ship_lag, t))
    return sorted(pts)

def worst_case_loss(plan: Plan) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Try every failure minute in two days; loss = failure time - newest covered time
    among points that had already arrived. Returns (worst loss, minute it happens)."""
    horizon = 3 * DAY
    pts = recovery_points(plan, horizon)
    worst, when = 0, 0
    for fail in range(DAY, 2 * DAY):                 # skip the first day (warm-up)
        covered = max(c for avail, c in pts if avail <= fail)
        loss = fail - covered
        if loss > worst:
            worst, when = loss, fail - DAY
    return worst, when

@dataclass
class Restore:
    data_gb: float
    net_gb_per_min: float      # download throughput from the offsite copy
    incr_gb: float             # size of all incrementals since the last full
    replay_min_per_hour_of_log: float
    fixed_min: int             # provisioning, validation, DNS and humans

def rto(plan: Plan, r: Restore) -> float:
    total = r.data_gb / r.net_gb_per_min
    if plan.incr_every:
        total += r.incr_gb / r.net_gb_per_min
    if plan.ship_every:
        # worst case: replay a whole day of log since the last full backup
        total += (plan.full_every / 60) * r.replay_min_per_hour_of_log
    return total + r.fixed_min

def fmt(m: float) -> str:
    h, mm = divmod(int(round(m)), 60)
    return f"{h}h{mm:02d}m" if h else f"{mm}m"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    plans = [
        Plan("nightly full only",            full_every=DAY, incr_every=None, ship_every=None),
        Plan("nightly full + 4-hourly incr", full_every=DAY, incr_every=240,  ship_every=None),
        Plan("nightly full + WAL every 5m, lag 2m", full_every=DAY, incr_every=None, ship_every=5, ship_lag=2),
        Plan("nightly full + WAL every 5m, lag 45m (stuck shipper)", full_every=DAY, incr_every=None, ship_every=5, ship_lag=45),
    ]
    rest = Restore(data_gb=800, net_gb_per_min=6, incr_gb=60, replay_min_per_hour_of_log=2.5, fixed_min=40)
    print(f"{'plan':58} {'worst RPO':>10} {'est. RTO':>9}")
    for p in plans:
        loss, when = worst_case_loss(p)
        print(f"{p.name:58} {fmt(loss):>10} {fmt(rto(p, rest)):>9}")
    print()
    print("RPO target 15m:", [p.name for p in plans if worst_case_loss(p)[0] <= 15])
    print("RTO target 3h: ", [p.name for p in plans if rto(p, rest) <= 180])
plan                                                        worst RPO  est. RTO
nightly full only                                              23h59m     2h53m
nightly full + 4-hourly incr                                    3h59m     3h03m
nightly full + WAL every 5m, lag 2m                                6m     3h53m
nightly full + WAL every 5m, lag 45m (stuck shipper)              49m     3h53m

RPO target 15m: ['nightly full + WAL every 5m, lag 2m']
RTO target 3h:  ['nightly full only']

Read the table carefully, because it teaches three things:

  • Backups alone give an RPO equal to the gap between them (just under 24 hours for a nightly job). Incrementals shrink that to their interval. Only a continuous log stream gets you to minutes.
  • The worst case includes the lag. When the log shipper silently stalled at 45 minutes behind, the same plan went from a 6-minute to a 49-minute RPO. Monitor shipping lag as an SLI against your RPO; the RPO you promise is the lag you alert on.
  • RPO and RTO trade against each other. The plan with the best RPO has the worst RTO here, because up to a day of log must be replayed on top of the nightly full. Taking more frequent full or incremental snapshots would shorten replay and bring the RTO back; the numbers let you see by how much.

The throughput and replay rates here are assumptions. In your system, replace them with figures measured in your last restore test; that is the point of the previous section.

Go deeper: what is missing from the model optional

The script assumes backups are instant and consistent, restores are sequential, and nothing goes wrong. Real restores contend for network and disk, may need a decryption key held somewhere else, and often discover a missing step only when run. Realistic RTOs add the time to detect the disaster, decide to invoke the plan (a human decision that is often the longest delay), and verify the result before reopening traffic. Add those to your own number.

  • Increase data_gb to 3200 as the data grows. Which plans stop meeting a 3-hour RTO?
  • Change the target to RPO 5 minutes. Does any plan meet it? What would you change about the shipper?

Distributed systems connection

Disaster recovery is the point where several earlier ideas meet. Asynchronous replication gives a nonzero RPO by construction (data chapter 15); consensus-based synchronous replication across regions can give a zero RPO at the cost of write latency (distributed chapter 18); and static stability and cells (chapter 15) decide whether your recovery depends on the failed thing. Chapter 19 turns these from plans into rehearsed behaviour.

Key takeaways

  • RPO is the maximum tolerable data loss expressed as time; RTO is the maximum tolerable time to restore service. Judge a plan by its achieved numbers, not its objectives.
  • Cost rises steeply as both shrink. The four AWS-named strategies are backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby and multi-site active/active.
  • Worst-case RPO is the backup or log interval plus any shipping lag. Monitor the lag.
  • Follow 3-2-1, make one copy immutable and logically separate, and remember that replication is not backup.
  • Be able to restore at the right granularity (one tenant, one table), not just the whole system.
  • Only a restore proves a backup, and only a timed restore proves an RTO. Automate and verify it.
  • Do not let recovery depend on the control plane or the region that failed. Rehearse evacuation.

Check your understanding

A database is backed up nightly at 02:00 with no log shipping. It is lost at 01:50. What is the data loss, and which objective does it relate to?

  1. About 10 minutes; RTO
  2. About 23 hours 50 minutes; RPO
  3. None, because the backup exists; neither
  4. About 24 hours; SLA
The newest recoverable data is from 02:00 the previous day, so almost a full day of writes is lost. How much data you lose is the recovery point, governed by RPO; RTO concerns how long recovery takes.

Which DR strategy keeps a scaled-down, fully working copy of the stack running in another region, able to serve traffic at once at reduced capacity?

  1. Backup and restore
  2. Pilot light
  3. Multi-site active/active
  4. Warm standby
Warm standby has everything already running at small scale. Pilot light keeps core data infrastructure but leaves application servers switched off; active/active runs full capacity and serves traffic everywhere.

Why does cross-region replication of a database not replace backups?

  1. It replicates deletions and corruption too, so a bad write or malicious delete reaches the copy within seconds
  2. Replication is always slower than backups
  3. Replicas cannot be read
  4. Replication is only used for caches
Replication preserves the current state, including mistakes. Backups with retention, versioning, PITR or immutability give you earlier states to return to.

Your log-shipping job normally has 2 minutes of lag, closes a segment every 5 minutes, and then stalls so that lag grows to 45 minutes. Roughly what is the worst-case RPO now?

  1. 5 minutes
  2. 2 minutes
  3. About 50 minutes (segment interval plus lag)
  4. 24 hours
Worst-case loss is roughly the unshipped segment interval plus the delay before a closed segment is safe offsite. This is why shipping lag deserves an alert tied to the RPO.

A DR runbook says "launch replacement instances in the recovery region using the cloud API, then update DNS". The disaster is a regional control-plane degradation. What is the main risk?

  1. DNS cannot be updated at all
  2. The plan depends on control-plane operations that may be unavailable or overloaded at the moment they are needed
  3. Instances are too expensive
  4. Runbooks cannot reference APIs
Creating resources on demand is a control-plane action. Pre-provisioned capacity and data-plane failover mechanisms are more statically stable, which is why warm standby and rehearsed, data-plane failover are favoured for tight RTOs.
