Toil, Automation & Ownership

Reliable systems are run by people, and how those people spend their time decides whether the system gets better or slowly worse. This chapter covers the SRE answer to that problem: name the repetitive work (toil), cap it, automate it carefully, and organise ownership so that the people who build a service feel its consequences. It ends with the measurements that research found separate high-performing teams.

Why this matters

Chapters 1 and 2 gave you numbers for reliability. Numbers do not hold a pager. A team that spends every week hand-fixing the same problems has no time to remove them, so the service gets bigger and the work grows with it until something gives: usually the people. Much of what separates well-run services from fragile ones is not clever technology but whether the organisation protects time for engineering, and whether ownership and on-call are arranged so that learning flows back to the code.

The SRE model in one paragraph

The idea behind site reliability engineering, as laid out in Google's Site Reliability Engineering book (2016), is to treat running a system as a software problem. Instead of staffing a large operations team that grows in proportion to the service, you hire engineers who can write code, give them the pager and a set of explicit reliability targets (the SLOs of chapter 2), and require that they spend a bounded share of their time on operational work. The rest goes to engineering projects that make that work smaller: automation, better tooling, safer deploys, removing the sources of pages. The error budget gives developers and SREs a shared, numerical way to decide when to move faster and when to slow down, which turns the old tension between "ship it" and "keep it stable" into arithmetic.

Not every company has an SRE team, and many that do use the title differently. The model is still worth knowing because its parts (targets, budgets, toil limits, blameless learning) can be adopted by any team that runs software.

Toil

Toil has a specific meaning in the SRE book. It is operational work that tends to be: manual (a human does it), repetitive (again and again), automatable (a machine could), tactical (interrupt-driven and reactive, not strategic), without enduring value (the service is no better afterwards), and growing linearly with the service (twice the traffic or twice the customers means twice the work). The last property is the dangerous one, because it means toil scales with success.

Examples: hand-provisioning accounts or quotas from tickets; restarting a service that leaks memory every few days; copying a config change to twenty hosts; clicking through a deploy checklist; manually resizing a cluster before every launch; acknowledging the same noisy alert every night. Notice what is not toil: designing a better capacity model, writing automation, doing a postmortem, a code review, or attending a planning meeting. Those can be unpleasant or tedious, but they leave something behind. Toil also is not "work I dislike": some tasks are uncomfortable and still valuable, such as the thoughtful judgment during an incident.

Analogy: bailing a leaky boat

If water comes in at a steady rate and you bail all day, the boat stays afloat, and you have done honest work. But as long as you spend all your time bailing, the hole stays open. Eventually the boat gets bigger (more passengers, more water), and you need more bailers. Engineering time is time spent finding and fixing the hole. A sensible captain makes sure someone is always allowed to stop bailing and repair the hull.

Where it breaks down: in a real boat the leak is obvious. In an organisation, toil is spread across many people and many small tasks, each too small to justify a project, which is why teams need an explicit measure to see it.

The 50% cap

The SRE book proposes that an SRE team should spend no more than about half its time on toil and other operational work (tickets, on-call, manual interventions), reserving at least half for engineering projects. The exact figure is a guideline, not a law, and the idea behind it is a feedback loop. If toil climbs past the cap, the team has a signal to push work back (to the developers, the product owner, or a planning process) or to invest in removing it, rather than silently absorbing more. Without a cap there is nothing to push against, because toil is invisible until it has consumed the team.

Measuring is the first step: for a month, have the team tag tickets and interrupts by type and estimate the hours. People are routinely surprised. The second step is to rank toil by hours saved per hour of automation, and attack the top items. The third is to guard the project time, so that "just this once" interruptions do not eat it.

The ladder of automation, and its irony

Automation is not a switch; it is a ladder, and each rung trades human effort for human understanding.

In 1983 the psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge published a short, influential paper called "Ironies of Automation" (in the journal Automatica), about industrial plants and aircraft. Its central observations apply uncomfortably well to software:

  • Automation leaves the humans the hard part. The designer automates everything that can be automated, and the operator is left with what could not be, which is the rare, strange, high-stakes remainder.
  • Skills decay without use. An operator who only watches a system that usually works loses the practised judgment needed when it fails.
  • Monitoring is a poor job for people. Humans are bad at staying alert to something that almost never happens. Yet the supervisory role is exactly that.
  • The more advanced the automation, the more crucial the human contribution when it breaks. The designer who assumed the human was the weak link ends up depending on that human in the worst moments.

Translate this to a team with an auto-remediation system that restarts crashing services, a deploy pipeline that does everything, and autoscaling that handles load. For months none of it needs attention. Then the automation misbehaves (it restarts a service into a crash loop, or scales up so hard that it overloads the database) and the on-call engineer is the first person in months who has to understand how the machinery works, under pressure. The practical responses are modest and effective: keep manual paths working and exercised (game days, chapter 19); make automation explainable (it should log what it did and why, and be easy to pause); put a limit on what it can do unattended (a rate limit on restarts, a maximum scale-up per minute); and treat the automation itself as a service with an owner, an SLO and a runbook.

Common misconception

"If we automate it, it is solved." Automating a bad process makes it fast and wrong. Automate after you understand the manual process well enough to write it down, and expect the automation to need maintenance. A script that nobody understands is also toil, just deferred.

Who owns what: "you build it, you run it"

The phrase "you build it, you run it" is associated with Werner Vogels of Amazon, who described this approach in a 2006 interview with ACM Queue: the team that writes a service also carries its pager. The argument is about feedback. If the people who write the code never feel its failures, they have no direct reason to prevent them, and the people who feel the failures cannot change the code. Putting the pager where the code is closes the loop: a fragile deploy script or a noisy alert gets fixed faster when the person it wakes is the person who can fix it. There is no single right organisation. Here are the common models and what they trade:

ModelHow it worksStrengthRisk
Throw it over the wallDevelopers build; a separate operations team runs itSpecialised operational skillNo feedback to developers; blame; slow handoffs
You build it, you run itThe product team owns code and production, including on-callTight feedback; clear ownershipEvery team must learn operations; uneven quality; burnout for small teams
Embedded or consulting SRESREs share production responsibility, set standards and review launches, and help teams get to a defined level of readinessDeep expertise where reliability is criticalScarce; becomes a gate or a dumping ground
Platform teamA team builds the shared tools (deploy, observability, runtime) as an internal product; product teams use them and still own their servicesReduces the burden of "you run it" for everyoneIf it ignores its users, it becomes bureaucracy

A platform team deserves a note, because it is how many organisations make "you run it" bearable. The goal is a "paved road": a default, well-supported way to deploy, monitor and page that makes the right thing the easy thing, with a product mindset (the platform has users, whose needs it must learn). The related framing in Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais, 2019) describes platform teams as reducing the cognitive load of the stream-aligned teams that build user-facing services. A platform that nobody voluntarily uses has failed, however elegant.

On-call is a design problem too

Whoever owns production must be reachable. A sustainable on-call rotation needs enough people to share the load (small rotations mean being on call too often), clear escalation if the primary does not answer, documented runbooks, compensation or time off for out-of-hours work, and above all a limit on interruptions. If a person is woken several times a night, the system, not the person, is the problem. Chapter 7 covers alert quality and fatigue; chapter 16 covers how a response is organised. The key point here is organisational: the cost of a noisy system lands on specific people at specific times, and a healthy team treats that load as a metric to reduce.

Measuring delivery: DORA

How would you tell if an engineering organisation is doing this well? The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) programme studied thousands of teams for years. Its findings were popularised in the book Accelerate (Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble and Gene Kim, 2018), which identified four measures that predict software delivery performance:

MetricWhat it asksDimension
Deployment frequencyHow often do you ship to production?Throughput
Lead time for changesHow long from a commit to running in production?Throughput
Change failure rateWhat fraction of deployments cause a failure needing a fix, rollback or patch?Stability
Time to restore serviceHow long to recover when something does fail? (This is chapter 1's MTTR.)Stability

The headline result is that throughput and stability were not a trade-off in the data: the teams that deployed most often and with the shortest lead times also tended to have lower change failure rates and faster recovery. That fits the logic of this course: small, frequent changes are easier to understand and to undo, and the skills that make rollback fast also make shipping safe. Later reports added a reliability measure as a fifth dimension.

Use these carefully. They are diagnostic measures for a team to improve its own system, not targets to rank people or teams against each other; the moment a metric becomes a target it is gamed (deploy a trivial change a hundred times). They are also correlations from survey and observational research, so they tell you what high-performing teams look like, not that copying the numbers will make you one. And note what they leave out: how users experienced the service. That is the job of SLOs, which is why the two work together.

Try it yourself: a toil audit

No code today. For one week, keep a note of every interrupt-driven or repetitive operational task you or your team does: a manual restart, a ticket you answered by hand, an alert you acknowledged and ignored. For each, note how long it took and whether a script could do it. At the end of the week, sort by total minutes and ask: which one item, automated, would save the most? How would the automation fail, and who would notice? If you do not run a service, do the same with your own recurring chores.

Distributed systems connection

Self-healing systems are distributed systems that act on partial information: a restart controller infers "dead" from missed heartbeats (the failure detectors of the Distributed Systems course) and can be wrong. The ironies of automation are therefore not only about people: an automatic remediation that fires on a false positive in a thousand-node fleet can do more harm than the failure it responds to. Limits, rate controls and a clear way to stop the automation are part of the design.

Key takeaways

  • SRE treats operations as software engineering, with explicit targets and a bounded share of time spent on operational work.
  • Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical work with no enduring value that grows with the service. Measure it, and cap it (the SRE book suggests about half of an SRE's time) so there is room to remove it.
  • Automation is a ladder. Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" (1983) warns that automating the routine leaves humans the rare hard cases, with less practice. Keep manual paths rehearsed, make automation observable and limited, and own it like a service.
  • "You build it, you run it" puts feedback where the code is. Platform teams and embedded SREs make it sustainable; throwing code over the wall removes the feedback.
  • On-call load is a metric to reduce, not a rite of passage.
  • DORA's four metrics (deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore) found that speed and stability go together. Use them to improve, not to rank, and pair them with SLOs.
