What Reliability Means

"Five nines" is the most quoted and least understood number in operations. This chapter turns it into arithmetic you can do on the back of an envelope: how nines translate into minutes, how dependencies multiply your failures, how redundancy divides them, why recovering fast usually beats failing less, and the quiet assumption (independence) that makes every one of those formulas too optimistic.

Why this matters

Every reliability decision is a trade between cost and probability: another replica, another region, an on-call rotation, a slower and safer deploy. You cannot make those trades sensibly without knowing what a target actually means in minutes, what your dependencies already cost you before you write a line of code, and which lever (fail less often, or recover faster) is cheaper. This chapter gives you the numbers that every later chapter in the course leans on, from error budgets to cells and blast radius. It also teaches you to distrust the numbers in exactly the right place.

Two ways to say "available"

Availability is the fraction of the time (or of the requests) for which a service does its job. You met the idea in networking chapter 15, which introduced nines and the series and parallel formulas in a page or so. This chapter takes the same formulas and pushes on them until they break, which is where the useful engineering is. If any of the first paragraphs below feels familiar, skim; the interesting material starts with MTTR and correlated failure.

There are two natural definitions, and they disagree more often than people expect.

Time-based

uptime / (uptime + downtime). Every minute is either "up" or "down". Simple to explain, easy to put in a contract. It needs a rule for what counts as "down" (all users failing? 5%? one region?), and that rule is where the arguments live.

Request-based

successful requests / total requests. Every request is a vote. A 10-minute total outage at 3 a.m. costs a few requests; the same outage at peak costs thousands, and the metric knows the difference. Partial failures (2% of requests erroring all day) show up naturally.

Google's Site Reliability Engineering book (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy, 2016) makes the case for the second form at scale, and so will we. Consider a service that answers /health every minute and is "up" 100% of the time, while 2% of real user requests fail with a 500 because one shard is sick. Time-based availability says 100%. Request-based says 98%, and 98% is what the users experience. For the rest of the course, unless a table says otherwise, "availability" means the fraction of valid requests that succeeded, and chapter 2 shows exactly how to define "valid" and "succeeded".

Analogy: the emergency department

Suppose a hospital's emergency department advertises "open 24/7". Time-based availability asks: were the doors unlocked? Request-based availability asks: of the patients who arrived, what fraction were seen and treated safely and soon enough? An ED can be "open" every minute of the year while turning away one patient in fifty because the one trauma bay is full. The second number is the one patients care about, and it is the one an ED director actually manages, shift by shift.

Where it breaks down: an ED's patients differ wildly in urgency, whereas we usually treat requests as interchangeable. Real services also have "triage": critical requests (checkout) matter more than optional ones (recommendations), which is why the next chapter lets you pick which requests count.

Nines, in minutes

Availability is quoted in nines: 99.9% is "three nines", 99.99% is "four nines". Each extra nine divides the allowed unavailability by ten. The figures below use a 365-day year (525,600 minutes), a 30-day month (43,200 minutes) and a 7-day week (10,080 minutes), and they are what a service may be completely down, or the equivalent in partial failure, while still meeting the target.

Read the right-hand end of that table slowly. At 99.99% you have about one minute a week. A human being cannot be paged, open a laptop, find the dashboard and understand what is wrong in a minute. So four nines and beyond cannot depend on human response: it has to come from redundancy and automatic recovery. At 99.9% you have ten minutes a week, which a well-drilled on-call engineer can still use, barely. At 99% you can afford about three and a half days of downtime a year, and you can do almost everything by hand. The number you choose silently decides what kind of system and what kind of team you must build.

Common misconception

"We have 99.99% availability, so users never see failures." The table counts total unavailability and says nothing about its shape. 52 minutes a year could be one bad Tuesday afternoon or 52 separate one-minute blips. Customers experience those very differently, and so does your on-call rota. This is why the SLO machinery of the next chapter looks at windows and burn rates, not just a yearly percentage.

Serial composition: dependencies multiply

Most request paths are chains. A request enters a load balancer, hits an application server, which calls an authentication service, a database and a payment provider. If the request needs every one of these to succeed, the stages are in series, and (assuming failures are independent, more on that later) the probability that all work is the product of their availabilities:

Aseries = a1 × a2 × … × an

Two consequences follow, and both surprise newcomers. First, the chain is never better than its weakest link, because every factor is at most 1. Second, it is usually much worse, because the shortfalls add up (for small failure probabilities, the unavailabilities almost add: ten components each 0.1% unavailable give about 1% in total). This is what people mean when they say "your dependencies cap your availability".

The consequence is a design rule: a service should be more available than anything it hard-depends on, or it should stop hard-depending on it. Turning a hard dependency into a soft one (serve the page without recommendations; use the cached price if the pricing service is down; accept the order and charge later) removes its factor from the product entirely. That is the "graceful degradation" of architecture chapter 17, and now you can put a number on what it buys.

Parallel composition: redundancy divides failures

Now suppose you run n interchangeable replicas and any one can answer. The service fails only if all of them are down together. If each is unavailable with probability (1 − a), and failures are independent:

Aparallel = 1 − (1 − a1)(1 − a2)…(1 − an)

Two replicas of a 99.9% box give 1 − 0.001² = 99.9999%, six nines. That arithmetic is why redundancy is the foundation of high availability. But three caveats keep it honest:

  • Capacity. The formula assumes one replica can carry all the load. If you need two of your three servers to handle peak traffic, losing two is an outage even though one is still running. That is a k-of-n problem, computed below.
  • Failover is not free. Something has to notice the failure and shift traffic: a health check, a leader election, a DNS change (data chapter 15). That machinery has its own failure modes and takes time, during which requests fail. A hot pair behind a load balancer fails over in seconds; a cold standby may take many minutes.
  • The load balancer is now a series element. You added a component that all traffic passes through. Redundancy moves the single point of failure; it does not always remove it.

Composing a real system

Here is a tiny library that does the composition, followed by a typical web stack, and the two ideas about "need k of n". Read the output with the formulas in mind.

MIN_YEAR = 365 * 24 * 60          # 525,600 minutes
MIN_MONTH = 30 * 24 * 60          # a 30-day month: 43,200 minutes
MIN_WEEK = 7 * 24 * 60            # 10,080 minutes

def downtime_minutes(a: float, period_min: float = MIN_YEAR) -> float:
    return (1 - a) * period_min

def series(*avail: float) -> float:
    out = 1.0
    for a in avail:
        out *= a
    return out

def parallel(*avail: float) -> float:
    fail = 1.0
    for a in avail:
        fail *= (1 - a)
    return 1 - fail

print(f"{'target':>9} {'per year':>10} {'per month':>10} {'per week':>10}")
for a in (0.99, 0.999, 0.9995, 0.9999, 0.99999):
    print(f"{a*100:>8.3f}% {downtime_minutes(a):>8.1f} m "
          f"{downtime_minutes(a, MIN_MONTH):>8.1f} m {downtime_minutes(a, MIN_WEEK):>8.2f} m")

print()
print("10 hard dependencies at 99.9%  :", f"{series(*[0.999]*10):.5f}")
print("two replicas of a 99.9% box    :", f"{parallel(0.999, 0.999):.6f}")
pair = parallel(0.999, 0.999)
print("10 such pairs in series        :", f"{series(*[pair]*10):.6f}")
lb, app, db = 0.9999, 0.999, 0.9995
print("LB -> 3 app replicas -> DB     :", f"{series(lb, parallel(app, app, app), db):.6f}")
print("same, but only 1 app           :", f"{series(lb, app, db):.6f}")

def k_of_n(k: int, n: int, a: float) -> float:
    """P(at least k of n independent replicas are up)."""
    from math import comb
    return sum(comb(n, i) * a**i * (1 - a)**(n - i) for i in range(k, n + 1))

print()
print("need 1 of 2 at 99% each        :", f"{k_of_n(1, 2, 0.99):.6f}")
print("need 2 of 3 at 99% each        :", f"{k_of_n(2, 3, 0.99):.6f}")
print("need 2 of 2 at 99% each        :", f"{k_of_n(2, 2, 0.99):.6f}")

def mtbf_avail(mtbf_h: float, mttr_h: float) -> float:
    return mtbf_h / (mtbf_h + mttr_h)

print()
base = mtbf_avail(500, 1.0)
print("MTBF 500h, MTTR 60 min         :", f"{base:.5f}")
print("MTBF doubled to 1000h          :", f"{mtbf_avail(1000, 1.0):.5f}")
print("MTTR cut to 30 min             :", f"{mtbf_avail(500, 0.5):.5f}")
print("MTTR cut to 6 min              :", f"{mtbf_avail(500, 0.1):.5f}")

Look at the numbers, not just the code. Ten dependencies at 99.9% give 99.004%, two nines. A pair of 99.9% boxes is 99.9999%, and ten such pairs in series are 99.999%, five nines, built entirely from unremarkable parts. In the web stack, tripling the application tier took the whole system from 99.84% to 99.94%, but it can never beat the database at 99.95% times the load balancer at 99.99%, about 99.94%. Past a point, more app servers are wasted money: find the stage with the smallest availability that is not yet redundant, and fix that one.

The "need 2 of 3" row deserves a second look. Requiring both of two replicas at 99% each gives 98.01%, worse than one box, which is the series formula in disguise. Requiring two of three gives 99.97%, better than one box but much worse than the 99.9999% you get when any one suffices. N+1 redundancy for capacity buys less availability than "any one will do", and you pay for it with an extra machine.

MTBF, MTTR and why recovery speed wins

The formulas so far treat availability as a given property of a component. Where does it come from? Components fail and are repaired, alternately, forever. Let the MTBF (mean time between failures) be the average length of the working stretch, and the MTTR (mean time to repair, or to recover) be the average length of the broken stretch. One full cycle lasts MTBF + MTTR, of which only MTTR is downtime, so:

availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)

The usual words in this field are loose, so be careful in conversation. "MTTR" is expanded as repair, recover, restore, respond or resolve by different teams, and those measure different things (respond stops the clock when someone looks; restore stops it when users are served again). Whatever your organisation picks, the quantity that matters for availability is the time users are affected, from the start of impact to the end of impact. MTBF is similarly slippery for software: a hardware disk has a physical failure rate, but a software release's "failure rate" depends on how often you change it.

Now the practical result. Take a service that fails on average every 500 hours and takes an hour to recover. The calculation is in the script above:

  • MTBF 500 h, MTTR 60 min: 99.80%.
  • Double the MTBF to 1,000 h (half as many failures): 99.90%.
  • Instead cut the MTTR to 30 minutes (same failures, half as long): also 99.90%.
  • Cut the MTTR to 6 minutes: 99.98%.

Halving the downtime per failure is exactly as good as halving the number of failures, as the algebra says it must be. The engineering is not symmetric, however. To double MTBF you must find and remove half of the ways your system can fail, including the ones you have not imagined, and every release brings new ones. To cut MTTR by ten you need good alerts, a fast rollback, a rehearsed runbook and automatic failover, all of which work whatever the cause. Recovery is a general-purpose defence; prevention is a specific one. That is why the SRE tradition leans so hard on MTTR: a team that can undo any change in two minutes can afford to change things all the time.

The mean hides the tail

Incident durations are not bell-shaped. Most are short, and a few are very long, so the average is driven by rare awful days. Cutting the median recovery time from 10 minutes to 5 may do nothing for your yearly downtime if one incident in twenty lasts six hours. When you measure MTTR, look at the whole distribution (for instance the 90th percentile), and ask what makes the long ones long: usually it is slow detection, unclear ownership or a dependency with no fast mitigation. Chapters 16 and 17 return to this.

None of this makes failure frequency unimportant. Frequent failures wear out engineers (alert fatigue), erode user trust even when each failure is brief, and make retries and timeouts matter more. The claim is only that when you must choose where the next engineer-month goes, shaving the recovery time is usually the better-value bet.

Where independence breaks

Every formula so far hid the same word: independent. We multiplied unavailabilities as if each component's failures were unrelated coin flips. Real failures cluster, because components share causes:

  • The same software build: a bug in release 4.2 is on every replica at once.
  • The same configuration pushed everywhere, the same expiring certificate, the same feature flag.
  • The same physical fate: one rack, one power feed, one availability zone, one network device.
  • The same dependency: all replicas call one DNS provider, one auth service, one cache that everyone has quietly come to rely on.
  • The same load: when one replica fails, its traffic lands on the survivors, which then fail too (a cascading failure, covered in chapter 11).
  • The same human: the operator who runs the wrong command on every server.

Analogy: two operating theatres, one power feed

A hospital builds two operating theatres so that surgery can continue if one is out of action, and computes a wonderful reliability figure by multiplying the two failure rates. Then the mains transformer for the whole wing fails and both go dark together. The two theatres were redundant against anything that affects only one of them, and no better than a single theatre against anything they share. Good hospitals respond with an independent generator, so that "mains failed" stops being a common cause.

Where it breaks down: the hospital can see its transformer. In software the shared thing is often invisible: a library, a flag, a quota, a deployment tool. Finding the hidden common causes is half the work.

A standard way to model this is the beta-factor model from reliability engineering: assume a fraction β of a component's failures are common-cause ones that take down every replica at once, and the rest are independent. The stage's unavailability becomes U = β·q + ((1 − β)·q)ⁿ. The second term shrinks fast as you add replicas; the first does not shrink at all. Here is a small Monte Carlo simulation (seeded, so reproducible) that makes the point without any formula:

import random

def simulate(n_replicas: int, p_indep: float, p_common: float, trials: int, seed: int = 11) -> float:
    """Fraction of time-slots where ALL replicas are down.
    p_indep: chance each replica is down in a slot, independently.
    p_common: chance a shared cause (bad deploy, shared DB, same AZ) takes all down."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    bad = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        if rng.random() < p_common:
            bad += 1
            continue
        if all(rng.random() < p_indep for _ in range(n_replicas)):
            bad += 1
    return bad / trials

a = 0.999
p = 1 - a
for n in (1, 2, 3):
    ideal = p ** n
    print(f"n={n}: independent model unavail {ideal:.2e}", end="  | ")
    for pc in (0.0, 0.0001, 0.0005):
        got = simulate(n, p, pc, 2_000_000)
        print(f"p_common={pc:<6} -> {got:.2e}", end="  ")
    print()
n=1: independent model unavail 1.00e-03  | p_common=0.0    -> 9.78e-04  p_common=0.0001 -> 1.09e-03  p_common=0.0005 -> 1.49e-03  
n=2: independent model unavail 1.00e-06  | p_common=0.0    -> 1.50e-06  p_common=0.0001 -> 9.10e-05  p_common=0.0005 -> 5.13e-04  
n=3: independent model unavail 1.00e-09  | p_common=0.0    -> 0.00e+00  p_common=0.0001 -> 9.00e-05  p_common=0.0005 -> 5.13e-04

Read the p_common=0.0 column first (the independent case), then move right. With zero common cause, two replicas of a 99.9% box reach roughly 10⁻⁶ unavailability, as promised (the simulated 1.5e-06 is sampling noise: with two million trials only a few events occur). The three-replica row shows 0.00e+00 because 10⁻⁹ events are too rare to appear in two million trials, which is itself a lesson about trying to test your way to nine nines. Now add a shared cause that is active just 0.01% of the time, under an hour a year. Two replicas jump from 10⁻⁶ to about 9×10⁻⁵, ninety times worse, and the third replica buys nothing at all: both rows show the same 9×10⁻⁵. Once a common cause dominates, adding copies is money wasted. The cure is to reduce what is shared: stagger deploys, spread across zones or regions, use different providers for critical dependencies, and make sure the "backup" path does not run through the same component as the primary.

You have already seen this pattern in the wild. The S3 outage case was a single mistyped command whose reach was far larger than intended: a common cause for services believed independent. Architecture chapter 18 described canaries and staged rollout as the answer to the "same build, same config" row of the list above; distributed chapter 18 described multi-region designs as the answer to the "same zone" row. Chapter 15 of this course (cells, shuffle sharding and static stability) is the systematic treatment: structure the system so that any one common cause can reach only a small fraction of customers.

Go deeper: deriving the formulas optional

MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Model a component as alternating up periods of mean length U and down periods of mean length D. Over a long run containing k cycles, total time is about k(U + D) and downtime is about kD, so the long-run fraction of time down is D/(U + D), and availability is its complement. This needs only the means, not the distributions, which is why the formula is so widely used. In reliability theory this is the steady-state availability of an alternating renewal process.

k-of-n. With n independent replicas each up with probability a, the number up is binomial. The service works if at least k are up, so A = Σ C(n,i)·aⁱ·(1−a)ⁿ⁻ⁱ for i = k…n. The script's k_of_n does exactly this. With k = 1 it reduces to the parallel formula, and with k = n to the series formula.

Small unavailabilities add. For small u, (1 − u)ⁿ ≈ 1 − n·u. That is why a chain of ten 0.1% components is about 1% down: you can add unavailabilities in your head when they are small. The approximation overstates the harm slightly for large n·u.

Durability is a different number

Storage systems quote two figures that look alike and mean different things. Availability asks whether you can read your data right now. Durability asks whether the data still exists at all, ever. Object stores typically advertise durability with a long run of nines (eleven is a common design target in public documentation) and availability with far fewer (three or four). Those are not contradictory: a storage service can be unreachable for an hour (an availability event, recoverable by waiting) while losing nothing, and a system can be reachable all year and then discover that a year-old object has silently vanished.

Availability nines

Measured in time or requests. Violations are temporary and users can usually retry. Improved by redundancy, fast failover and safe deploys. A breach costs trust and revenue.

Durability nines

Measured per object per year, as the chance of permanent loss. Violations are irreversible. Improved by many copies or erasure codes across independent failure domains, fast re-replication, checksums and scrubbing. A breach can end a company.

The two share a hidden ingredient: repair time. Data is lost when enough replicas fail before the first failure has been repaired, so durability depends on how quickly lost copies are rebuilt, which is the MTTR of storage. It also depends on independence in the same unforgiving way: three copies on three disks in one rack, written by one buggy code path, are not three independent copies. And, as the GitLab case showed, the durability you actually have is the durability of the copies you can restore, which is why chapter 18 insists that a backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup.

How many nines do you need?

This is judgment, and here is one opinion. The right target is not the highest you can afford but the one at which users stop noticing and at which the next nine costs more than it earns. A few considerations pull in the same direction:

  • Users reach you through their own phone, Wi-Fi and ISP, which are not five-nines systems. Past a point your improvement is drowned in noise on their side.
  • Each nine tends to cost far more than the last: more regions, more automation, slower and more careful releases, and a team that can sustain them.
  • Not every feature is equal. Checkout may deserve four nines while the "recently viewed" widget deserves two. A single global target for everything is usually wrong in both directions.
  • Dependencies cap you. If your payment provider offers 99.95%, promising 99.99% for checkout needs a fallback path, not hope.

The next chapter turns this judgment into a mechanism: choose a target per user journey, measure it honestly, and treat the gap between the target and 100% as a budget to spend.

Distributed systems connection

Replication, quorums and consensus (the Distributed Systems course) are the mechanisms that make the parallel formula real: they let several machines act as one service that survives the loss of some. The beta-factor lesson is the practical limit of all of them: a quorum of replicas running the same code with the same configuration in one data centre protects you from a dead machine and from nothing else. Reliability engineering is largely the work of finding what the replicas share.

Key takeaways

  • Prefer request-based availability (good requests over valid requests) to time-based: it weights outages by their real impact and sees partial failures. At 99.99% you have about a minute a week, which humans cannot respond to, so very high targets require automation.
  • Hard dependencies in series multiply: ten 99.9% dependencies give about 99.0%. Your service cannot beat its weakest hard dependency; making a dependency soft removes its factor.
  • Redundancy in parallel multiplies unavailabilities (two 99.9% copies give 99.9999%), but only if one copy can carry the load, failover works, and the failures are independent. "Need 2 of 3" buys far less than "any 1 will do".
  • Availability is MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Halving MTTR is worth as much as halving failures and is usually far cheaper, because detection, rollback and failover work for every cause. Look at the distribution of recovery times, not just the mean.
  • Independence is the assumption that breaks. Common causes (same build, config, zone, dependency, operator) put a floor under unavailability that more replicas cannot lower; reduce what is shared instead.
  • Durability nines and availability nines measure different things: temporary inaccessibility versus permanent loss. Durability depends on repair speed, independence, and being able to restore.

Check your understanding

A request needs five services in series, each 99.95% available and failing independently. Roughly what is the best availability of the chain?

  2. 99.75%

A health-check endpoint answers every minute, so the service is "up" for every one of the 1,440 minutes in a day, yet 2% of real user requests returned 500 all day. What do time-based and request-based availability say?

  1. Both say 98%
  2. Time-based says 98%, request-based says 100%
  3. Both say 100%
  4. Time-based says 100%, request-based says 98%
Time-based availability only asks whether the service was "up" and a passing health check says yes every minute. Request-based availability counts what users experienced: 98% succeeded. This gap is why SLIs are defined over real requests, not over probes.

A service has MTBF of 100 hours and MTTR of 1 hour. What is its availability, and what is the cheapest way to reach 99.5%?

  1. About 99.0%. Halving the MTTR to 30 minutes gives about 99.5%, matching the effect of doubling the MTBF to 200 hours
  2. About 99.9%. It already exceeds the target
  3. About 90%. Only a tenfold MTBF increase would help
  4. About 99.0%. Only increasing MTBF can change availability
100 / 101 is about 99.01%. With MTTR at 0.5 hours it is 100 / 100.5, about 99.5%, and with MTBF at 200 hours it is 200 / 201, also about 99.5%: algebraically the same. In practice shortening recovery (faster detection, automated rollback) is usually the cheaper lever.

You run three replicas that fail independently at 99.9% each, yet measured availability of the stage is stuck near 99.99%. Which is the most likely explanation?

  1. The formula for parallel composition is wrong
  2. You need a fourth replica
  3. A common cause, such as a shared build, config or zone, takes all replicas down together and puts a floor under unavailability
  4. MTTR is irrelevant for replicated systems
The formula is right under its assumption. When replicas share a cause of failure, the unavailability of that shared cause is a lower bound, and extra replicas do not reduce it. Reducing what is shared (staggered deploys, separate zones, different providers) does.

An object store is unreachable for four hours, then comes back with every object intact. Which statement is correct?

  1. Durability was violated, availability was not
  2. Availability was violated, durability was not
  3. Both were violated
  4. Neither: four hours is within normal variation
Nothing was lost, so durability is intact. Users could not access their data for four hours, so availability suffered. The two are separate promises with separate engineering: replication and repair for durability, redundancy and safe operations for availability.

Roughly how much downtime does a 99.9% target allow in a 30-day month?

  1. About 4 minutes
  2. About 7 hours
  3. About 43 minutes
  4. About 8 hours
0.1% of 43,200 minutes is 43.2 minutes. (About 4 minutes is four nines; 7 hours is two nines; 8.8 hours is three nines but per year.) Memorising "three nines is about 43 minutes a month and 9 hours a year" is worth the effort.
