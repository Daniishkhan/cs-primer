Roblox: 73 Hours Down

In late October 2021 Roblox went down for 73 hours. A Consul cluster that every backend service depended on slowed dramatically, first because of a new streaming feature under heavy load and then because of a pathology in the BoltDB storage engine. Monitoring depended on the same cluster, and recovery meant a careful cold restart with players admitted in stages.

The pattern in one sentence

One coordination cluster that every service and every diagnostic tool depends on is a single point of failure, and recovery from total failure is its own engineering problem, not just the reverse of the outage.

The cast

This page states only what Roblox’s post-incident report says. Mechanism explanations are labelled as such. Background: chapter 15 (dependencies, hard and circular), chapter 16 (incident response) and chapter 17 (postmortems).

Consul
HashiCorp’s tool for service discovery, health checks, session locking and a key-value store; the post says Roblox ran it as 5 voters plus 5 non-voting read replicas using Raft.
Nomad and Vault
The post names these as the scheduler and the secrets store of the same “HashiStack” that depend on Consul.
Streaming
A feature in Consul 1.10 meant to cut CPU and bandwidth use by pushing updates, in place of long polling.
BoltDB
The storage engine Consul used for its Raft log. It never shrinks its file, so freed pages are tracked in a freelist.
Cold restart
Bringing a system up from fully down, with no caches warm and no services running.

What happened

All times Pacific. Roblox says it ran about 50 million daily players on over 18,000 servers and 170,000 containers. On 28 October 2021, the first signs were at 13:37: Vault was degraded and one Consul server showed high CPU, though players were not yet affected. At 16:35 concurrent users dropped to 50% of normal. Full access did not return until 16:45 on 31 October, 73 hours after the incident began. Roblox states that no user data was lost and there was no unauthorised access.

When Consul was unhealthy, the post explains, services could not find each other, and Nomad and Vault could not schedule containers or retrieve secrets. The post states the conclusion plainly: “the system failed because Consul was a single point of failure, and Consul was not healthy.”

Two problems inside Consul

Streaming and a contended channel

In the months before the outage Roblox had upgraded from Consul 1.9 to 1.10 to use the new streaming feature. On 27 October at 14:00 streaming was enabled on a traffic-routing backend service, and the same change added 50% more nodes to that service ahead of expected year-end traffic. It ran for about a day without trouble. HashiCorp later found that streaming uses fewer Go channels than long polling, which under very high combined read and write load meant heavy contention on a single channel; the 128-core dual-socket NUMA servers made it worse. Normal KV apply time is under 300 ms; during the incident the median KV write took about 2 seconds. Disabling streaming everywhere finished propagating at 15:51 on 30 October, after which median KV writes fell to 300 ms.

The BoltDB freelist

Consul stores its Raft log in BoltDB and snapshots to trim old entries. The post says the incident workload triggered pathological freelist performance: the log store file was 4.2 GB but held only 489 MB of real data, and the freelist was 7.8 MB with nearly a million free page IDs. Each Raft append, often 16 kB or less, forced the whole 7.8 MB freelist to be rewritten, causing back pressure, full TCP buffers and writes of 2 to 3 seconds on unhealthy leaders. A database compaction using existing BoltDB tools cleared it.

Common misconception: "it was a Consul bug, so it was HashiCorp’s fault"

The post describes a combination: a feature newly enabled at scale, a data-structure pathology, a workload mix earlier benchmarks had not reproduced, and a single cluster serving every service. HashiCorp worked with Roblox throughout and built benchmarks to reproduce the contention. The system-level lesson, in Roblox’s own words, is the single point of failure.

Monitoring that depended on Consul

Roblox says its telemetry depended on Consul, so when Consul failed the data needed to diagnose it was missing. This delayed identifying the cause. The post lists early suspicion of slow hardware, replacing a node, then replacing all Consul nodes with 128-core NVMe machines by 19:00, with the cluster still unhealthy. On 29 October at 02:00 to 04:00 the cluster was shut down and restored from a snapshot taken hours earlier while internal traffic was blocked with iptables; performance degraded again once the block was lifted. A second attempt, with non-essential Consul use disabled and health checks reduced from every 60 seconds to every 10 minutes, restarted at 16:00 on 29 October and was unhealthy again by 02:00 on 30 October. Debug logs and OS metrics that morning showed KV writes blocked for long periods; a return to 64-core servers did not help; streaming was identified and disabled that afternoon. Some leaders remained slow, so the team prevented those servers from being elected.

The cautious cold restart

After the fix, Roblox did not simply turn everything back on. Earlier attempts had already used the load-control tricks above (a snapshot reset with traffic blocked, non-essential services scaled down, slower health checks). With Consul stable (about 54 hours in, per the post), the post describes first restoring the caching system, from 20:00 on 30 October to 05:00 on 31 October. The caches handle about a billion requests per second. Redeploying them exposed stale scheduling data in Consul KV (likely from the snapshot reset), a node the scheduler treated as healthy when it was not, and a deployment tool built for incremental changes rather than bootstrapping from scratch. At 05:00 on 31 October, 61 hours in, Consul and caching were healthy. Services were restarted at the right capacity and verified, and the team was ready to admit players by 10:00. Players were admitted gradually through DNS steering, in random percentages in roughly 10% steps, checking database load, cache performance and stability at each step. Full access came at 16:45.

The general lesson: after a long outage, a fully restarted system meets all its clients at once with cold caches, and a rush of returning traffic can knock it over again. Metered re-admission is the same idea as load shedding in chapter 11, applied in reverse.

Contributing factors

  • A single Consul cluster served all backend services, and Nomad and Vault depended on it.
  • A new feature (streaming) enabled at scale together with a 50% node increase on a busy service, with a read-and-write mix not reproduced in earlier benchmarks.
  • A storage-engine pathology (BoltDB freelist) that made every write expensive once the file had grown.
  • Telemetry and diagnostics that depended on the system they monitor.
  • Large amounts of obsolete KV data adding load (the post says it later deleted them).
  • Deployment and caching tools not designed for a cold start from zero.

What they changed

  • Removed the circular dependency so telemetry no longer relies on the systems it monitors, and added Consul and BoltDB alerting that fires before the dangerous state is reached.
  • Built a geographically distinct data center and began work on multiple availability zones.
  • Split critical services into dedicated Consul clusters, migrated core services off Consul KV and deleted large amounts of obsolete KV data.
  • Planned to replace BoltDB with its successor bbolt.
  • Redesigned cache deployment for cold starts, worked with HashiCorp on Nomad enhancements for large job turn-up, and built faster machine configuration tooling.
  • Will reintroduce streaming only after a redesigned implementation is tested at Roblox’s scale; HashiCorp is building benchmarks to reproduce the contention.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: the hospital with one records room

Every ward, pharmacy and lab finds patients, doctors and drug orders through one records room. When the room jams, all wards stall, and the charts that would explain the jam are inside the room. After it is fixed, you cannot open all the doors at once; you readmit patients ward by ward, checking each time.

Where it breaks down: a records room is a single building; Consul was a replicated Raft cluster, which is why the failure came from its shared performance, not from losing a copy.

Key takeaways

  • 73 hours: a Consul cluster serving every backend service became slow, and services could not find each other or be scheduled.
  • Streaming contention and a BoltDB freelist pathology together made writes take around 2 seconds, against under 300 ms normally.
  • Telemetry depended on Consul, so diagnosis suffered; Roblox removed that circular dependency.
  • Recovery required a cold restart, gradual load control and metered player admission in roughly 10% steps.
  • Changes: separate Consul clusters, more data centers and zones, bbolt, cold-start tooling, streaming only after at-scale testing.
