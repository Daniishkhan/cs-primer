Slack's January 2021 Outage

On 4 January 2021 Slack went from nearly perfect message delivery to a severe outage when an AWS Transit Gateway failed to scale fast enough for the traffic that came back after the holidays. Slack’s own autoscaling and provisioning then met the same network trouble, turning a degraded service into a hole it took hours to climb out of.

The pattern in one sentence

Automation that reacts to the wrong signal under a shared bottleneck can amplify the problem: scaling, health checks and provisioning all need to be tested for how they behave when the network is the thing that is failing.

The cast

This page states only what Slack’s engineering post says. Where the post defers to AWS, so does this page. Background: chapter 10 (capacity and load testing) and chapter 11 (overload and feedback loops).

AWS Transit Gateway (TGW)
An AWS-managed network hub that connects many VPCs. The post says Slack used TGWs to link its VPCs, and that they are meant to scale transparently.
Web tier
The fleet of web servers (Apache) that handles Slack’s client requests.
Autoscaling group
A set of instances the cloud adds to or removes automatically from metrics such as CPU. The post says the web tier scaled on CPU utilisation and Apache worker-thread utilisation.
Provision-service
Slack’s internal service that configures and tests new instances and performs infrastructure housekeeping.
Cold cache
A client with nothing cached, which must fetch more data on first connection.

What happened

On 4 January 2021 (all times Pacific) an external monitoring service paged Slack as error rates rose. Early on, Slack’s own dashboarding and alerting service became unavailable, and recent changes were rolled back although they were not the cause. By about 6:57 am 99% of messages were being sent successfully, against the usual 99.999%. At 7:00 am a routine spike in traffic at the top of the hour, together with network packet loss, saturated the web tier.

The packet loss traced to an overloaded Transit Gateway. Slack attributes the overload to the jump in traffic after the holidays, when clients came back with cold caches and pulled more data on first connection; its own serving systems scaled up, but the TGWs did not scale fast enough. On the TGW internals the post defers to AWS: it reports that engineers at AWS had found the trigger, a saturated part of AWS networking infrastructure dropping packets, and that AWS was reviewing its TGW scaling algorithms. AWS’s own monitoring alerted its engineers, who raised TGW capacity manually. Slack does not itself explain how the TGWs work inside, and neither does this page.

The provisioning service under load

Between 7:01 and 7:15 am the autoscaler tried to add about 1,200 web servers. Provision-service runs over the same degraded network, so its calls were slow and used more resources. Provisioning so many instances at once hit two bottlenecks: the Linux open-files limit, which the post calls the most significant, and an AWS quota. Instances that could not be configured properly then ran into autoscaling-group size limits, and the overloaded service also blocked provisioning of the dashboard service Slack needed to see the problem. Provision-service was back in operation around 8:15 am and healthy instances began entering service.

Feedback loops

The post gives a careful account of the autoscaling behaviour. Packet loss made threads wait longer, which lowered CPU, so the autoscaler scaled down; as conditions worsened thread utilisation rose and triggered a large scale-up. Automation also marked instances unhealthy when they could not reach their backends and replaced them, and downscaling ended engineers’ SSH sessions on instances being deprovisioned. Slack disabled downscaling to aid investigation and preserve capacity.

This is the general pattern of a control loop reacting to a signal that is not capacity. CPU is a good proxy for load when threads are busy computing, and a poor one when they are waiting on a lossy network. General observation, not from the post: a scaler that adds machines when the problem is the network between them adds load to the shared bottleneck.

By 9:15 am the web tier had enough working hosts to serve traffic; Slack was degraded but no longer down. At about 10:40 am AWS’s capacity change was live in all Availability Zones and the network, error rates and latency returned to normal.

Common misconception: "more autoscaling is always the fix"

Adding instances helps when the constraint is the number of servers. When the constraint is a shared network hop or a provisioning bottleneck, more instances can make every other instance slower. Slack’s own post shows the scaler working as configured while conditions worsened.

Contributing factors

  • A managed network hub that did not scale as fast as the traffic after the holidays; Slack’s own services scaled, which moved the bottleneck to the TGWs.
  • A top-of-the-hour traffic pattern on top of cold client caches.
  • Autoscaling signals (CPU, thread use) that moved in opposite directions under packet loss.
  • A provisioning service that shared the degraded network and hit an open-files limit and an AWS quota at the moment of mass scale-up.
  • Dashboard and alerting instances reached over the TGW, so observability suffered too.

What they changed

  • During the incident they disabled web-tier downscaling.
  • They planned to run dashboard and alerting instances in the same VPC as their databases, removing the TGW dependency for observability.
  • They planned to load test provision-service regularly and re-evaluate health-checking and autoscaling configurations to prevent overload.
  • They planned to request pre-emptive TGW upscaling at the end of the next holiday season, ahead of the return to work.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: the emergency department that calls in extra staff by the same jammed phone line

A surge of patients arrives, and the doctors are slowed by a failing hallway. Management reads “doctors look idle” (they are waiting) and sends some home, then reads “now all busy” and calls in hundreds, but the phone line used to onboard them is the same jammed line. The fix is not more doctors but a bigger hallway, and alarms that do not depend on the hallway.

Where it breaks down: real staff do not vanish when told to go home; cloud instances do, along with the engineers’ open SSH sessions.

Key takeaways

  • A routine hourly spike plus cold client caches overloaded an AWS Transit Gateway; the post defers to AWS for what happened inside it.
  • The autoscaler read CPU and thread signals that both worsen or improve for the wrong reasons under packet loss, creating a loop.
  • Provision-service shared the degraded network and hit an open-files limit and an AWS quota when asked for about 1,200 servers.
  • Slack’s own dashboards depended on the troubled network path.
  • Planned changes include shared-fate removal for observability, load testing provisioning and asking AWS for TGW capacity ahead of known peaks.
