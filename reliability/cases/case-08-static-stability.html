Static Stability at AWS

A design case from the AWS Builders’ Library: how to build systems that keep working when the machinery that changes them is down, by relying on the data plane and on capacity already provisioned across Availability Zones.

The pattern in one sentence

Do the work of recovery in advance: keep spare capacity running in other zones so a failure needs no control-plane action at the moment it is least available.

The cast

This is a design case, not an incident. It rests on the AWS Builders’ Library article on static stability using Availability Zones, and it is the design-side companion to chapter 15 and to the AWS 2021 case, which showed a control plane impaired while running workloads carried on. Recovery planning is the subject of chapter 18.

Availability Zone (AZ)
A separate location within an AWS Region, built so that a problem in one is unlikely to affect another.
Control plane
The machinery for changing resources, such as launching an instance.
Data plane
The day-to-day machinery that keeps resources functioning, such as routing packets or reading and writing volumes.
Static stability
A system that keeps operating correctly when a dependency is impaired, without having to take any action in response (this definition is a plain restatement; the article gives its own).
Overprovision
Run more capacity than the current load needs, so that a failure can be absorbed by capacity that already exists.

Control plane against data plane in EC2

The article describes launching an EC2 instance as a lot of work: finding a physical server, allocating a network interface in the right subnet, preparing EBS volumes, generating IAM role credentials, installing security group rules and propagating configuration. That is the control plane, the part that changes things. The data plane is the work that keeps resources functioning once they exist, such as routing packets according to VPC route tables or reading and writing EBS volumes. The article says it is much simpler than the control plane and targets higher availability.

The consequence is the heart of the article. Each physical host has local access to the information it needs to route traffic in and out of its VPC. If the control plane is impaired, hosts may miss updates (new instances, new security group rules), but traffic that is already flowing keeps working. A design that relies only on the data plane during a failure is statically stable with respect to that control plane.

Pre-provisioning instead of reacting

The tempting response to losing a zone is to scale up in the remaining zones. That needs the very control plane that may be impaired, at a moment when many other customers are doing the same. The article instead recommends that stateless services run in Auto Scaling groups across three or more zones, overprovisioned so the survivors can take the load without launching anything. With three zones it overprovisions by 50%, so that each zone runs at about 66% of its load-tested capacity. (Quick check: total capacity 1.5 times the load, so each zone holds 0.5 of the load but carries one third, which is two thirds of its capacity; with one zone gone, each of the two survivors carries one half, exactly 100%.) For stateful active-standby systems the pattern is a warm standby in a different zone from the primary. In both cases the work of failing over is done before anything fails.

There is a cost: you pay for the extra capacity all the time. The article does not put a number on this cost beyond the 50% example; I read it as the price of not depending on a reaction. The number of zones sets the price, since with more zones less spare capacity is needed to cover the loss of one (general arithmetic, using the same reasoning as above).

The load balancer example

The article’s first worked example is a public HTTPS service in eu-west-1: an Application Load Balancer in front of an Auto Scaling group across three zones. If one zone is impaired, its instances fail health checks and the load balancer moves traffic to the others with nobody doing anything. The article says Elastic Load Balancing itself is provisioned to handle the loss of a zone without scaling up, and the same structure applies to a worker fleet consuming from an SQS queue.

Zonal against regional dependencies

A second example concerns NAT gateways. A NAT gateway is a zonal resource, so the article advises creating one in each zone, with separate route tables, so that a connectivity problem stays inside one zone. The article extends the reasoning to call chains: with two regional services each spreading calls across three zones, the chance a request avoids an impaired zone is 4/9, whereas with zonal services it is 2/3 regardless of chain length. The price is complexity, for AWS and for customers.

The brief for this case also mentioned Lambda. The article I fetched does not discuss Lambda, so I have not included an example of it here.

Common misconception: "auto scaling will save us when a zone fails"

Auto scaling is a control-plane action. It is excellent for ordinary growth, but if your recovery plan requires launching instances during the impairment, you have a dependency on the thing that is impaired. Static stability does not forbid scaling; it says that surviving a zone failure must not depend on it.

Design factors

  • Keep recovery on the data plane: use capacity, routes and endpoints that already exist.
  • Spread across at least three zones, so that losing one leaves two to carry the load.
  • Overprovision enough that survivors absorb the load: 50% in the three-zone example.
  • Use health checks and load balancing so traffic moves away from an impaired zone automatically.
  • Prefer zonal resources (such as one NAT gateway per zone) to limit the zones a request can touch.

What it costs, and what you get

  • Cost: extra running capacity and extra moving parts, such as per-zone resources and route tables.
  • Benefit: failure handling that is exercised in advance and does not rely on a control plane under stress.
  • Related practice from the article’s framing: the key is to anticipate impairments before they happen.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: the hospital generator that is already running

A hospital in a storm has two options when the grid fails. It can phone a company and ask for a generator to be driven over, which depends on roads and phone lines that the storm may have taken out. Or it can have a generator already on site, already fuelled and wired in, so that when the lights flicker nothing needs to be requested. The second costs more every day of fine weather and costs nothing at all in the storm.

Where it breaks down: software spare capacity can be left idle but verified, which is why the article sizes it against a load-tested number.

Key takeaways

  • The data plane is simpler and built for higher availability than the control plane; design so a failure needs only the data plane.
  • Overprovision across three or more zones: at 50% extra, each zone at about 66% load means a lost zone leaves the others at 100%.
  • Let health checks and load balancers move traffic; do not rely on operators or API calls during the impairment.
  • Zonal resources keep problems inside one zone and improve the odds along call chains.
  • Static stability is paid for in advance, in capacity and complexity.
