The Tail at Scale

How do you keep a service fast when a request needs answers from thousands of servers and every one of them is occasionally slow? In a 2013 paper in Communications of the ACM, Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso set out lessons from Google’s serving systems: why rare slowness dominates at large fan-out, what Google measured, and which techniques (hedged requests, tied requests, micro-partitions and others) tame the tail.

The pattern in one sentence

When a request waits on many servers, the slowest one decides the latency, so at scale you must design to tolerate slowness you cannot eliminate, using spare replicas and cancellation instead of hoping for uniform speed.

The cast

This is a paper, not an incident, so there is no outage to retell. It is Google’s own account of what it learned serving interactive requests across thousands of machines, and this page is organised as lessons from Google’s serving systems. Everything below that carries a number comes from the paper; explanations marked general are mine. Background: chapter 9 (tail latency at scale) and chapter 8 (queueing and the latency knee).

Tail latency
The slowest few percent of responses (the 99th or 99.9th percentile) as opposed to the median. See chapter 4 for why percentiles must be handled with care.
Fan-out
One user request spawning sub-requests to many servers whose answers are merged. The paper describes a root server, intermediate servers and many leaf servers.
Tail-tolerant
The paper’s term, by analogy with fault-tolerant: building a predictably responsive whole out of less-predictable parts.
Hedged request
The same request sent to a second replica after a delay, taking whichever answer arrives first.
Tied request
Copies enqueued on two servers at once, each aware of the other so that one can cancel the other when it starts.
Micro-partition
One of many small data partitions, far more numerous than machines, which can be moved between machines to balance load.

What the paper argues

Dean and Barroso (Communications of the ACM, February 2013) start from user experience: the paper says systems that respond within 100 ms feel more fluid than slower ones, and cites Google search updating results as the user types. Their central claim is that brief high-latency episodes, unimportant in a moderate-size system, can dominate overall performance at large scale. Their answer is not to remove every cause of slowness, which they call impractical in shared environments, but to design software that masks it, the way fault-tolerant systems mask failing parts.

Where variability comes from

The paper lists causes at the software level: shared resources (applications contending for CPU cores, caches, memory bandwidth and network bandwidth), background daemons that cause multi-millisecond hiccups, global resource sharing (network switches, shared file systems), maintenance activities (data reconstruction, log compaction, garbage collection), and layers of queueing that amplify the rest. It adds hardware causes: CPUs that run above their average power envelope and then throttle, SSD garbage collection that the paper says can increase read latency by a factor of 100 even at modest write activity, and power-saving modes that cost latency when waking.

Why fan-out makes rare slowness common

The paper’s example: a server that usually answers in 10 ms but has a 99th-percentile latency of one second. If a request touches one such server, one request in 100 is slow. If it needs answers from 100 such servers in parallel, 63% of requests take more than a second. Even if only one request in 10,000 is slow per server, a fan-out of 2,000 servers leaves almost one in five requests over a second. This is the arithmetic from chapter 9.

The paper backs this with measurements from a real Google service whose root server fans out through intermediate servers to many leaves. The table below reproduces its Table 1.

Read down the 99th-percentile column: one leaf at 10 ms, but 140 ms to wait for all of them. The paper points out that waiting for the slowest 5% of leaves accounts for half of that 140 ms, so techniques that target the slow outliers pay off disproportionately.

Lesson 1: reduce the variability you can

The paper first lists ordinary engineering: service classes so that requests a user is waiting for go ahead of batch work, kept-short low-level queues (its storage servers keep few operations outstanding in the OS disk queue and use their own priority queues instead), breaking long requests into smaller pieces so cheap ones are not stuck behind expensive ones (search uses time-slicing), and throttling or splitting up background work. For large fan-out it notes a counter-intuitive option: synchronising background activity across machines, so that all of them pause briefly together rather than a few always being slow. Caching, it says, does not directly address tail latency unless the whole working set fits.

Lesson 2: tolerate what you cannot remove

The paper separates two time scales: within-request techniques that act over tens of milliseconds, and cross-request adaptations that act over tens of seconds to minutes.

Hedged requests

A naive duplicate-everything version adds unacceptable load. The paper’s version waits until the first request has been outstanding for more than the 95th-percentile expected latency for that class of request, which limits extra load to about 5%. Its measured example: reading 1,000 keys from a BigTable table spread over 100 servers, a hedge after a 10 ms delay cut the 99.9th-percentile latency for retrieving all 1,000 values from 1,800 ms to 74 ms while sending just 2% more requests. It suggests marking hedges as lower priority, and notes that faster cancellation allows more aggressive hedging.

Tied requests

The paper observes that much latency variation is queueing before execution starts; once a request runs, its completion time varies far less. Tied requests exploit that. In Google’s cluster file system, a BigTable read with a tied request after 1 ms reduced median latency by 16% on an otherwise idle cluster and by nearly 40% at the 99.9th percentile (98 ms down to 61 ms). With a concurrent large sorting job on the same disks, the tied-request profile was nearly identical to an idle cluster without tied requests, which the authors say lets workloads be consolidated onto one cluster. The disk-utilisation overhead was under 1%. Probing queues first and picking the least loaded server is described as less effective for three reasons: load changes between probe and send, service times are hard to estimate, and many clients may pick the same server at once.

Common misconception: "a hedge is just a retry"

A retry waits for failure or a timeout, and then asks again. A hedge asks a different replica while the first is still pending, and cancels the loser. The paper adds a limit: the technique works only when the cause of slowness does not hit all replicas at once. If every replica is slow because the system is overloaded, hedges add load to the very thing that is struggling (chapter 11; the paper does not discuss this case in detail).

Micro-partitions, selective replication and probation

For slower, coarser imbalance, the paper describes Google systems that create many more partitions than machines and assign them dynamically. With on average 20 partitions per machine, load can be shed in roughly 5% steps and in 1/20th of the time compared with one partition per machine. BigTable machines manage between 20 and 1,000 tablets each, and failure recovery speeds up because many machines each take over one small unit. Selective replication adds extra copies of hot items, as Google web search does for popular documents. Latency-induced probation excludes a slow machine from serving while shadow requests keep measuring it; the paper calls it peculiar that removing serving capacity under high load can improve latency.

Good enough and canary requests

For large information-retrieval systems the paper describes returning slightly incomplete “good-enough” results once a sufficient fraction of leaves has answered, taking care that such results stay rare, and skipping non-essential subsystems (ads, spelling correction) that do not answer in time. Canary requests protect against a request that exercises an untested code path: the root sends it to one or two leaves first and only fans out if they respond, flagging the request as dangerous otherwise. The paper says such requests tend to be used for every request in all of Google’s large fan-out search systems. It also notes mutations are easier: quorum algorithms such as Paxos need only three to five replicas and so are inherently tail-tolerant.

Contributing factors

As a paper it has no failure to assign blame to, but the factors it names make a useful checklist:

  • Scale itself: fan-out turns a per-server 1-in-100 event into a per-request near-certainty.
  • Shared environments: co-located workloads and shared infrastructure create variability that application developers do not control.
  • Queueing at several layers, which amplifies small stalls.
  • Background work that is harmless on average but not at the moment it runs.
  • Hardware heterogeneity: the paper expects variability to grow with power optimisation and fabrication limits.

What they changed

The paper describes techniques “that have been effective in several of Google’s large-scale software systems”: hedged and tied requests in storage, micro-partitioning in BigTable and web search, probation, good-enough results and canary requests in search. It adds that many techniques can be encapsulated in baseline libraries, and that they let utilisation rise without lengthening the tail, avoiding wasteful overprovisioning. It does not give an organisation-wide before-and-after.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: the emergency department with one slow lab

A patient needs results from a hundred tests before the doctor can decide. Each lab is quick 99 times in 100, but the patient is only done when the slowest has answered, so almost every patient waits on some lab. Ordering the same test from a second lab if the first is late, and telling the first to stop if the second finishes, keeps the whole visit short.

Where it breaks down: blood tests cost a patient nothing extra, while duplicate requests cost real capacity. If the waiting room is already full, duplicate orders make everybody wait longer.

Key takeaways

  • At large fan-out, rare slowness is common: the paper’s own figures are 63% of requests slow at 100 servers with 1-in-100 outliers, and nearly one in five at 2,000 servers with 1-in-10,000.
  • Table 1 in the paper: a 10 ms leaf 99th percentile became 140 ms for the slowest-of-all.
  • Hedged requests sent after the 95th-percentile delay cost about 5% extra load; the BigTable example fell from 1,800 ms to 74 ms at the 99.9th percentile for 2% more requests.
  • Tied requests add cross-server cancellation, cut tail latency by up to nearly 40% at the 99.9th percentile in the paper’s benchmark, at under 1% extra disk utilisation.
  • Micro-partitions, selective replication, probation, good-enough results and canary requests handle slower or correlated problems.
  • Eliminating all variability is impractical; design to tolerate it.

Check your understanding

A service has per-server 1-in-100 slow requests (over one second) and fans out to 100 servers. What fraction of requests is slow, according to the paper?

  1. About 1%
  2. About 10%
  3. 63%
  4. Almost none
The chance that at least one of 100 servers is slow is about 63%, the point marked in the paper’s figure.

Why does a hedged request wait for the 95th-percentile expected latency before being sent?

  1. To make sure the first request has failed
  2. To limit the extra load to about 5% while still helping the slowest requests
  3. Because replicas need time to warm up
  4. To avoid TCP slow start
Hedging immediately would double the load. Waiting until a request is already slower than 95% of its class limits duplicates to roughly 5%.

What extra thing do tied requests do that hedged requests do not?

  1. Send to three replicas
  2. Let the servers tell each other when a copy starts so the other can cancel
  3. Retry on timeout
  4. Cache the result
Each copy is tagged with the identity of the other server, and the one that starts first sends a cancellation to its counterpart.

For which kind of slowness do hedged and tied requests help, per the paper?

  1. Slowness that affects all replicas at the same time
  2. Slowness that does not tend to hit multiple replicas simultaneously
  3. Only network partitions
  4. Only crashes
The paper says the class of techniques is effective only when the cause of variability does not simultaneously affect multiple replicas.

What problem do canary requests address?

  1. Slow disks
  2. A request that exercises an untested code path and could crash thousands of leaves at once
  3. Cache misses
  4. Clock skew
The root sends the request to one or two leaves first and only fans out if they respond successfully.
