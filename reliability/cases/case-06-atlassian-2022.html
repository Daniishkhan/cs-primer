Atlassian: The Two-Week Restore

In April 2022 a maintenance script run with the wrong kind of ID deleted 883 sites belonging to 775 Atlassian customers. Backups existed, but restoring individual customers out of shared, multi-tenant data stores was slow and largely manual, and restoration stretched until 18 April.

The pattern in one sentence

Backups are only as good as your ability to restore at the granularity of the damage, at the speed the business can bear, while still being able to talk to the people affected.

Site
A customer’s cloud instance of Atlassian products, identified by a site ID and a URL.
App ID
The identifier of an app installed on a site, here the “Insight – Asset Management” app.
RPO
Recovery point objective: how much recent data you can afford to lose. The post says the one-hour RPO was met.
RTO
Recovery time objective: how long recovery may take. The post says the RTO was missed.
Soft delete
Marking data deleted but keeping it recoverable for a period before it is really erased.

What happened

On 5 April 2022 a maintenance script was run to delete an app on sites where it was no longer needed. Atlassian says the team supplied site IDs instead of app IDs, so the script deleted whole sites. The deletion API accepted both kinds of ID without checking which it received. In the post’s account, peer review looked at which endpoint was called but not at the IDs, staging did not catch the error because those IDs do not exist in staging, and an earlier production run on 30 sites had used correct IDs and succeeded. The second run covered 883 sites and affected 775 customers. Atlassian reports there was no cyberattack and no unauthorised access to data.

Deletion ran from 07:38 to 08:01 UTC. The first support ticket came at 07:46, the major incident process started at 08:17, and the team confirmed at 08:53 that its own script had caused the deletions. First sites were restored on 8 April, and the post gives 18 April as the date all affected sites were back.

Backups existed, restore was the problem

Atlassian did have backups, and the post says most customers were restored to a point five minutes before deletion; the one-hour RPO was met. What it could not do was restore a large number of sites across many products at once. The post explains that each data store holds data for many customers, so the affected customers’ data had to be extracted from backups by hand, and that site-level deletions had no runbooks that could be automated at this scale.

This is the granularity lesson in chapter 18. Rolling back a whole shared store would also have rolled back customers who had done nothing wrong, so recovery had to work tenant by tenant, and the tooling for that existed for single sites, not hundreds. (The tenancy reasoning is general; the post states the extraction was manual.)

The post describes two attempts. Restoration 1 created new site identifiers, which meant updating many records, including those of third-party apps, in about 70 mostly sequential steps taking roughly 48 hours per batch. It restored 53% of impacted users, across 112 sites, between 5 and 14 April. Restoration 2 reused the old identifiers and ran more steps in parallel, about 12 hours per site, and restored about 47% of impacted users across 771 sites between 14 and 17 April. The post says that restoration 1 alone would have taken about three weeks. For 57 customers, some Confluence and Insight databases came back to earlier points because of inconsistent manual backup handling; Atlassian says it recovered that data and helped those customers restore it.

Common misconception: "we have backups, so we have disaster recovery"

Backups answer “does the data exist?”. Recovery answers “how fast, for whom, at what granularity?”. The post’s own summary is that the recovery point was met and the recovery time was missed. A backup you can only restore wholesale, or only one customer at a time by hand, is a different capability from the one you assumed.

Communication problems

The deletion also removed the site URLs and System Admin contacts of the affected customers. The normal support form required a valid cloud URL, so customers could not file tickets the usual way. Many billing and technical contacts on record were outdated, which delayed proactive outreach. Atlassian acknowledges that public communication came too late, that it did not give clear restoration estimates, and that it lacked a comprehensive incident communications playbook. This echoes the circular dependency in the AWS 2021 case: the channel customers needed to reach you ran through the thing that had broken.

Contributing factors

  • A destructive script and API that accepted two kinds of ID with no check on which it received.
  • Peer review that examined the endpoint, not the IDs, and a staging environment in which the wrong IDs could not exist.
  • A bulk deletion that was permanent, with no soft-delete step to catch the mistake.
  • No automated way to restore many sites across multiple products, and backups shaped around whole shared stores.
  • Contact details and support routes stored in the same instances that were deleted.
  • No playbook for communicating during a large, long incident.

What they changed

  • Bulk site deletes were blocked immediately, and the plan is to prevent deletion of customer data without a soft-delete step, to require staged rollouts and tested rollback plans, and to delete only after deactivation.
  • Disaster recovery: faster multi-site, multi-product restoration to meet the RTO, with regular exercises that restore all products for large sets of sites.
  • A playbook and tooling for large-scale incident management, with regular simulations.
  • Key contact information backed up outside the product instance, a path for customers without a valid site URL or Atlassian ID to reach support, a unified account-based escalation system and wider 24/7 escalation coverage.
  • An updated communications playbook using the DACI framework, 24/7 backups for each role and quarterly readiness audits.

The pattern to remember

Analogy: the archive that stores whole buildings

A city archives a complete photograph of every apartment block each night. One morning the walls of 775 flats vanish, spread across a few hundred blocks. The photographs exist, but the only way to use them is to take a photo of a whole block, find the missing flat, and rebuild it by hand without disturbing the neighbours who are still living there. Each rebuild is slow, and the office that tells residents what is happening has lost their phone numbers.

Where it breaks down: software restores can be automated and rehearsed; the fix is practising at the scale you might need.

Key takeaways

  • A script that accepts two kinds of ID without checking which one it has is waiting for a mix-up. Validate types and make destructive actions reversible.
  • Test and review the data a destructive tool will run on, not just the code path; staging that lacks the real IDs cannot catch the error.
  • Design restore for the granularity of the damage. Per-store backups do not restore per-tenant losses quickly.
  • Meeting the recovery point while missing the recovery time still means a long outage.
  • Keep contact details and support routes outside the system whose failure you must announce.
