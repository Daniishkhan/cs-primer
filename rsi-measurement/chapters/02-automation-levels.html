Automation Levels for AI Research

How much of AI research is already done by AI? Epoch proposes a six-level scale for each task, Anthropic measured its own work against it, and OpenAI states a milestone. This chapter explains the scale and the method, marks every figure as self-reported, and shows how to score your own loop.

Why this matters

This chapter serves Artifact 4's write-up and its Level-1 report. A claim that "the loop does AI research" is meaningless until you say which tasks it does, how much of the work they are, and how much a person still steers. Three organisations published numbers on exactly this in 2026, and each number depends on a method you must understand before you quote it. After this chapter you can read the scale, explain how the published shares were produced and why they are self-reported, avoid the most common misreading (a share of work is not a speed-up), and score your own Artifact 4 loop task by task. Chapter 03 prices the loop; chapter 04 audits claims; chapter 01 is the test that a loop improves at all.

A question about tasks, not jobs

"What fraction of AI research is automated?" sounds like one question and is really many. A researcher's week contains reading papers, writing code, launching and monitoring runs, deciding what to try next, and writing the report. A language model may do the third task almost alone and the fourth hardly at all. So any honest answer starts by splitting the work into tasks and rating each one. That is the approach of the three sources below: Epoch AI proposes the task list and the rating scale, Anthropic applies a version of it to its own staff, and OpenAI states a milestone for a particular kind of worker.

Analogy: driver-assistance levels

Cars are described on a scale from no automation, through assistance, to a vehicle that drives itself with no human at the wheel. The scale does not say that "cars are 40% automated". It says what the system and the human each do on a given road. Epoch's scale for research tasks works the same way: a rating per task says who does what, and an overall share is built from those ratings afterwards.

Where it breaks down: a car's level is a property of one vehicle on one kind of road, but a research task's level depends on who is rating it and what counts as finished. Two raters often disagree by a level on the same task, as the agreement figures later in this chapter show.

Epoch's scale: levels 0 to 5

Epoch AI is a research organisation that studies AI trends. On 17 June 2026 three of its researchers, Jean-Stanislas Denain, Joe Kwon and Anson Ho, published a Gradient Updates post titled "Toward an O*NET for AI R&D". O*NET is the US government database that breaks occupations into tasks; the post asks what the equivalent would be for AI research and development. The authors state that the post reflects their views and not necessarily Epoch's. It does two things.

First, it splits AI research into six categories that follow the research cycle, each with subcategories (category 4, "Run", has monitoring runs, hardware infrastructure operations and inference reliability engineering) and over sixty tasks in all. Second, it rates how much AI automates each task on a scale from 0 to 5. The levels, in the post's wording, are:

LevelNameWhat the post says
0Not usedAI adds nothing
1MarginalOccasionally convenient for lookups, brainstorming or polishing write-ups, but it does not change how the work is done or who does it
2AssistsMeaningful speed-ups to parts of the task; the human mostly drives and reviews everything
3CollaboratesAI does large chunks under close human direction; the human still makes the calls and stitches things together
4LeadsAI completes most of the task end to end from a high-level ask; the human supervises, course-corrects and approves outputs
5AutonomousEnd to end with little or no human involvement

The post uses numbers 0 to 5; Anthropic writes "AL0 to AL5" (automation level) for the same scale. Epoch is open about the limits of its first attempt. The ratings are subjective. The task list follows today's human workflows, so an AI that does research by routes unlike a human's would need a different list. Some tasks may become unnecessary. And the authors note that an explosion of progress could begin before full automation, since partial uplift comes first. The full task list is in a companion document that I did not read.

Anthropic's method

Anthropic, an AI developer, published a post titled "Measuring the pace of AI development" through its Anthropic Institute, with figures dated "as of August 2026". Its method is the interesting part, because it shows what the numbers are made of.

  1. Inventory the work. Each week of July 2026 the company sampled 20% of staff in each model R&D department, and a Claude research agent listed the tasks they had done, drawing on Slack and internal documents. The result was about 15,000 tasks.
  2. Organise it into a frozen tree. The tasks were grouped into a tree of 542 nodes, of which 378 are leaves. "Frozen" means the tree is fixed, so future measurements use the same basket.
  3. Rate each node. For each node a Claude research agent gathered evidence, and a separate Claude judge assigned a level from Epoch's scale.
  4. Weight by time. Nodes were weighted by person-time, as a proxy for importance, which Anthropic calls "a crude approximation".

State of play, October 2026: Anthropic's reported share

Anthropic reports, as of August 2026, that "Claude 'leads' 26% of Anthropic's AI R&D work". In Epoch's scale "leads" is level 4, so this is the level-4 share by person-time, an inference from the definitions because the post does not write "level 4" beside the number. The same section reports that the share at or above "collaborates" (level 3) is above 90%, and that Claude is not fully autonomous (level 5) for any measured subset. These figures are self-reported: Anthropic computed them internally, using its own models as researcher and judge, and the post says third-party verification is planned, not complete. It lists its own caveats: real room for disagreement on borderline cases (especially between levels 3 and 4); model-human exact agreement of 59% against human-human exact agreement of 35%, with the two within one level 97% of the time; a frozen basket that cannot show new kinds of work (a check against a January 2026 basket found no rise in novel tasks); and no common methodology for comparing labs. Source: Anthropic Institute, fetched and read. Expect these numbers to be revised at each update.

Weighting: time share against task count

The choice to weight by person-time matters, because one task that takes ten hours a week counts ten times as much as one that takes one. The script below scores a hypothetical Artifact 4 team on seven tasks. The hours and levels are my own illustrative ratings, not data from Epoch or Anthropic. It prints, for each level, the share of hours and the share of tasks, then the cumulative share at levels 3 and 4 or above.

"""Score your own loop on a 0 to 5 automation scale, weighted by time.

The tasks, hours and levels are illustrative ratings for a hypothetical
Artifact 4 run, not data from Epoch or Anthropic.

Run: python3 automation_share.py
"""

# ── 1. Task inventory: (task, person-hours a week before AI, level 0-5) ──
TASKS = [
    ("write the inner-loop agent's code edits", 6.0, 4),
    ("propose outer-loop rewrites of the agent", 4.0, 4),
    ("run evaluations and monitor jobs", 3.0, 5),
    ("decide which rewrite to keep", 2.0, 3),
    ("check for evaluator hacking", 2.0, 2),
    ("pick the next research question", 1.5, 1),
    ("write the report and its figures", 3.0, 3),
]
NAMES = {0: "none", 1: "marginal", 2: "assists", 3: "collaborates",
         4: "leads", 5: "autonomous"}

# ── 2. Weighted and unweighted shares per level ──────────────────────────
total_hours = sum(h for _, h, _ in TASKS)
print(f"{'level':<14}{'hours':>7}{'time share':>12}{'task share':>12}")
for level in range(6):
    hours = sum(h for _, h, lv in TASKS if lv == level)
    count = sum(1 for _, _, lv in TASKS if lv == level)
    print(f"{level} {NAMES[level]:<12}{hours:>7.1f}"
          f"{hours / total_hours:>12.0%}{count / len(TASKS):>12.0%}")

# ── 3. Cumulative: how much is at least this automated? ──────────────────
for floor in (3, 4):
    share = sum(h for _, h, lv in TASKS if lv >= floor) / total_hours
    print(f"time at level {floor} or above: {share:.0%}")
level           hours  time share  task share
0 none            0.0          0%          0%
1 marginal        1.5          7%         14%
2 assists         2.0          9%         14%
3 collaborates    5.0         23%         29%
4 leads          10.0         47%         29%
5 autonomous      3.0         14%         14%
time at level 3 or above: 84%
time at level 4 or above: 60%

By hours, 47% of the work is at level 4 and 60% is at level 4 or above. By task count the level-4 share is 29%, because the large tasks (editing code, proposing rewrites) are the automated ones and the small judgement tasks (picking the next question, checking for hacking) are not. A headline "x% automated" can move by tens of points depending on this choice alone, so a published share without its weighting is not comparable with another.

Who agrees with the rater?

The rating step is the weakest link, since a model judge assigns most levels. Anthropic's check is to compare the judge with humans. Two numbers do different jobs. Exact agreement is the share of tasks on which two raters give the same level. Within-one agreement counts a one-level difference as agreement, which is forgiving where the levels are fuzzy. A third number corrects for luck: Cohen's kappa compares the observed agreement with the agreement two raters would reach by assigning levels independently with the same frequencies. A kappa of 0 is no better than chance and 1 is perfect agreement. The script computes all three on two made-up raters.

"""How often do two raters agree on a 0-5 level: exactly, and within one?

Ratings are made up for illustration.  Run: python3 agreement.py
"""

# ── 1. Two raters label the same twelve tasks ────────────────────────────
HUMAN = [4, 3, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, 1, 3, 4, 2, 3]
JUDGE = [4, 4, 4, 2, 5, 3, 3, 1, 4, 4, 3, 3]

# ── 2. Exact agreement and within-one agreement ──────────────────────────
n = len(HUMAN)
exact = sum(h == j for h, j in zip(HUMAN, JUDGE)) / n
within_one = sum(abs(h - j) <= 1 for h, j in zip(HUMAN, JUDGE)) / n
print(f"exact agreement      {exact:.0%}")
print(f"within one level     {within_one:.0%}")

# ── 3. What would two raters guessing independently score? ───────────
# Chance exact agreement uses each rater's own label frequencies.
freq_h = {lv: HUMAN.count(lv) / n for lv in range(6)}
freq_j = {lv: JUDGE.count(lv) / n for lv in range(6)}
chance = sum(freq_h[lv] * freq_j[lv] for lv in range(6))
kappa = (exact - chance) / (1 - chance)
print(f"chance exact agreement {chance:.0%}")
print(f"Cohen's kappa          {kappa:.2f}")
exact agreement      67%
within one level     100%
chance exact agreement 28%
Cohen's kappa          0.54

Here the raters agree exactly on 67% of tasks and within one on all of them, but chance alone would give 28% exact agreement, so kappa is 0.54: moderate. Anthropic's reported 59% exact agreement between the model and humans, against 35% between humans, says that the model is not noisier than people. It does not say that either is right: two raters can share a bias, and the post is explicit that borderline cases remain disputed.

A share of work is not a speed-up

Common misconception

"If AI leads 26% of research work, research is 26% faster." A share of work at a high level says who does a task, not how long it takes. A task can be at level 4 and still need the same wall-clock time, or be much faster. And the work a human still does becomes the bottleneck. Treat the share as a description of the workflow and the speed-up as a separate measurement.

The bottleneck is Amdahl's law from computer architecture: if a fraction f of a job is made s times faster and the rest is unchanged, the whole job is faster by 1 / ((1 − f) + f/s). The script shows what it implies for shares like those above. The speed-ups are illustrative.

"""A share of work at a high automation level is not a speed-up.

Amdahl's law: if a fraction f of the time is sped up by a factor s and the
rest is unchanged, the whole job is faster by 1 / ((1 - f) + f / s).
Shares and speed-ups are illustrative.  Run: python3 amdahl.py
"""

# ── 1. Overall speed-up for a share f of work made s times faster ────────
def speedup(f, s):
    return 1 / ((1 - f) + f / s)


print(f"{'share f':>8} {'s=2':>7} {'s=10':>7} {'s=100':>7} {'s=inf':>7}")
for f in (0.26, 0.5, 0.9):
    row = [speedup(f, s) for s in (2, 10, 100)]
    ceiling = 1 / (1 - f)
    print(f"{f:>8.0%} " + " ".join(f"{v:>6.2f}x" for v in row)
          + f" {ceiling:>6.2f}x")

# ── 2. The remaining human share sets the ceiling ────────────────────────
f = 0.9
print(f"with {f:.0%} automated, a human share of {1 - f:.0%} caps the gain "
      f"at {1 / (1 - f):.0f}x")
 share f     s=2    s=10   s=100   s=inf
     26%   1.15x   1.31x   1.35x   1.35x
     50%   1.33x   1.82x   1.98x   2.00x
     90%   1.82x   5.26x   9.17x  10.00x
with 90% automated, a human share of 10% caps the gain at 10x

A quarter of the work made a hundred times faster gives only 1.35x overall, and even at 90% automated the human 10% caps the gain at tenfold. This is why the economic papers of chapter 03 care about bottlenecks and why a lab might report that the least automatable tasks grow as a share of researcher effort.

Systems connection

Amdahl's law is the same reason that adding cores to a program with a serial section stops helping, and the same reason a pipeline runs at the speed of its slowest stage. The slow stage of a research loop is often the human review of results, or the evaluation run itself.

OpenAI's stated milestone

State of play, October 2026: OpenAI's "research intern"

I could not read OpenAI's own page: the fetch of "Research acceleration: The view inside OpenAI" returned HTTP 403. What follows comes from a search-engine summary of that post, dated 6 September 2026, and is therefore second-hand and may be incomplete. The summary says OpenAI reports that its measurements show it has reached a goal announced the previous autumn, of having an automated research intern by September 2026, defined as a system that can carry out well-defined research tasks under human direction, including tasks that would take a skilled researcher a few days. It says OpenAI is making strong progress toward an automated AI researcher by March 2028, that agents still need significant human steering as task complexity rises, and that compute is a gating factor. This is self-reported: OpenAI set the goal and measured it, and I found no independent verification. I do not quote its numbers here because I could not check them against the page.

The shape of the claim matters more than the number. "Intern" is defined by a kind of task (well-defined, a few days, under direction), which corresponds to the Epoch scale's level 3 to 4 for a bounded piece of work. A claim at that level is compatible with Anthropic's picture, where the large majority of work is at level 3 or above and a minority is led by the model. Neither is evidence of level 5, and neither says that research gets faster by a stated factor.

Scoring your own loop

Use the same procedure for Artifact 4. List the tasks of the research loop itself, split into steps a person would otherwise do: proposing a rewrite, writing the patch, running evaluations, deciding what to keep, checking for hacking, writing the report. Rate each on the 0 to 5 scale, weight by the hours the task would take a person, and report both the time-weighted and the task-count shares with the weights shown. Have a second rater (a colleague, or a different model) rate independently and report exact agreement, within-one agreement and kappa. Put the table in the write-up next to the Level-1 results. It will show a reader where your loop is autonomous and where you steered it, which is a more honest statement than "it ran unattended".

Try it yourself

Edit the TASKS list in automation_share.py to describe your own week of research work, with real hours. Predict first: will the time share at level 4 or above be higher or lower than the task-count share? Then rate the same tasks conservatively and generously, and see how far the headline moves. If one rating change moves the headline by more than ten points, that task is where your rater agreement needs work.

Key takeaways

  • Automation is rated per task, on a 0 to 5 scale from "not used" to "autonomous"; an overall share is built from the ratings.
  • Epoch (17 June 2026) proposed the scale and a task list; Anthropic applied it to its own work (as of August 2026) and reports a level-4 share of 26% by person-time. Both are self-reported or authors' views.
  • The weighting (time or count) and the rater (model or human) change the headline; ask for both.
  • Report exact agreement, within-one agreement and kappa for any rating step.
  • A share of work is not a speed-up; the unautomated share caps the gain.
  • OpenAI's September 2026 research-intern claim is self-reported and could only be seen second-hand here.

Two of the seven tasks are at level 4 and they account for 10 of 21.5 hours. Weighting changes the headline whenever automated tasks are larger or smaller than average, and there is no general rule that time shares are higher.

Two raters agree exactly on 67% of tasks, but chance agreement is 28%. Which statistic corrects for this?

  1. Within-one agreement
  2. The standard error
  3. Cohen's kappa
  4. Kendall's tau
Kappa subtracts the agreement expected by chance and rescales, giving 0.54 here. Within-one agreement is more forgiving, not chance-corrected, and the other two measure different things.

Using Amdahl's law, a loop makes 50% of the work 10 times faster. About how much faster is the whole job?

  1. 5 times
  2. 1.8 times
  3. 10 times
  4. 1.5 times
1 / (0.5 + 0.5/10) = 1.82. Five times would hold only if the whole job were affected, and the unchanged half caps the gain at 2 times however fast the other half gets.
