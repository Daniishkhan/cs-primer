Reading Pack: Meta-Learning & Beyond

Artifact 4 stands on a wider literature than its own chapters can cover. This reading pack points at learned optimisers, in-context learning as meta-learning, interpretability and co-evolution in earlier courses, and at the three 2026 successors of the Darwin Gödel Machine, with a note on what to take from each.

Why this matters

This is a reading chapter. It is kept open while building Artifact 4 and is never required, because the artifact needs the four conditions, the economics and the audit checklist of chapters 01 to 04, not the papers below. It exists for the moment you are stuck or curious: the discovered agent behaves oddly and you want to know how to look inside it, your outer loop saturates and you wonder whether the evaluator should change, or a reviewer asks how your design differs from the 2026 successors of the Darwin Gödel Machine. After this chapter you know which earlier chapter or paper to open for each of those, and what to take from it. Read chapters 01 and 04 first, because every paper here is a claim to audit.

Three threads and one family

Artifact 4 sits where several older ideas meet. Each has a chapter in the path already, so this page points at them and says what to take from each for a self-improving loop. The newest material, three 2026 successors of the Darwin Gödel Machine, sits at the end.

Learned optimisers and in-context learning

A learned optimiser is an optimiser (the rule that updates a network's weights) that is itself trained, usually by running many small training jobs and improving the rule on how well they end. That is a self-improver in the plainest sense: an inner process, the training run, and an outer process that improves how it learns. A2 chapter 24 covers it together with in-context learning, the way a language model adapts to a task from examples in its prompt without any weight change, which can be read as the same two-level structure with the outer loop already trained. Take from it the vocabulary of meta-learning (the inner loop, the outer loop, a distribution of tasks) and the warning that an outer loop trained on one task distribution may not transfer to another, which is your generalisation condition in another setting.

Reading a network

A2 chapter 23 introduces probes and the residual stream, the tools for asking what a trained network represents. Artifact 4's discovered agent is code and not weights, so you can read it directly, and a 2026 report of such a system warns that the evolved agent can be hard to understand: Weco's post lists complexity and dead code in its final version among its limitations. The question "what did the loop actually change?" is a small interpretability question. A practical approach is to diff the discovered agent against its starting point, remove changes one at a time, and see which ones carry the gain, which is the ablation the artifact's write-up asks for.

Co-evolution

A7 chapter 22 covers populations that train each other: problems that get harder as solvers improve, and populations that share what they learn. The idea matters here because a self-improving loop that saturates its evaluator stops learning. The first of the three successors below makes exactly that point and then changes the evaluator along with the agent. The roadmap also lists a literacy pack (interpretability chapters, Clune's work on AI-generating algorithms, Stanley and Lehman on open-endedness, POET and population-based training) as parallel reading; I have not checked those sources for this page.

The successors of the Darwin Gödel Machine

The Darwin Gödel Machine (A7 chapter 13) keeps an archive of coding agents, picks one, asks a language model to rewrite it, and keeps the result if it scores well. Three papers from 2025 and 2026 change one piece of that design each. I read the arXiv abstract pages (and, for the first, search summaries of the conference listing) and not the full papers. All results are the authors' own.

Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM)
Wang, Piękos, Nanbo, Laakom, Chen, Ostaszewski, Zhuge and Schmidhuber, "Huxley-Gödel Machine: Human-Level Coding Agent Development by an Approximation of the Optimal Self-Improving Machine" (arXiv 2510.21614, submitted 24 October 2025; accepted at ICLR 2026, where the conference page lists it as an oral). The authors argue that choosing which agent to expand by its benchmark score is flawed, because score does not reliably predict how good the agent's descendants will be. They call that gap the Metaproductivity-Performance Mismatch and propose a metric, CMP, that aggregates the benchmark performance of an agent's descendants (named for Huxley's idea of a clade, a lineage descended from one ancestor). HGM estimates CMP and uses it to steer the search. They report that it beats earlier self-improving coding-agent methods on SWE-bench Verified and Polyglot with fewer CPU hours, and that an agent optimised with one model on one benchmark and tested with a different model on another matches the best officially checked human-engineered coding agents. The abstract gives no percentages. Take from it the idea that the quantity a self-improver should select on is the improver's descendants, not its own score, which is close to what the ignition test of chapter 01 measures after the fact.
HyperAgents
Zhang, Zhao, Yang, Foerster, Clune, Jiang, Devlin and Shavrina, "Hyperagents" (arXiv 2603.19461, submitted 19 March 2026). The authors note that the meta-level mechanism of earlier systems is fixed and hand-written, which caps how fast they can improve. A hyperagent puts a task agent and a meta agent in one editable program, so the procedure that generates improvements is itself editable. Their instance is DGM-Hyperagents. They report improvement across diverse domains over time, better results than baselines without self-improvement or open-ended exploration and than prior self-improving systems, and meta-level improvements (such as persistent memory and performance tracking) that transfer across domains and accumulate across runs. The abstract page gives no figures. Take from it the move from a fixed improver to an editable one: that is the structure ignition tests, since an improver that can edit itself has something to improve.
Red Queen Gödel Machine (RQGM)
Iacob and fourteen co-authors including Nicholas D. Lane, "The Red Queen Gödel Machine: Co-Evolving Agents and Their Evaluators" (arXiv 2606.26294, submitted 24 June 2026, third version 8 October 2026). Most self-improving agents are judged by a fixed evaluator, so progress stalls once the agent saturates it; RQGM lets learned evaluators improve alongside the agents they guide, with the scoring rule updated at epoch boundaries. The abstract reports, on a coding task (DeepSWE), a held-out pass rate of 82.1% with a co-evolved reviewer against 75.0% for a fixed-evaluator baseline at low reasoning effort; a grader for olympiad proofs that beats static baselines at three times lower search cost; reviewers that are equally stringent towards AI and human work; and writers reaching 1.78 to 1.86 times higher acceptance rates under an agent-as-a-judge panel. The authors call it a preliminary preprint and work in progress. Take from it a caution as much as a method: an evaluator that changes is also an evaluator the loop may learn to please, so the hygiene rules of Artifact 3 matter more, not less.

Common misconception

"The newest system supersedes the older one, so I should build Artifact 4 on it." These papers answer different questions: which agent to expand, whether the improver can be edited, whether the evaluator should change. Artifact 4's gate is a measurement of your own loop under the four conditions, and a simpler loop measured honestly is a better artifact than a complicated one measured loosely.

Key takeaways

  • Learned optimisers and in-context learning give the vocabulary of inner and outer loops (A2 chapter 24).
  • Interpretability is the habit of asking what changed; for an evolved agent, diff and ablate (A2 chapter 23).
  • Co-evolution explains why a fixed evaluator saturates (A7 chapter 22).
  • HGM selects on descendants, HyperAgents makes the improver editable, and RQGM lets the evaluator evolve; all three are preprint or conference claims by their authors.
  • Audit each with the checklist of chapter 04 before relying on it.

Check your understanding

What is the Metaproductivity-Performance Mismatch that the Huxley-Gödel Machine addresses?

  1. An agent's benchmark score rises while its cost per step also rises
  2. An agent's own benchmark score does not reliably predict how good its descendants will be
  3. Public and private scores disagree
  4. A human baseline is tuned more than the loop
The authors argue that picking the next agent to expand by its own score is flawed, since score is a poor guide to self-improvement potential. Option 3 is a baseline problem from chapter 01, and option 2 is the winner's curse.

What does the Red Queen Gödel Machine change relative to a fixed-evaluator design?

  1. It removes the evaluator and trusts the agent's own judgement
  2. It makes the agent smaller
  3. It replaces self-modification with fine-tuning
  4. It lets learned evaluators improve alongside the agents, updating the scoring rule at epoch boundaries
The idea is that a fixed evaluator saturates; co-evolving it keeps the pressure on. It does not remove the evaluator, and it keeps agents as the thing being rewritten.

A hyperagent differs from the Darwin Gödel Machine mainly in that:

  1. The meta-level procedure that generates improvements is itself editable
  2. It does not use an archive
  3. It runs without a foundation model
  4. It was evaluated only on proofs
Hyperagents put the task agent and the meta agent in one editable program. The fixed, hand-written meta level is the limitation the authors name in the DGM.
