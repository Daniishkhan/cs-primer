Reading Self-Improvement Claims

Most of what you will read about self-improving systems is a paper or a post written by the people who built them. This chapter gives you an audit checklist, applies it to the Darwin Gödel Machine, AlphaEvolve, the AI Scientist and AIDE², and shows how to check a claim's numbers against each other.

Why this matters

This chapter serves Artifact 4's write-up and the review you do of Weco's claim. Your own result will be read by someone who has just read several others, and the quickest way to be believed is to show that you read them the way they will read you. Everything in this field arrives as a paper or a post written by the people who built the system, so the reader is the only auditor. After this chapter you can apply a ten-point checklist to any self-improvement claim, state for each point whether the source reports it, tell a claim of discovery from a claim of self-improvement, and catch inconsistencies in a claim's own numbers with a short script. It pairs with A7 studio 2, which is a timed practice of the same skill, and with Case 1, which audits the AIDE² claim in full. The tests it uses are the four of chapter 01.

Three kinds of claim

A paper about a self-improving system can claim one of three different things, and the evidence needed differs.

A discovery claim
"The system found a better algorithm, program or result." The evidence is the artifact itself and a checker that anyone can run. AlphaEvolve's 4×4 matrix-multiplication procedure is of this kind: you can verify the procedure without trusting the system that found it.
A capability claim
"The agent scores higher on a benchmark after the process." The evidence is a score, so everything in chapter 01 applies: baseline, split, trend, budget.
A self-improvement claim
"The system improved its own ability to improve." The evidence needed is the capability claim plus a successor that is a better improver than its predecessor, which is the ignition test.

Most confusion comes from using the first kind of evidence for the third kind of claim. A verified discovery is strong evidence that a search found something real, and no evidence at all that the search got better at searching.

A ten-point checklist

The list below is the working tool of this chapter. Each point is a question with a failure it catches. Answer each as one of three states: reported (the source says it and shows how), claimed (the source says it without showing how), or absent (not in what you read). Write down which source passages you used, so that your audit can be checked.

  1. What was measured? The score the loop optimised, or a different one? Public or private?
  2. Fair baseline. Same budget, same attempts, human-tuned? Who tuned it, for how long?
  3. Trend. How many separate improvements, and does each clear the noise? How many seeds?
  4. Unseen tasks. Did the gain hold on tasks the search never touched, and how different were they?
  5. Budget. Fixed in advance, stated in dollars or GPU-hours, the same for every arm?
  6. Evaluator. Outside the agent's write access? Hacking rate reported next to the score?
  7. Who measured it? The builders, or someone else? Is the code released, and has anyone reproduced it?
  8. Asymmetries. Does the outer loop use a stronger model than the inner one? Was one arm given a better tool?
  9. Selective reporting. Are rejected proposals, failed runs and negative results shown?
  10. Claim against evidence. Does the headline say more than the numbers: "recursive" for a one-step gain, "discovered" for something the authors selected from many runs?

Analogy: reading a company's accounts

A company's annual report is prepared by the company. A careful reader does not ask whether the profit figure is large; they ask which accounting rules were used, whether the comparison year was restated, whether the auditor was independent, and what the notes at the back concede. The checklist is the same practice applied to a research claim: the headline is the profit, and the notes are where the limitations live.

Where it breaks down: accounts follow shared standards and are audited by outsiders under law. For self-improving systems no standard exists, almost every report is self-reported, and external audits are planned more often than performed.

Applying it: four systems

The audits below use only what I fetched and read. Where I read an abstract page or a search summary and not the paper, I say so, and the entry for that question is "absent from what I read", which is not the same as "the paper does not do it".

The Darwin Gödel Machine, or DGM (2025)

Zhang, Hu, Lu, Lange and Clune (arXiv 2505.22954, submitted 29 May 2025, revised March 2026) describe a coding agent that edits its own code, keeps an archive of agents, samples one, and uses a foundation model to produce a variant that is tested on coding benchmarks. The abstract reports performance on SWE-bench (a benchmark of real software-engineering issues) rising from 20.0% to 50.0% and Polyglot from 14.2% to 30.7%, and says the DGM beats baselines that lack either self-improvement or open-ended exploration, and that experiments ran with sandboxing and human oversight. Against the checklist: the baselines are ablations of the method itself (point 2, reported at abstract level); the benchmarks are the ones used during search, so unseen-task transfer is not in the abstract (point 4); budgets are not in the abstract (point 5). It is a capability claim with self-improvement framing. A later paper, the Huxley-Gödel Machine, argues that benchmark score is a poor guide to which agent will produce good descendants. Chapter 05 returns to it.

AlphaEvolve (2025)

Novikov and colleagues (arXiv 2506.13131, submitted 16 June 2025) describe an evolutionary coding agent in which language models make code changes that evaluators score. The abstract reports a procedure for 4×4 complex-valued matrix multiplication using 48 scalar multiplications, "the first improvement, after 56 years, over Strassen's algorithm in this setting", plus improvements to a data-centre scheduler, a chip circuit and the training speed of the language model underlying AlphaEvolve itself. The matrix result is a discovery claim and is checkable by anyone who can multiply matrices. The training-speed result sounds like self-improvement, but the abstract does not say whether the faster model made the next round of discovery better, so it is a capability gain on a component. I did not read the full paper, so I cannot say what compute the search used.

The AI Scientist (2026)

Sakana AI and collaborators published "Towards End-to-End Automation of AI Research" in Nature on 26 March 2026. I did not fetch the paper or the Nature page. A search summary of the Nature record, Sakana's announcement and a news release from the University of British Columbia says that the system generates ideas, writes code, runs experiments, drafts a manuscript and reviews its own work, that one of its manuscripts passed the first round of peer review at a workshop of a major machine-learning conference, and that the workshop's acceptance rate was 70%. The same summary says secondary coverage notes weak ideas, shaky rigour and hallucinations. Even taking those at face value, the checklist's questions are visible. Is a pass at a workshop with a 70% acceptance rate a strong test (point 4 and point 10)? Who selected the manuscript from how many generated (point 9)? This is a claim about end-to-end automation of a pipeline, not about a system improving its own improver, and a reader should keep it in the capability category.

AIDE² (2026)

The case is read in full in Case 1. In outline, using the Weco blog post of 14 July 2026 and the abstract of the technical report (arXiv 2609.26457). Point 1: public and private scores are separate, and private held-out scores are the true objective (reported). Point 2: a human-tuned baseline, AIDEhuman, is the Level-1 reference (reported). Point 3: seven successive improvements in 100 steps, with run-to-run noise of roughly 0.02 to 0.045 stated for one table (reported; seeds not in what I read). Point 4: MLE-Bench Lite (machine-learning-engineering competitions), ALE-Bench Lite (heuristic algorithm engineering) and WeatherBench 2, the last far out of distribution with a $15 budget per agent (reported). Point 5: a fixed cost per evaluation (reported). Point 6: a reward-hacking rate on KernelBench kernels is reported next to the scores, and the post says that its statistical anti-hacking layer has a bug and has no effect on the final version (reported, and honest). Point 7: the authors are the builders, and I found no independent replication (absent). Point 8: the outer loop uses a stronger model than the inner loop, which the post lists as a limitation (reported). Point 9: about 95 rejected proposals from one seed are tabulated (reported). Point 10: the headline says "first experimental evidence of consistent recursive self-improvement" while the same post says ignition was not demonstrated and that the efficiency difference is not significant, so the word "recursive" rests on a Level-1 result.

Common misconception

"It was published at a good venue, so it has been checked." Review for a venue judges whether the work is clear, novel and plausible. It does not rerun the experiments. A Nature paper, an oral talk at the ICLR conference (International Conference on Learning Representations) and an arXiv preprint can all contain self-reported numbers that nobody has reproduced. A venue is evidence about review, and replication is evidence about results.

Check a claim's numbers against each other

A claim usually states several figures, and some of them constrain each other. A short script catches inconsistencies that reading does not. The script below takes figures reported for AIDE² (all self-reported) and checks three things: whether "about nine in ten rejected" fits seven accepts in 100 steps; how large each paired gain is against the run-to-run noise range the post gives; and how much the hacking rate moved against the starting agent and against the human agent.

"""Check a claim's own numbers against each other before believing it.

Inputs are figures Weco reports for AIDE2 (blog post, 14 July 2026; paper,
arXiv 2609.26457, 22 Sept 2026), all self-reported.

Run: python3 claim_arithmetic.py
"""

# ── 1. Figures as reported ───────────────────────────────────────────────
STEPS, IMPROVEMENTS = 100, 7
PAIRED_GAIN = {"AIDE47": 0.053, "AIDE85": 0.042}   # MLE-Bench Lite vs AIDE0
NOISE_LOW, NOISE_HIGH = 0.02, 0.045                # run-to-run noise range
HACK_RATE = {"AIDE0": 63, "AIDE47": 42, "AIDE85": 34, "AIDEhuman": 42}

# ── 2. Does "about nine in ten rejected" match 7 accepts in 100 steps? ───
rejected = (STEPS - IMPROVEMENTS) / STEPS
print(f"rejected share if only {IMPROVEMENTS} of {STEPS} were kept: "
      f"{rejected:.0%}")

# ── 3. How big is each gain next to the noise the post itself reports? ───
for name, gain in PAIRED_GAIN.items():
    print(f"{name}: gain {gain:.3f} = "
          f"{gain / NOISE_HIGH:.1f} to {gain / NOISE_LOW:.1f} noise widths")

# ── 4. Hacking rate: points and relative change against both references ──
base, human = HACK_RATE["AIDE0"], HACK_RATE["AIDEhuman"]
for name in ("AIDE47", "AIDE85"):
    rate = HACK_RATE[name]
    print(f"{name}: {rate}%  vs AIDE0 {rate - base:+d} points "
          f"({(rate - base) / base:+.0%}),  vs human {rate - human:+d} points")
rejected share if only 7 of 100 were kept: 93%
AIDE47: gain 0.053 = 1.2 to 2.6 noise widths
AIDE85: gain 0.042 = 0.9 to 2.1 noise widths
AIDE47: 42%  vs AIDE0 -21 points (-33%),  vs human +0 points
AIDE85: 34%  vs AIDE0 -29 points (-46%),  vs human -8 points

Seven accepts in 100 steps is 93% rejected, which is consistent with "about nine in ten". The paired gains of +0.053 and +0.042 are 1.2 to 2.6 and 0.9 to 2.1 noise widths, taking the post's stated range of 0.02 to 0.045 as applying to this metric, which I cannot be sure of from the text I read. The authors report p = 0.0024 and p = 0.0041 for the paired gains; I could not check how many tasks or seeds those tests pooled, and a gain of one to two noise widths needs that pooling to reach such p-values. The hacking-rate figures show 63% falling to 34%, a 29-point (46%) drop against the starting agent, and 8 points below the human agent for the final version, but equal to the human agent for version 47. Two numbers in the sources disagree on the face of it. The arXiv abstract page reports a fall from 55% to 32% on a separate task family, and the blog reports 63% to 34% on kernels. These are different task sets, so they need not conflict, but a write-up that quotes "reward hacking fell from 55% to 32%" without naming the task family is quoting loosely.

Systems connection

The audit is the habit of an engineer reading a vendor's benchmark: ask who ran it, on what hardware, against which version, and whether the numbers can be regenerated. Attested results (A11) are the engineering response: instead of trusting a report, the evaluator produces a record that the agent could not have written.

Key takeaways

  • Separate discovery, capability and self-improvement claims; each needs different evidence, and ignition is the extra evidence for the third.
  • The ten-point checklist answers each question as reported, claimed or absent, with the passage cited.
  • Almost every figure in this field is self-reported; say so, and date it.
  • Peer review is not replication.
  • Check a claim's numbers against one another with a script; the sources may disagree on their face.
  • "Absent from what I read" is a statement about your reading, not about the work.

Check your understanding

AlphaEvolve found a procedure for 4×4 complex matrix multiplication using 48 scalar multiplications. Which kind of claim is this, and why does it matter?

  1. A self-improvement claim, because the system improved itself
  2. A capability claim, because a benchmark score rose
  3. A claim that cannot be checked without access to the system
  4. A discovery claim, which anyone can verify by running the procedure without trusting the system
The result is an artifact with a checker. That makes it strong evidence that the search found something real, and no evidence of self-improvement.

A source reports a gain but does not state the compute budget of the baseline. How do you record point 5 of the checklist?

  1. Absent, with a note that this is about what you read
  2. Reported, because a gain implies a budget
  3. Failed, because the authors must have hidden it
  4. Not applicable
The checklist records what the source says, not why it does not say it. Absent is a statement about the reading, not an accusation.

The AIDE² post says its headline result is "first experimental evidence of consistent recursive self-improvement" and also that ignition was not demonstrated. What does the checklist flag?

  1. A fair baseline is missing
  2. The budget was exceeded
  3. The headline says more than the strongest test: "recursive" rests on a Level-1 result
  4. Nothing, since the post was honest
The post is candid, and that candour is what lets you see the gap. Point 10 asks whether the headline claim exceeds the evidence, and here the gap is between a Level-1 result and the Level-2 word "recursive". The baseline and budget are reported.

A paper with a result is accepted as an oral at a top conference. What does this tell you about replication?

  1. It has been replicated by the reviewers
  2. Nothing directly: review judges clarity, novelty and plausibility, not rerunning the experiments
  3. The numbers are independently verified
  4. It rules out reward hacking
Reviewers rarely rerun experiments. Acceptance is evidence about the review process, not about reproducibility.

In the claim-arithmetic output, the final AIDE² version has a 34% hacking rate against 42% for the human agent, and version 47 has 42%. Which statement follows?

  1. Both versions are less prone to hacking than the human agent
  2. Version 47 is worse than the human agent
  3. Hacking was eliminated
  4. Only the final version is below the human agent; version 47 equals it
42 minus 42 is zero points and 34 minus 42 is minus 8 points. The post also says the final version's statistical anti-hacking layer has a bug, so the reduction is not attributed to that layer.

You read only an abstract and the abstract does not mention seeds. What can you write in your audit?

  1. The paper used one seed
  2. The paper used three seeds, as the standard demands
  3. Seeds were absent from what I read, so I checked or flagged it for the full paper
  4. Seeds do not matter for a benchmark paper
An abstract is the wrong place to look for seeds. The honest entry names the limit of your reading and does not guess in either direction.
