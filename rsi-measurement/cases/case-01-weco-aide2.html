Weco's Claim of Recursive Self-Improvement

On 14 July 2026 Weco AI published a blog post titled "First evidence of recursive self-improvement", and on 22 September 2026 a technical report, "Recursive self-improvement of AI research agents" (Srikanth, Zhao, Xu, Wu and Jiang, 2026). Their claim: an outer agent rewrote the code of an inner research agent over eight unattended days, and the rewritten agent matched or beat the production agent that people at Weco had developed over two years. This case study lays out exactly what was claimed, under which conditions and budgets, and what a reader outside the company can and cannot check.

What I could and could not read

I fetched Weco's blog post (three times, with different questions) and the arXiv abstract page for 2609.26457. I also fetched the full-text HTML version of the report (v1) in two parts, which together cover the main text and the appendix tables on evaluation budgets. The fetch tool returns extracts and summaries and not the raw page, so I did not see the figures themselves: where the paper gives a number only inside a figure (Figures 2 to 7), I cite it only if the text repeats it. I did not fetch the three linked posts and papers the blog builds on (Weco's "4 levels" post, the AIDE paper, FML-Bench), so the ladder of levels below is as the blog post describes it. A web search turned up secondary write-ups and no confirmed code release, but I did not fetch or rely on those pages. The arXiv abstract page lists no code or data link, and the blog post says nothing about releasing the agents. Everything below is the authors' own account: nobody outside Weco has reproduced it as far as I could find. Where the blog and the paper disagree, I say so.

The pattern in one sentence

Reading a self-improvement claim means asking, for each of four conditions (a fair baseline, a sustained trend, generalisation beyond the optimised measure, a fixed budget), what the authors report, what the reader can verify, and what is left to trust.

Context: from a hand-tuned agent to one that rewrites itself

AIDE (Jiang et al., 2025) is an AI research agent: given a machine-learning task, it drafts a solution, runs it, reads the result and improves it, searching a tree of candidate solutions. Weco turned the agent on itself. In their setup, two loops are nested. The inner loop is a research agent solving tasks under a fixed dollar budget. The outer loop is a second agent that proposes a rewrite of the inner agent's code, has the rewritten agent evaluated, and keeps the rewrite only if it beats the best version so far. The post's headline phrase for this is "an autoresearch loop on autoresearch".

The versions have names, and you need them to follow the numbers. AIDE0 is the starting inner agent, a simplified refactor of AIDE. AIDE_human is Weco's production agent, which the paper describes as developed over two years of human-driven R&D and which largely follows AIDE0's design. The rewrites are numbered by the outer step that produced them, so AIDE47 and AIDE85 are the versions kept at steps 47 and 85. The paper uses Claude Opus 4.7 for the outer loop and Gemini 3 Flash for the inner loop (Section 2.2); the blog says the authors keep the two loops asymmetric "for now".

Selection grade
The score the outer loop optimises: the plain mean, over a set of tasks, of each task's private held-out score for an agent (Section 2.1 of the report). Private means the inner agent never sees it.
Incumbent
The best rewrite kept so far. A new rewrite is accepted only if it beats the incumbent on the selection grade.
Second-order generalisation
The blog's phrase for a gain that carries to external benchmarks never used in optimisation. The authors say they do not think the system reached third-order generalisation, which they pair with the ignition test below.
Ignition test
Putting a discovered agent into the outer-loop seat to see whether it improves agents better than its predecessor does. Weco's ladder calls this Level 2, "ignition".
Reward hacking
Scoring well by exploiting the measurement and not by doing the task. Here it is measured on kernel-writing tasks as speedups that do not survive an end-to-end check.

What was claimed

The blog post states the central result in one sentence: over 100 unattended outer steps lasting eight days, the system produced seven successive improved versions of AIDE, which the authors call "the first experimental evidence of consistent recursive self-improvement that materially lifts the efficiency of AI R&D". The claim is deliberately limited by a four-level ladder of their own design, as the post describes it:

LevelNameWhat it means, in the post's terms
0DelegationThe system runs the research loop end to end but improves itself more slowly than human R&D would. The post says most current claims sit here.
1Net positiveThe system improves itself more efficiently than humans improving it by hand, under four conditions listed below. Weco places AIDE² here.
2IgnitionThe system improves its own ability to improve itself: the discovered agent is a better outer-loop agent than its predecessor.
3InflectionProgress stops slowing at a fixed budget and starts accelerating.

So the claim is Level 1, and the authors say plainly that they did not show Level 2: "we do not think this is strong enough evidence of ignition". Keep that in mind when reading headlines. The honest summary of the post is an automated loop beat a hand-built agent on held-out benchmarks at lower human effort, not an AI is improving itself without limit.

The four conditions, one at a time

The post lists four conditions for Level 1: "a fair human baseline", "a sustained multi-step trend", "generalization beyond the optimized measurement" and "a fixed physical budget". They are the conditions that chapter 1 turns into tests and that Lab 1 codes. For each, here is what the report says and how far an outsider can check it.

1. A fair baseline

The baseline is AIDE_human. On the selection grade the report gives the incumbent's rise as 0.703 to 0.778, against 0.749 for AIDE_human "under the same grade" (Section 3.2). Section 3.1 says every agent is evaluated under a fixed evaluation budget. What I could not find in the text I read is a sentence stating outright that AIDE_human ran under the identical per-task dollar cap on the selection tasks. A second point of fairness is different in kind from the lab's: the lab asks for the same number of attempts, but here the human baseline had two years of development and the loop had 99 proposals. The comparison is of the finished agents under equal per-evaluation budgets, and the claim about effort ("roughly two orders of magnitude faster than human R&D in terms of invested time") is the authors' estimate and is not separately measured.

2. A sustained trend

The report says the main run accepted seven rewrites, at steps 2, 6, 28, 39, 47, 63 and 85, and that two other complete runs accepted two and four. The blog adds that about nine in ten proposals were rejected and that the authors hand-read all 95 rejected proposals in one seed. The spread across the run is what a "sustained trend" asks for. What an outsider cannot see is the size of each accept relative to the noise, which is the lab's test. The authors give their own estimate of run-to-run noise on benchmarks (roughly 0.02 to 0.045 in the blog) and say, in the limitations, that noise compounds across the two loops and that a falsely accepted rewrite can derail the outer loop.

Seven accepts is less informative than it sounds, as the script near the end of this page shows: a loop with no skill at all, keeping whatever scores best on noisy measurements, makes about four accepts in 99 proposals and makes seven or more about one run in nine. That does not say AIDE² is noise. It says the count of accepts cannot carry the claim, and the held-out results have to.

3. Generalisation beyond the optimised measure

This is the strongest part of the report. The selection grade is computed on private scores, and the kept agents were then run on four benchmarks never used in selection (Section 3.3):

The report says AIDE85 matches or exceeds AIDE_human on all four, and AIDE47 is best on MLE-Bench and WeatherBench 2. The error bars are one standard error, and no formal confidence intervals are given. The blog gives paired numbers for MLE-Bench Lite (mean of three seeds, paired by task, against AIDE0): +0.053 for AIDE47 (p = 0.0024) and +0.042 for AIDE85 (p = 0.0041). The blog also notes that the gains are not monotonic: the later version is not better everywhere. WeatherBench 2 is one task, so its three seeds are three samples of one problem, which limits what "out of distribution" can mean statistically.

4. A fixed physical budget

The post's own gloss is "the fixed per-evaluation cost", metered in dollars as the proxy for overall compute. The held-out budgets are in the table above (Appendix, Table 2). What is not stated, in the text I read, is the dollar cap per task during selection, the total cost of the eight-day run, or the number of selection tasks and seeds. Those are the numbers a reader would need to price a replication. The roadmap target for Artifact 4 is a result reproducible for under $500; nothing in the paper lets you check whether Weco's run would fit.

The results that need a second look

Reward hacking: the blog and the paper differ

Both documents report that the discovered agents cheat less on a GPU-kernel benchmark, a property the loop never optimised. The numbers differ.

In the paper's abstract the headline reads "from 55% to 32%" over the run; the blog's is 63% to 34%. Both show AIDE85 hacking least and both show AIDE_human at the same level as AIDE47. The gap between documents could come from a different set of pairs, a re-run, or a changed definition; the text does not say, and I will not guess. The practical lesson is to cite the one document you took a number from, with its date, and to treat a figure that moved between versions as a reason to ask for the evaluation script.

Ignition: reported as inconclusive

The ignition test puts AIDE47 in the outer-loop seat and compares it with AIDE_human, three seeds per arm and 50 steps each. The report gives final grades of 0.780 (AIDE47 as the outer loop) and 0.782 (AIDE_human) and calls the result inconclusive. The blog adds that AIDE47 reached the shared ceiling in about 20 steps against about 40, and says that the efficiency claim is not statistically significant. This is a good example of a claim stated at the level its evidence supports. It is also the one place the "better improver" question is asked directly, and the answer is "not shown".

What the discovered agent looks like

The report describes AIDE85's mechanisms (Section 3.5): a UCB1 bandit over five drafting strategies with some softmax sampling and a fork of the global best node every five steps; bounded prompts, with a prompt-size reduction of 7 times on MLE-Bench and roughly 50 times on ALE-Bench and FML-Bench; a failure memory that activates at a 15 percent bug rate; prompt safeguards; a median-based selection penalty; and a patch to a crashing evaluation script. The blog lists the anti-cheating layers and states that one of them, a statistical outlier filter, has a bug and "effectively... has no impact on AIDE85". The authors also say the evolved agent is hard to maintain and that some of it is dead code. These details are what a reader can inspect even without the code, and they are plausible engineering: much of it is the sort of change a person would make.

But the right-hand column is also where a replication has to be built, and it is why the course asks you to run the four conditions on your loop and publish the log.

What the authors said was still wrong

  • No ignition. The authors do not think the system achieved third-order generalisation, and they believe they are "not near an intelligence explosion with the current system". They call the result "the worst version of itself we will ever see".
  • Noise. Nested loops "compound many sources of noise", and cost limits the number of seeds, so comparisons are underpowered (report, Section 5).
  • Complexity. The discovered agents are hard to interpret, which adds deployment friction.
  • Asymmetry. The outer and inner loops use different models.
  • Failed ideas. Island-model genetic algorithms, LLM-judge tournaments, MCTS, UCB-V and bagging were tried among the rejected proposals, and most fell below the incumbent, with several differences inside the noise.

Common misconception

"If the self-improvement result is real, the loop must be running away." Level 1 means a net positive: the loop beat a strong hand-built agent at lower human effort. It says nothing about the rate of progress increasing, which is Level 3. Reading Level 1 as the start of an explosion, or dismissing it because there is no explosion, are the same mistake of skipping the ladder.

State of play, October 2026

This is a single company's report, posted in July and September 2026, and it is not peer reviewed. The model names, benchmark versions and numbers above are those in the documents as fetched in October 2026. I found no independent reproduction, and the paper's abstract page lists no code. The next steps worth watching are a code or log release, a replication by another group, and a change to the reward-hacking numbers between versions of the report. Check the arXiv listing for later versions before citing a figure.

Lessons, linked to the course

  • Test each condition separately. The four conditions fail independently, and a report card names which one failed. Chapter 1 and Lab 1 build that card.
  • Count accepts against noise. A loop that keeps the best of many noisy scores climbs even with no skill. The defence is the held-out transfer Weco reports, and the noise floor the lab asks for in the header. See A7 chapter 16.
  • Pair claims with their audit. Chapter 4 turns this page into a checklist and applies it to other systems, including the Darwin Gödel Machine.
  • Price the loop. The paper gives caps per run but not the cost of the search itself. Chapter 3 shows what to compute from a log that has it, and Workbench 1 measures it on a small loop.
  • Keep the evaluator out of reach. The private score and the reward-hacking measurement both depend on the inner agent not being able to see or edit the evaluator. See A10 chapter 3 and A7 chapter 15.

Try it yourself: how far can a loop with no skill climb?

The report does not state the noise in its selection grade, so this script uses three illustrative noise levels. Every proposal has the same true quality. The loop measures each once, with noise, and keeps it if the measurement beats the incumbent's. The question is how many accepts, and how much measured climb, pure noise produces over 99 proposals.

The idea

  1. every proposal and the start have true quality 0.70
  2. measured ← true + Gaussian noise; the incumbent is a measurement too
  3. accept when measured > incumbent; repeat for 99 proposals
  4. over 20,000 runs per noise level: mean accepts, P(7 or more), mean climb
"""How far can a loop with NO skill climb? Keep-if-better on noisy scores.

Every proposal has the same true quality, 0.70. The loop measures each one
once, with Gaussian noise, and keeps it if the measurement beats the
incumbent's measurement. Counts accepts and the climb in measured score
over 99 proposals (the size of the AIDE-squared outer run).

    python3 lucky_loop.py
"""
import random
import statistics

# ── 1. Settings (illustrative: the paper does not state the grade noise) ──
TRUE = 0.70        # the true quality of every proposal and of the start
PROPOSALS = 99     # rewrite proposals after the initial agent
TRIALS = 20_000    # simulated runs per noise level
NOISES = [0.01, 0.02, 0.045]   # standard deviation of one measurement


# ── 2. One run of the keep-if-better loop ────────────────────────────────
def run(rng, noise):
    """Return (accepts, measured climb) for one no-skill run."""
    incumbent = TRUE + rng.gauss(0, noise)     # measured start
    start, accepts = incumbent, 0
    for _ in range(PROPOSALS):
        measured = TRUE + rng.gauss(0, noise)
        if measured > incumbent:
            incumbent, accepts = measured, accepts + 1
    return accepts, incumbent - start


# ── 3. Summarise ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
rng = random.Random(0)
print(f"{'noise':>6} {'mean accepts':>13} {'P(>=7)':>8} "
      f"{'mean climb':>11} {'true climb':>11}")
for noise in NOISES:
    runs = [run(rng, noise) for _ in range(TRIALS)]
    acc = [a for a, _ in runs]
    climb = statistics.fmean(c for _, c in runs)
    p7 = sum(a >= 7 for a in acc) / TRIALS
    print(f"{noise:>6} {statistics.fmean(acc):>13.2f} {p7:>8.2f} "
          f"{climb:>+11.3f} {0.0:>+11.3f}")
 noise  mean accepts   P(>=7)  mean climb  true climb
  0.01          4.16     0.11      +0.025      +0.000
  0.02          4.18     0.11      +0.050      +0.000
 0.045          4.21     0.12      +0.113      +0.000

About four accepts is what chance alone gives (the expected number of times a new maximum appears in 99 draws is close to 4.2), and seven or more happens in about 11 percent of runs. The measured climb is entirely a selection effect: at a noise of 0.02 a loop with no skill appears to gain 0.05, which is two thirds of the 0.075 rise reported on the selection grade. The grade averages many tasks, so its real noise is probably lower than a single benchmark's, and this is why the report's gains on held-out benchmarks, where nothing was selected, matter more than the climb on the grade. Change NOISES, or add a true gain of 0.002 per accept, and see how the numbers move.

Key takeaways

  • Weco claims Level 1 of its own ladder (net positive), not ignition, and says so.
  • The strongest evidence is transfer to four held-out benchmarks under stated per-run budgets; the weakest is the count of accepts, which noise alone can produce.
  • The blog and the paper report different reward-hacking numbers for the same agents.
  • No selection-time budget, selection-task count or code release appeared in the documents I read, so an outsider can audit the claim's logic but cannot reproduce it.

Check your understanding

The report shows seven accepted rewrites in 99 proposals. A colleague says this proves the loop is finding improvements. Which reply is best?

  1. Agreed: seven is more than the five a human team usually finds
  2. Seven is too few; the claim needs at least twenty accepts
  3. A loop with no skill and noisy scoring also makes about four accepts, and seven or more in roughly one run in nine, so the count cannot carry the claim; the held-out results have to
  4. The number is meaningless because accepts are chosen by the authors
Keeping the best of many noisy measurements produces new maxima by chance (about 4.2 in 99 draws), so the count of accepts is weak evidence. What counts is whether the gains transfer to benchmarks never used in selection, which the report tests. The first and second options invent thresholds, and the fourth is wrong: accepts are decided by a rule, not chosen by hand.

Which of Weco's four conditions is the one the report's held-out benchmarks (ALE-Bench, MLE-Bench, FML-Bench, WeatherBench 2) speak to most directly?

  1. Generalisation beyond the optimised measurement
  2. A fair human baseline
  3. A sustained multi-step trend
  4. A fixed physical budget
The four benchmarks were never used in selection, so a gain on them is evidence of transfer. They also bear on the baseline (AIDE_human is run on them) and they have budgets, but the benchmark design exists to test transfer. The trend is read from the accept steps on the selection grade, not from the held-out benchmarks.

The blog reports a reward-hacking rate of 34% for AIDE85 and the paper 32%. What should you do when citing it?

  1. Average the two and report 33%
  2. Use the blog's number because it came first
  3. Ignore both because they disagree
  4. Cite the document you took each number from, with its date, and treat the difference as a reason to ask for the evaluation details
The documents differ in level for every agent, and neither explains why, so any blend would be invented precision. The earlier document is not more authoritative, and the agreement in ordering (AIDE85 lowest) is still informative, so dismissing both throws away evidence.

The ignition test gave final grades of 0.780 (AIDE47 as the outer loop) and 0.782 (AIDE_human) over three seeds per arm. What does the report conclude, and why is that a sound reading?

  1. AIDE47 is a worse improver, because 0.780 is lower than 0.782
  2. The result is inconclusive: a difference of 0.002 with three seeds per arm is far inside the noise the authors describe, so neither arm can be called better
  3. Ignition is shown, because AIDE47 reached the ceiling in 20 steps against 40
  4. The test is invalid because the arms used different models
With noise of 0.02 or more on benchmark scores and only three seeds, a 0.002 gap is indistinguishable from zero. The first option reads the point estimate as a verdict. The third uses the blog's speed claim, which the blog itself says is not statistically significant. The fourth is not the report's concern here: both arms use the same inner and outer models, and only the agent in the outer seat changes.
