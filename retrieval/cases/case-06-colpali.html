ColPali: Retrieving Pages as Images

Most document search starts by turning every PDF page into text: detect the layout, run OCR, chunk the result, perhaps caption the charts. In 2024 researchers at Illuin Technology and CentraleSupélec, with colleagues at Equall.ai and ETH Zürich, argued in "ColPali: Efficient Document Retrieval with Vision Language Models" that this pipeline is the real bottleneck, and that a vision-language model can embed the picture of the page directly. The paper is two contributions at once: a benchmark, ViDoRe, built to test the idea, and a model, ColPali, that does well on it. This case reads both, rebuilds the paper's storage figures, and looks at what the benchmark can and cannot tell you.

What I could and could not read

I read the full text of the ColPali paper (arXiv 2407.01449, version 6 of 28 February 2025, the ICLR 2025 camera-ready), including its appendices, after extracting the text from the PDF. I fetched and read the abstract pages of two follow-up benchmark papers, ViDoRe V2 (arXiv 2505.17166) and ViDoRe V3 (arXiv 2601.08620), but not their full text. Descriptions of the V3 leaderboard and of newer models come only from search-result summaries and community pages; I did not open the live leaderboard, so I quote no current rankings. I did not run ColPali. The two toys below use synthetic vectors and illustrate mechanisms; they do not reproduce the paper's results. Authorship overlap between the ColPali paper and the follow-up benchmarks matters for one point in the critique section.

The pattern in one sentence

Instead of reconstructing a page's text and structure before indexing it, embed the rendered page as a grid of image patches, one vector per patch, and let the query match patches directly with late interaction, so that charts, tables and layout need no separate extraction step.

Context: the ingestion pipeline is the bottleneck

The paper starts from an observation about industrial retrieval: "the primary performance bottleneck for efficient document retrieval stems not from embedding model performance but from the prior data ingestion pipeline". Indexing a PDF means running a parser or OCR to extract words, a layout model to separate paragraphs, titles, tables and figures, a chunking strategy, and, if the pages contain charts, perhaps a captioning step that asks a vision model to describe each one in words so that a text embedder can use it. The authors report that, in their experiments, improving the ingestion pipeline gives much better results on visually rich documents than improving the text embedding model does.

The course covers each stage of that pipeline: PDFs & Layout, OCR and Tables, Forms & Key-Value Extraction. Chapter 21, Visual Document Retrieval, explains the idea of embedding the page image. This case is the primary-source companion to that chapter: what exactly the paper measured, with which data, and what the numbers do and do not support.

Page-level retrieval
The task in the paper: given a query, return the right page. A "document" in this benchmark is one page.
ViDoRe
The Visual Document Retrieval Benchmark introduced with ColPali: ten page-retrieval tasks covering text, tables, figures and infographics in English and French.
nDCG@5
The paper's main metric. With a single relevant page, it equals 1 divided by log2(rank + 1) if the page is in the top 5, and 0 otherwise, averaged over queries and shown as a percentage. See Relevance Metrics.
Patch
A small square tile of the page image. The vision encoder turns each tile into a vector. ColPali's backbone yields 1,024 of them per page.
Late interaction (MaxSim)
Score a page by letting every query token find its best-matching page vector, then summing those best matches. Introduced for text by ColBERT; see Late Interaction & Learned Sparse.
PaliGemma-3B
An open vision-language model of about three billion parameters that feeds image patch embeddings from a SigLIP vision encoder into a Gemma-2B language model. ColPali is a fine-tune of it.
Token pooling
Merging similar vectors in a multi-vector representation to shrink the index.

Half of the contribution: the benchmark

The paper's first claim is that text retrieval benchmarks (the authors cite MS MARCO, BEIR and MTEB) judge embedding models on text passages, and so say nothing about whether a system can find the right page when the answer is in a chart. ViDoRe is their response. It has ten tasks, five repurposed from existing question-answering datasets and five built for the paper. Candidate pools are deliberately small so that slow systems, such as captioning pipelines that "can take dozens of seconds per page", can be evaluated at all.

How the queries were made matters. For the five academic tasks, the question of each existing question-answer pair becomes the query and its page becomes the one relevant document. For the five practical tasks, the authors crawled about 100 public PDF documents per topic and had Claude-3 Sonnet write up to three questions per page for 10,000 sampled pages per theme, using a prompt that asks for questions "that could be asked by a user without knowing the existence and the content of the corpus". Four volunteer annotators, described in the appendix as "colleagues and collaborators of the authors", then spent about three hours each filtering the questions down to 100 per topic. Each topic keeps 1,000 pages as its candidate pool.

The baselines are meant to represent a normal industrial pipeline. The authors use Unstructured, an off-the-shelf tool, at its highest-resolution settings: layout detection, OCR for non-native PDFs, table reconstruction and by-title chunking. They test three variants: text only; plus OCR on tables, charts and images; and plus captioning, where Claude-3 Sonnet writes a description of each visual element. Chunks are scored with BM25 or with BGE-M3, a multilingual dense embedder, and a page's score is the maximum over its chunks (the authors say they checked that max-pooling beat concatenating the chunks). They also test three contrastive vision-language models that embed whole images with one vector: Jina-CLIP, Nomic Embed Vision and SigLIP.

The other half: the model

ColPali ("ColBERT" + "PaliGemma") adds a linear projection to PaliGemma-3B that maps each output vector, for image tokens and text tokens alike, to 128 dimensions, the size used in ColBERT. A page is rendered as an image and passed through the model; the result is one 128-dimensional vector for each of the 1,024 image patches (plus six text tokens of a fixed prompt, "Describe the image"). A query is passed through the language model alone, giving one vector per query token. The page score is the late-interaction sum

LI(q, d) = ∑i maxj ⟨Eq(i), Ed(j)⟩

where Eq and Ed hold the query and page vectors. Training is contrastive: given a batch of query-page pairs, push each pair's score above the score of the hardest other page in the batch. The training set has 118,695 query-page pairs, of which 63 per cent come from the training splits of public academic datasets and 37 per cent are synthetic (web-crawled PDF pages with Claude-3 Sonnet-written questions, built the same way as the benchmark's practical tasks, with no document shared with the test sets). It is all English by design, so that French results measure zero-shot transfer. The model is trained with low-rank adapters (LoRA, rank and alpha both 32) for one epoch on 8 GPUs with batch size 32 and learning rate 5e-5.

What the paper reports

The main results are nDCG@5 on all ten tasks. The averages of the rows that cover every task, from the paper's Table 2, are below. The text-only rows have no average because the authors did not compute text-only scores on tasks that are purely visual.

Four readings of Table 2 are worth making, all from its numbers.

  • The multi-vector step is the large jump. Fine-tuning SigLIP on the document data lifts the average from 51.4 to 58.6 (BiSigLIP). Putting the image patches through PaliGemma's language model and averaging them into one vector (BiPali) gives 58.8. Keeping one vector per patch and scoring with MaxSim (ColPali) gives 81.3, a gain of 22.5 points. The authors list their own ordering of the factors: task-specific data, pairing a language model with the vision model, and multi-vector embeddings.
  • Adding visual information to the text route helps little in the average. The best text pipeline (parsing plus captioning plus BGE-M3) averages 67.0. The paper observes "little difference" between BM25 and BGE-M3 on these pipelines, which it reads as evidence of a visual-information bottleneck rather than a retriever problem.
  • The gap is widest on visual tasks and narrow on text-dense ones. On ArxivQA (figures) ColPali scores 79.1 against 40.1 for the best text-route baseline; on InfoVQA (infographics) 81.8 against 71.9; on TabFQuAD (tables) 83.9 against 70.8. On the text-dense French Shift Project reports, ColPali scores 73.2 and the best text pipelines score 73.2 (OCR plus BGE-M3) and 73.1 (captioning plus BGE-M3): a tie. The authors note that Shift is "the most text-dense" set when they discuss pooling.
  • On text-centred topics the margins are small. On the Healthcare task the strongest text pipeline (captioning plus BGE-M3) reaches 91.3 and ColPali 94.4, a gain of 3.1 points; on Energy it is 85.9 against 91.0. Most of the average gain comes from the visual tasks.

Cost: time, query latency and bytes

The paper measures three operational properties alongside accuracy. Indexing takes 7.22 seconds per page for the Unstructured pipeline with captioning, against 0.12 for SigLIP and 0.39 for ColPali (appendix Table 5: layout detection 0.81, OCR 2.67, captioning 3.71, encoding 0.03), on an NVIDIA L4 GPU. Query encoding takes about 22 milliseconds for BGE-M3 on 15 tokens and about 30 milliseconds for ColPali's language model, and the late-interaction scoring adds "≈ 1 ms per 1000 pages in the corpus". Storage is where the method pays: 257.5 KB per page for ColPali in float16, against 8.60 KB for BGE-M3 and, for BM25 sparse vectors, 1.56 KB.

The arithmetic reproduces the paper's 257.5 figure exactly, which also shows that its "KB" is 1,024 bytes. A million pages at that size is about 246 GiB before any index structure; a hundred million is about 24 TiB. This is why the paper spends a section on token pooling: merge similar patch vectors and keep fewer. It reports (using hierarchical mean pooling following Clavié et al., 2024, a paper I did not fetch) that a pool factor of 3 removes 66.7 per cent of the vectors and keeps 97.8 per cent of the original performance, and it adds a caution: Shift, "composed of the most text-dense documents", is an outlier that degrades more.

The second toy shows why pooling behaves that way. Pages are synthetic: sparse pages have 6 distinct "facts" (4 noisy patches each) and mostly blank background patches; dense pages have 22 facts and little background. Queries pick two facts from a page. Pages are scored with MaxSim and the vectors are merged with a greedy agglomerative mean pooling that I wrote as a stand-in for the paper's method.

"""Toy: MaxSim page retrieval and token pooling. Synthetic vectors are a stand-in for ColPali patch embeddings."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
D, P, PAGES, FACTS = 64, 96, 300, 3000                  # dims, patches per page, pages, size of the fact vocabulary
unit = lambda v: v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
vocab = unit(rng.normal(size=(FACTS, D)))
blank = unit(rng.normal(size=D))                       # the "white background" patch

def make_page(n_facts):
    ids = rng.choice(FACTS, n_facts, replace=False)    # which facts this page shows
    content = np.repeat(vocab[ids], 4, axis=0)         # each fact covers 4 patches
    content = unit(content + 0.9 / np.sqrt(D) * rng.normal(size=content.shape))
    back = unit(blank + 0.3 / np.sqrt(D) * rng.normal(size=(P - len(content), D)))
    return ids, unit(np.vstack([content, back]))

def pool(E, factor):                                   # greedy agglomerative mean pooling down to len(E)/factor vectors
    cl = [[i] for i in range(len(E))]; cen = E.copy()
    while len(cl) > max(1, len(E) // factor):
        S = unit(cen) @ unit(cen).T; np.fill_diagonal(S, -9)
        a, b = np.unravel_index(S.argmax(), S.shape)
        cl[a] += cl[b]; cen[a] = E[cl[a]].mean(0); del cl[b]; cen = np.delete(cen, b, axis=0)
    return cen

def run(n_facts, factors=(1, 2, 3, 5, 8)):
    pages = [make_page(n_facts) for _ in range(PAGES)]
    qs = []
    for g in range(PAGES):                             # query: 2 facts from page g, noisy
        ids = rng.choice(pages[g][0], 2, replace=False)
        qs.append((g, unit(vocab[ids] + 1.6 / np.sqrt(D) * rng.normal(size=(2, D)))))
    out = []
    for f in factors:
        E = [pool(p[1], f) if f > 1 else p[1] for p in pages]
        nd = []
        for g, q in qs:
            s = np.array([(q @ e.T).max(1).sum() for e in E])      # MaxSim: sum over query tokens of best patch
            rank = int((s > s[g]).sum()) + 1
            nd.append(1 / np.log2(rank + 1) if rank <= 5 else 0.0)  # nDCG@5 with one relevant page
        out.append((f, len(E[0]), 100 * np.mean(nd)))
    return out

sparse, dense = run(6), run(22)
print(f"{'pool':>4} {'vectors/page':>12} | {'sparse page (6 facts)':>22} | {'dense page (22 facts)':>22}")
for (f, n, a), (_, _, b) in zip(sparse, dense):
    print(f"{f:>4} {n:>12} | {a:>10.1f} ({100*a/sparse[0][2]:>5.1f}%) | {b:>10.1f} ({100*b/dense[0][2]:>5.1f}%)")
pool vectors/page |  sparse page (6 facts) |  dense page (22 facts)
   1           96 |       95.4 (100.0%) |       84.8 (100.0%)
   2           48 |       95.5 (100.1%) |       80.7 ( 95.1%)
   3           32 |       95.5 (100.1%) |       87.4 (103.0%)
   5           19 |       95.3 ( 99.9%) |       75.2 ( 88.6%)
   8           12 |       95.4 (100.1%) |       40.4 ( 47.7%)

On sparse pages, 96 vectors can be merged down to 12 and the score stays at about 95, because the discarded redundancy is background. On dense pages almost every patch carries something, so merging starts to combine different facts: the score falls from 84.8 to 40.4 at a pool factor of 8, and is already below 90 per cent of baseline at a factor of 5. The jump at a factor of 3 (87.4, above the baseline) is noise in a 300-query toy. The paper's real result is the 97.8 per cent at factor 3 and the Shift outlier; my toy only shows why an outlier like Shift should exist.

What the ablations say

The paper's ablation section is more informative than a single headline number, and it includes two reports that cut against the thesis.

  • Resolution is not free. A variant with half the patches (512) scores 24.8 nDCG@5 points lower on average. A different backbone, Idefics2-8B with only 64 patches after a resampler, beats the 512-patch ColPali by 20.1 points but is 4.7 below the 1,024-patch ColPali and "about twice as slow" in training and inference. The authors summarise a trade-off between accuracy, latency and index size.
  • The backbone matters. Training the same recipe on Qwen2-VL 2B with 768 patches (ColQwen2-VL) improves on ColPali by 5.3 points. The authors read this as better generative models giving better retrievers.
  • Negative results are reported. A late-interaction version of SigLIP (ColSigLIP) gave "abysmal" performance, which the authors attribute to SigLIP's pretraining only using a pooled vector in its loss. A mixed SigLIP and PaliGemma encoder did worse than vanilla SigLIP.
  • Small details move scores. Removing the five ColBERT-style query-augmentation tokens made no significant English difference but improved the French tasks (+9.8 on Shift, +6.3 on TabFQuAD). Using a standard in-batch negative loss instead of the hardest-negative pairwise loss cost 1.6 points, and unfreezing the vision encoder cost 0.7.
  • Fine-tuning is cheap. Adding 1,552 French table examples (the only French data in training) improved TabFQuAD by 2.6 nDCG@5 and Recall@1 by 5, with a 0.4-point gain overall. Training only on a disjoint dataset, DocMatix, lowered the average by 2.2 and still beat the closest baseline by over 12 points, which the authors use to argue the results are not an artefact of training on the benchmark's own training splits.

What the critics and the successors say

The most pointed assessment of ViDoRe comes from its own authors' successor papers. The V2 paper (Macé, Loison and Faysse, May 2025; abstract read) says V1 was "approaching saturation with top models exceeding 90% nDCG@5", which made it hard to tell whether models were improving, and it presents four new multilingual datasets as a harder replacement. The V3 paper (Loison et al., January 2026, revised April 2026; abstract read) moves from page retrieval to retrieval-augmented generation: 10 datasets, about 26,000 pages and 3,099 human-verified queries, each in 6 languages, with, it reports, about 12,000 hours of human annotation. Its stated findings are that visual retrievers beat text-based ones, that late-interaction models and text reranking help, that hybrid or purely visual contexts can improve answers, and that models still struggle with non-text elements, open-ended queries and fine-grained visual grounding.

Three limits are worth keeping in mind when you read the original numbers, and the first two are visible in the paper's own text:

  • Small pools. Most ViDoRe tasks have 500 to 1,600 candidate pages, chosen so that slow systems can be evaluated. A page ranked in the top 5 of 1,000 is a weaker result than one ranked in the top 5 of 10 million. The paper does not claim otherwise; it points to optimised late-interaction engines for millions of documents.
  • Machine-written queries, friendly annotators. The practical tasks use questions generated by a language model looking at the page, filtered by four volunteers who were colleagues of the authors. That is reasonable for a first benchmark and also means the questions are written to be answerable from one page. One search-result summary of the V3 paper (which I did not verify against the full text) says its annotators wrote queries without seeing the document content, to reduce bias toward simple extractive questions.
  • Overlapping authorship. Faysse is an author of the ColPali paper, V2 and V3; Viaud and Hudelot of the ColPali and V3 papers; Macé and Loison of V2 and V3. That is normal for a benchmark's maintainers, but a model built by the benchmark's authors is not independent confirmation. The paper releases data, code, models and the leaderboard under open licences, which is what lets others check.

State of play, October 2026

The ViDoRe leaderboard on Hugging Face and the benchmark's move into MTEB (reported in the search summaries I saw, not verified by me on the live pages) mean the original ColPali is no longer near the top: newer models in the same family, such as ColQwen-style models on larger backbones, and models from other labs, appear on V2 and V3. I am deliberately quoting no current ranking, because it changes with each submission and because different community pages quote different metrics and subsets. If you are choosing a model, read the live leaderboard, note which version of ViDoRe and which metric it uses (V1 used nDCG@5; leaderboard summaries I saw for V3 quote nDCG@10), and then test on your own pages.

Lessons

  • Remove stages only where the stage was the loss. The text route loses charts and tables; the image route loses exact text. Chapter 21 shows a toy where an identifier that differs by two digits scores almost the same as the right one. Use the image route for visual content, keep a lexical route for identifiers, and fuse them with Hybrid Search & Rank Fusion.
  • Count bytes before you commit. About 257.5 KiB per page is a design constraint, not a footnote. Plan pooling, quantisation or an approximate index from chapter 11 early; the companion case FAISS: Billion-Scale Similarity Search shows what compression buys at scale.
  • Build a benchmark that matches your documents. ViDoRe is a shortlist filter, in the sense of Public Benchmarks & Their Limits. Your own queries and judgements, built as in Building a Test Collection, are what decides.
  • Ask what the retrieved page is for. A page that matches is only the first half of an answer; chapters 16 and 17 cover what happens next. Studio 3, Design a Document-Processing Pipeline, asks you to choose between the routes.

Common misconception

"ColPali does OCR, so it replaces OCR." The paper shows that ColPali's late-interaction heatmaps can light up the right words (for the query token "hour", the words "hourly" and "hours"), and calls this "strong OCR capabilities". That is the model using text it can see to rank pages. It does not output a transcript. If you need the text itself (to quote, to cite, to extract a value, to send to a language model that cannot see images), you still need a transcription step or a vision-language model that writes one.

Key takeaways

  • ColPali embeds each page as about a thousand patch vectors and scores pages with MaxSim; it needs no layout detection, OCR or captioning at index time.
  • On the paper's ViDoRe benchmark it averages 81.3 nDCG@5 against 67.0 for the best text pipeline compared, indexing 18 times faster per page, at about 30 times the bytes per page.
  • The gain over single-vector models comes mostly from keeping one vector per patch, not from the image encoder alone.
  • On text-dense pages the advantage disappears: the paper's Shift Project result is a tie.
  • V1 saturated; V2 and V3 exist because the authors themselves found it too easy. Test on your own documents.

Check your understanding

In the paper's Table 2, BiPali (one averaged vector per page from PaliGemma) scores about the same as BiSigLIP, while ColPali scores about 22 points higher than BiPali. What does this suggest about where ColPali's gain comes from?

  1. From the larger language model alone
  2. From training on 118,695 pairs rather than fewer
  3. From keeping one vector per patch and scoring with late interaction
  4. From the choice of the Claude-3 Sonnet query generator
BiPali and ColPali share the backbone, the training data and the loss family; they differ in whether the patch vectors are averaged into one or kept and matched with MaxSim. The training set and generator are the same for both, so they cannot explain the difference between them.

A team indexes 2 million pages with float16 ColPali-style vectors (1,030 vectors of 128 dimensions per page). Roughly how much memory do the raw vectors need?

  1. About 490 GiB
  2. About 16 GiB
  3. About 4 TiB
  4. About 49 GiB
1,030 × 128 × 2 bytes = 263,680 bytes, about 257.5 KiB per page, so 2 million pages need about 490 GiB. Pooling by a factor of 3 would cut that to roughly 163 GiB at the cost the paper reports. The other figures come from forgetting the vector count per page or the byte width.

On the Shift Project task (text-dense French reports) ColPali scored 73.2. Which conclusion does that support?

  1. ColPali fails on French because it was trained only on English
  2. OCR pipelines are always better on text-heavy pages
  3. The Shift result proves token pooling is harmless
  4. On pages that are mostly text, the best text pipelines in the paper (73.2 and 73.1) matched ColPali, so its advantage is concentrated on visual content
The tie is the evidence. It does not show failure on French (ColPali matched the best French text pipeline despite English-only training), it does not show that OCR always wins, and the paper actually notes Shift is where pooling hurts most.

Why does the ViDoRe V2 paper's statement that V1 was "approaching saturation with top models exceeding 90% nDCG@5" matter to someone choosing a model?

  1. It shows ColPali is no longer useful
  2. When most models score near the ceiling, small differences on that benchmark carry little information about which will do best on harder or different documents
  3. It proves the V1 queries were wrong
  4. It means nDCG@5 should not be used
Saturation reduces a benchmark's power to discriminate. It does not mean the model is useless or the queries invalid, and nDCG@5 is still a sensible metric. It is a reason to prefer harder benchmarks and your own test set.
