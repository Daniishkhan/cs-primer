The Chatbot's Promise: Air Canada

In November 2022 a passenger asked an airline's website chatbot how bereavement fares work, followed its answer, and was later told by the airline that the answer was wrong. In February 2024 a British Columbia tribunal ruled that the airline was responsible for what its chatbot had said. The sum at stake was small, about C$812. The reasoning is the part worth studying, because it settles a question every team shipping an answering system will face: when the system makes a statement, whose statement is it?

What I could and could not read

I could not read the decision itself. The official text of Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, on the Civil Resolution Tribunal's site and on CanLII returned "403 Forbidden" to every fetch. I read five secondary accounts that quote the decision: a McCarthy Tétrault legal blog post, a Barry Sookman legal blog post (16 February 2024), a Mondaq article (23 February 2024), a Daily Hive news report (15 February 2024) and a Travel Market Report legal analysis (22 February 2024), and I saw a further set of search results that agree on the headline facts. I name which source supplies each figure. A few details, including the exact chatbot wording and the fare amounts, differ slightly between them, and I flag the differences. Nothing public that I read describes how Air Canada's chatbot was built, so I make no claim that it used retrieval at all. This page uses the case to illustrate grounding; it is not a post-mortem of Air Canada's system.

The pattern in one sentence

If your system tells a customer something, the organisation will be held to it, so an answer that cannot be traced to the organisation's own current policy text should not reach the customer as an assertion.

Context: what happened

The facts below come from the secondary sources and agree across them unless noted.

  • The booking. Jake Moffatt's grandmother died on 11 November 2022 (Daily Hive). He consulted the chatbot on Air Canada's website about bereavement fares, which are reduced fares for travel after a death in the family. According to the sources, the chatbot told him he could book now and apply for the reduced fare afterwards. The screenshot quoted in coverage has the bot saying to submit the ticket for a reduced bereavement rate "within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued" (Daily Hive; Travel Market Report). He booked Vancouver to Toronto on 11 November for $794.98 and the return on 16 November for $845.38 (Travel Market Report; Daily Hive says the return was about a week later).
  • The page the bot linked to said otherwise. The chatbot's text linked to Air Canada's "Bereavement travel" page. That page said the bereavement policy "does not apply to requests for bereavement consideration after travel has been completed" (Travel Market Report quoting the decision).
  • The refusal and the admission. Mr Moffatt applied on 17 November (Daily Hive), within the window the bot had given and with a death certificate (Mondaq), and was refused because retroactive claims were not allowed. On 8 February 2023 an airline representative told him the chatbot had used "misleading words" and that it would be updated (Mondaq; Travel Market Report).
  • The claim. He claimed the difference between the fare he paid and the bereavement fare, which Daily Hive gives as $880, in the Civil Resolution Tribunal (CRT), British Columbia's online tribunal for small claims (the Canadian equivalent, in the words of one source, of small claims court).
Negligent misrepresentation
In the tribunal's reasoning (as reported), a careless false statement on which someone reasonably relies and so suffers a loss.
Duty of care
An obligation to take reasonable care not to cause foreseeable harm, here by giving accurate information.
Grounded answer
An answer each claim of which can be traced to a source text the system actually retrieved (chapter 16).
Source of truth
The one document an organisation treats as authoritative for a rule, such as the bereavement policy page.
Abstention
Declining to answer, or handing over to a human, when the evidence does not support an answer.

What Air Canada argued, and what the tribunal said

According to the sources, Air Canada argued that Mr Moffatt had not followed the proper procedure for bereavement fares, that retroactive claims were not allowed, and that it could not be held liable for information provided by the chatbot. The tribunal described one version of that argument, as the airline's suggestion that the chatbot "is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions" (Mondaq quoting the decision). Daily Hive reports the tribunal calling it "a remarkable submission". Air Canada also pointed to the correct policy on the linked page, to the terms of its Domestic Tariff and to a $200 coupon it had offered as a goodwill gesture; sources say the tribunal rejected the tariff defence because the airline did not supply the tariff's actual language, and rejected the attempt to set off the coupon (Travel Market Report).

The tribunal's findings, as reported by the sources:

  • Duty of care. Air Canada owed a duty of care "given their commercial relationship as a service provider and consumer", and the standard "requires a company to take reasonable care to ensure their representations are accurate" (McCarthy, quoting the decision).
  • The chatbot is part of the website. Quoted by the Sookman blog: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website," whether from a static page or a chatbot.
  • Breach. "I find Air Canada did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate" (Sookman; Mondaq).
  • Reliance. The tribunal found Mr Moffatt reasonably relied on the chatbot and would not have flown at short notice had he known he would pay the full fare (McCarthy).

The outcome

The tribunal ordered damages of $650.88, which Mondaq describes as the difference between the fare paid and the bereavement fare, plus $36.14 in pre-judgment interest and $125 in tribunal fees, a total of $812.02 (Mondaq and Daily Hive give the same three figures; the sum is my arithmetic). Travel Market Report adds that the tribunal found he should have paid $979.48 for the flights rather than the $1,630.36 he paid, and 1,630.36 minus 979.48 is indeed 650.88. The two flight prices that the same source lists total $1,640.36, ten dollars more than $1,630.36; I could not resolve that difference without the decision, so I rely on the damages figure, which three sources agree on. Daily Hive reports an Air Canada spokesperson saying the airline would comply and regarded the matter as closed.

What the case does and does not establish

  • It is a small-claims-level tribunal decision. The CRT is not a superior court, and one search summary I read stressed it is not a court ruling. As I understand it, a CRT decision does not bind other courts, so it is persuasive evidence of how a reasoning could go, not settled law for Canada or elsewhere. I did not research the legal status further.
  • The reasoning is ordinary, not special to AI. The tribunal applied negligent misrepresentation to a statement on a commercial website. The AI part was the airline's defence that the bot was a separate entity, which the tribunal rejected.
  • Several details were helped by what the airline did not do. Daily Hive reports that Air Canada gave no counter-evidence on what the bereavement fare would have cost, which left the claimant's figures standing. The sources do not tell us what a fuller defence would have looked like.
  • Which part of the system failed is not public. Possibilities include a model answering from its general training, a retrieval step missing the policy page, a stale document, or a model overriding the page. The sources do not say, and a good engineer would not guess in a report.

A toy you can run

The toy is mine and shows one defence, not the one Air Canada did or did not have. It takes a policy text and two scripted model drafts (via the shared stand-in model from lab-support/llm.py; neither is a real model or Air Canada's system). A draft is a JSON object with an answer and the exact quotes from the policy that support it. The gate refuses to send any draft whose quotes are not found verbatim in the policy, which is the quote-checking idea of chapter 16 and lab 5. The policy text and drafts are invented to resemble the case.

"""Toy: a quote-or-refuse gate between a model's draft and the customer.
Policy text and both drafts are INVENTED by me for illustration (the policy sentence paraphrases
the one the tribunal quoted). The models are scripted stand-ins, not Air Canada's system."""
import json
from llm import ScriptedModel, text_of

POLICY = ("Bereavement travel. Reduced fares are available for travel because of an imminent death or a death "
          "in the immediate family. The bereavement policy does not apply to requests for bereavement "
          "consideration after travel has been completed.")

drafts = {
 "ungrounded": {"answer": "You can book now and apply for the reduced fare afterwards, within 90 days of the ticket date.",
                "quotes": ["apply within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued"]},
 "grounded":   {"answer": "Reduced fares must be requested before you travel; they cannot be claimed afterwards.",
                "quotes": ["does not apply to requests for bereavement consideration after travel has been completed"]},
}
def gate(draft_json, source):
    d = json.loads(draft_json)
    missing = [q for q in d["quotes"] if q not in source]
    if not d["quotes"] or missing:
        return "REFUSE and hand over to a human. Unsupported quote(s): %s" % missing
    return "SEND: " + d["answer"]

for name, d in drafts.items():
    model = ScriptedModel(lambda m, t, s, d=d: json.dumps(d))        # stand-in for a real model call
    reply = text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": "Can I get a bereavement fare after I fly?"}], system=POLICY))
    print("%-10s -> %s" % (name, gate(reply, POLICY)))

# What the gate cannot catch: a real quote used to support a wrong claim.
sneaky = json.dumps({"answer": "You can claim afterwards.", "quotes": ["Reduced fares are available for travel"]})
print("%-10s -> %s" % ("misused", gate(sneaky, POLICY)))
ungrounded -> REFUSE and hand over to a human. Unsupported quote(s): ['apply within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued']
grounded   -> SEND: Reduced fares must be requested before you travel; they cannot be claimed afterwards.
misused    -> SEND: You can claim afterwards.

Reading the output: the ungrounded draft cites a sentence, "apply within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued", that the policy does not contain, so the gate refuses and hands over to a human; the grounded draft quotes the real sentence and is sent. The third line is the limitation to remember. A real quote can be attached to a wrong claim, and a verbatim-quote check cannot tell. It stops invented sources, not misreadings. That needs a check that the cited span entails the claim, which is an entailment or judge step with its own error rate (grounding and abstention), and sampling by humans.

Try it yourself

Make a third scripted draft that quotes the policy's real first sentence ("Reduced fares are available for travel because of an imminent death...") while telling the customer they can claim afterwards. Confirm the gate sends it. Then add a rule that every answer containing a number of days, an amount or a date must have that exact number in a quoted span, and see which of your drafts it catches. State of play, October 2026

I did not research legislation or later cases, so I make no claim that other courts or regulators have followed this reasoning, and I did not check whether Air Canada still runs a chatbot or how it is built. Governance and regulatory expectations for AI systems move quickly; the dated treatment is in governance in the evals course. The enduring engineering point does not depend on the law: a customer-facing assertion needs a traceable source and a policy for when there is none.

The lessons

  • One source of truth, and the answer comes from it. The contradiction here was between two statements on one site. Retrieval of the current policy page, and quotation from it, makes the page the authority (chapter 13, chapter 16).
  • Design for the absence of evidence. If the policy text is not retrieved, or does not cover the case, the right output is a hand-over, not a plausible sentence. Abstention is a feature to test, not a failure (grounding and abstention).
  • Test the failures that cost money. Policy questions about refunds, deadlines and eligibility deserve their own evaluation set, with expected quotes, and an owner (how RAG fails).
  • Treat stale and conflicting sources as incidents. A staff member acknowledged the answer was misleading in February 2023; a feedback route from complaints to the bot's owners closes that loop (AI incidents).
  • Do not rely on disclaimers. The "check the linked page" and "separate entity" arguments did not move the tribunal, as reported. Product wording that tells people to double-check is not a substitute for accuracy.

Key takeaways

  • On 14 February 2024 the BC Civil Resolution Tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's wrong statement about bereavement fares, awarding about C$812 in total.
  • The tribunal, as reported, rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate legal entity and held that the airline is responsible for all information on its website.
  • The primary decision could not be fetched here; the account rests on five secondary sources that quote it.
  • A grounding gate that checks quotes against the policy blocks invented support but not a real quote used for a wrong claim.

The toy gate accepts any draft whose quotes appear verbatim in the policy. What can still get through?

  1. A draft with no quotes
  2. A draft quoting a sentence that is not in the policy
  3. A draft that cites two quotes
  4. A draft quoting a real sentence but drawing a conclusion the sentence does not support
The check tests that the quote exists, not that it entails the claim. Missing or invented quotes are refused; misuse of a real quote is not, which is why entailment checks and sampling are needed.

The tribunal, as reported, did not accept the idea that the customer should have checked another page. Grounding with abstention addresses the cause; disclaimers and model size do not remove the liability.
