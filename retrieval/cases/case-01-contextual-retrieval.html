Contextual Retrieval

A chunk that says "revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter" is easy for a person reading the whole report and nearly useless to a search index that sees only the chunk. In September 2024 Anthropic published "Introducing Contextual Retrieval", which fixes this by having a model write a short situating note for every chunk and prepending it before the chunk is embedded and indexed. The post reports how much each step (context, hybrid search, reranking) lowered the rate at which the right chunk was missing from the top 20.

The pattern in one sentence

A chunk is indexed as the words it contains, so put the missing "which document, which section, which period" into the chunk's text before you embed and index it, then stack hybrid search and a reranker on top and measure each layer.

Context: the orphan chunk

A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system cuts documents into chunks, indexes the chunks, and at question time pulls the best few into the prompt. Chapter 14, Chunking & Contextual Retrieval, explains why documents are cut and the cost: a chunk is read without the pages around it. Anthropic's post says traditional RAG chunks are usually no more than a few hundred tokens and that cutting them apart can destroy context. Its worked example is a chunk reading "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter." The chunk names neither the company nor the quarter, so a question such as "What was ACME Corp's revenue growth in Q2 2023?" has nothing in the chunk to match on, lexically or semantically. The post's fix adds the missing facts (ACME Corp, Q2 2023, the previous quarter's revenue of $314 million) to the chunk text.

Chunk
A passage cut from a document, the unit that is embedded, indexed and retrieved.
Contextual chunk
A chunk with a short, model-written note prepended that situates it within its document.
Contextual Embeddings / Contextual BM25
The post's names for embedding, or building the BM25 index over, the contextual chunk instead of the bare one.
Rank fusion
Merging the ranked lists of two retrievers into one list (chapter 10 covers reciprocal rank fusion).
Reranker
A slower model that reads the query and each candidate together and re-scores a short list.
Retrieval failure rate (top 20)
The post's metric: 1 minus recall@20, the share of relevant chunks that did not appear in the top 20 retrieved.

The mechanism, step by step

Step 1: ask a model to situate each chunk

For every chunk, the post says, the whole document goes into a prompt inside <document> tags and the chunk inside <chunk> tags, followed by the instruction "Please give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within the overall document for the purposes of improving search retrieval of the chunk," and a request to answer with only that context. The post used Claude 3 Haiku for this and says the output is typically 50 to 100 tokens. The note is prepended to the chunk, and the combined text is what gets indexed.

The obvious worry is cost: the document is sent once per chunk. The post's answer is prompt caching (the provider stores the processed document prefix so later calls that reuse it are cheaper). Under stated assumptions (800-token chunks, 8,000-token documents, 50 tokens of instruction, 100 tokens of context per chunk) it puts the one-time cost of contextualising a corpus at $1.02 per million document tokens. To see where the cost comes from without any prices: under those assumptions an 8,000-token document yields ten 800-token chunks, so ten calls each carry the document plus a chunk and the instruction, about 8,850 input tokens. Without caching that is roughly 88,500 input tokens to contextualise 8,000 tokens of document, about eleven times the document's size (my arithmetic). Caching makes the repeated 8,000-token prefix cheap to re-read, which is what brings the bill down to the post's figure. That is a price from September 2024 and an indexing-time cost: it is paid once per chunk, not per query. See the KV cache chapter for why a reused prefix is cheap to serve.

Step 2: index the contextual text twice

The same contextual chunk feeds two indexes. Contextual Embeddings embed it with a dense model (chapter 7), so the vector now encodes the company and period as well as the sentence. Contextual BM25 builds the lexical index (chapter 2) over the same text, so "ACME" and "Q2" are now terms the chunk contains. Lexical search matters here because exact tokens such as names, identifiers and error codes are precisely what the context adds and what embeddings tend to blur. At query time both indexes are searched and their results merged and de-duplicated with rank fusion, as in chapter 10.

Step 3: rerank a longer list down to a shorter one

For the final configuration the post retrieves the top 150 chunks, passes them with the query to a reranker, keeps the top 20 and sends those to the generating model. The reranker in the experiments was Cohere's; the post notes that Voyage also offers one, which was not tested. A reranker reads query and chunk together, which is more accurate than comparing two independent vectors and much slower, so it runs on a short list (chapter 8). The post is explicit about the price: reranking adds latency and cost at query time, so there is a trade-off between considering more chunks and answering quickly.

The results, and how to read them

The post evaluates on several datasets (it names codebases, fiction, ArXiv papers and science papers) with the metric 1 minus recall@20: the share of relevant chunks that were not in the 20 retrieved. Lower is better. For the best embedding setup in its main charts, Gemini Text 004, it reports these top-20 failure rates:

The last column is my arithmetic from the post's rates. It shows where the gain came from: the context step removed the most, and each later step removed a smaller absolute amount from a smaller pool. The post also states that the improvement held across every embedding model combination it tried, and that Voyage and Gemini embeddings were the most effective overall.

What else the post tested

  • How many chunks to pass. It compared 5, 10 and 20 chunks and found 20 best, while warning that more chunks can also distract the generating model.
  • Cheaper context. Adding a generic document summary to each chunk gave "very limited gains" in its words, and hypothetical document embeddings and summary-based indexing performed poorly. The useful context was specific to the chunk.
  • When not to bother. For a knowledge base under about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages) the post says you can put the whole thing in the prompt and skip retrieval, using prompt caching to keep that cheap. Long context versus retrieval and the Lost in the Middle case study explain why this is not free of problems.

A toy you can run

The toy below is mine, not Anthropic's. It builds twelve chunks from four invented quarterly reports whose "Revenue", "Costs" and "Outlook" paragraphs read alike, then asks six questions that each have one right chunk. It ranks with a hand-written BM25, with a hashed character-trigram "embedder" (a deterministic stand-in for a trained embedding model, as in chapter 7) and with the two fused by reciprocal rank fusion. The "context" is the document title plus section heading, a rule standing in for the model call. Six queries is far too few to estimate a failure rate; the point is to see the mechanism.

"""Toy contextual retrieval: BM25 + hashed n-gram 'embedder' + RRF, with and without chunk context.
The context here is built by a rule (document title + section heading): a stand-in for the LLM call."""
import math, re
from collections import Counter

DOCS = {
 "ACME Corp, Q2 2023 results": {
   "Revenue": "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter. Subscriptions drove most of the change.",
   "Costs": "Operating costs rose by 5% because of new hires. Hosting spend was flat.",
   "Outlook": "Management expects growth to continue next quarter. No change to guidance."},
 "ACME Corp, Q3 2023 results": {
   "Revenue": "The company's revenue grew by 8% over the previous quarter. A large contract closed early.",
   "Costs": "Operating costs rose by 2% as hiring slowed. Hosting spend fell.",
   "Outlook": "Management raised guidance for the year. Growth is expected to continue."},
 "Borealis Ltd, Q2 2023 results": {
   "Revenue": "The company's revenue fell by 4% over the previous quarter. Retail demand was weak.",
   "Costs": "Operating costs rose by 1% because of freight. Hosting spend was flat.",
   "Outlook": "Management expects growth to resume next quarter. Guidance is unchanged."},
 "Borealis Ltd, Q3 2023 results": {
   "Revenue": "The company's revenue grew by 6% over the previous quarter. Retail demand recovered.",
   "Costs": "Operating costs fell by 3% as freight eased. Hosting spend was flat.",
   "Outlook": "Management raised guidance. Growth is expected to continue."},
}
chunks = []  # (id, title, section, text)
for title, secs in DOCS.items():
    for sec, txt in secs.items():
        chunks.append((len(chunks), title, sec, txt))

# query -> (title, section) of the one relevant chunk
QUERIES = [
 ("ACME Corp Q2 2023 revenue growth over previous quarter", ("ACME Corp, Q2 2023 results", "Revenue")),
 ("ACME Corp Q3 2023 operating costs hiring", ("ACME Corp, Q3 2023 results", "Costs")),
 ("Borealis Q2 2023 revenue previous quarter", ("Borealis Ltd, Q2 2023 results", "Revenue")),
 ("Borealis Q3 2023 guidance raised", ("Borealis Ltd, Q3 2023 results", "Outlook")),
 ("ACME Q2 2023 management outlook next quarter", ("ACME Corp, Q2 2023 results", "Outlook")),
 ("Borealis Q3 2023 operating costs freight", ("Borealis Ltd, Q3 2023 results", "Costs")),
]

def tok(s): return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())

def bm25_rank(docs, q, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    toks = [tok(d) for d in docs]; N = len(toks); avg = sum(map(len, toks)) / N
    df = Counter(t for d in toks for t in set(d)); qs = tok(q); sc = []
    for d in toks:
        tf = Counter(d); s = 0.0
        for t in qs:
            if t in tf:
                idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + .5) / (df[t] + .5))
                s += idf * tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(d) / avg))
        sc.append(s)
    return sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: (-sc[i], i))

def ngram_vec(s, n=3, dim=512):
    v = [0.0] * dim; s = " " + " ".join(tok(s)) + " "
    for i in range(len(s) - n + 1):
        v[hash_(s[i:i+n]) % dim] += 1
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(x*x for x in v)) or 1
    return [x / norm for x in v]
def hash_(g):  # deterministic (Python's hash() is salted per run)
    h = 2166136261
    for c in g: h = ((h ^ ord(c)) * 16777619) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    return h
def dense_rank(docs, q):
    qv = ngram_vec(q); sc = [sum(a*b for a, b in zip(qv, ngram_vec(d))) for d in docs]
    return sorted(range(len(docs)), key=lambda i: (-sc[i], i))

def rrf(*ranks, k=60):
    s = Counter()
    for r in ranks:
        for pos, i in enumerate(r): s[i] += 1 / (k + pos + 1)
    return sorted(s, key=lambda i: (-s[i], i))

def gold_of(t, s): return next(c[0] for c in chunks if c[1] == t and c[2] == s)

def run(texts, label):
    print(label)
    for name, fn in (("BM25", lambda q: bm25_rank(texts, q)), ("dense", lambda q: dense_rank(texts, q)),
                     ("hybrid", lambda q: rrf(bm25_rank(texts, q), dense_rank(texts, q)))):
        ranks = [fn(q).index(gold_of(*g)) + 1 for q, g in QUERIES]
        f1 = sum(r > 1 for r in ranks) / len(ranks); f3 = sum(r > 3 for r in ranks) / len(ranks)
        print("  %-7s gold ranks %s | miss@1 %3.0f%% | miss@3 %3.0f%%" % (name, ranks, 100 * f1, 100 * f3))

print("chunks:", len(chunks), "| queries:", len(QUERIES), "| 'miss@k' = gold chunk not in the top k")
run([c[3] for c in chunks], "bare chunks")
run(["%s. %s. %s" % (c[1], c[2], c[3]) for c in chunks], "with prepended context")
print()
print("bare chunk 0     :", chunks[0][3])
print("contextual chunk0:", "%s. %s. %s" % (chunks[0][1], chunks[0][2], chunks[0][3]))
chunks: 12 | queries: 6 | 'miss@k' = gold chunk not in the top k
bare chunks
  BM25    gold ranks [4, 1, 2, 1, 2, 2] | miss@1  67% | miss@3  17%
  dense   gold ranks [4, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2] | miss@1  50% | miss@3  17%
  hybrid  gold ranks [4, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2] | miss@1  50% | miss@3  17%
with prepended context
  BM25    gold ranks [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1] | miss@1   0% | miss@3   0%
  dense   gold ranks [2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1] | miss@1  17% | miss@3   0%
  hybrid  gold ranks [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1] | miss@1   0% | miss@3   0%

bare chunk 0     : The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter. Subscriptions drove most of the change.
contextual chunk0: ACME Corp, Q2 2023 results. Revenue. The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter. Subscriptions drove most of the change.

Reading the output: with bare chunks the right chunk is often not first, and for one query ("ACME Corp Q2 2023 revenue growth") it is fourth, because three other chunks match "revenue" and "previous quarter" equally well and the ACME/Q2 words are in no chunk at all. Prepending the title and heading puts those words into the text and the right chunk goes to rank one under BM25 and hybrid. The trigram embedder still misses one rank-one placement, a reminder that dense similarity of short, near-identical sentences is fuzzy and that a lexical leg adds a different kind of evidence. A real contextualiser would also write things the heading does not contain, such as the previous quarter's revenue figure in the post's example.

What the post cautions, and what to be sceptical about

The post lists its own caveats: chunk size, boundaries and overlap affect retrieval; embedding models differ in how much they gain; a custom prompt (it gives a glossary of key terms as an example) may do better in a specific domain; more retrieved chunks help recall but can distract the generator; and reranking adds runtime latency and cost. Its closing advice is "Always run evals" (eval harnesses). I would add the points a careful reader should hold alongside the headline numbers:

  • It is a vendor's own evaluation. The datasets, the chunking, the metric and the baseline were all chosen by the authors. A 67% relative reduction on those sets is a reason to test the technique on your data, not a forecast for it.
  • Recall@20 is a retrieval metric, not an answer metric. It says the right chunk was in the 20, not that the generator used it. Chapters 4 and 17 separate the two.
  • The headline stacks three changes. Context, a lexical leg and a reranker each carry a cost: indexing spend, an extra index, and query-time latency. The post's per-step numbers let you decide which ones earn their place.
  • Context is generated text. A model writes it, so it can be wrong or invent a detail, and an error becomes part of what the chunk "says". Sample some generated notes by eye before indexing a million chunks.

State of play, October 2026

The post is from September 2024, and the models it names (Claude 3 Haiku for context, Gemini Text 004 and Voyage embeddings, a Cohere reranker) are the ones it tested then. I did not verify which are current, and model names and prices change monthly, so treat the technique as the durable part and the model choices and the $1.02 figure as dated. The technique has since become a common option in RAG tooling, but I did not survey that, so I make no claim about adoption.

The lessons

  • Fix the representation before tuning the ranker. The biggest single step in the post was changing what is indexed. Chapter 14 shows the same idea next to parent-child retrieval and structure-aware chunking.
  • Hybrid retrieval is a measurement, not a ritual. The lexical leg removed a further 0.8 points in this evaluation; chapter 10 shows how to fuse and how to check the gain on your own gold set (chapter 5).
  • Move cost to indexing when you can. Context is paid once per chunk; a reranker is paid per query. Put the budget where your traffic and latency target say.
  • Report each layer separately. The post's four-bar progression is the template for an ablation (error analysis): every added component must show what it removed.

Key takeaways

  • Chunks lose their surroundings; prepend a short model-written note (50 to 100 tokens in the post) before embedding and BM25 indexing.
  • Reported top-20 failure rates: 5.7% baseline, 3.7% with context, 2.9% adding contextual BM25, 1.9% adding a reranker over the top 150.
  • The one-time context cost was put at $1.02 per million document tokens under the post's assumptions, using prompt caching (September 2024 price).
  • The numbers are the vendor's, on its datasets. Re-measure on your own queries and judgements.

Check your understanding

Why does prepending a note to a chunk help a BM25 index and not only an embedding index?

  1. BM25 needs every chunk to be longer
  2. The note puts words such as the company and period into the chunk's text, and BM25 can only match words that are present
  3. BM25 ignores document length once a note is added
  4. The note replaces the need for tokenisation
A lexical index matches terms. A bare chunk with no company name cannot match a query that names the company. Length normalisation still applies (option 3 is wrong), and tokenisation is unchanged.

In the post, the top-20 failure rate falls from 5.7% to 1.9% with all three steps. Which statement is correct?

  1. Reranking alone produced the 67% reduction
  2. The 67% is independent evidence from several labs
  3. Each step was measured against the previous step, so the percentages add up to 100%
  4. The percentages are all relative to the 5.7% baseline, and the contextual step removed the most misses in absolute terms
35%, 49% and 67% are cumulative reductions from the 5.7% baseline; the steps removed 2.0, 0.8 and 1.0 points. The results are Anthropic's own evaluation.

A team has 40 million chunks and a strict query latency budget. Which part of the technique costs them per query rather than once?

  1. The reranker over the top 150 candidates
  2. Generating the context note
  3. Prepending the note to the chunk
  4. Building the BM25 index
Context generation and indexing happen at ingest. The reranker runs on every query, which is why the post flags its latency and cost trade-off.

Which result would make you doubt that contextual retrieval is helping on your own corpus?

  1. Generated notes are 80 tokens long
  2. Recall@20 goes up on your gold queries
  3. Recall@20 on your gold queries is unchanged, because your chunks already name their subject
  4. The indexing bill is visible on the invoice
The technique repairs chunks that lack identifying context. If yours already contain it, there is little to repair. Short notes and an indexing bill are expected, and a recall gain would be the intended outcome.
