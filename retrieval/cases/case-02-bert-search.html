BERT Comes to Web Search

For two decades web search ranked pages largely by the words a query shared with them, and small words such as "to", "for" and "no" were the first things a search engine learned to ignore. In October 2019 Google announced that it was using a language model called BERT to read queries as whole sentences, and a month later Microsoft described the same move at Bing, with the engineering needed to afford it. This case follows what each company said, what the claims rest on, and what is not known.

Fetched and read: Google's post "Understanding searches better than ever before" (Pandu Nayak, 25 October 2019); Microsoft's Azure blog post "Bing delivers its largest improvement in search experience using Azure GPUs" (Jeffrey Zhu, 18 November 2019); and the abstract pages of the BERT paper (Devlin et al., 2018) and of "Passage Re-ranking with BERT" (Nogueira and Cho, 2019). I read abstracts only for those two papers, not the full texts, so where I describe how BERT is trained I am relying on general knowledge of the paper and say so. Google's 2020 statement that BERT reached almost every English query is known to me only through a search summary of press coverage (not fetched). A Search Engine Land article returned an error when fetched, and I read Microsoft's post at its Azure blog address rather than the Bing blog copy. Both companies' posts are announcements, not evaluations: neither gives a ranking metric or a before-and-after score for search as a whole. I make no claim about how well BERT works in either engine beyond what they said.

The pattern in one sentence

Treat a query as a sentence to be read, not a bag of keywords to be matched, and then pay for the model that can read it: the language gain and the serving bill arrive together.

Context: the little words

A classic search engine, like the BM25 ranker of chapter 2, scores a page by counting how many of the query's terms it contains and how rare they are. Chapter 1 explains why it also discards stop words, very common words such as "the", "for" and "to" that appear in almost every document and so carry almost no weight as signals. The trade-off is hidden in that choice. In the sentence "a Brazilian traveller to the USA" the word "to" is the only thing that says which country the person is going to.

Google's 2019 post makes the point with examples. Nayak writes that Search "is about understanding language", that 15 percent of the billions of daily searches are ones Google has never seen before, and that people often type "strings of words that they think we'll understand" rather than natural questions. His examples:

  • "2019 brazil traveler to usa need a visa." Results used to show pages about U.S. citizens travelling to Brazil. The post says BERT understands that "to" changes the direction of travel.
  • "parking on a hill with no curb." The old system put too much weight on "curb" and ignored "no", so it returned how to park with a curb.
  • "do estheticians stand a lot at work." The old system matched "stand-alone" because it matched the string "stand"; the intent was how physically demanding the job is.
  • "can you get medicine for someone." The words "for someone" are what make it a question about picking up a prescription for another person.

The same post also shows a failure: for "what state is south of Nebraska," BERT's best guess, Nayak says, was a result about "South Nebraska," and he adds that "Search is not a solved problem". That candour is useful when reading the rest of the claims.

Stop word
A very common word removed before matching because it appears in nearly every document; whether to remove it is a design choice.
Bag of words
Treating a text as a count of its terms with word order and grammar thrown away.
BERT
"Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers", a language model from Google researchers (Devlin et al., 2018) that reads each word in the context of the words on both sides of it.
Pre-training and fine-tuning
First train a model on a large body of text for a general task, then adapt it to a specific job such as ranking.
Inference
Running a trained model on one new input; here, scoring one query against one candidate.

The mechanism

Reading a word with both neighbours

From the BERT paper's abstract, which I read: BERT is pre-trained on unlabelled text so that its representation of each word is conditioned on context to both the left and the right, and the pre-trained model can then be fine-tuned for a task by adding one output layer. Google's post says the same thing in plain language: "BERT models can therefore consider the full context of a word by looking at the words that come before and after it." In general knowledge of the paper (not re-read for this case), the pre-training game is to hide some words in a sentence and predict them from the rest, which forces the model to learn how words depend on each other. The network is a stack of Transformer layers, the attention mechanism of chapter 9 of the deep learning course; in the sentence about Brazil, the representation of "to" depends on "traveler" before it and "usa" after it.

The abstract reports state-of-the-art results on eleven language tasks, including a GLUE score of 80.5% (a 7.7-point absolute improvement), 86.7% on MultiNLI (4.6 points), and a SQuAD v1.1 test F1 of 93.2 (1.5 points). Those are benchmark results on reading tasks, not on web search.

From language understanding to ranking

The bridge to search was made in public by Nogueira and Cho in "Passage Re-ranking with BERT" (first posted 13 January 2019). Their abstract reports a fine-tuned BERT at the top of the MS MARCO passage-retrieval leaderboard, "outperforming the previous state of the art by 27% (relative) in MRR@10" (the metric of chapter 4). Their setup is the cross-encoder of chapter 8: the query and a candidate passage are fed in together, so every query word can attend to every passage word, and the model outputs a relevance score. That construction is why the model can notice "no curb" against "curb". It also explains the cost: the model must run once for every query-document pair that it scores, and it cannot be precomputed.

What neither Google's nor Microsoft's post says is the exact wiring, such as how many candidates the model scores or whether it runs as a re-ranker, a query encoder or both. The posts say what the model understands, not how its score is combined with the engines' other signals. The dense, bi-encoder alternative, which embeds query and document separately, is the subject of chapter 7; it is cheaper per query and less precise on exactly this kind of word-level distinction.

What each company reported

Google, 25 October 2019

The post says BERT would help Search understand "one in 10 searches in the U.S. in English," with more languages and locales to follow, and calls it, in the post's phrase, the "biggest leap forward in the past five years". It also says a BERT model was being used to improve featured snippets in the two dozen countries where they are offered, with notable gains in Korean, Hindi and Portuguese, and that Google used the latest Cloud TPUs (Google's AI accelerators) to serve results for the first time. Learning from English, where most web content exists, could be applied to other languages, according to the post. A year later, according to press coverage of Google's Search On event in October 2020 that I saw only through a search summary, Google said BERT was used in "almost every query in English", up from about 10 percent at launch. I did not read Google's primary statement for that, so treat it as reported.

Microsoft Bing, 18 November 2019

Zhu's post says Bing's use of large Transformer models went into production in April 2019 and was by then applied to every Bing search query globally, and its title calls the result Bing's largest improvement in search experience. Its example is "what can aggravate a concussion", where "aggravate" signals that the user wants things to avoid or do, not causes. The more instructive part is the serving story, because it prices the language gain:

  • A distilled three-layer BERT model benchmarked at 77 ms per inference on twenty CPU cores, the starting point for the work below.
  • On one GPU virtual machine, the unoptimised model took about 20 ms per inference, around three times faster.
  • After optimising with NVIDIA's TensorRT, a batch of four inferences took 9 ms, which the post puts at an 8x latency speed-up and 34x throughput improvement over the non-GPU model.
  • With Tensor Cores and mixed precision, a batch of 64 inferences took 6 ms, a throughput improvement of about 800x compared with the CPU.
  • At global scale, about 2,000 Azure GPU virtual machines across four regions served over one million BERT inferences per second.

Batching is the mechanism worth noticing. A GPU is fastest when it does many identical operations at once (parallelism and GPUs), so scoring 64 candidates together costs hardly more time than scoring one. Re-ranking suits this: a search must score many candidate pages anyway, and they can be batched.

A toy you can run

This toy is mine. It cannot show BERT working, because I did not train one. What it can show is the information that a term-matching ranker discards, using the two ideas from Google's post. Part one builds two documents with the same bag of words and shows that every bag-of-words weighting scores them the same, however you set the BM25 constants. Part two ranks a "with no curb" page against a "with a curb" page, once with a common stop list (which contains "no" and "to") and once keeping every word.

"""Toy: what a bag-of-words ranker throws away. Two queries after Google's 2019 BERT post.
Documents are invented by me. BM25 is a bag-of-words ranker; this is not a model of BERT."""
import math, re
from collections import Counter
STOP = set("a an the for to of in on with from no and or is are do does".split())   # a typical stop list, includes 'no' and 'to'
def tok(s): return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())
def bm25(docs, q, drop_stop, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    f = (lambda ts: [t for t in ts if t not in STOP]) if drop_stop else (lambda ts: ts)
    D = [f(tok(d)) for d in docs]; N = len(D); avg = sum(map(len, D)) / N
    df = Counter(t for d in D for t in set(d)); out = []
    for d in D:
        tf = Counter(d); s = 0.0
        for t in f(tok(q)):
            if t in tf:
                s += math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + .5) / (df[t] + .5)) * tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(d) / avg))
        out.append(round(s, 3))
    return out
def show(title, q, docs):
    print(title, "| query:", q)
    for drop in (True, False):
        print("  BM25, stop words %-7s" % ("removed" if drop else "kept"), bm25(docs, q, drop))
    print()

d0 = "Visa rules for a traveler from the USA to Brazil."
d1 = "Visa rules for a traveler from Brazil to the USA."
print("same bag of words:", Counter(tok(d0)) == Counter(tok(d1)))
show("Direction", "brazil traveler to usa need a visa", [d0, d1])
print("  (the query wants document 1; no bag-of-words weighting can prefer it)\n")
show("Negation", "parking on a hill with no curb", [
 "Parking on a hill: turn the front wheels toward the curb. The curb stops a rolling car.",
 "Parking on a hill with no curb: turn the front wheels toward the road edge."])
print("  (the query wants document 1)")
same bag of words: True
Direction | query: brazil traveler to usa need a visa
  BM25, stop words removed [0.729, 0.729]
  BM25, stop words kept    [1.094, 1.094]

  (the query wants document 1; no bag-of-words weighting can prefer it)

Negation | query: parking on a hill with no curb
  BM25, stop words removed [0.594, 0.57]
  BM25, stop words kept    [1.026, 2.358]

  (the query wants document 1)

In the direction example, the scores are identical (1.094 with stop words kept, 0.729 with them removed): no change to term weighting can prefer document 1, because the two documents are the same multiset of words. In the negation example, stop-word removal leaves the "curb" page ahead (0.594 against 0.57), the wrong order; keeping "no" reverses it (2.358 against 1.026), but only because "no" happens to occur in one page. Keeping stop words is a patch, not a fix: it rewards the presence of the token "no", not its scope, so it cannot tell "a hill with no curb" from "a hill, no parking, near a curb". Distinguishing meaning from presence is the work BERT is for.

Try it yourself

Edit d0 and d1 so the documents use different wording for the same facts ("flying out of the USA into Brazil"). Does BM25 now separate them, and for what reason? Then add a third document containing the exact phrase "from Brazil to the USA" and see how much a phrase-level match is worth compared with a single repeated word. This is the gap that synonyms and phrases (chapter 3) tried to close by hand.

What to be sceptical about

  • No metric was published. "One in 10" says how many searches were affected, not how much results improved. The post's evidence is a handful of hand-picked examples, which, as Nayak's South Nebraska example shows, includes a failure. Whether the median query improved is not in either source.
  • The academic result is on a benchmark, not on the web. The 27% relative MRR@10 gain is on MS MARCO passages, one benchmark with the biases discussed in chapter 6. It supports "BERT-style re-rankers are strong", which is a narrower claim than "web search got better by that amount".
  • Selective use. Google says BERT helped with 1 in 10 searches at launch. The post does not say whether that was by design or a limit of the first deployment. Bing says all queries; neither says how many candidates per query the model scored.
  • Cost shapes design. A model that needs thousands of GPUs, or cannot be afforded at all, ends up shaping what is possible. The Bing post's account of distilling to three layers before anything else is typical: you shrink the model to the budget, not the budget to the model.

State of play, October 2026

This case is from 2019 and 2020 and describes one generation of model. The durable ideas are that queries are language, that term matching discards meaning, and that a model that reads the pair costs one inference per candidate. Which models power web search today, and how much of each ranking they decide, is not something I checked; both companies have said little in detail, and any such claim should be dated and sourced. In retrieval for AI applications the same split reappears as cheap first-stage retrieval followed by an expensive reader (chapters 7 and 8).

The lessons

  • Check what your analyser throws away. Stop-word lists and stemming (chapter 1) are cheap and usually right, and they fail on queries where the small word is the meaning: direction, negation, "for someone". Find those queries in your logs.
  • Read the long tail. Fifteen percent of daily queries were new to Google; the head can be tuned by hand and the tail cannot (chapter 3).
  • Put the expensive reader where there are few candidates. Retrieve broadly and cheaply, then read a short list closely: the two-stage design of chapters 7, 8 and 10.
  • Price inference before you promise a model. Batching, distillation, quantisation and hardware turned a 77 ms CPU call into a service handling a million inferences a second. Budget for that work (tail latency).
  • Be wary of announcements. Ask what was measured, on what, against what baseline; if the answer is "examples", you have a motivation, not a result.

Key takeaways

  • Bag-of-words ranking cannot distinguish documents that share words but differ in order or negation; small words often carry that meaning.
  • BERT reads each word with both neighbours; used as a cross-encoder it scores query and passage together, at one inference per pair.
  • Google (25 October 2019) said BERT helped with one in 10 US English searches; Bing (18 November 2019) said transformers served every query, after taking a 77 ms CPU call to a batched GPU call of 6 ms for 64.
  • Neither post gives an overall relevance metric. Read them as announcements with examples.

Check your understanding

Two documents contain exactly the same words in a different order, and a query matches both. What does a BM25 ranker do?

  1. Prefers the one whose order matches the query
  2. Prefers the shorter one
  3. Gives them the same score, because BM25 sees only term counts and document length
  4. Rejects the query as ambiguous
BM25 uses term frequency, rarity and length. Identical bags of words with equal length score equally; the toy output shows 1.094 for both.

Why does a BERT cross-encoder cost far more per query than a bi-encoder (a separate embedding for each side)?

  1. It must run once per query-document pair, and cannot be precomputed for the documents
  2. It uses more training data
  3. It has to be retrained for each query
  4. It cannot use a GPU
The pair is encoded together, which is what lets query words attend to document words, but it means no document-side vectors can be stored ahead of time.

Google said BERT affected "one in 10" US English searches. What does this tell you about quality?

  1. Quality improved by 10%
  2. Ten percent of results were wrong before
  3. BERT was evaluated on one in ten queries
  4. How many searches were affected, not how much their results improved
The figure is coverage. The post gives no relevance metric, so the size of the improvement is unknown from that source.

In Bing's serving story, which change brought the most dramatic throughput gain, per the post?

  1. Distilling to three layers alone
  2. Moving to GPUs, optimising the model and batching with Tensor Cores and mixed precision, for about 800x versus CPU
  3. Adding more CPU cores
  4. Reducing the number of queries
The post reports 77 ms on CPU, then a sequence of GPU steps ending at 6 ms for a batch of 64, about 800x the CPU throughput. Distillation was already in the 77 ms starting point.
