Open Document Conversion Pipelines

Two open-source projects now turn PDFs into text and structure for language-model pipelines, and they disagree about how. Docling, from IBM Research Zurich, chains small specialised models (a layout detector, a table-structure model, optional OCR) and takes every word from the PDF or from OCR. olmOCR, from the Allen Institute for AI, asks one fine-tuned 7-billion-parameter vision-language model to write the page, helped by the PDF's own text. This case reads both papers, rebuilds the control logic and the evaluation style of the second, and asks what each evaluation actually lets you conclude.

What I could and could not read

I read the full text of four papers after extracting them from PDF: the "Docling Technical Report" (arXiv 2408.09869, version 5, December 2024), "Docling: An Efficient Open-Source Toolkit for AI-driven Document Conversion" (arXiv 2501.17887, January 2025), the olmOCR paper (arXiv 2502.18443, version 3, 2 July 2025, including appendices) and the DocLayNet abstract (arXiv 2206.01062). I fetched the abstract pages of olmOCR 2 (arXiv 2510.19817) and OmniDocBench (arXiv 2412.07626), the olmOCR and Docling GitHub READMEs, and the docling-eval benchmark notes (read as setup only; I did not open its result files). The Granite-Docling model is known to me only from search-result summaries. The primary sources contain no head-to-head comparison of Docling and olmOCR; I did not run either tool, so I make no claim about which converts your documents better. The toys below use scripted stand-ins and hand-made pages.

The pattern in one sentence

Recover a page's structure either with a chain of specialised models that never write text of their own, or with one vision-language model that does write it but is anchored to the file's own text, and in both cases judge the result with checks that match the failures you fear, not with one similarity score.

Context: a PDF stores glyphs, not paragraphs

The olmOCR paper makes the problem concrete. Its Figure 2 shows how a PDF holds the title of the paper itself: as individual characters, each with a position matrix and a font, for example the letter "o" followed by "l", "m" and "O" at slightly different horizontal offsets. There are no words, no headings, no reading order, no table cells. In the authors' words, PDFs "originated as a means to specify how digital documents should be printed onto physical paper" and "lack basic structure necessary for coherent prose, such as ground truth reading order". Chapter 18, PDFs & Layout, explains this in depth; OCR and Tables, Forms & Key-Value Extraction cover the stages. This case is about two complete systems that assemble those stages, and about how their authors measured them.

The conversion matters for two reasons the papers give. Retrieval-augmented generation needs clean text and metadata to chunk and cite (RAG End to End, Chunking). And language-model training needs text: olmOCR's stated motivation is that "PDF documents have the potential to provide trillions of novel, high-quality tokens for training language models".

Linearisation
Flattening a page into a single stream of text in natural reading order, with headings, lists, tables and equations in text form (Markdown, LaTeX). olmOCR's term.
Layout analysis
Predicting the boxes and classes (paragraph, title, table, figure, caption) of a page's elements.
DocLayNet
A human-annotated layout dataset from IBM Research: 80,863 pages from diverse sources, 11 element classes, with part of it annotated two or three times to measure agreement between annotators.
TableFormer
IBM's vision-transformer model that predicts the row and column structure of a table from an image.
Document anchoring
olmOCR's technique: put the page image and the text blocks with coordinates that the PDF itself contains into the model's prompt, so the model can copy rather than invent.
Silver data
Training labels produced by a strong model instead of by people. olmOCR's 260,000 training pages are labelled by GPT-4o.
Unit-test evaluation
Scoring an extraction with many small pass/fail checks (is this sentence present, does this header not appear, is this cell below that one) instead of one similarity score.

Design 1: a chain of specialised models (Docling)

Docling's pipeline runs in order on every document. A PDF backend parses the file for its programmatic text tokens with their coordinates and renders a bitmap of each page. A layout model, an object detector derived from RT-DETR and re-trained on DocLayNet "among other proprietary datasets", looks at the page image (fed at 72 dpi, which the report says a single CPU processes in under a second) and predicts boxes and classes. The boxes are cleaned of overlaps and intersected with the PDF's text tokens to group them into paragraphs, titles, list items, captions, figures and tables. Each detected table is cropped and sent, with its text cells, to TableFormer, whose structure prediction is matched back to the PDF cells "to avoid expensive re-transcription of the table image-crop", which also makes it language-agnostic. If a page has bitmaps or is scanned, an OCR engine (EasyOCR in the first release, Tesseract added later) runs at higher resolution, 216 dpi. A final assembly step corrects reading order, matches figures with captions, detects language, and builds a typed document object that serialises losslessly to JSON or lossily to Markdown and HTML.

The authors present the no-generation property as a design choice. Their 2025 paper says vision-language-model converters "are prone to hallucinations, i.e., their output may contain false information which is not present in the source document" and are expensive to run, whereas Docling's modular approach, "leveraging modular, task-specific models which recover document structures and features only", takes all text from the programmatic PDF or from OCR, which "ensures faithful conversion, without the risk of generating false content". They also name the price: "it necessitates maintaining a diverse set of models for different document components, such as formulas or figures." The code is MIT-licensed, and the project's README says it is hosted by the LF AI & Data Foundation after being started by the AI for knowledge team at IBM Research Zurich.

Design 2: one model writes the page (olmOCR)

olmOCR takes the opposite route and makes the vision-language model the whole pipeline. The key idea is document anchoring. For a born-digital PDF, the toolkit extracts the text blocks and image locations with pypdf, formats them as lines such as [71x398]1 Introduction after a "Page dimensions" line, and puts them in the prompt next to a rasterised image of the page. Blocks at the start and end of the page are prioritised until a character limit is reached; if the prompt exceeds the model's token limit, it is regenerated with exponentially lower limits. The authors report that, without anchoring, models tended to complete unfinished sentences or invent text where the image was ambiguous, and that anchored prompts gave "significantly fewer hallucinations". For scanned pages with no digital text the model gets only the image.

The training data come from the pipeline's own machinery. The authors sampled PDFs from an internal crawl of 240 million PDFs plus public-domain books from the Internet Archive, filtered out non-English documents, files pypdf could not parse, spam, fillable forms and very short text, and took up to three random pages per PDF. The result, olmOCR-mix-0225, is 258,641 pages from 102,825 PDFs (the abstract says "260,000 pages"). By the authors' estimate from classifying a sample of 707 pages, 55.9 per cent are academic papers, with brochures at 11.2, legal documents at 10.2, books at 6.8, tables at 5.6, diagrams at 4.7, slideshows at 1.9 and other at 3.7. The labels are GPT-4o outputs (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) produced with anchoring and a JSON schema; the paper records that the authors tried other models in October 2024 and rejected Gemini 1.5 for frequent recitation errors, GPT-4o mini for excess hallucination, and Claude Sonnet 3.5 as too expensive. The student is Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct, fine-tuned for 10,000 steps (about 1.2 epochs) on 8 H100 GPUs; one training run took 16 node-hours. A typical training prompt is about 3,000 tokens: roughly 1,000 for the page image (rendered to at most 1,024 pixels on the long edge) and 1,800 for the anchor text.

The toy below is the control loop of that diagram, with a scripted stand-in for the model (the standard llm.py of this course). The replies are invented to exercise each branch, so the output shows the logic and not olmOCR's behaviour. It also prints the anchor text for a page with a small character budget: the first, second, second-to-last and last blocks are kept and the middle one is dropped, following the "start and end first" rule.

"""Toy: the control loop around a page-reading VLM, after Poznanski et al. (2025): document anchoring,
validation, rotation, retries with rising temperature, fallback to plain PDF text. The model is a scripted stand-in
(llm.py) whose replies are invented to exercise each branch; nothing here is olmOCR's code or output."""
import json
from llm import ScriptedModel, text_of

BLOCKS = {"p1": [("71x459", "Figure 1"), ("71x398", "1 Introduction"), ("71x351", "Systems (Cottier et al., 2024)."),
                 ("71x300", "Open-weights models are only the start."), ("71x84", "Command R (Cohere, 2024a)")]}
BLOCKS.update({p: BLOCKS["p1"] for p in ("p2", "p3", "p4")})

def anchor(page, limit):
    """Page size line, then '[XxY]text' blocks. Blocks at the start and end of the page are added first, up to `limit` characters."""
    blocks, order = BLOCKS[page], []
    for i in range(len(blocks)):                     # start, end, second, second-to-last, ...
        order += [i] if i == len(blocks) - 1 - i else [i, len(blocks) - 1 - i]
    chosen, used = set(), 0
    for i in dict.fromkeys(order):
        line = "[%s]%s" % blocks[i]
        if used + len(line) > limit: break
        chosen.add(i); used += len(line)
    return "Page dimensions: 612.0x792.0\n" + "\n".join("[%s]%s" % blocks[i] for i in sorted(chosen))

def reply(valid=True, rot=0, body="1 Introduction\nSystems (Cottier et al., 2024)."):
    return json.dumps({"is_rotation_valid": valid, "rotation_correction": rot, "natural_text": body})
LOOP = " ".join(["the end"] * 150)                  # a degenerate, endlessly repeating generation
SCRIPT = {"p1": [reply()], "p2": [reply(False, 90, "garbled"), reply()], "p3": [LOOP, LOOP, reply()], "p4": [LOOP] * 4}
seen = {}
def scripted(messages, tools, system):
    page = text_of(messages[-1]).split("PAGE=")[1].split()[0]
    n = seen[page] = seen.get(page, -1) + 1
    return SCRIPT[page][n]
model = ScriptedModel(scripted)

TEMPS, MAX_TOKENS = (0.1, 0.45, 0.8, 0.8), 120      # rising temperature schedule is my choice; the paper reports 0.1 -> 0.8
def convert(page):
    rotation, spent, log = 0, 0, []
    for attempt, temp in enumerate(TEMPS, 1):
        prompt = "PAGE=%s ROTATE=%d\n%s" % (page, rotation, anchor(page, limit=140))
        r = model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": prompt}], max_tokens=MAX_TOKENS, temperature=temp)
        spent += min(r.usage["output_tokens"], MAX_TOKENS)       # a real server stops at the cap
        try:
            out = json.loads(text_of(r))
            if r.stop_reason == "max_tokens": raise ValueError
        except ValueError:
            log.append("t=%.2f degenerate" % temp); continue          # retry with a higher temperature
        if not out["is_rotation_valid"] and rotation == 0:
            rotation = out["rotation_correction"]; log.append("rotate %d" % rotation); continue
        return out["natural_text"], attempt, spent, log
    return "\n".join(t for _, t in BLOCKS[page]), len(TEMPS), spent, log + ["fallback: plain PDF text"]

print(anchor("p1", limit=140)); print()
for page in ("p1", "p2", "p3", "p4"):
    _, attempts, spent, log = convert(page)
    print("%s attempts=%d output_tokens=%3d  %s" % (page, attempts, spent, "; ".join(log) or "clean first time"))

Page p3 shows why the paper keeps the retry cap and the temperature schedule: two degenerate generations cost 270 tokens against 30 for a clean page. Page p4 shows the fallback: after four failed attempts the system returns the plain text blocks, which is worse in reading order but contains only text that was in the file. The authors name repetition as "the most common failure we experience", and they note that if retries are frequent, total generation throughput can fall significantly. One detail I had to interpret: the appendix prose says the pipeline rotates the page "if is_rotation_valid is set to true", while the schema describes that field as whether the page is oriented correctly. I implemented the second reading (rotate when the model says the orientation is not valid), which is the one that makes sense.

How Docling was evaluated

The two Docling reports evaluate speed, not accuracy. The 2024 report times the standard pipeline on 225 pages (three arXiv papers and two IBM Redbooks) with OCR disabled: on an Apple M3 Max with 16 cores it processed 1.27 to 1.34 pages per second with the native backend, and on a 16-core Xeon E5-2690 0.60 to 0.92, with a peak memory of about 6.2 GB for the native backend and about 2.5 GB for the lighter pypdfium backend (which the report warns gives worse tables). The 2025 paper uses a larger set: 89 PDFs with 4,008 pages, 56,246 text items, 1,842 tables and 4,676 pictures, drawn largely from DocLayNet plus samples of the CCpdf corpus. It reports the following, which I have regrouped.

The comparison with other open tools is also about time: on CPU the paper gives 3.1 seconds per page for Docling, 3.3 for MinerU, 4.2 for Unstructured and over 16 for Marker; with the L4 GPU, 0.49 for Docling, 0.21 for MinerU and 0.86 for Marker. Turning off OCR saved 50 to 60 per cent of runtime; turning off table recognition saved 16 to 24 per cent. These numbers are useful for capacity planning (Load Testing & Capacity Planning), but they say nothing about whether the output is right.

Quality is deferred. The 2025 paper's own future-work section says the team will "focus on building an open-source quality evaluation framework for the tasks performed by Docling", including layout analysis, table structure, reading order and text transcription, to allow "transparent quality comparisons based on publicly available benchmarks such as DP-Bench ... OmnidDocBench ... and others", with "results ... published in a future update of this technical report". Accuracy evidence for the components lives in earlier papers: DocLayNet's abstract reports that the object detectors trained on it fall "approximately 10%" behind the agreement between human annotators, in mAP. A docling-eval project now exists; the benchmark note I read defines its metrics (layout mAP, table TEDS, reading-order ARD, and text metrics such as edit distance and BLEU) on OmniDocBench-derived ground truth but I did not open its result files, so I quote no scores.

How olmOCR was evaluated

olmOCR's authors built a benchmark for the job. olmOCR-Bench has 7,010 test cases over 1,402 PDF pages (the abstract rounds to 1,400). Each test is a pass-or-fail check that is "simple, unambiguous, and deterministically machine-verifiable". The rationale is stated in the paper: model-based judges can favour their own output, and soft metrics such as edit distance or ROUGE "might fail to reveal fine-grained yet semantically important content extraction errors", such as a wrong subscript in a formula. The test types are text presence (a sentence appears, fuzzily matched), text absence (headers, footers and page numbers do not), natural reading order (one segment comes before another), table accuracy (a cell has a given value and neighbours with given properties) and math formula accuracy (the rendered symbols appear in the right relative positions). Every page also gets a baseline test: some alphanumeric output, no endless repeated n-grams at the end, and no stray Chinese, Japanese or emoji characters.

The pages come from seven sources, built differently: arXiv mathematics papers, old scanned maths textbooks, tables, historical scans from the Library of Congress, header/footer pages, multi-column pages and dense tiny-print pages. Many tests were drafted by models (GPT-4o, Gemini Flash 2.0, Claude Sonnet 3.7) and then reviewed manually; olmOCR itself was used to find candidate pages with formulas. The score of a system is the pass rate within each source, averaged over sources (a macro-average), with a 95 per cent confidence interval from bootstrapping 10,000 samples. The authors write that they built the benchmark after training the preview model "to prevent unfairly iterating on the benchmark" and that they decontaminated it against the training set by URL.

The paper's Table 4 gives the overall pass rates above. Read it as the authors' result on their own benchmark, with these points: the intervals are about plus or minus one point, so neighbours such as Mistral OCR (72.0) and Marker (70.1) are not clearly separated; document anchoring helps modestly for olmOCR itself (74.7 to 75.5) and a lot for Gemini Flash 2 (57.8 to 63.8); and the authors report that the fine-tuned 7B model beats its own teacher, GPT-4o, on this benchmark (75.5 against 69.9 anchored), while its base model Qwen 2 VL scores 31.5 without fine-tuning.

The second toy shows why unit tests were chosen. I made three extractions of one hand-written page (two columns, a header and footer, a table, a formula): A reads the PDF in raw line order, B has perfect layout but one wrong symbol in the formula, and C is fluent but changes one number in the table and drops a sentence. I then scored them with a similarity ratio and with nine pass/fail tests of the kinds above.

"""Toy: olmOCR-Bench-style pass/fail unit tests versus a similarity score. Three hand-made extractions of one page."""
import difflib, random, re
REF = """Revenue rose 4.5% in the third quarter, led by retail. Costs were flat.
Outlook: We expect growth to continue in the fourth quarter.
| Region | Q2 | Q3 |
|---|---|---|
| North | 2.4% | 4.5% |
| South | 1.1% | 3.0% |
x_i^2 + y_i^2 = r^2"""
A = """ACME Corp Confidential
Revenue rose 4.5% in the third Outlook: We expect growth
quarter, led by retail. Costs were to continue in the fourth quarter.
flat.
Region Q2 Q3 North 2.4% 4.5% South 1.1% 3.0%
x i 2 + y i 2 = r 2
Page 7"""
B = REF.replace("x_i^2", "x_1^2")                                   # layout right, one formula symbol wrong
C = REF.replace("3.0%", "3.6%").replace(" Costs were flat.", "") + "\nPage 7"   # fluent, but a number changed and a sentence lost

norm = lambda s: re.sub(r"\s+", " ", s).strip()
def near(text, snippet, max_edits=2):           # approximate substring match (Sellers' edit-distance DP)
    t, s = norm(text), norm(snippet); prev = [0] * (len(t) + 1)
    for i, cs in enumerate(s, 1):
        cur = [i]
        for j, ct in enumerate(t, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1, cur[j-1] + 1, prev[j-1] + (cs != ct)))
        prev = cur
    return min(prev) <= max_edits
def cell_grid(text):
    return [[c.strip() for c in l.strip("|").split("|")] for l in text.splitlines() if l.startswith("|") and "---" not in l]
def table_above(text, cell, above):             # does `cell` have `above` directly over it in a table?
    g = cell_grid(text)
    return any(g[r][c] == cell and g[r-1][c] == above for r in range(1, len(g)) for c in range(len(g[r])))
def before(text, x, y):
    t = norm(text); return x in t and y in t and t.index(x) < t.index(y)

TESTS = [("presence", lambda t: near(t, "Revenue rose 4.5% in the third quarter, led by retail.")),
         ("presence", lambda t: near(t, "We expect growth to continue in the fourth quarter.")),
         ("absence",  lambda t: "ACME Corp Confidential" not in t),
         ("absence",  lambda t: "Page 7" not in t),
         ("order",    lambda t: before(t, "Costs were flat.", "We expect growth")),
         ("order",    lambda t: before(t, "Revenue rose", "Outlook")),
         ("table",    lambda t: table_above(t, "3.0%", "4.5%")),
         ("table",    lambda t: table_above(t, "4.5%", "Q3")),
         ("formula",  lambda t: "x_i^2+y_i^2=r^2" in t.replace(" ", ""))]

def score(text):
    by = {}
    for kind, fn in TESTS: by.setdefault(kind, []).append(fn(text))
    return by
random.seed(0)
print(f"{'extraction':10} {'similarity':>10} {'tests passed':>12} {'macro pass rate':>16} {'95% bootstrap CI':>18}   failed")
for name, text in (("A raw order", A), ("B layout", B), ("C fluent", C)):

    macro = 100 * sum(sum(v) / len(v) for v in by.values()) / len(by)
    boots = sorted(100 * sum(random.choices(flat, k=len(flat))) / len(flat) for _ in range(2000))
    failed = [k for (k, _), p in zip(TESTS, flat) if not p]
    sim = difflib.SequenceMatcher(None, norm(REF), norm(text)).ratio()
    print(f"{name:10} {sim:>10.3f} {sum(flat):>9}/{len(flat)} {macro:>15.1f}% {boots[50]:>9.0f} to {boots[1950]:.0f}   {','.join(failed) or '-'}")

Extraction B has a similarity ratio of 0.996 to the reference and still fails the formula test; extraction C has 0.943 and silently changes a table value. That is a check on arithmetic, not on the prices: the GPU rental rate and API prices have moved since February 2025. Note also that the $176 figure uses the paper's own throughput; a sustained run depends on the mix of pages and the retry rate.

The authors also test usefulness for training data. They took the 7.9 million PDFs behind the peS2o corpus, a widely used set of linearised scientific articles originally extracted with Grobid and rules, re-extracted them with olmOCR, and continued pretraining an intermediate OLMo-2-7B checkpoint on 50 billion tokens of each version. The average over six benchmark tasks rose from 53.9 to 55.2 (+1.3 points). The movement was uneven: HellaSwag rose from 57.4 to 62.6, MMLU stayed at 61.1, and Natural Questions and WinoGrande slipped slightly. The paper reports one comparison per task without intervals, so small differences may be noise.

What happened next, and what the later work says

The projects have been converging. In October 2025 the olmOCR authors released olmOCR 2 (Poznanski, Soldaini and Lo; abstract read): a 7B model, olmOCR-2-7B-1025, trained with reinforcement learning where the rewards are binary unit tests, using synthetic documents with known HTML ground truth to produce the tests. The abstract claims state-of-the-art results on olmOCR-Bench with the largest gains in math formula conversion, table parsing and multi-column layouts. The Docling team, for its part, released Granite-Docling-258M, a single small vision-language model for conversion (a Hugging Face mirror page seen in search results lists 17 September 2025), and the Docling README now lists a VLM pipeline option. So the "chain versus one model" split has partly dissolved: the layout-model project ships an end-to-end model, and the end-to-end project trains on test-based rewards.

A neutral yardstick exists: OmniDocBench (Ouyang et al., CVPR 2025; abstract read) covers nine document sources, 19 layout categories and 15 attribute labels, and its authors evaluate "both pipeline-based methods and end-to-end vision-language models". Docling's 2025 paper names it as a target for its own quality evaluation. I did not read its results.

State of play, October 2026

The olmOCR README I fetched in October 2026 shows an olmOCR-Bench table with, among others, olmOCR v0.4.0 (released 21 October 2025) at 82.4 plus or minus 1.1, Chandra OCR 0.1.0 at 83.1 plus or minus 0.9, Infinity-Parser 7B at 82.5, PaddleOCR-VL at 80.0, Marker 1.10.1 at 76.1, MinerU 2.5.4 at 75.2, DeepSeek-OCR at 75.7 and Mistral OCR at 72.0. Rows marked with an asterisk are not explained on that page, and the table is maintained by the benchmark's authors. Docling does not appear in it. The Docling README lists support for many formats and a granite_docling VLM option. Leaderboards like this change every few weeks; use them to build a shortlist, then test on your own pages.

Limits and caveats

  • The two evaluations answer different questions. Docling's papers measure speed and cite component accuracy; olmOCR's measure accuracy on a benchmark its authors built and cost under assumed prices. Neither lets you rank the two systems.
  • Benchmarks reflect their builders' choices. olmOCR-Bench tests were partly model-drafted and its pages partly chosen with the system under test. The authors took steps (building it after training, URL decontamination) and the intervals are reported, but a third party has to confirm the ranking. The README's leaderboard is again maintained by the same team.
  • Presence tests cannot see everything. A test that expects a sentence to be present passes even if the model adds an invented sentence next to it. Absence tests and the baseline checks catch some of this; they do not measure faithfulness in general. This is my reading of the test design, not a claim in the paper.
  • The training labels are another model's output. A student fine-tuned on GPT-4o transcriptions can inherit the teacher's habits.
  • PDFs are untrusted input. Both tools parse files that anyone can craft. Text hidden in a PDF can reach the model through the anchor text. Injection & Untrusted Input describes the class of problem.

Lessons

  • Choose by failure mode. A chain of extractors fails by omission or mis-ordering; a generative reader fails by invention or loops. Decide which you can tolerate and add the matching tests. Lab 6 builds a pipeline from boxes to reading order and measures character error rate.
  • Wrap the model in a loop you can observe. Validation, bounded retries, a deterministic fallback and a per-page record of which branch ran are the same ideas as in Failure, Timeouts & Retries, applied to a page reader.
  • Write tests, not one score. Pass/fail checks that name a capability tell you what to fix and are easy to extend with your own documents. Chapter 20, Tables, Forms & Key-Value Extraction, shows table-specific scoring.
  • Price the pipeline in pages per dollar. A 70-fold range between rows in the cost table is larger than most accuracy differences. Studio 3, Design a Document-Processing Pipeline, asks you to route pages by type and budget.

Common misconception

"A model-based reader is more accurate than a pipeline, so it should replace it." On olmOCR-Bench the fine-tuned model scored 75.5 against 61.5 for MinerU and 70.1 for Marker, but several general VLMs without fine-tuning scored lower than the pipeline tools (Qwen 2 VL 31.5, Gemini Flash 2 57.8). The result depends on fine-tuning and on the benchmark's mix of pages. And a model can write text that is not on the page, which a chain of extractors cannot. Measure both on your documents, with tests that include "must not appear" cases.

Key takeaways

  • Docling chains a PDF parser, a DocLayNet-trained layout model, TableFormer and optional OCR; all text is copied or read, none is generated.
  • olmOCR fine-tunes a 7B vision-language model on 258,641 GPT-4o-labelled pages, anchors it with the PDF's own text blocks, and wraps it in validation, retries, rotation and a plain-text fallback.
  • Docling's papers report speed (for example a median of 0.79 s per page on CPU) and defer quality evaluation; olmOCR reports pass rates on a 7,010-test benchmark it built, and about $176 per million pages.
  • Unit tests expose which capability failed where a similarity score cannot; they need enough tests to give narrow intervals.
  • The primary sources never compare the two systems. Run your own comparison on your own documents.

Check your understanding

Docling's 2025 paper argues its design "ensures faithful conversion". What is the mechanism behind that claim?

  1. The layout model is trained on human-annotated data, so it cannot make mistakes
  2. Docling checks every output sentence against a second model
  3. All text comes from the PDF itself or from OCR; the models only predict structure, so none of them writes text
  4. Docling always runs OCR at 216 dpi
The claim is about generation, not accuracy: models that only predict boxes and table structure cannot invent sentences. It does not mean errors are impossible (DocLayNet's detectors are reported about 10 per cent behind annotator agreement), there is no second-model check, and OCR runs only on bitmaps or scans.

An extraction scores 0.99 on a similarity ratio against the reference but fails the formula test. What does this show about the two kinds of evaluation?

  1. A similarity score can hide a small error that matters; a unit test names it
  2. Unit tests are more accurate than similarity because they are binary
  3. The similarity ratio was computed wrongly
  4. Formula tests are too strict to be useful
One changed symbol alters few characters, so edit-based similarity stays near 1 while the formula is wrong. Binary tests are not inherently more accurate, and the sample here is too small to say the test is too strict; the point is that the test targets the capability.

olmOCR raises the sampling temperature from 0.1 towards 0.8 on retries. Why?

  1. Higher temperature makes the transcription more accurate
  2. It reduces the cost of each attempt
  3. It forces the model to output valid JSON
  4. Its most common failure is an endless repetition, and more randomness lowers the chance of falling into the same loop again
The appendix says increasing the temperature reduces the likelihood of repetitions, and that failed pages are reprocessed up to N times before falling back to plain extraction. It does not improve accuracy in general, does not reduce cost (retries add cost), and JSON validity comes from fine-tuning, not temperature.

Why can you not conclude from the primary sources that olmOCR is better than Docling?

  1. olmOCR is much slower than Docling
  2. Neither paper evaluates the other system: Docling's reports measure speed and defer quality evaluation, and olmOCR's benchmark table does not include Docling
  3. Docling is open source and olmOCR is not
  4. The olmOCR benchmark has too many tests
The two evaluations address different questions on different data, and a README leaderboard that omits Docling is not a comparison. Both projects are open source, and a large number of tests narrows, rather than widens, the uncertainty. Speed is not the issue either; they were not measured against each other.
