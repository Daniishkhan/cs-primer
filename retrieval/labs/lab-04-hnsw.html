Lab 4: HNSW from Scratch

Chapter 11 introduced the hierarchical navigable small-world graph, the index behind most in-memory vector search, and measured how much work it saves over a brute-force scan. This lab builds one. You write the beam search inside a layer, the random layer assignment, the neighbour-selection heuristic, insertion and the layered query, then run the experiment that every vector-index decision rests on: a curve of recall against the number of similarity computations, for different graph settings. By the end you can point at each of the three knobs, M, efconstruction and ef, and say what it does to build cost, memory, recall and query cost, because you have counted.

Why this lab matters

The vector index is the part of a retrieval system that decides what you can afford, and its settings cap your recall before any reranker or language model sees a result (chapter 11). Teams routinely set M = 16 and ef = 40 because a library default said so, then discover that recall on their data is 0.9 and nobody knows which knob to turn. Writing the structure removes the mystery: ef is the width of a priority-queue beam, M is the number of links a node may keep, the layers are a skip list over the same graph, and "recall" is a number you can compute by comparing against brute force. You will also see the failure modes in the code rather than in a diagram: why a greedy walk alone gets stuck, why a node that is linked only to its own cluster strands everything beyond it, and why building the whole graph costs as much as several hundred brute-force queries. The vectors here are small and synthetic; the structure and the counting are what transfer. Hybrid retrieval, the other half of the dense side of the course, is in Lab 3.

Synthetic vectors, counted work, standard library only

The data are 3,000 unit vectors of 32 dimensions in 40 loose clusters, plus 100 query vectors drawn the same way, produced by data.py from a fixed seed. They imitate the clumpy structure of real text embeddings (chapter 7) and are not embeddings of anything. Two consequences. Recall and cost numbers on real embeddings, with different dimensionality and different clumpiness, will differ, so do not carry a number out of this page. And at 3,000 vectors brute force is perfectly good (chapter 11 said a scan is right below a few hundred thousand vectors); the lab uses a size where the pure-Python index builds in about two seconds, and what it teaches is the mechanism. Cost is measured as similarity computations, counted by the index itself, rather than seconds, because pure-Python timing says little about a C++ implementation and the count is identical on every machine. Python 3.9 or newer, no packages. The full check takes about three seconds.

What you'll build

Six milestones, from warm-up to the finished index:

  1. The exact baseline and the metric: brute-force top-k by dot product, and ANN recall, the fraction of the exact answer an index returns.
  2. Beam search inside one layer: the best-first search with a candidate heap, a result heap, a visited set and a width ef, tested on a graph you do not have to build.
  3. Levels and neighbour selection: the exponentially decaying random layer of a node, the simple "keep the closest" rule and the paper's heuristic that spreads links apart.
  4. Insertion: find neighbours layer by layer from the top, link both ways, prune over-full lists, and promote the entry point.
  5. Query: greedy descent through the upper layers, then a beam of width ef on layer 0.
  6. Recall against effort: sweep ef, record recall and similarity computations per query, and read off the cheapest setting that meets a recall target.

Milestone 1: the exact baseline, and what recall means

Every approximate index is judged against the exact answer, so start there. exact_topk(vectors, q, k) computes the dot product of the query with every vector and returns the ids of the k largest, best first, ties by the smaller id. That is N similarity computations per query: 3,000 here, and the 100 million of chapter 11's arithmetic at scale. It is also the ground truth, computed once, for everything that follows.

ANN recall (approximate-nearest-neighbour recall) at k is the fraction of the exact top-k that an index returns, averaged over queries: for each query, |found ∩ truth| / |truth|. Order inside the list does not matter, and extra results do not lower it. This is a statement about agreement with brute force over the same vectors. It is not the relevance recall of chapter 4, which compares results with human judgements.

Common misconception

"The index has recall 0.95, so 5% of my answers are wrong." Recall 0.95 means that 5% of the exact top-10 neighbours are missing from the index's top 10. If the passage that answers the question was the exact top-1 and the index found it, nothing was lost; the missing neighbours may sit at ranks 8 to 10, which a reranker rarely needs. Conversely, an ANN recall of 1.0 does nothing for you if the embedding model ranks the answer 40th. Tune the index with ANN recall against brute force, and decide whether the loss matters with the end-to-end metrics of Lab 2.

def exact_topk(vectors, q, k):
    """Ids of the k vectors with the largest dot product with `q`, best first, ties by smaller id."""
    scored = [(-dot(v, q), i) for i, v in enumerate(vectors)]
    return [i for _, i in heapq.nsmallest(k, scored)]


def recall_at_k(found, truth):
    """Mean over queries of |found ∩ truth| / |truth|. `found` and `truth` are lists of id lists."""
    if not truth:
        return 0.0
    return sum(len(set(f) & set(t)) / len(t) for f, t in zip(found, truth)) / len(truth)

Milestone 2: beam search inside one layer

The idea behind a graph index is that you can find a vector's neighbours by walking. Give every vector links to a few nearby vectors, forming a proximity graph. To search, start at some node, look at its neighbours, move to whichever is most similar to the query, and repeat until no neighbour improves on where you are. That is a greedy walk. It touches a handful of nodes rather than all of them. It also has two weaknesses. It can stop in a local optimum, a node that beats all its neighbours but is not the nearest overall; and it returns one answer, when you want the top 10.

Best-first beam search fixes both with one change: instead of keeping one current node, keep a short list of the best nodes found so far, of size ef, and keep expanding the most promising node you have not expanded yet. The search stops when the best unexpanded candidate is no better than the worst of your ef results, because expanding it could not improve the list. With ef = 1 the beam collapses to the greedy walk; with a larger ef the search explores several branches and escapes local optima, at the cost of more similarity computations. This one function, search_layer, is the engine of both insertion and querying, so it is worth getting exactly right.

It needs three structures. A visited set, so no node is scored twice in one search (the graph has cycles; without it you recompute and may never terminate). A candidate heap ordered best-first, holding nodes found but not yet expanded. A result heap of the best ef nodes so far, ordered worst-first, so the node to evict when a better one arrives is at the top. Python's heapq is a min-heap, so the candidate heap stores negated similarities. The loop:

  1. Score the entry points; put them in both heaps and mark them visited.
  2. Pop the best candidate. If the result heap is full and this candidate is worse than the worst result, stop.
  3. For each unvisited neighbour of the candidate on this layer, mark it visited and score it. If the result heap is not full or the neighbour beats the worst result, push it onto both heaps; if the result heap now exceeds ef, evict the worst.
  4. Return the results sorted best first (ties by smaller id).

You can test this without building a layered index, because the check hands you a graph. Eight points on a circle, 45 degrees apart, each linked to its two neighbours in a ring, and a query at 100 degrees:

Solution spoiler 
def search_layer(self, q, entry_points, ef, layer):
    """The `ef` nodes of `layer` most similar to `q` found by a beam search from `entry_points`.

    Returns [(similarity, node_id), ...], best first (ties by smaller id)."""
    visited = set(entry_points)
    s0 = self.sims(q, list(entry_points))
    cand = [(-s, e) for s, e in zip(s0, entry_points)]        # max-heap on similarity
    heapq.heapify(cand)
    best = [(s, e) for s, e in zip(s0, entry_points)]         # min-heap: the ef best so far
    heapq.heapify(best)
    while len(best) > ef:
        heapq.heappop(best)
    while cand:
        neg, node = heapq.heappop(cand)
        if len(best) >= ef and -neg < best[0][0]:             # the best open candidate is worse than
            break                                             # the worst kept result: nothing left to gain
        fresh = [n for n in self.links[layer][node] if n not in visited]
        if not fresh:
            continue
        visited.update(fresh)
        for s, n in zip(self.sims(q, fresh), fresh):
            if len(best) < ef or s > best[0][0]:
                heapq.heappush(cand, (-s, n))
                heapq.heappush(best, (s, n))
                if len(best) > ef:
                    heapq.heappop(best)
    return sorted(best, key=lambda t: (-t[0], t[1]))

Milestone 3: levels and neighbour selection

A single-layer proximity graph has a second problem, which the check at the end of milestone 2 measures: starting from a fixed node, a greedy walk (ef = 1) finds the true nearest neighbour for only 20% of the queries on the 400-node graph (the check prints this), and even a wide beam from a fixed entry point wastes effort crawling across the graph one short link at a time. HNSW borrows the skip list. Give each node a random top layer; layer 0 holds every node, layer 1 a random sample, layer 2 a sample of that, and so on. Each layer is its own proximity graph over the nodes it holds, so the upper layers are sparse and their links span long distances. Searching starts at the top, takes long strides to the right region, drops a layer when it cannot improve, and does the careful beam search only on the dense bottom layer.

The layer of a new node is drawn as floor(-ln(U) * mL) with U uniform on (0, 1] and mL = 1/ln M. That gives the probability of reaching layer l or higher as M−l: about 1 node in M reaches layer 1, 1 in M2 reaches layer 2, and the number of layers grows like logMN.

Go deeper why the formula gives M-l

If U is uniform on (0, 1], then −ln U is exponentially distributed with rate 1: P(−ln U ≥ x) = e−x. The level is the integer part of −ln U · mL, so level ≥ l exactly when −ln U ≥ l/mL = l ln M, which has probability e−l ln M = M−l. Choosing mL = 1/ln M therefore ties the thinning rate of the layers to M: with M = 12, each layer holds about a twelfth of the one below. For one million nodes and M = 16 you would expect 62,500 nodes on layer 1, about 3,900 on layer 2, 244 on layer 3, and a top layer with a handful. In code, draw U as 1.0 - rng.random(): random() returns values in [0, 1), and 0 would make the logarithm undefined.

Which neighbours to keep

Both insertion and pruning must choose, from a list of candidates sorted by similarity to a base node, which m to link. The simple rule keeps the m most similar. Its flaw appears on clustered data, which is what real embeddings are: a node inside a tight cluster has all its m nearest neighbours in that cluster, so it links only inward. The cluster is well connected internally and weakly connected to the rest, and a search that arrives at the cluster's edge cannot find a way across.

The paper's heuristic (Algorithm 4) walks the candidates best first and keeps a candidate only if it is closer to the base node than to any neighbour already kept. In the example above, the base is at 0 degrees and the candidates at 10, 20 and −80 degrees. The 10 degree candidate is kept. The 20 degree one is more similar to the 10 degree neighbour (cosine 0.985) than to the base (0.940), so a link to it adds nothing the first link does not already provide, and it is dropped. The −80 degree candidate is far from the base (0.174) but even farther from the kept neighbour (cosine 0.0), so it stays, and it points somewhere new. The result is a node with links in different directions, including long ones that cross between clusters. Two details matter in the check: the comparison is strict (a copy of an already-kept vector has equal similarity and is dropped), and a candidate that fails is dropped even if that leaves fewer than m links, which is why the heuristic's degrees come out below the cap.

def random_level(rng, mL):
    """floor(-ln(U) * mL) with U uniform in (0, 1]: P(level >= l) = M**-l when mL = 1 / ln(M)."""
    return int(-math.log(1.0 - rng.random()) * mL)


def select_simple(self, candidates, m):
    """The m most similar candidates. `candidates` is [(similarity to the base node, id)], best first."""
    return list(candidates[:m])


def select_heuristic(self, candidates, m):
    """Algorithm 4: walk the candidates best first; keep one only if it is MORE similar to the base
    node than to every neighbour already kept; stop at m. Same input and output shape as select_simple."""
    kept = []
    for s, c in candidates:
        if all(s > dot(self.vectors[c], self.vectors[r]) for _, r in kept):
            kept.append((s, c))
            if len(kept) == m:
                break
    return kept

Milestone 4: inserting a node

Insertion builds the index one vector at a time, and it reuses everything you have written. Inserting a vector means searching for its neighbours and linking to them (Algorithm 1 of the paper). The procedure for a new node with random top layer L, in an index whose current top layer is T:

  1. Register. Append the vector and its level; make sure links has a dict for layers 0 to L; give the node an empty neighbour list on each. If it is the first node, it becomes the entry point and you are done.
  2. Descend the layers above L. From the entry point, for each layer from T down to L + 1, run search_layer with ef = 1 and carry the best node down. These layers are only used to get to the right region quickly; the new node does not live on them.
  3. Link on each layer from min(L, T) down to 0. Run search_layer with efconstruction to find a pool of candidates, choose M of them with the heuristic, and set them as the new node's neighbours. Add the new node to each chosen neighbour's list: links are made both ways. Because the neighbour now has one more link, its list may exceed its cap (2M on layer 0, M above); then call the given shrink, which re-selects its neighbours with the same heuristic. Seed the next layer's search with every node found, not only the best.
  4. Promote. If L is higher than the old top layer T, the new node becomes the entry point.

Two design points are worth saying aloud. The new node picks only M neighbours but may be picked by many, so lists grow past M and have to be pruned; that is why layer 0 allows 2M. And the quality of the whole index depends on efconstruction: a wider beam during insertion finds better neighbours and builds a better graph, at the price of more computations per insertion. The graph is built once, so efconstruction is a setting you pay for in build time and cannot change afterwards without rebuilding.

At 768 dimensions the same links are about 2% of a 3,072-byte vector.

  • If recall of the layer-0 search is low but nothing crashes, look for a missing reverse link, or seeding the next layer with only the best node.

def add(self, vec):
    """Insert one vector; return its id (0, 1, 2, ... in order of insertion)."""
    i = len(self.vectors)
    self.vectors.append(vec)
    L = random_level(self.rng, self.mL)
    self.level.append(L)
    while len(self.links) <= L:
        self.links.append({})
    for layer in range(L + 1):
        self.links[layer][i] = []
    if self.entry is None:
        self.entry = i
        return i
    top = self.level[self.entry]
    ep = [self.entry]
    for layer in range(top, L, -1):                            # above the new node's layers: just descend
        ep = [self.search_layer(vec, ep, 1, layer)[0][1]]
    for layer in range(min(L, top), -1, -1):                   # from there down: find, link, prune
        found = self.search_layer(vec, ep, self.ef_construction, layer)
        chosen = self.select_heuristic(found, self.M)
        self.links[layer][i] = [c for _, c in chosen]
        for _, c in chosen:
            self.links[layer][c].append(i)
            if len(self.links[layer][c]) > (2 * self.M if layer == 0 else self.M):
                self.shrink(layer, c)
        ep = [n for _, n in found]                             # the whole beam seeds the next layer down
    if L > top:
        self.entry = i
    return i

Milestone 5: searching the whole structure

A query has the same shape as an insertion without the linking. Start at the entry point on the top layer. For each layer from the top down to layer 1, run search_layer with ef = 1: a greedy walk that gets you into the right neighbourhood using the sparse, long-range links, and hands the best node it found to the next layer down. On layer 0, run search_layer with width ef (raised to k if the caller asks for more results than the beam holds) and return the first k ids. The cost has two parts: a few dozen computations to descend, almost independent of N, and the layer-0 beam, which grows with ef. That is why the structure scales: doubling the collection adds roughly one more step to the descent.

ef is the dial you turn at run time. It does not change the graph, so a live index can serve a latency-sensitive route at ef = 20 and a quality-sensitive one at ef = 100. Try it on query 0:

Solution spoiler 
def search(self, q, k=10, ef=50):
    """The ids of the k nodes most similar to `q`, best first. `ef` is the beam width on layer 0."""
    if self.entry is None or k <= 0:
        return []
    ep = [self.entry]
    for layer in range(self.top_layer, 0, -1):
        ep = [self.search_layer(q, ep, 1, layer)[0][1]]
    found = self.search_layer(q, ep, max(ef, k), 0)
    return [n for _, n in found[:k]]

Milestone 6: recall against search effort

The result that matters is not any single query but the recall curve: for a sequence of ef values, the mean recall@10 over many queries and the mean number of similarity computations per query. It answers the question every deployment asks, "what is the cheapest setting that reaches the recall we need?", and it is how chapter 11 told you to set parameters: from a measured curve on your data and not from a library default. recall_curve runs every query at every ef, takes the growth of dist_calls as the cost, and returns one row per ef. ef_for_recall reads the answer off the curve: the smallest ef whose recall reaches a target, or None if none does.

Once both work, the lab's last script sweeps the settings that are fixed at build time. It builds five indexes over the same 3,000 vectors, then measures each one's recall and cost for six values of ef. The outputs are three small tables, and the figure that follows plots them.

def recall_curve(index, queries, truth, k=10, efs=(10, 20, 40, 80, 160)):
    """For each ef: the mean recall@k and the mean number of similarity computations per query.

    Returns [{"ef": ef, "recall": float, "dist_calls": float}, ...] in the order of `efs`."""
    rows = []
    for ef in efs:
        before = index.dist_calls
        found = [index.search(q, k, ef) for q in queries]
        rows.append({"ef": ef, "recall": recall_at_k(found, truth), "dist_calls": (index.dist_calls - before) / len(queries)})
    return rows


def ef_for_recall(curve, target):
    """The smallest ef in `curve` whose recall is at least `target`; None if no row reaches it."""
    for row in sorted(curve, key=lambda r: r["ef"]):
        if row["recall"] >= target:
            return row["ef"]
    return None

Read the figure first, then the tables.

  • Every curve climbs steeply and then flattens. Early effort buys a lot of recall (for M = 8, going from ef = 10 to 40 raises recall from 0.554 to 0.886), later effort buys little (from 80 to 160 adds 0.032 for 443 more computations). The standard operating point is the knee: the last place where a doubling of effort still buys a worthwhile gain.
  • M changes where ef puts you more than it changes the curve. At ef = 10 the three graphs give recall 0.37, 0.55 and 0.72, but at different costs (107, 178 and 281 similarity computations), so compare them against cost and not against ef. Plotted that way, M = 8 and M = 16 nearly coincide, and recall 0.95 costs about 700 computations for all three: ef = 160 at M = 4 (715 computations, 0.918 already, 0.982 at ef = 320), ef = 80 at M = 8 (720, 0.966) and ef = 40 at M = 16 (696, 0.974). Only the smallest M is visibly worse at equal cost (0.81 at 418 computations, against 0.886 at 440 for M = 8). What M changes is the bill around the curve: layer-0 degree 6.2, 12.3 and 23.0, so memory for links nearly quadruples, and insertion costs 349, 503 and 699 similarity computations. On this data a bigger M buys a lower ef for the same cost and a higher ceiling, not cheaper queries.
  • efconstruction is paid once and cannot be changed later. At M = 16, building with efconstruction = 16 gives a visibly worse graph (0.871 at ef = 40, against 0.974) and costs 237 computations per insertion. Going from 64 to 200 raises the build cost by two thirds (699 to 1,167 per insertion, 3.1 seconds against 1.9) for no measurable gain here (0.973 against 0.974): past some point a wider construction beam finds the same neighbours. That point depends on the data, which is exactly why you measure.
  • Compare with brute force. Brute force is 3,000 computations a query at recall 1.0. At M = 16 and ef = 40 the index does the same job at 0.974 for 696, about a quarter of the work. At this size the saving is real but modest; the advantage grows with N, because the descent cost grows roughly with log N while the scan grows with N.

Systems connection

Each hop in a graph search jumps to an unrelated memory address, so an in-memory HNSW is limited by memory latency, not arithmetic, and an unmodified graph on an SSD is slow (chapter 11 names DiskANN for that case). Count bytes and cache misses as well as similarity computations when you size a real deployment: see performance engineering. The graph is also awkward under change. Insertion works as you wrote it, but deleting a node leaves holes in other nodes' lists, so most implementations mark nodes as deleted and filter them out until a rebuild. A permission filter applied after the search can leave you with fewer than k results, the subject of chapter 12. And a new embedding model means a new index, because vectors from different models are not comparable. The indexes behind search engines and vector stores are covered in specialised stores.

What to take away

  • HNSW is a layered proximity graph. Search is a greedy descent through sparse upper layers, then a beam of width ef on layer 0, and every step is counted in similarity computations.
  • The beam search with a visited set, a candidate heap and a bounded result heap is the single engine behind both insertion and querying.
  • Levels are exponentially decaying (1 in M per layer), so the structure behaves like a skip list; the neighbour heuristic keeps links pointing in different directions, which is what holds clustered data together.
  • ef is the run-time dial; M and efconstruction are paid at build time in cost and memory and cannot be changed without rebuilding. Setting them is a matter of reading a recall curve, not of copying a default.
  • ANN recall measures agreement with brute force on the same vectors. Whether the missing neighbours matter is decided by relevance metrics on judged queries.

Check your understanding

An index has ANN recall@10 of 0.95 against brute force over the same embeddings. A colleague says "so 5% of our users get a wrong answer". What is the right correction?

  1. They are right: ANN recall and answer accuracy are the same quantity
  2. ANN recall counts missing neighbours, which may be low-ranked or recoverable by a reranker; whether any user gets a wrong answer is decided by relevance metrics on judged queries, not by recall against exact search
  3. ANN recall of 0.95 means 95% of queries are answered perfectly and 5% fail completely
  4. Recall cannot be defined for an approximate index
Recall compares the index with exact search over the same vectors, so it can be computed (option 4 is false) and it counts neighbours, not queries (option 3 is false): the 5% is spread over lists. If the missing neighbours are ranks 8 to 10, or if exact search itself ranked the answer 40th, the user impact is unrelated to the 0.05. Milestone 1's metric tunes the index; Lab 2's metrics decide the matter.

Your index, built with M = 16, serves queries at ef = 40 with recall@10 of 0.90 and you need 0.97. You cannot rebuild this week. Which change is available, and what does it cost?

  1. Raise efconstruction; it costs more memory
  2. Raise M; it costs nothing at query time
  3. Lower ef; it reduces the beam's noise
  4. Raise ef, the query-time beam width; it costs more similarity computations (latency) per query, with diminishing returns
Only ef acts at query time. M and efconstruction shape the graph at insertion and take effect only after a rebuild. In the lab's curve, going from ef = 20 to 40 at M = 16 raised recall from 0.859 to 0.974 for about 265 more computations a query. Lowering ef narrows the beam and can only lower recall.

With M = 16 and level drawn as floor(−ln(U) / ln M), roughly how many of one million nodes would you expect on layer 1 and on layer 2?

  1. About 62,500 and 3,900
  2. About 500,000 and 250,000
  3. About 16 and 256
  4. About 160,000 and 16,000
The probability of reaching layer l is M−l: 1/16 of the nodes (62,500) reach layer 1 and 1/256 (3,906) reach layer 2. Halving per layer (option 2) is what a skip list with probability 1/2 does; M sets the thinning rate here. The lab's 3,000 nodes with M = 12 gave 238 and 21, close to 250 and 21.

Why does the paper's neighbour-selection heuristic keep a candidate only if it is closer to the new node than to every neighbour already kept, instead of simply keeping the M closest?

  1. It makes insertion faster, because fewer candidates are compared
  2. It guarantees every node gets exactly M links
  3. On clustered data the M closest nodes all lie in the same cluster, so a node would link only inward; the test spreads links in different directions and keeps clusters connected to each other
  4. It removes duplicate vectors from the index
The heuristic does more work per insertion, not less (option 1), and it often keeps fewer than M links (option 2): the lab's layer-0 degrees averaged 17.8 against a cap of 24. A candidate that is closer to an already-kept neighbour than to the base node adds no new direction. Identical copies are dropped by the strict comparison, but that is a side effect, not the purpose (option 4).

In search_layer, a node is marked visited the first time it is seen, and its similarity is computed only then. What goes wrong if you skip the visited set?

  1. Nothing: the heaps already ignore duplicates
  2. The graph has cycles, so nodes are re-scored and re-pushed repeatedly: the similarity count inflates, and the search can revisit the same region many times
  3. Recall rises, because nodes are examined more than once
  4. The result heap would hold more than ef entries
Each node usually has several neighbours that link back to it, so without a visited set it is scored once per incoming link and may be pushed as a candidate many times. Recall does not improve, since a repeated node adds no new information; the cost simply grows, and the check counts exactly one similarity per node. The result heap is capped by eviction regardless (option 4), and the heaps do not deduplicate (option 1).

A product team adds a permission filter that allows 2% of documents. They keep the HNSW search as it is (recall@10 of 0.97 at ef = 80) and filter the top 10 afterwards. What do they find?

  1. Recall stays at 0.97, because the filter only removes results
  2. Results improve, because the filter removes noise
  3. The index automatically searches more deeply until it has 10 permitted results
  4. Most queries return far fewer than 10 results, often none, because only about 0.2 of the top 10 are expected to pass a 2% filter
If permission is independent of similarity, about 2% of the top 10 pass the filter: on average 0.2 results. The 0.97 recall is about neighbours of the query in the whole collection, not about neighbours that are allowed. Handling filters (searching until enough permitted results are found, or filtering inside the graph walk) is the subject of chapter 12, and it is a major reason systems with permissions need care.
