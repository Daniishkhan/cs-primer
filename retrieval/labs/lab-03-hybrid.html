Lab 3: Hybrid Retrieval & Reranking

Chapters 7, 8 and 10 argued that a lexical retriever and a dense retriever fail on different queries, that fusing their lists recovers some of what each misses, and that a model which reads the query and a passage together can fix what fusion cannot. This lab builds that whole pipeline on a collection of 60 judged queries: cosine search over vectors, a measurement of where each retriever fails, reciprocal rank fusion, a normalised score fusion with an honestly tuned weight, and a reranker. Every stage is scored with the metrics of Lab 2, so each claim in the chapters becomes a number you produced.

Why this lab matters

Most production retrieval is this pipeline with bigger parts: two first-stage retrievers, a merge, a reranker, and a team arguing about whether each stage earns its cost. The arguments are usually settled by three demo queries. Building it with a harness attached changes that. You will see a result that surprises people who have only read about hybrid search: reciprocal rank fusion can come out no better than the stronger retriever on its own, because an equal vote for a retriever that is blind on a third of the queries dilutes the good list. You will see a tuned fusion weight look better than it is until you score it honestly, and a cheap reranker with five hand-weighted features lift nDCG@10 by about eight points because it reads word order, which neither retriever can. And you will count the price of that reranker in passage reads, the number that decides whether it ships. The stages are the ones in chapter 10 and chapter 8; the discipline of measuring each one comes from chapter 4 and chapter 5.

A constructed collection and a stand-in embedder: read this before the numbers

The embedder is not a trained model. embedder.py turns text into vectors by hashing character trigrams, weighting them by a TF-IDF formula and normalising. It shares the shape of a real bi-encoder (a fixed-length vector per text, computed offline for documents, compared by cosine) and one useful behaviour (it matches spelling variants such as "cancel" and "cancelling"). It knows nothing about synonyms, so "money back" does not land near "refund", and it ignores word order. A trained embedder from chapter 7 would beat it on both. The collection is also constructed: 378 short passages and 60 queries written for this lab by make_data.py (hand-written cases plus seeded filler), with graded judgements from 0 to 3. Every number on this page describes this collection and this stand-in and nothing more general. The shapes (which retriever wins on which kind of query, why a fusion helps or does not, why a tuned weight flatters) are the lesson. Everything is deterministic: CRC32 hashing, no clock, no network. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. The full check takes about ten seconds.

What you'll build

Six milestones, from warm-up to the finished pipeline:

  1. Dense retrieval: cosine similarity, a heap-based top-k, and a retriever that embeds the query and searches the stored document vectors.
  2. Measure and compare: run a retriever over all queries, score nDCG@10 and recall@20 per query, split the queries by which retriever won, and compute the headroom a perfect merge could reach.
  3. Reciprocal rank fusion: merge ranked lists by position alone, with a constant k, optional weights and a candidate depth.
  4. Score fusion: min-max and z-score normalisation, a weighted sum with weight α on the lexical side, and an α chosen by leave-one-query-out instead of on the queries it is scored on.
  5. Pair features: query term coverage, adjacent-pair overlap in order, and identifier match: what a model that reads both texts can see and a single vector cannot.
  6. The reranker: a hand-weighted sum of those features plus the first-stage scores, applied to the top 30 candidates, with the cost counted in passages read.

The collection, in one paragraph

The 60 queries come in four kinds of 14, 18, 12 and 16, each built to separate the retrievers. Code queries contain an identifier ("E4021 payment gateway", "clause 14.2 supplier contract") and the collection holds near-identical sibling passages for neighbouring identifiers (E4022, E4023) that mean something else. Variant queries use another surface form of the answer's words ("cancelling subscriptions ahead of renewals" against a passage about cancelling a subscription before its renewal date, or a typo such as "pasword"). Order queries come with a mirror-image passage: "move money from checking to savings" has an answer and a passage that says "from savings to checking", with the same words in the opposite direction, judged 0. Plain queries overlap in both words and sub-words, and two have a keyword-stuffed distractor. Passage ids are opaque and shuffled, so an id never hints at which passage is right. Grades follow chapter 4: 3 answers the query, 1 is partly useful, 0 is judged and not relevant; every other passage is unjudged and counts as 0. Because most partly useful passages share almost no words with the query, both retrievers miss many of them: that is why recall@20 tops out near 0.8.

Milestone 1: dense retrieval

A dense retriever has an offline half and an online half, and the split is the whole design (chapter 7). Offline, every document is embedded once into a vector. Online, only the query is embedded, and the retriever returns the stored vectors closest to it. The document vector therefore cannot depend on the query, which is why a dense retriever is fast and also why it cannot read a pair. The starter gives you the offline half (DenseRetriever.__init__ embeds every document); you write the online half.

The similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors: their dot product divided by the product of their lengths (A0 chapter 5). The stand-in's vectors already have length 1, but your function should not assume it; a zero vector (a text with no letters or digits) has no direction and scores 0. For the top k, a full sort of every document is wasteful when k is 20 and the collection is a million: heapq.nsmallest keeps only k candidates in memory and costs O(n log k). Ties between equal cosines are broken by document id, as in every earlier lab, so a result never flickers between runs.

Before writing code, see what the stand-in can and cannot do. This script (save it next to the other files) prints the cosine of six pairs of strings:

Common misconception

"A dense retriever understands what the query means, so it is simply the better retriever." The stand-in is a reminder of what the vector actually is: a fixed-size summary of the text, built without knowing the query. A trained model summarises more usefully than character trigrams, and it does capture synonyms, but it is still a summary. It can blur two nearly identical identifiers or two opposite directions, and nothing in the cosine tells you when it has. That is why chapter 10 recommends measuring each retriever on its own before fusing them, which is the next milestone.

Solution spoiler 
def cosine(a, b):
    """Cosine similarity of two vectors; 0.0 if either has length zero."""
    na, nb = math.sqrt(dot(a, a)), math.sqrt(dot(b, b))
    if na == 0.0 or nb == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return dot(a, b) / (na * nb)


def cosine_topk(qvec, doc_vecs, k):
    """The k documents most similar to `qvec`: [(doc_id, cosine), ...], best first, ties by doc id."""
    if k <= 0:
        return []
    # heapq.nsmallest on (-cosine, id) keeps only k candidates in memory: O(n log k), not a full sort
    best = heapq.nsmallest(k, ((-cosine(qvec, v), d) for d, v in doc_vecs.items()))
    return [(d, -neg) for neg, d in best]


class DenseRetriever:
    """Offline: embed every document once. Online: embed the query, compare, return the top k."""

    def __init__(self, docs, embedder):
        self.embedder = embedder
        self.vecs = {d: embedder.embed(t) for d, t in docs.items()}

    def search(self, query, k=20):
        return cosine_topk(self.embedder.embed(query), self.vecs, k)

Milestone 2: measure both retrievers, and find the headroom

Chapter 10 opens with a rule that is easy to skip: measure each retriever alone before you merge them. The reason is practical. If one is far weaker, an equal-weight merge drags the better one down; if they fail on the same queries, merging adds nothing. You need three things from a measurement: one score per query (so you can see where each retriever fails, not only an average), a way to say which retriever won each query, and an upper bound on what any merge could achieve.

Your evaluate takes a search function, a function from query text and a depth k to a ranked list of (doc_id, score) pairs, so it works for a retriever's .search method, a fusion or a reranker alike. It calls the function once per query with k equal to the larger of the two cut-offs (20 here), reads off the grades of the ranked ids from the judgements, and returns nDCG@10 and recall@20 for every query plus their macro means. The ideal DCG comes from all of the query's judged grades, not just the retrieved ones, as Lab 2 established.

Then the diagnosis. winners splits queries by which of two systems did clearly better (by a margin of 0.2 nDCG here, because differences smaller than that on one query are noise), and union_recall is the number that bounds everything that follows: the recall of the set of passages found by either retriever in its top 20. A fusion can only reorder what the retrievers returned. If the union holds 0.81 of the relevant passages, no fusion or reranker working on those lists can exceed a recall of 0.81, and a pipeline that gets close to it has nothing left to gain from merging. The first script runs the three functions you are about to write; the second shows what each retriever returns for three queries.

The split is the story of chapter 10 in miniature. BM25 wins clearly on 9 queries and all of them are code queries: an identifier is a rare term, BM25's idf makes it decisive, and the dense stand-in spreads its attention over the sibling codes. Dense wins clearly on 12, ten of them variant queries that BM25 cannot match at all. On 39 queries neither wins by 0.2. Recall@20 shows the same asymmetry (0.625 against 0.792), and the union reaches 0.808, only a little above dense alone. The headroom for fusion on this collection is therefore mostly in ordering: the passages are mostly already in one of the two lists, and the question is whether the merge puts them near the top.

def evaluate(search_fn, coll, k_ndcg=10, k_recall=20):
    """Run every query and score it. `search_fn(text, k)` returns [(doc_id, score), ...]."""
    ndcg, recall = {}, {}
    depth = max(k_ndcg, k_recall)
    for q in coll.queries:
        qid, qrel = q["qid"], coll.qrels[q["qid"]]
        ranking = [d for d, _ in search_fn(q["text"], depth)]
        grades = grades_for(ranking, qrel)
        ndcg[qid] = ndcg_at_k(grades, k_ndcg, list(qrel.values()))
        recall[qid] = recall_at_k(grades, k_recall, coll.n_relevant(qid))
    return {"ndcg": ndcg, "recall": recall, "ndcg_mean": _mean(ndcg.values()), "recall_mean": _mean(recall.values())}


def winners(per_a, per_b, margin=0.2):
    """Split query ids by which system did clearly better: {"a": [...], "b": [...], "tie": [...]}."""
    out = {"a": [], "b": [], "tie": []}
    for qid in sorted(per_a):
        diff = per_a[qid] - per_b[qid]
        out["a" if diff > margin else "b" if diff < -margin else "tie"].append(qid)
    return out


def union_recall(search_a, search_b, coll, k=20):
    """Mean recall of the SET of documents in either system's top k: the most a perfect merge could reach."""
    total = 0.0
    for q in coll.queries:
        found = {d for d, _ in search_a(q["text"], k)} | {d for d, _ in search_b(q["text"], k)}
        qrel = coll.qrels[q["qid"]]
        hits = sum(1 for d in found if qrel.get(d, 0) >= 1)
        total += hits / coll.n_relevant(q["qid"])
    return total / len(coll.queries)

Milestone 3: reciprocal rank fusion

You now hold two ranked lists per query and the scores behind them. Chapter 10's first warning is that the scores cannot be added: BM25 is an unbounded sum of idf-weighted terms whose size depends on the query, while a cosine sits in a narrow band. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF; Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, 2009) avoids the problem by throwing the scores away and keeping only positions. Each document earns, from every list it appears in, a contribution that depends only on its rank r in that list, and the contributions are added:

RRF(d) = ∑lists w / ( k + ranklist(d) )

with ranks starting at 1, a constant k (60 in the original paper and in Elasticsearch) and a weight w per list that defaults to 1. A document missing from a list earns nothing from it. The values from chapter 10's worked example are your first hand-check: with k = 60, a document at ranks 1 and 1 scores 2/61 = 0.03279, one at ranks 3 and 2 scores 1/63 + 1/62 = 0.03200, and a document that only one list returned, at rank 4, scores 1/64 = 0.01562. Chapter 10 also derived a rule you will test: a document at rank r in both lists beats a document that is first in only one list exactly when r < k + 2. So k is a tolerance for how far down both lists a document may sit and still outrank a solitary first place.

The second function wraps this into a search function you can hand to evaluate. Two details are easy to get wrong and both are in the check. First, depth: ask each retriever for depth results (50 by default), not for the final k, because fusion can only reorder what the lists contain and a document at rank 40 in both lists should still be able to win. Second, identity and ties: fused scores often tie, so order by score descending and then by document id, using the given rank helper. The check also multiplies one retriever's scores by a million and expects an identical result; if yours changes, you used the scores.

Solution spoiler 
def rrf_scores(rankings, k=60, weights=None):
    """Sum of weight / (k + rank) over the lists a document appears in (ranks start at 1)."""
    weights = weights or [1.0] * len(rankings)
    out = {}
    for w, ranking in zip(weights, rankings):
        for r, d in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            out[d] = out.get(d, 0.0) + w / (k + r)
    return out


def make_rrf(retrievers, depth=50, rrf_k=60, weights=None):
    """A search function that asks each retriever for `depth` results and fuses the rankings."""
    def search(text, k=20):
        rankings = [[d for d, _ in r.search(text, depth)] for r in retrievers]
        fused = rrf_scores(rankings, rrf_k, weights)
        return [(d, fused[d]) for d in rank(fused)[:k]]
    return search

This is the surprising table. RRF recovers BM25's misses on recall (0.800, against 0.625 for BM25 and 0.792 for dense), but its nDCG@10 of 0.782 is no better than dense alone (0.786), and no setting in the table changes that by more than about 0.01: k from 1 to 200, the weights and the depth all leave it within a point of dense alone. Chapter 10 explained why this can happen. Fusion rewards agreement, and on the 18 variant queries BM25 returns only a handful of passages that match on a stray word, none of them relevant, and each of those earns a contribution that lifts it into the fused list among the dense results. On the code queries the dense list puts sibling codes high and RRF counts them as votes. The equal vote costs the stronger retriever on the queries where the weaker one is blind, and gains on the queries where the weaker one is right, and on this mix the two nearly cancel. Chapter 10 said it plainly: RRF's value is a floor that needs no tuning, and a gain over the better retriever is not guaranteed.

Common misconception

"Hybrid search always beats both retrievers." Fused recall can only be as good as the union of the lists, and fused ranking is a separate question. Here RRF has better recall than either retriever and no better nDCG than the stronger one. Before you ship a fusion, compare it with each retriever alone on judged queries (milestone 2) and with a paired interval (Lab 2); with 60 queries a difference of 0.004 is nowhere near an effect.

Milestone 4: score fusion, and a weight tuned honestly

The other family of fusion keeps the scores but fixes their scales first. Rescale each list to a common range and add the lists with a weight. Min-max normalisation maps a list's lowest score to 0 and its highest to 1: (s − min) / (max − min). The z-score subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation (here the population version, dividing by n, so that three scores 1, 2, 3 become −1.2247, 0, 1.2247). Both have a weak spot that chapter 10 spelled out: one outlier compresses everything else. A BM25 list with scores 41.0, 6.1, 5.9, 5.8 and 5.5 becomes 1.00, 0.017, 0.011, 0.008 and 0.000 under min-max, and the four lower passages are no longer distinguishable. A list with no spread at all (every score equal) must become all zeros rather than a division by zero.

The convex combination is then score(d) = α · norm(lexical) + (1 − α) · norm(dense), with a document that is missing from a list counting as 0 for that list. The weight α says how much to trust the lexical side. In this collection that is the interesting number: BM25 is decisive on code queries and blind on variants, and a single global α has to compromise.

Choosing α is where fusion experiments go wrong. The tempting procedure is to sweep α from 0 to 1, take the value with the best mean nDCG@10 and report that mean. The reported number is then the best of eleven tries on the same 60 queries, which is optimistic by construction. The honest estimate is leave-one-query-out: for each query, choose α on the other 59, then score the chosen α on the one left out, and average those 60 held-out scores. The result is what you would get if α were chosen on a sample and used on new queries, and it is usually lower. The starter gives you alpha_table, which builds {alpha: [nDCG@10 for each query]} with each query's candidate lists fetched only once; you write the two functions that read it.

Choosing α precisely matters less than avoiding the extremes. None of these differences is large next to the interval a 60-query set gives you, which is the point of the next callout.

Common misconception

"I tuned α on 60 judged queries, so 0.831 is what production will see." Tuning on the queries you evaluate on is selection bias: the maximum of eleven noisy estimates is higher, on average, than the value of any one of them. The leave-one-out figure is a better estimate but it is still one collection of 60 queries; a production query log has different proportions of code and variant queries, and the best α moves with them. Re-tune after any change to the analyser, the embedder or the chunking, and report an interval, not a point.

def min_max(scores):
    """Rescale to [0, 1] (lowest 0, highest 1). A list with no spread becomes all zeros."""
    lo, hi = min(scores.values()), max(scores.values())
    return {d: (s - lo) / (hi - lo) if hi > lo else 0.0 for d, s in scores.items()}


def z_score(scores):
    """(s - mean) / population standard deviation. A list with no spread becomes all zeros."""
    vals = list(scores.values())
    m = sum(vals) / len(vals)
    sd = math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in vals) / len(vals))
    return {d: (s - m) / sd if sd else 0.0 for d, s in scores.items()}


def convex(a, b, alpha, norm=min_max):
    """alpha * norm(a) + (1 - alpha) * norm(b); a document missing from a list counts as 0 for it."""
    na, nb = norm(a) if a else {}, norm(b) if b else {}
    return {d: alpha * na.get(d, 0.0) + (1 - alpha) * nb.get(d, 0.0) for d in set(na) | set(nb)}


def make_convex(lexical, dense, alpha=0.5, depth=50, norm=min_max):
    """A search function: weighted sum of the two retrievers' normalised score lists."""
    def search(text, k=20):
        fused = convex(dict(lexical.search(text, depth)), dict(dense.search(text, depth)), alpha, norm)
        return [(d, fused[d]) for d in rank(fused)[:k]]
    return search


def best_alpha(table):
    """`table` is {alpha: [nDCG per query]}. The alpha with the highest mean; ties go to the smaller alpha."""
    return max(sorted(table), key=lambda a: _mean(table[a]))


def leave_one_out(table):
    """Choose alpha on all queries but one, score the one left out; repeat for every query.

    Returns (mean held-out score, [alpha chosen for each query])."""
    n = len(next(iter(table.values())))
    held, chosen = [], []
    for i in range(n):
        rest = {a: v[:i] + v[i + 1:] for a, v in table.items()}
        a = best_alpha(rest)
        chosen.append(a)
        held.append(table[a][i])
    return _mean(held), chosen

Milestone 5: features of a query-passage pair

Fusion changes the order of two lists that were each made without looking at the other side. What neither list can do is read the query and a passage together, and chapter 8 showed why that matters: the document vector is fixed before the query arrives, so whatever the vector forgot is gone. A cross-encoder is a neural model that takes the query and the passage as one input and outputs a relevance score. You cannot train one here, so this lab uses the idea it embodies with a deliberately transparent stand-in: compute a few pair features, numbers that depend on both texts, and combine them with weights. The features are the evidence a real cross-encoder would exploit, written out.

Texts are reduced to content terms with the given stems(text) (lower-case, stop words removed, each word cut to its first five letters, identifiers containing a digit kept whole). That crude stemming is what lets "cancelling" and "cancel" (cance) meet. Then three features:

  • Coverage: the fraction of the distinct query terms that appear anywhere in the passage. It rewards a passage that mentions everything the query asked for.
  • Bigram overlap: the fraction of the query's adjacent term pairs that occur adjacent and in the same order in the passage. For "move money from checking to savings", the content terms are move, money, check, savin and the pairs are (move, money), (money, check) and (check, savin). A passage that runs the other way, "from savings to checking", keeps only the first pair: 1/3. This single feature is what separates the two directions, which neither the cosine nor BM25 can.
  • Identifier match: among the query's tokens that contain a digit, the fraction that appear verbatim in the passage. It is a guard against a near-identical sibling code, and it is 0.0 for a query with no identifier.

The pair feature vector also carries the cosine and the BM25 score of the pair, passed in by the caller; BM25 is scaled to 0..1 within the candidate list so that it is comparable with the others. The order is fixed by FEATURES = ["cosine", "bm25", "coverage", "bigram", "id_match"] so the weights of milestone 6 line up. This script computes the three features you write for five passages:

def coverage(q_terms, d_terms):
    """Fraction of the DISTINCT query terms that occur somewhere in the document (0.0 if the query is empty)."""
    wanted = set(q_terms)
    if not wanted:
        return 0.0
    return len(wanted & set(d_terms)) / len(wanted)


def bigram_overlap(q_terms, d_terms):
    """Fraction of the query's adjacent term pairs that occur adjacent, in the same order, in the document.

    0.0 if the query has fewer than two terms."""
    q_pairs = list(zip(q_terms, q_terms[1:]))
    if not q_pairs:
        return 0.0
    d_pairs = set(zip(d_terms, d_terms[1:]))
    return sum(1 for p in q_pairs if p in d_pairs) / len(q_pairs)


def id_match(query, doc_text):
    """Among the query's tokens that contain a digit (identifiers), the fraction found verbatim in the document.

    0.0 if the query has no such token. Tokens come from `analyse`, so 'E4021-B' gives 'e4021' and 'b'."""
    wanted = [t for t in analyse(query) if any(c.isdigit() for c in t)]
    if not wanted:
        return 0.0
    have = set(analyse(doc_text))
    return sum(1 for t in wanted if t in have) / len(wanted)


def pair_features(query, doc_text, cos, bm25_norm):
    """[cosine, bm25 (already scaled to 0..1), coverage, bigram_overlap, id_match], in FEATURES order."""
    q_terms, d_terms = stems(query), stems(doc_text)
    return [cos, bm25_norm, coverage(q_terms, d_terms), bigram_overlap(q_terms, d_terms), id_match(query, doc_text)]

Milestone 6: the reranker, and what it costs

A reranker takes the top candidates from a first stage, scores each (query, passage) pair, and reorders them. It runs on a few dozen candidates and not on the collection because reading a pair is expensive: for a real cross-encoder every pair is a forward pass through a neural network, and the cost grows with the candidate depth (chapter 8 sizes this properly). Here the cost is counted in pairs read: your reranker increments pairs_scored for every candidate it scores, so you can plot gain against cost the way you would plot recall against latency.

The score is a weighted sum of the five features, WEIGHTS = [1, 1, 1, 2, 1]: cosine, BM25, coverage, bigram (doubled, because getting the order right is the thing a pair reader adds) and identifier match. These are round numbers set once by hand and then left alone; the lab did not fit them to the 60 queries, but they were chosen knowing what kinds of query the collection contains, so treat the resulting gain as optimistic in the way milestone 4 warned about. A learned reranker would fit its parameters on training pairs that are kept apart from the evaluation queries. The weights are an argument, so you can ablate them.

The candidate list comes from a first stage, RRF in the main script. The reranker asks it for depth candidates (30 by default), embeds the query once, gets BM25 scores for the query, and scales BM25 to 0..1 within the candidates: dividing by the largest BM25 score among them, or by nothing if that is 0. Scaling within the candidate list is the same per-query normalisation as min-max, and for the same reason: a raw BM25 score is not comparable between queries. Then the features, the weighted sum, and the top k of the reordered list, ties by document id. The reranker never adds a passage the first stage did not return, which is why recall after reranking depends on the first stage's depth and ranking depends on the reranker.

def rerank_score(features, weights=WEIGHTS):
    """The weighted sum of the features."""
    return sum(w * f for w, f in zip(weights, features))


class Reranker:
    """Take a first stage's top `depth` candidates, score each (query, passage) pair, reorder.

    `first_stage(text, depth)` returns [(doc_id, score)]; `dense` supplies cosines (its .vecs and
    .embedder); `bm25` supplies BM25 scores. `pairs_scored` counts how many pairs were read: the cost."""

    def __init__(self, first_stage, docs, dense, bm25, depth=30, weights=WEIGHTS):
        self.first_stage, self.docs, self.dense, self.bm25 = first_stage, docs, dense, bm25
        self.depth, self.weights = depth, weights
        self.pairs_scored = 0

    def search(self, text, k=20):
        cands = [d for d, _ in self.first_stage(text, self.depth)]
        if not cands:
            return []
        qvec = self.dense.embedder.embed(text)
        lex = self.bm25.scores(text)
        top = max((lex.get(d, 0.0) for d in cands), default=0.0)       # scale BM25 within THIS candidate list
        scored = {}
        for d in cands:
            self.pairs_scored += 1
            f = pair_features(text, self.docs[d], cosine(qvec, self.dense.vecs[d]), lex.get(d, 0.0) / top if top else 0.0)
            scored[d] = rerank_score(f, self.weights)
        return [(d, scored[d]) for d in rank(scored)[:k]]

Systems connection

Hybrid retrieval is a scatter-gather: the query goes to two backends and the answer is as slow as the slower of them, so the tail-latency arithmetic of tail latency at scale applies, and each retriever needs a deadline and a fallback (return the other list alone, flagged as degraded). The reranker is a second, serial fan-out of depth independent reads, which is why it is usually batched on an accelerator and why its depth is a latency-budget decision as much as a quality one. The index underneath the dense side is a specialised store, and the next lab builds the approximate nearest-neighbour graph that replaces the brute-force cosine scan you wrote in milestone 1.

  • Swap in a real model. If you have an embedding model installed, replace TrigramEmbedder with a wrapper that calls it (same embed method) and compare the dense row. The harness does not change.

What to take away

  • Measure each retriever alone and per query before merging. The split by query kind (BM25 wins on identifiers, dense on spelling variants) predicts what fusion can do, and the recall of the union is the ceiling.
  • Reciprocal rank fusion needs no scores and no tuning, recovers recall, and is a safe default, but it is not guaranteed to beat the stronger retriever's ranking. Here it did not.
  • A normalised weighted sum has one parameter and a plateau of good values. Tune it by leave-one-query-out and quote the held-out number, which is lower than the best in-sample one.
  • A reranker earns its place by reading what a single vector forgets, here word order. Its gain, its depth and its cost in pairs read are three numbers to report together, and an ablation shows which features are doing the work.
  • Every figure above describes a constructed collection and a stand-in embedder. The method transfers; the numbers do not.

With k = 60, passage A is first in the BM25 list and absent from the dense list. Passage B is ranked 30th in both lists. Which is higher after reciprocal rank fusion?

  1. B, with 2/90 = 0.0222 against 1/61 = 0.0164
  2. A, because a first place always wins
  3. They tie
  4. It depends on the raw BM25 score of A
B beats A whenever its rank in both lists is below k + 2 = 62, and 30 is. RRF uses positions only, so the raw BM25 score is irrelevant (option 4). You sweep the fusion weight α from 0 to 1 on all 60 judged queries and find the best mean nDCG@10 is 0.831 at α = 0.7. Which figure is the more honest estimate of what a deployed system would score?

  1. 0.831, because it is the maximum
  2. The mean of the eleven sweep values, because it averages out luck
  3. 0.823, because α = 0.5 is the conventional default
  4. The leave-one-query-out estimate (0.812 here), where α is chosen without the query it is scored on
The maximum of eleven estimates computed on the same queries is biased upwards: some of its height is luck that α has been fitted to. Neither BM25, the dense stand-in, nor fusion of the two can separate "move money from checking to savings" from "move money from savings to checking", but the reranker does. Why?

  1. The reranker uses a larger embedding model
  2. Both first-stage representations are order-blind bags of terms or sub-words built without the query, while the reranker's bigram feature is computed from the query and the passage together and depends on order
  3. The reranker looks at more candidates, so it eventually finds the right one
  4. Fusion removes the order information, and the reranker restores it
The two passages contain the same words, so BM25 scores them equally and the stand-in gives them identical vectors (cosine 1.000). Fusing two lists that both tie cannot break the tie. In the depth experiment, reranking RRF's top 10 scored nDCG@10 0.865 and recall@20 0.783, and its top 30 scored 0.867 and 0.817, at one third of the pair reads for the top 10. What is the best conclusion for this collection?

  1. Always use depth 10: the cheaper pipeline is always the better one
  2. Always use depth 30: more candidates can only help
  3. The ordering of the top ten is decided almost entirely within the first ten candidates, so depth 10 gives nearly all the nDCG gain; deeper lists only add recall at ranks 11 to 20, which matters if the reranker's output feeds a consumer that reads more than ten
  4. The two settings are identical, because the nDCG@10 values are within 0.002
The metrics measure different things. nDCG@10 is almost unchanged, while recall@20 rises by 0.034 because the reranker can promote passages from ranks 11 to 30 into the top 20. Whether that matters depends on how the output is used (a RAG prompt that takes 20 passages versus a results page of 10), which no number in the table can decide for you. Options 1 and 2 turn a trade-off into a rule, and option 4 ignores the second metric.

A BM25 score list for one query is 41.0, 6.1, 5.9, 5.8, 5.5. After min-max normalisation, what has happened to the four lower passages, and what is the consequence for a weighted sum with a dense list?

  1. They are removed from the list, so only the top passage can be returned
  2. They are squeezed to between 0.000 and 0.017, so BM25 can barely tell them apart and the dense side decides their order
  3. They keep their relative gaps, so BM25 and dense contribute equally
  4. They all become 1.0, since normalisation raises the minimum
The range is 41.0 − 5.5 = 35.5, so 6.1 maps to 0.6/35.5 = 0.017 and 5.5 to 0. One outlier uses the whole scale. The four lower passages differ by about 0.01 of the lexical side, while the dense side still spreads its own list over 0 to 1, so it decides their order. Z-scores or rank-based fusion are less sensitive to a single high score. Nothing is removed (option 1) and the relative gaps are not preserved at the scale that matters (option 3).
