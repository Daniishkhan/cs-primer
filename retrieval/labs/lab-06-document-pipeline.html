Lab 6: A Document Understanding Pipeline

Chapters 18, 19 and 20 argued that an OCR engine hands you words and boxes, not a document, and that everything a retrieval system needs (reading order, sections, tables, fields) has to be rebuilt from geometry and then checked against a measurement. This lab rebuilds it on a four-page synthetic report: you write the character error rate, group words into lines, put a two-column page in reading order with XY-cut, strip the running header and footer, find paragraphs and sections, recover a table's cell grid from word boxes, and read a form into typed fields with a review queue. Every stage is scored against the truth the pages were made from, so each claim in the chapters becomes a number you produced.

Why this lab matters

Most document pipelines fail quietly. The characters are mostly right, so nobody looks, and then a chunk is built from a left column spliced into a right one, or a table becomes a stream of numbers without headers, or the page number "3" ends up inside a sentence. Retrieval embeds the damage. This lab makes the damage visible and measurable. On the two-column page you will see a character error rate of 0.70 from a transcription whose own error is about 1 per cent, because the reading order is wrong, and then 0.011 once XY-cut is in place. You will see a table whose structure is perfectly recovered and whose exact-match score is 0.96 because the engine misread one digit, and a form field read at 0.93 confidence that is wrong and is caught only by a validator. The method of each stage is in chapter 18, chapter 19 and chapter 20; case study C7 shows how two real open pipelines were evaluated, and studio S3 asks you to design one at scale.

Synthetic pages and a simulated OCR engine: read this before the numbers

The OCR is simulated. make_data.py is a small typesetter. It lays out a four-page report of an invented council, then plays the part of an OCR engine: each word gets a box, a transcription that is occasionally wrong (a letter confused with a look-alike, such as 0 for O), and a confidence that is only loosely related to whether it is wrong. Of 692 words, 31 are misread, a character error rate of 0.9 per cent before any of your code runs. Table and form regions are given, as if by a layout detector (chapter 18 treats detection as a separate step); recognition of what is inside them is yours. The pages are far cleaner than real scans: no skew, no noise, one font. Every figure on this page describes these four pages and this simulated engine, and the method transfers where the numbers do not. Everything is deterministic and seeded; Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only; the full check takes about a second.

What you'll build

Six milestones, from the measuring stick to a finished pipeline:

  1. The character error rate: edit distance by dynamic programming, then CER with a stated normalisation, so every later stage can be scored.
  2. Lines and reading order: cluster words into lines that keep columns apart, then order lines with recursive XY-cut and compare it with sorting by position.
  3. Running headers and footers: find lines that repeat in the page margins across pages, tolerate an OCR slip in one of them, and never remove a line from the middle of a page.
  4. Paragraphs and sections: join lines into paragraphs (and undo hyphenation), then detect headings by size and numbering and build a section outline with each section's text.
  5. A table from boxes: rows from vertical position, columns from whitespace that most rows leave empty, cell spans from overlap, scored by cell F1.
  6. A form into typed fields: match labels (with a misread tolerated), pair each with its value inline, to the right or below, and decide which fields a person should check.

Milestone 1: the character error rate

Chapter 19 defined the standard measure of recognition. The edit distance (Levenshtein, 1966) between two strings is the fewest single-character insertions, deletions and substitutions that turn one into the other, and the character error rate (CER) divides it by the length of the reference:

CER = (S + D + I) / Nref

It is a cost, not a probability: it can exceed 1 when the hypothesis has many insertions. You compute the distance by dynamic programming, filling a table whose cell (i, j) is the distance between the first i characters of one string and the first j of the other. Each cell is the cheapest of three moves: a deletion from the cell above, an insertion from the cell to the left, or a substitution from the diagonal, which costs nothing if the two characters are equal. Row i needs only row i − 1, so two rows are enough, and that matters on a page of 1,500 characters.

The second half of the milestone is a policy. A CER without a normalisation rule cannot be compared with another, so cer normalises both strings first (the given normalise: compatibility normalisation, which turns the ligature ﬁ into "fi", and runs of whitespace into one space) and says so. Try five pairs, and then the two cautions from chapter 19 about what order does and what accuracy means for a number you act on:

Common misconception

"Our OCR is 99 per cent accurate, so the text is reliable." The 99 is an average over characters, and the characters that matter (digits, names, units, negations) are a small minority; errors also cluster on poor scans, so they are not independent. Report CER on normalised text, report it for the fields you act on, and look at the worst pages. The fixture's raw engine is at 0.9 per cent CER overall, and the registration read as "KXl9" instead of "KX19" at a confidence of 0.93 is one of the errors that matters.

def edit_distance(a, b):
    """Levenshtein distance between two strings: the fewest single-character insertions,"""
    # Two rows of the dynamic-programming table are enough: row i only needs row i - 1.
    prev = list(range(len(b) + 1))
    for i, ca in enumerate(a, 1):
        cur = [i]
        for j, cb in enumerate(b, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1,                  # delete ca
                           cur[j - 1] + 1,               # insert cb
                           prev[j - 1] + (ca != cb)))    # substitute (free if equal)
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]


def cer(ref, hyp, normalise_text=True):
    """Character error rate: edit_distance(ref, hyp) / len(ref), after `normalise` if asked."""
    if normalise_text:
        ref, hyp = normalise(ref), normalise(hyp)
    if not ref:
        return 0.0 if not hyp else 1.0
    return edit_distance(ref, hyp) / len(ref)

Milestone 2: lines, and reading order by XY-cut

An OCR engine reports words: a box, a string, a confidence. It reports them in whatever order it found them, and the fixture's order is shuffled to make the point. Two steps turn them into text in reading order.

First, lines. A line is the set of words that sit on the same row. Two words belong together if they overlap vertically by at least half the smaller height and the horizontal gap between them is small, at most gap_factor times the taller height. The second condition is what separates columns: a word space is about one character wide (6 points here), a column gutter is 23. Both conditions scale with the type, so a 22-point title keeps its 13-point word spaces and still counts as one line. Chains matter: A joins B, B joins C, so you grow each line as you go. make_line (given) builds the line dict from its words.

Second, reading order, the sequence in which a person would read the blocks of a page (chapter 18). Sorting by top edge and then left edge is right for one column and wrong for two: left line, right line, left line. Recursive XY-cut (Nagy and Seth, 1984) is the classic fix. Look for the widest empty band that cuts the page horizontally or vertically; split there; recurse on each part. A horizontal cut puts the top part first, a vertical cut the left part first. If no band is wider than a minimum (4 points here), stop and fall back to sorting. Here is what it sees on page 1:

Common misconception

"XY-cut solves reading order." It solves pages that can be cut by straight lines, and only when the gaps you want to cut at are the widest ones. A magazine-style pinwheel (four blocks round a pull-quote) has no straight cut, and the fall-back sort puts blocks in the wrong place; a page whose paragraphs are spaced further apart than its gutter is cut across the columns. The check includes a pinwheel and expects the fall-back. In production, flag pages where the fall-back fired and send them to a heavier method or a person.

# =============================================================================
def group_lines(words, gap_factor=1.5):
    """Group words into lines. Two words are on one line if they overlap vertically by at least"""
    lines = []                                           # each: {"words": [...], "x1", "y0", "y1"}
    for w in sorted(words, key=lambda w: (w["box"][0], w["box"][1])):
        x0, y0, x1, y1 = w["box"]
        h = y1 - y0
        best = None
        for ln in lines:
            overlap = min(y1, ln["y1"]) - max(y0, ln["y0"])
            if overlap < 0.5 * min(h, ln["y1"] - ln["y0"]):
                continue
            gap = x0 - ln["x1"]
            if gap <= gap_factor * max(h, ln["y1"] - ln["y0"]):
                best = ln
                break
        if best is None:
            lines.append({"words": [w], "x1": x1, "y0": y0, "y1": y1})
        else:
            best["words"].append(w)
            best["x1"] = max(best["x1"], x1)
            best["y0"], best["y1"] = min(best["y0"], y0), max(best["y1"], y1)
    return naive_order([make_line(ln["words"]) for ln in lines])


def _widest_gap(items, axis, min_gap):
    """The widest empty band along one axis. axis 0 = x (a vertical cut), 1 = y (a horizontal cut)."""
    s = sorted(items, key=lambda it: it["bbox"][axis])
    reach = s[0]["bbox"][axis + 2]
    best, at = 0.0, None
    for n, it in enumerate(s[1:], 1):
        gap = it["bbox"][axis] - reach
        if gap > best:
            best, at = gap, n
        reach = max(reach, it["bbox"][axis + 2])
    if at is None or best <= min_gap:
        return 0.0, None, None
    return best, s[:at], s[at:]


def xy_cut(items, min_gap=4.0):
    """Recursive XY-cut (Nagy and Seth, 1984; chapter 18). Find the widest empty band, horizontal"""
    items = list(items)
    if len(items) <= 1:
        return items
    gx, lx, rx = _widest_gap(items, 0, min_gap)
    gy, ty, by = _widest_gap(items, 1, min_gap)
    if gx == 0.0 and gy == 0.0:
        return naive_order(items)
    first, second = (lx, rx) if gx > gy else (ty, by)    # cut along the widest whitespace first
    return xy_cut(first, min_gap) + xy_cut(second, min_gap)

Milestone 3: running headers and footers

Every page of a report carries the same header and a page number, and an extractor that keeps them puts "Harbourside Council | Annual Review 2026" and "Page 2 of 4" into the middle of a sentence when pages are concatenated. They are boilerplate: text that repeats on many pages and is not content. The classic detector is repetition: a line in the top or bottom margin that occurs on several pages is a running header or footer. Three details decide whether it works.

  • Shape, not text. "Page 2 of 4" and "Page 3 of 4" differ by a digit. Compare lines by their shape (the given shape lower-cases and turns each run of digits into #), and both become page # of #.
  • Zone as well as text. A line is a candidate only if it sits wholly in the top or bottom margin (8 per cent of the page height here). A body line that says the same words in the middle of a page is content, whatever else it says.
  • Fuzzy repetition. The engine read page 3's header as "Councii". An exact comparison would see two different headers and keep both. Treat two shapes as the same line if cer(a, b) is at most 0.15, which uses your milestone 1. And require evidence: a cluster must occur on at least two pages, and on at least half of them (max(2, ceil(0.5 * n))), because one page shows no repetition at all.

Common misconception

"A page number is text at the bottom that looks like a number, so strip any such line." Rules like that eat table totals, footnote markers and the year in a title. Detecting boilerplate by repetition across pages in a fixed zone is more robust than detecting it by content, and it fails more gracefully: on a one-page document it removes nothing, which is correct. What it cannot see is a page number that appears only once, or a header that changes by section; both want a layout model that labels page-header and page-footer regions, as DocLayNet's classes do (chapter 18).

# =============================================================================
# Milestone 3: running headers and footers
# =============================================================================
def find_boilerplate(pages_lines, page_height, margin=0.08, tol=0.15, min_share=0.5):
    """Find lines that repeat in the top or bottom margin across pages: running headers, footers,"""
    need = max(2, math.ceil(min_share * len(pages_lines)))
    clusters = []                                        # [zone, representative shape, set of pages]
    for p, lines in enumerate(pages_lines):
        for ln in lines:
            zone = zone_of(ln, page_height, margin)
            if zone is None:
                continue
            s = shape(ln["text"])
            for c in clusters:
                if c[0] == zone and cer(c[1], s) <= tol:
                    c[2].add(p)
                    break
            else:
                clusters.append([zone, s, {p}])
    return [(z, s) for z, s, pages in clusters if len(pages) >= need]


def strip_boilerplate(lines, boilerplate, page_height, margin=0.08, tol=0.15):
    """Return `lines` without those in a margin zone that match a boilerplate (zone, shape)"""
    kept = []
    for ln in lines:
        zone = zone_of(ln, page_height, margin)
        s = shape(ln["text"])
        if zone is not None and any(z == zone and cer(b, s) <= tol for z, b in boilerplate):
            continue
        kept.append(ln)
    return kept

Milestone 4: paragraphs and the section outline

Lines in reading order are still not structure. A chunker (chapter 14) wants paragraphs to cut between and headings to start sections at, and a retrieval index wants the heading to travel with the text it introduces. Two functions recover both from geometry.

lines_to_paragraphs merges lines. A new paragraph starts when the gap above the line is wider than half the previous line's height (a line gap is 2 points, a paragraph gap 8 to 12), when the line height changes by more than 15 per cent (a heading beside body text), or at a column break, when the next line is higher on the page and the previous line ended a sentence. A column break in the middle of a sentence is a continuation: on page 1 the paragraph "…although it depends on" at the foot of the left column and "residents continuing to separate glass…" at the head of the right is one paragraph that happens to cross the gutter. Inside a paragraph, lines are joined with a space, except that a line-end hyphen after a letter, followed by a lower-case start, is removed and the pieces fuse: "infra-" and "structure" become "infrastructure". That rule is a trade-off, not a truth: it also fuses a real compound that happened to break at its hyphen, and it keeps the hyphen when the next word is capitalised.

detect_sections then reads the paragraphs of the whole document. def lines_to_paragraphs(lines, gap_ratio=0.5):
    """Merge lines, already in reading order, into paragraphs: {"text", "bbox", "height", "n_lines"}."""
    paras = []
    prev = None
    for ln in lines:
        x0, y0, x1, y1 = ln["bbox"]
        new = prev is None
        if prev is not None:
            px0, py0, px1, py1 = prev["bbox"]
            ph = py1 - py0
            if y0 - py1 > gap_ratio * ph:
                new = True
            elif abs(ln["height"] - prev["height"]) > 0.15 * prev["height"]:
                new = True
            elif y0 < py0 and prev["text"].rstrip()[-1:] in TERMINAL:
                new = True
        if new:
            paras.append({"text": ln["text"], "bbox": list(ln["bbox"]), "height": ln["height"], "n_lines": 1})
        else:
            p = paras[-1]
            p["text"] = _join(p["text"], ln["text"])
            p["bbox"] = [min(p["bbox"][0], x0), min(p["bbox"][1], y0), max(p["bbox"][2], x1), max(p["bbox"][3], y1)]
            p["n_lines"] += 1
        prev = ln
    return paras


def detect_sections(paragraphs):
    """Turn the document's paragraphs (all pages, in order, each with a "page") into sections:"""
    bh = body_height(paragraphs)
    sections = []
    for p in paragraphs:
        text = p["text"]
        level = None
        if p["n_lines"] == 1 and len(text.split()) <= 12 and text.rstrip()[-1:] not in ".,;":
            m = NUMBERED.match(text)
            if p["height"] >= 1.8 * bh:
                level = 0
            elif m:
                level = len(m.group(1).split("."))
            elif p["height"] >= 1.25 * bh:
                level = 1
        if level is not None:
            sections.append({"level": level, "title": text, "page": p.get("page"), "text": ""})
        else:
            if not sections:
                sections.append({"level": 0, "title": "", "page": p.get("page"), "text": ""})
            cur = sections[-1]
            cur["text"] = (cur["text"] + " " + text).strip()
    return sections

Milestone 5: a table from word boxes

A table read as running text is a stream of numbers whose headers are somewhere else (chapter 20). To recover the grid you need table structure recognition: rows, columns, and cells that span several columns. Detection (where the table is) is given; here you rebuild its structure from the boxes of the words inside it, with the whitespace method of chapter 20. It has four steps.

  1. Rows. Cluster the words by vertical position: a word joins the current row if its centre is within 0.6 of its height of the row's mean centre. Number the rows from the top.
  2. Cells. Within a row, sort by x and join consecutive words whose gap is below min_gap (12 points). "Planning applications" stays one cell, and so does "Requests (thousands)".
  3. Columns. An x position is empty in a row if no cell of that row covers it. An x is a column break candidate if it is empty in at least vote (70 per cent) of the rows; a run of such positions at least min_gap wide, not touching the table's left or right edge, is a separator, and you take its centre. The vote is what lets a spanning heading, which occupies a gutter in its own row only, leave that separator standing.
  4. Placement. A cell's first column is the strip containing its left edge plus one; its last is the strip containing its right edge minus one; its colspan is the difference plus one.

Common misconception

"Split each row on runs of spaces." Rows are not independent: a cell that is empty in one row is not a column break, and a heading that spans two columns is not two cells. The grid is decided by all the rows voting on where the whitespace valleys are. It also fails abruptly, as chapter 20's drift experiment showed: one wrong decision about a column boundary corrupts every cell that depends on it, so the score falls off a cliff and not along a slope. The fixture has no skew; if you want to see the cliff, shear the table's words in make_data.py and watch the structure F1.

# =============================================================================
# Milestone 5: a table from boxes
# =============================================================================
def build_table(words, min_gap=12.0, vote=0.7):
    """Rebuild a table's cell grid from word boxes (chapter 20's whitespace method)."""
    if not words:
        return []
    # 1. rows
    rows = []                                            # each: {"cy": mean centre, "words": [...]}
    for w in sorted(words, key=lambda w: (w["box"][1] + w["box"][3]) / 2):
        cy, h = (w["box"][1] + w["box"][3]) / 2, w["box"][3] - w["box"][1]
        if rows and abs(cy - rows[-1]["cy"]) <= 0.6 * h:
            rows[-1]["words"].append(w)
            rows[-1]["cy"] = sum((v["box"][1] + v["box"][3]) / 2 for v in rows[-1]["words"]) / len(rows[-1]["words"])
        else:
            rows.append({"cy": cy, "words": [w]})
    # 2. cells in each row
    row_cells = []
    for r in rows:
        cells = []
        for w in sorted(r["words"], key=lambda w: w["box"][0]):
            x0, x1 = w["box"][0], w["box"][2]
            if cells and x0 - cells[-1]["x1"] < min_gap:
                cells[-1]["x1"] = max(cells[-1]["x1"], x1)
                cells[-1]["text"] += " " + w["text"]
            else:
                cells.append({"x0": x0, "x1": x1, "text": w["text"]})
        row_cells.append(cells)
    # 3. column separators from whitespace that most rows leave empty
    lo = int(math.floor(min(w["box"][0] for w in words)))
    hi = int(math.ceil(max(w["box"][2] for w in words)))
    empty = []
    for x in range(lo, hi + 1):
        blanks = sum(1 for cells in row_cells if not any(c["x0"] <= x <= c["x1"] for c in cells))
        empty.append(blanks >= vote * len(row_cells))
    seps, i = [], 0
    while i < len(empty):
        if empty[i]:
            j = i
            while j < len(empty) and empty[j]:
                j += 1
            if j - i >= min_gap and i > 0 and j < len(empty):
                seps.append(lo + (i + j - 1) / 2)
            i = j
        else:
            i += 1

    def strip(x):
        return sum(1 for s in seps if s < x)             # how many separators lie to the left of x
    # 4. place the cells
    out = []
    for r, cells in enumerate(row_cells):
        for c in cells:
            first, last = strip(c["x0"] + 1), strip(c["x1"] - 1)
            out.append((r, first, last - first + 1, c["text"]))
    return sorted(out)

Milestone 6: a form into typed fields, and what to review

A form is the other common shape: labels and values. Key-value extraction finds the label ("Date of birth"), finds its value, and returns the pair under a canonical field name. The association is spatial, and the page uses three conventions in one form: the value on the label's own line (Full name: Maria Okafor), the value to the right with a gap wide enough that group_lines made it a separate line, and the value below the label. A fourth case is a field left blank. FORM_SCHEMA (given) is the contract with downstream code: each canonical name has a label, a kind (text or choice) and whether it is required.

extract_form works field by field. Find the label line, tolerating a misread character (compare the first len(label) characters of the line with the label using cer and a tolerance of 0.15). Then try, in order, the words after the label on the same line, the nearest line to the right on the same row, and the nearest line just below. The distance limit on "below" is what stops a blank field, Email:, from taking the next label as its value. Each result carries a confidence: the minimum over the words of the value. A choice field reads glyph and option pairs (☒ Resident ☐ Visitor ☐ Business) and returns the ticked names. This engine writes tick boxes as characters, which makes the choice readable as text; a checkbox is a visual fact, and most engines drop it, as chapter 20 warned.

The last function is the point of the milestone. review_flags decides which fields a person should look at. A field is flagged if it is required and missing, if its confidence is below a threshold (0.8 by default), or if its value fails a validator. Run it on the real form:

Common misconception

"Low confidence marks the wrong words, so review the low-confidence ones." Confidence is information worth keeping and it is not a guarantee. def extract_form(lines, schema=FORM_SCHEMA):
    """Find each schema field's value among the form's lines (group_lines output)."""
    out = {}
    for name, spec in schema.items():
        label = spec["label"]
        found = None
        for ln in lines:
            if cer(label.lower(), ln["text"][:len(label)].lower()) <= 0.15:
                found = ln
                break
        if found is None:
            out[name] = {"value": None, "conf": None}
            continue
        n_label_words = len(label.split())
        after = found["words"][n_label_words:]
        if spec["kind"] == "choice":
            ticked, current = [], None
            for w in after:
                if w["text"] in (TICKED, UNTICKED):
                    current = [w["text"] == TICKED, []]
                    if current[0]:
                        ticked.append(current)
                    else:
                        ticked.append(None)
                elif current is not None:
                    current[1].append(w["text"])
            values = [" ".join(c[1]) for c in ticked if c]
            out[name] = {"value": values, "conf": min([w["conf"] for w in after], default=None)}
            continue
        value_words = after
        if not value_words:
            fx0, fy0, fx1, fy1 = found["bbox"]
            fh = fy1 - fy0
            right = [o for o in lines if o is not found
                     and min(fy1, o["bbox"][3]) - max(fy0, o["bbox"][1]) >= 0.5 * fh and o["bbox"][0] >= fx1]
            below = [o for o in lines if o is not found
                     and o["bbox"][1] >= fy1 - 1 and o["bbox"][1] - fy1 <= 1.2 * fh and abs(o["bbox"][0] - fx0) <= 20]
            if right:
                value_words = min(right, key=lambda o: o["bbox"][0])["words"]
            elif below:
                value_words = min(below, key=lambda o: o["bbox"][1])["words"]
        if value_words:
            out[name] = {"value": " ".join(w["text"] for w in value_words), "conf": min(w["conf"] for w in value_words)}
        else:
            out[name] = {"value": None, "conf": None}
    return out


def review_flags(fields, threshold=0.8, schema=FORM_SCHEMA, validators=VALIDATORS):
    """The names of the fields a person should look at, sorted. A field is flagged if"""
    flagged = []
    for name, spec in schema.items():
        f = fields.get(name, {"value": None, "conf": None})
        v = f["value"]
        if v is None or v == []:
            if spec["required"]:
                flagged.append(name)
            continue
        if f["conf"] is not None and f["conf"] < threshold:
            flagged.append(name)
        elif name in validators and not validators[name](v):
            flagged.append(name)
    return sorted(flagged)

What to take away

  • CER is edit distance over reference length, with a stated normalisation. A page-level CER on concatenated text measures recognition and reading order together; score order separately.
  • Lines come from vertical overlap and a horizontal gap that scales with the type; XY-cut orders blocks by cutting the widest whitespace first, and fails where no straight cut exists or the gaps are the wrong size.
  • Boilerplate is text that repeats in the margins across pages. Compare by shape and tolerate a misread; require evidence; never remove from the middle of a page.
  • Paragraphs, headings and sections come from gaps, heights and numbering together, no one of which is enough. Hyphen fusing is a trade-off.
  • A table's grid is decided by all rows voting on whitespace valleys. Score structure and text separately, because they fail separately.
  • Confidence is useful and sometimes confidently wrong. Validators that ignore it catch what it misses, and a review queue is a threshold on both.
  • Every figure above describes four synthetic pages and a simulated engine. The method transfers; the numbers do not.

On page 1, the naive reading order gives a CER of 0.701 and XY-cut gives 0.011, although the OCR engine's transcription of every word is identical in both. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The naive method makes more recognition errors than XY-cut
  2. A CER computed on the concatenated text of a page also measures reading order, so ordering errors can dominate it even when few words are misread; the two should be scored separately
  3. XY-cut has corrected some of the engine's misread characters
  4. CER is unreliable and should not be used on multi-column pages
Edit distance compares characters in sequence, so a word in the wrong place is deleted from one position and inserted at another. Nothing about the recognition changed between the two runs; only the order of the words did. CER remains the right tool for recognition, provided order is held fixed or measured on its own (option 4 throws away a good measure). Options 1 and 3 attribute the difference to recognition, which it is not.

A new report has two columns with a gutter of 12 points, but 14 points of extra space between its paragraphs, lined up across both columns. What does XY-cut with its widest-band rule do?

  1. It reads the columns correctly, because it always cuts vertically first
  2. It fails to cut at all, and falls back to the naive order
  3. It cuts the gutter first, because gutters are always wider than paragraph gaps
  4. It cuts at the paragraph gaps first, because a horizontal band across the whole page is wider than the gutter, so it reads the page in horizontal slices: top of both columns before the bottom of both
XY-cut has no notion of "column"; it takes the widest empty band. Where the paragraph gaps in the two columns coincide in height, a horizontal band exists across the whole page and is 14 points wide, wider than the 12-point gutter, so the horizontal cut wins. This is the sense in which the method depends on the relative size of gaps and needs thresholds tuned to your documents. Option 1 invents a rule the algorithm does not have, and option 3 states as a law what is only true of this fixture.

Your boilerplate detector is run on a one-page document that has a header and a page number. What should it return, and why?

  1. Nothing: a single page shows no repetition, so there is no evidence that any margin line is boilerplate, and removing text without evidence risks removing content
  2. Both lines, because lines in the margin are always boilerplate
  3. Only the page number, because it contains a digit
  4. An error, because the detector needs at least two pages
Repetition across pages is the evidence the method is built on, and with one page there is none; the lab requires a line to occur on at least two pages, and on at least half of them. A first page's "Draft" stamp or title block would be removed by option 2. Shape and zone identify the page number, not the presence of a digit (option 3), and an error (option 4) is unfriendly for an input that is perfectly legal; return an empty list and let the layout stage decide.

The line "1. Write to the service manager, giving your name and a reference number." starts with a section number and is set in the same size as the body text. What keeps detect_sections from treating it as a heading?

  1. Its height is below 1.25 times the body height
  2. It contains more than one word
  3. Headings may not end in a full stop, and this line ends in one; it is also long, at 13 words, against the limit of 12
  4. Numbered paragraphs are always body text
The number alone would make it a heading, since a numbered line is one of the three ways in. The guards that apply to every heading are what stop it: one line, at most 12 words, and no terminal full stop, comma or semicolon. Option 1 is irrelevant to numbered headings (the subheading "1.1 Waste and recycling" is also below 1.25), option 2 would exclude almost every heading, and option 4 would remove "1. Service performance" as well.

The table is recovered with structure F1 1.00 and exact F1 0.96. What does the 0.04 correspond to?

  1. A wrong column boundary that affected four per cent of the cells
  2. One cell, "871.2", that the engine read as "87l.2": the grid is right and one cell's text is not
  3. A spanning cell that was split into two
  4. Four per cent of the rows being missing
Structure F1 compares only (row, column, span), and it is 1.00, so every cell is in the right place with the right span. Exact F1 also requires the text, and one of the 25 cells differs: a digit read as a letter. A wrong column boundary (option 1), a split heading (option 3) or a missing row (option 4) would lower the structure F1 as well.

The registration "KXl9 ZTR" was read at confidence 0.93 and the review threshold is 0.8. What catches it?

  1. Raising the threshold to 0.9
  2. Nothing: a confidence of 0.93 is trustworthy
  3. A validator that checks the value against the format of a registration, which does not depend on the engine's opinion of itself
  4. Rereading the page with a second pass of the same engine
The engine was confidently wrong; its confidence tells you nothing about this word. A format check (two letters, two digits, three letters) rejects the lower-case L at once. A threshold of 0.9 would still let a 0.93 through (option 1); it would take 0.95 to flag it, and that flags 198 words of 692, 29 per cent of the document. Re-reading with the same engine tends to repeat its mistake (option 4).
