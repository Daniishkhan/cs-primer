Lab 5: RAG with Verified Citations

Chapter 16 argued that a citation is a claim about evidence and must be checked like any other claim, and chapters 13 and 14 supplied the pipeline and the chunks it runs on. This lab builds the whole loop on a small collection of council policies: cut documents into chunks that remember where they came from, retrieve and label them for a model, parse its quote-first reply, and run every claim through three gates before a user can see it. Then you measure what the gates let through and what they cost in unanswered questions. One of the fourteen answers is wrong and gets past every gate you write. Finding out why is half the lesson.

Why this lab matters

A language model will attach a plausible citation to a sentence it made up, and a reader who sees a footnote relaxes. The Air Canada chatbot case (case study C4) is what that costs when the sentence is a refund policy. The fix is not a better prompt. It is a verifier that treats the model's output as untrusted input, which is a short and mostly mechanical piece of code once you have chunks that carry offsets and quotes that can be compared with the source. You will write that code, watch each of six kinds of defect die at the gate designed for it, and then see the limit of the cheap gates: a claim with one plausible wrong phrase can pass a word-overlap check, so a runtime verifier lowers the rate of wrong answers without removing it. The last milestone puts a number on both sides of the trade, the wrong answers that remain and the right answers you refused, using the risk-coverage curve of chapter 16. The pipeline is the one in chapter 13, the chunker is from chapter 14, and the failure that remains when everything here passes is the subject of chapter 17.

A scripted model and a small collection: read this before the numbers

The language model is a script. fixtures.py holds, for each of the 14 questions, the answer a quote-first model might plausibly write, including the mistakes real models make: a number changed inside a quote, a citation to a source that was never shown, a real quote under a claim it does not support. That is deliberate. A real model makes these mistakes unpredictably and rarely enough that a lab could not show you each one on demand, and a lab that needs an API key cannot run in a check. The script reads the prompt your code builds and quotes from whichever labelled source holds the text, so your assemble has to follow the lab's labelling contract for the answers to resolve. The documents are six short policies of an invented council (the same council as Lab 6), the questions are constructed, and fourteen of them are an illustration of the mechanics, not evidence about any real system (chapter 5 says how large a judged set must be). The verifier's third gate is a word-overlap stand-in for the entailment model of chapter 16. Everything is deterministic: BM25 for retrieval, no clock, no network. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only. The full check takes under a second.

What you'll build

Six milestones, from chunking to the measurement that decides whether the system ships:

  1. Chunks with offsets: split text into sentences, then pack whole sentences into chunks, each remembering the exact span of the original it came from.
  2. The context: turn ranked chunks into labelled source lines within a token budget, dropping duplicates and cutting by rank.
  3. Gates 1 and 2: does the label exist, and is the quote in that source? Tolerant about formatting, never about content, with an offset in the original document.
  4. Gate 3: does the quote support the claim? Numbers, negation and word coverage as a stand-in for entailment, and the combined verdict for one claim.
  5. The reply and the pipeline: parse the quote-first format, then answer, reject or abstain, in two policies.
  6. Measure: score answers against the questions, compute risk and coverage, and see what a support threshold buys.

Milestone 1: chunks that remember where they came from

A citation is useful only if a reader can follow it, and following it means finding a place in a document. So a chunk is not just a string. It is a string plus an address: which document, and which characters of it. Chapter 16 puts it as a design decision to "store document version, offsets and the quote", and the reason is that everything downstream depends on it. A highlight in a viewer needs the offsets. A check that a quote occurs in its source needs the source text exactly as indexed. A fix of a wrong answer starts from "which chunk was this?". If the chunker returns only text, you can only reconstruct that by searching again.

The unit is the sentence, for the reason chapter 14 gave: a quote that straddles a cut can never be verified against either half. Sentence splitting here is a pattern, not a language model. A sentence ends after ., ! or ? when whitespace follows and then a capital letter, a digit or an opening quote; "9.50" has no whitespace after its dot, so it never splits. A blank line also ends a sentence, so a title stands alone. The starter gives you the boundary pattern, and your sentence_spans turns it into spans: (start, end) offsets into the original text, excluding the whitespace between sentences, so text[start:end] is the sentence exactly as written, newlines and double spaces included.

Then chunk_document packs whole sentences up to a character limit. Two details decide whether the chunks are good. A sentence longer than the limit becomes a chunk on its own and is never cut. And consecutive chunks overlap by a number of sentences, so a fact that lies across a boundary is complete in at least one chunk, but the overlap must never stop the walk: each chunk starts at least one sentence after the previous one started. Run this on the parking document to see the result:

Common misconception

"Cleaning the text first makes everything easier, so collapse whitespace and fix the ligatures when you chunk." Do that and the offsets no longer point into the document you were given, and the highlight lands on the wrong characters. Normalise a copy for comparison (milestone 3 does exactly that) and keep the original untouched. The chunk text must equal text[start:end] of the real document, and check.py tests it on every chunk it builds.

def sentence_spans(text):
    """The sentences of `text` as (start, end) offsets into `text`, in order."""
    spans, start = [], 0
    for m in _BOUNDARY.finditer(text):
        if text[start:m.start()].strip():
            spans.append((start, m.start()))
        start = m.end()
    if text[start:].strip():
        spans.append((start, len(text.rstrip())))
    # a span must not begin or end in whitespace
    out = []
    for s, e in spans:
        while s < e and text[s].isspace():
            s += 1
        while e > s and text[e - 1].isspace():
            e -= 1
        out.append((s, e))
    return out


def chunk_document(doc_id, text, max_chars=260, overlap=1):
    """Cut a document into chunks made of WHOLE sentences, keeping offsets."""
    sents = sentence_spans(text)
    chunks, i = [], 0
    while i < len(sents):
        j = i + 1                                           # the chunk holds sentences i .. j - 1
        while j < len(sents) and sents[j][1] - sents[i][0] <= max_chars:
            j += 1
        s, e = sents[i][0], sents[j - 1][1]
        chunks.append({"id": f"{doc_id}#{len(chunks)}", "doc": doc_id, "start": s, "end": e, "text": text[s:e]})
        if j >= len(sents):
            break
        i = max(i + 1, j - overlap)                         # step back `overlap` sentences, but advance
    return chunks

Milestone 2: the context the model sees

Retrieval returns a ranked list of chunks. The model needs a prompt. Chapter 13 called the step in between assembly and pointed out that it is the stage people forget to design, although it fixes three things a verifier later relies on. It decides which chunks the model may use, by a token budget. It decides how each is labelled, so the model can cite a short handle (S2) that your code maps back to a chunk, instead of a filename or a URL it would have to produce from memory. And it decides what the model sees as one line per source, with provenance beside the text.

The contract in this lab is exact, because the scripted model and the verifier both depend on it. A source line is [Sn] (doc) text, with the chunk's whitespace collapsed so that each source is one line. Before labelling, a chunk whose folded text equals an earlier chunk's is dropped (overlap and copies waste budget, and a model over-weights repeated text). The kept lines are numbered S1, S2… in rank order. Lines are added while the running total of count_tokens(line) stays within the budget, and assembly stops at the first line that does not fit: cutting by rank means the third-best chunk never gets in because the fourth-best is shorter. The function returns the context, the label map, and how many chunks did not make it.

Notice the split. The displayed text has its whitespace collapsed, and the label maps to the original chunk, newlines and all. Gate 2 will compare quotes against the original, not against the display copy. This script shows what the model receives for two questions, and what a smaller budget does:

And the budget is a hard cap: at 120 tokens only the best chunk fits, and the other three are counted as dropped, which is the number a log line should carry ("retrieved 4, kept 1").

Systems connection

The label is a handle, in the same sense as a file descriptor or an opaque session token: the caller (the model) holds a small name, and only your code can turn it into the thing it stands for. That is why never asking a model to write a URL or a file path from memory is a security rule as well as an accuracy one. A string the model typed is untrusted input, and the rule for those is to validate before use. A handle that has to be in a map you built cannot name anything you did not put there.

Solution spoiler 
def assemble(chunks, budget):
    """Turn ranked chunks (best first) into a labelled context within a token budget."""
    seen, kept, used = set(), [], 0
    for ch in chunks:
        key = fold(ch["text"])
        if key in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(key)
        line = f"[S{len(kept) + 1}] ({ch['doc']}) {' '.join(ch['text'].split())}"
        cost = count_tokens(line)
        if used + cost > budget:
            break
        used += cost
        kept.append((line, ch))
    context = "\n".join(line for line, _ in kept)
    labels = {f"S{n}": ch for n, (_, ch) in enumerate(kept, 1)}
    return context, labels, len(chunks) - len(kept)

Milestone 3: gates 1 and 2, the pointer and the quote

The model's reply is untrusted text, and each citation in it passes through gates, cheapest first (chapter 16's "four gates"). Here are the ones this lab builds, with the verdict each failure gets:

This milestone writes the two string gates. Gate 1 asks whether the label the quote cites is one of the sources in this request's context; a model that cites S9 when you showed it four sources has made something up. Gate 2 asks whether the quoted words occur in that source. Both are cheap, and together they stop the commonest fabrications. The interesting question is what "occur" means.

A byte-for-byte match rejects honest quotes, because converted documents contain line breaks in the middle of sentences, non-breaking spaces, ligature characters and curly quotes, and a model copying text normalises them without noticing. The opposite error is a fuzzy match, which forgives a changed digit. Chapter 16's rule is to be tolerant about formatting and never about content, and the way to keep it is to compare normalised copies and carry a map back to the original. norm_with_map returns the normalised text (compatibility normalisation, curly quotes straightened, case folded, runs of whitespace as one space, no space at either end) together with, for every normalised character, the index of the original character that produced it. A ligature expands to two characters with the same origin; a soft hyphen produces none. locate normalises both the source and the quote, finds one in the other with str.find, and maps the match back to a (start, end) span of the original, newlines included.

The fifth is a paraphrase, which gate 2 correctly refuses to match; deciding whether a paraphrase is acceptable is gate 3's job.

Common misconception

"Gate 2 proves the answer is right." It proves the quote is in the source. A model can copy a perfectly real sentence and draw the wrong conclusion from it, and the quote check passes. This is why there is a gate 3, and why even gate 3 does not make an answer true about the world: grounded means supported by the evidence given, not correct.

verify_quote(label, quote, labels) composes the gates for one quote and adds one more rule: a quote shorter than MIN_QUOTE_CHARS (15) is TOO_SHORT, because "the" is in every document and proves nothing. The order of checks is part of the contract: a missing label is reported before anything else, then the length, then the text. On success it returns the document id and the span in the document: the chunk's own start plus the offset inside the chunk, which is the number a viewer needs to highlight.

# =============================================================================
def norm_with_map(text):
    """Normalise `text` for comparison AND remember where each character came from."""
    out, origin = [], []
    for i, ch in enumerate(text):
        piece = unicodedata.normalize("NFKC", ch)
        piece = "".join(TYPOGRAPHIC.get(c, c) for c in piece).casefold()
        for c in piece:
            if c.isspace():
                if out and out[-1] != " ":
                    out.append(" ")
                    origin.append(i)
            else:
                out.append(c)
                origin.append(i)
    while out and out[-1] == " ":
        out.pop()
        origin.pop()
    return "".join(out), origin


def locate(text, quote):
    """Where does `quote` occur in `text`? (start, end) offsets into the ORIGINAL `text`, or None."""
    nt, origin = norm_with_map(text)
    nq, _ = norm_with_map(quote)
    if not nq:
        return None
    at = nt.find(nq)
    if at < 0:
        return None
    return origin[at], origin[at + len(nq) - 1] + 1


def verify_quote(label, quote, labels):
    """Gates 1 and 2 for one quote. Returns {"verdict", "doc", "span"}."""
    bad = {"verdict": None, "doc": None, "span": None}
    chunk = labels.get(label)
    if chunk is None:
        return dict(bad, verdict="BAD_POINTER")
    if len(quote.strip()) < MIN_QUOTE_CHARS:
        return dict(bad, verdict="TOO_SHORT")
    hit = locate(chunk["text"], quote)
    if hit is None:
        return dict(bad, verdict="FABRICATED_QUOTE")
    return {"verdict": "OK", "doc": chunk["doc"], "span": (chunk["start"] + hit[0], chunk["start"] + hit[1])}

Milestone 4: gate 3, does the span support the claim?

Gate 3 is the hard one. Given a premise (the quoted span) and a hypothesis (the claim), does the premise entail the hypothesis, contradict it, or say nothing either way? That is natural language inference, and chapter 16 lists four ways to do it, in rising order of cost and ability: lexical and numeric checks, a trained entailment model, a language-model judge with a binary rubric, and typed comparison for amounts and dates. This lab builds the first because it runs in microseconds, needs no model, and is what you would put in front of the others in production to reject quickly. It has three rules, and a claim must satisfy all of them.

  1. Numbers. Every number in the claim must appear among the quote's numbers. This catches the commonest fabrication: a changed figure.
  2. Negation. The claim and the quote must agree about whether they contain a negation word (not, no, never, cannot, without, or an n't form). "Gift cards do not expire" is not supported by "Gift cards expire after one year", whatever the overlap.
  3. Coverage. The share of the claim's content words (stop words dropped, a trailing plural "s" removed) that also occur in the quote must be at least 0.75. That share is the support score, reported even when another rule fails.

Here are six pairs and what the stand-in says. Read the last column against your own judgement before you read the next paragraph:

The fourth is the one to remember. "Within 30 days of purchase" differs from "within 30 days of delivery" by one word that changes what a customer is entitled to, and 83 per cent of the claim's words are in the quote, so it is supported. No threshold fixes this: lower it and you accept more wrong claims, raise it and you reject the paraphrases in rows five and six, which are fine. A word-overlap check cannot see meaning, and the lab's question q08 ("start of the school year" for "start of term") is exactly this case. A trained entailment model or a judge calibrated on human labels is what closes the gap, at a cost chapter 16 works through; what you build here is the cheap layer that runs first.

verify_claim then combines all the gates for one claim. It checks, in order, that the claim cites something (UNCITED), that every cited id exists among the model's quotes and every quote passes verify_quote (the first failing one, in the order cited, decides the verdict), and finally that the cited quotes jointly support the claim. "Jointly" matters: a sentence such as "Refunds within 30 days go to the original payment method" draws on two passages, and gate 3 runs on the quotes joined, which is also how citation recall is defined in the ALCE benchmark (Gao et al., 2023). It returns the claim, the verdict, the score and the verified spans.

def supports(claim, quote, threshold=0.75):
    """A crude stand-in for an entailment model (chapter 16): does `quote` support `claim`?"""
    cw, qw = content_words(claim), content_words(quote)
    if not cw:
        return False, 0.0
    score = len(cw & qw) / len(cw)
    nums_ok = {w for w in cw if w[0].isdigit()} <= {w for w in qw if w[0].isdigit()}

    def negated(text):
        return any(t in NEG or t.endswith("n't") for t in _tokens(text))
    return (nums_ok and negated(claim) == negated(quote) and score >= threshold), score


def verify_claim(claim, cites, quotes, labels):
    """Run the gates on one claim. `cites` are its quote ids; `quotes` maps id -> {"src", "text"}."""
    out = {"claim": claim, "verdict": None, "score": 0.0, "spans": []}
    if not cites:
        return dict(out, verdict="UNCITED")
    if any(c not in quotes for c in cites):
        return dict(out, verdict="BAD_POINTER")
    spans = []
    for c in cites:
        v = verify_quote(quotes[c]["src"], quotes[c]["text"], labels)
        if v["verdict"] != "OK":
            return dict(out, verdict=v["verdict"])
        spans.append((v["doc"],) + v["span"])
    ok, score = supports(claim, " ".join(quotes[c]["text"] for c in cites))
    if not ok:
        return dict(out, verdict="UNSUPPORTED", score=score)
    return dict(out, verdict="OK", score=score, spans=spans)

Milestone 5: the reply, and a pipeline with three outcomes

Now the whole loop. The model is asked to answer in two steps, a technique chapter 16 calls quote first: copy the passages that help, character for character, as <quote id="q1" src="S2">…</quote> inside <quotes>, and then write the answer inside <answer> with the quote id after each sentence. Quoting first has two effects. It makes citations mechanically checkable, because a quote is a string you can search for. And it gives the model a way to say nothing: <quotes>NONE</quotes> means the sources do not contain an answer. The prompt (SYSTEM and build_prompt) is given; so is split_claims, which cuts an answer into sentences and their trailing [qN] markers.

parse_response is the reader. It returns whether the model abstained, the quotes by id (each with its source label and text, whitespace around the text stripped), and the claims with the ids they cite. One rule deserves a sentence: a reply with no <quotes> tag at all is not an abstention. It is a malformed answer whose claims cite quotes that do not exist, and it must fail at the gates, not be mistaken for "the sources do not say".

Then Pipeline.answer glues everything. It retrieves and assembles. If nothing was retrieved it abstains without calling the model: this is chapter 16's first place to abstain, before generation, and it costs nothing. Otherwise it asks the model, parses, verifies every claim, and applies a policy:

  • reject (the default): if any claim fails any gate, withhold the whole answer and say so. This is the reading of the lab's title, "reject any answer whose quotes are not in the source", and it is right for answers people act on.
  • drop: keep the claims that pass and show only those, refusing only if none pass. This answers more questions and shows the reader a partial answer, which may mislead by omission. It suits settings where an unsupported sentence is an annoyance, not a liability.

An answer is shown as its verified claims, each followed by its sources as [doc:start-end], which is the span in the document that a viewer would highlight. A support threshold gives the third exit: if the weakest shown claim scores below min_support, the pipeline abstains. Run the whole pipeline on the fourteen questions:

# Milestone 5: read the model's reply, and the pipeline with three outcomes
# =============================================================================
def parse_response(text):
    """Read a quote-first reply."""
    block = re.search(r"<quotes>(.*?)</quotes>", text, re.S | re.I)
    if block and block.group(1).strip().upper() == "NONE":
        return {"none": True, "quotes": {}, "claims": []}
    quotes = {}
    if block:
        for m in re.finditer(r'<quote\s+id="(q\d+)"\s+src="(S\d+)"\s*>(.*?)</quote>', block.group(1), re.S):
            quotes[m.group(1)] = {"src": m.group(2), "text": m.group(3).strip()}
    ans = re.search(r"<answer>(.*?)</answer>", text, re.S | re.I)
    claims = [{"text": t, "cites": c} for t, c in split_claims(ans.group(1))] if ans else []
    return {"none": False, "quotes": quotes, "claims": claims}


def answer(self, question, mode="reject", min_support=0.0):
    """Answer `question` from the documents, or refuse. Returns a dict with"""
    context, labels, _ = assemble(self.retrieve(question), self.budget)
    base = {"claims": [], "support": 0.0, "context": context, "labels": labels}
    if not labels:
        return dict(base, status="abstained", text=ABSTAIN)
    reply = self.model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": build_prompt(question, context)}], system=SYSTEM)
    parsed = parse_response(text_of(reply))
    if parsed["none"] or not parsed["claims"]:
        return dict(base, status="abstained", text=ABSTAIN)
    verdicts = [verify_claim(c["text"], c["cites"], parsed["quotes"], labels) for c in parsed["claims"]]
    good = [v for v in verdicts if v["verdict"] == "OK"]
    base["claims"] = verdicts
    if (mode == "reject" and len(good) < len(verdicts)) or not good:
        return dict(base, status="rejected", text=REJECT)
    support = min(v["score"] for v in good)
    if support < min_support:
        return dict(base, status="abstained", text=ABSTAIN)
    shown = " ".join(v["claim"] + "".join(f" [{d}:{s}-{e}]" for d, s, e in v["spans"]) for v in good)
    return dict(base, status="answered", text=shown, support=support)

Milestone 6: measure it

A verifier that runs is not a verifier you can trust. You need to know what it lets through and what it costs. Chapter 16 gives the vocabulary, and the abstention section of that chapter gives the curve. For each question, score_answer assigns one of four outcomes:

OutcomeThe question isThe systemCost
correctanswerableanswered, and the answer contains the expected phrasenone
wrongeitheranswered with the wrong content, or answered at all when there was no answerthe one that matters: a user acts on it
missedanswerablerejected or abstainedan unhelpful answer, recoverable
safenot answerablerejected or abstainednone

From those, evaluate computes coverage (the share of questions that were answered) and risk (the share of answered questions that were wrong). They pull against each other: every answer you refuse lowers coverage and, if the refusal was right, lowers risk. It also counts the verdicts of every claim the model made, which is the number to watch in production: a spike in BAD_POINTER after a deployment means the context-building changed its labels, a rise in FABRICATED_QUOTE after a model upgrade means the new model copies less faithfully, and a rise in UNSUPPORTED means retrieval is returning less relevant passages. Gate failures are a smoke alarm for the rest of the pipeline.

Then risk_coverage(rows, thresholds) is chapter 16's table. Each answered question has a support score (the weakest of its claims), and a threshold t keeps the answers with support at least t. For each threshold, coverage is the share of the rows kept and risk the share of the kept rows that are wrong. # =============================================================================
# Milestone 6: measure it
# =============================================================================
def score_answer(question, result):
    """Classify one result against its question row (qid, text, answerable, phrase, doc):"""
    _, _, answerable, phrase, _ = question
    if result["status"] == "answered":
        if not answerable:
            return "wrong"
        return "correct" if fold(phrase) in fold(result["text"]) else "wrong"
    return "missed" if answerable else "safe"


def evaluate(pipeline, questions, mode="reject", min_support=0.0):
    """Run every question and summarise."""
    outcomes, verdicts, rows = {}, Counter(), []
    answered = 0
    for q in questions:
        res = pipeline.answer(q[1], mode=mode, min_support=min_support)
        outcomes[q[0]] = score_answer(q, res)
        verdicts.update(v["verdict"] for v in res["claims"])
        if res["status"] == "answered":
            answered += 1
            rows.append((res["support"], outcomes[q[0]] == "correct"))
    counts = Counter(outcomes.values())
    return {"outcomes": outcomes, "counts": counts, "answered": answered,
            "coverage": answered / len(questions), "risk": counts["wrong"] / answered if answered else 0.0,
            "verdicts": verdicts, "rows": rows}


def risk_coverage(rows, thresholds):
    """The risk-coverage table of chapter 16. `rows` are (support, is_correct) for answered"""
    table = []
    for t in thresholds:
        kept = [ok for s, ok in rows if s >= t]
        wrong = sum(1 for ok in kept if not ok)
        table.append((t, len(kept) / len(rows) if rows else 0.0, wrong / len(kept) if kept else 0.0))
    return table

What to take away

  • A chunk is a string plus an address. Keep the offsets into the original, compare normalised copies, and map back, so a citation can be highlighted and a quote can be checked.
  • Treat the model's reply as untrusted input. Check the pointer, then the quote, then the support, cheapest first, and let the first failure decide the verdict.
  • Be tolerant about formatting and never about content. A fuzzy match that forgives a typo forgives a changed digit.
  • A word-overlap gate catches changed numbers, missing negations and unrelated claims, and passes a plausible one-word slip. The expensive gates exist for that case.
  • Reject or drop is a policy with a cost on each side. Refusals can be right, so count them separately from misses, and report coverage and risk together.
  • Fourteen questions show the mechanism, not the rates. The numbers on this page describe a constructed collection and a scripted model.

After a deployment, the daily count of BAD_POINTER verdicts goes from near zero to a fifth of all claims, while the other verdicts hold steady. What is the most likely cause?

  1. The context assembly changed how it labels sources, so the labels the model cites no longer match the label map the verifier checks against
  2. The retriever is returning less relevant passages
  3. The new model copies quotes less faithfully
  4. The support threshold was lowered
A bad pointer means a cited label is not in the map for this request, which is a plumbing fault between assembly and verification (a renumbering, an off-by-one, labels built after the budget cut). Irrelevant retrieval would show as UNSUPPORTED (option 2); less faithful copying as FABRICATED_QUOTE (option 3); and gate 3's threshold cannot affect a gate 1 verdict (option 4). This is why chapter 16 treats gate failures as a smoke alarm.
