Hybrid Search & Rank Fusion

BM25 and dense retrieval fail on different queries, so running both and merging the lists is usually better than choosing one. The merge is harder than it looks, because the two systems score on incompatible scales. This chapter explains reciprocal rank fusion, score normalisation and weighted sums, when each is safe, and how to tell whether fusion is helping.

Why this matters

Hybrid retrieval is the default first stage in most production RAG and enterprise search systems, and also one of the easiest places to ship a quiet bug: a fusion that looks sensible on three demo queries and hides a normalisation problem that ruins a whole class of queries. After this chapter you can write both fusion methods in a few lines, explain from the arithmetic why each one behaves as it does, choose between them with evidence from judged queries, and recognise the cases where adding a second retriever makes results worse. It builds on the BM25 of chapter 2, the dense retrieval of chapter 7 and the metrics of chapter 4, and it feeds the reranker of chapter 8.

Two retrievers, two kinds of mistake

Lexical and dense retrievers are wrong in opposite ways. BM25 needs the query's words to appear in the document: it is excellent on identifiers, error codes, product names and rare terms, and blind to paraphrase ("end the contract" against "terminate this agreement"). A dense retriever matches meaning and is robust to wording, but it can blur exact strings: two error codes that differ by one digit look nearly identical in embedding space, and a rare name may not be represented at all. If the failures were the same, combining them would add nothing. Because they differ, a document that one misses is often found by the other.

The listing below runs both on the 12-query constructed corpus introduced in chapter 8 (same corpus.py and common.py; BM25 against the stand-in LSA embedder), together with the fusion methods this chapter builds. Read only the first two rows for now: nDCG@10 per query for each retriever alone.

"""Rank fusion and score fusion over two retrievers (chapter 10)."""
import statistics
from common import *

def rrf(rankings, k=60, weights=None):
    """Reciprocal rank fusion: sum of w / (k + rank) over the lists a document appears in."""
    weights = weights or [1.0] * len(rankings)
    out = {}
    for w, ranking in zip(weights, rankings):
        for r, d in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            out[d] = out.get(d, 0.0) + w / (k + r)
    return out

def min_max(scores):
    lo, hi = min(scores.values()), max(scores.values())
    return {d: (s - lo) / (hi - lo) if hi > lo else 0.0 for d, s in scores.items()}

def z_score(scores):
    m, sd = statistics.mean(scores.values()), statistics.pstdev(scores.values())
    return {d: (s - m) / sd if sd else 0.0 for d, s in scores.items()}

def convex(a, b, alpha, norm=min_max):
    """alpha * lexical + (1 - alpha) * dense, after normalising each score list."""
    na, nb = norm(a), norm(b)
    return {d: alpha * na.get(d, 0.0) + (1 - alpha) * nb.get(d, 0.0) for d in set(a) | set(b)}

def first_stage(q, depth=10):
    """What each retriever returns: its top `depth` documents with their raw scores."""
    lex, den = bm25(q), dense(q)
    lex = {d: lex[d] for d in rank(lex)[:depth]}
    den = {d: den[d] for d in rank(den, floor=-1)[:depth]}
    return lex, den

Analogy: two judges on different scales

Two judges rank the same ten contestants. One gives marks out of 10 (and rarely above 7), the other out of 100 (and rarely below 60). Adding the marks lets the second judge decide everything, because their numbers are bigger and vary more. You have two honest options: ignore the marks and add up placings ("first by one judge, third by the other"), or rescale each judge's marks to a common range before adding. The first is reciprocal rank fusion. The second is score normalisation.

Where it breaks down: real judges are independent. Two retrievers over the same chunks share their blind spots: if a passage was cut badly or the answer is on a page nobody indexed, both will miss it, and agreement between them is weaker evidence than agreement between independent experts.

The scale problem

Why not simply add the two scores? Because they are not measured in the same units. A BM25 score is an unbounded sum of idf-weighted term contributions: its size depends on how many query terms there are, how rare they are, and the collection. A cosine similarity lives between −1 and 1 and usually clusters in a narrow band. The next listing prints both scores for the query "how do I get my money back", with each score list rescaled two ways. The BM25 scores in the output are real; the cosines come from the stand-in embedder.

In the table, look at the BM25 columns. Document r2 scores 8.24 and the next three score about 2.7. After min-max rescaling (subtract the list's minimum, divide by its range) r2 becomes 1.00 and everything else collapses to between 0.00 and 0.01: one very strong match has used up the whole scale, so BM25 can no longer say that r1, a relevant passage, is any better than t1 or t2, which are about moving money between accounts. Z-scores are less affected by one outlier, but a list of ten scores gives very rough estimates of the mean and the spread.

Common misconception

"Hybrid search means adding the BM25 score and the vector score." Raw scores are not additive: their units and spreads differ, and BM25's scale changes with the query (more query terms, higher scores). The sum is then dominated by whichever retriever happens to produce bigger numbers for that query, and the weights you thought you set mean nothing. Always either normalise each list per query or fuse on ranks.

Fusing ranks: reciprocal rank fusion

The cleanest escape from the scale problem is to throw the scores away and keep only the positions. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF; Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual Rank Learning Methods", 2009) gives each document, for every list it appears in, a contribution that depends only on its rank r in that list, and adds the contributions:

Plug in numbers. Two short ranked lists, fused with k = 60 and with k = 1.

The idea

  1. BM25 list: d1 d6 d5 d8; dense list: d6 d3 d1 d2 d5
  2. per list, per document: vote ← 1 / (k + rank); absent → no vote
  3. RRF(d) ← sum of its votes; sort descending
  4. repeat with k = 60 and k = 1 to see the effect of k
"""Reciprocal rank fusion by hand: two ranked lists, k = 60 and k = 1."""

# ── 1. Two ranked lists, best first ──────────────────────────────────────
BM25 = ["d1", "d6", "d5", "d8"]            # keyword retriever
DENSE = ["d6", "d3", "d1", "d2", "d5"]     # embedding retriever
K_VALUES = [60, 1]                         # the paper's k, and a sharp one


# ── 2. Each list gives 1 / (k + rank); a missing document gives nothing ──
def rrf(lists, k):
    """Sum 1 / (k + rank) over every list a document appears in."""
    score = {}
    for ranking in lists:
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranking, 1):          # ranks start at 1
            score[doc] = score.get(doc, 0.0) + 1 / (k + rank)
    return score


# ── 3. Fuse and sort ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
for k in K_VALUES:
    print(f"k = {k}")
    print("doc  bm25 rank  dense rank  RRF")
    fused = rrf([BM25, DENSE], k)
    for doc, s in sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
        r1 = BM25.index(doc) + 1 if doc in BM25 else "-"
        r2 = DENSE.index(doc) + 1 if doc in DENSE else "-"
        print(f"{doc:4s} {r1!s:>9s} {r2!s:>11s}  {s:.5f}")
    print()

Step through

RRF for "how do I get my money back" (k = 60)

  1. Two lists, scores discarded. BM25 matched only four passages; the dense retriever returned its top ten (six shown). All RRF needs is the order in each list.
  2. Each rank earns 1 / (60 + rank). Rank 1 earns 0.01639, rank 2 0.01613, and so on. The weights decay slowly: rank 1 is worth only 1.15 times rank 10, so no single list can win by its first place alone.
  3. Add the contributions per document. A document in both lists gets two terms; one in only one list gets one. r2 is first in both, so it earns 0.03279. The dense-only documents d5 and d3 get about half of that.
  4. Check against the judgements. r2 and r1 are the relevant passages. The other two documents that appear in both lists, t1 and t2, are not: they are about moving money between accounts. t1 finishes above r1 and t2 only a hair below it, because both retrievers liked them. Agreement is not correctness.

What k does

Look at the weights 1/(k + r). With k small, the top ranks dominate: for k = 1 rank 1 is worth 5.5 times rank 10. With k large, all ranks within the lists' depth become nearly equal, and what matters is simply how many lists a document appears in. The figure plots each weight relative to rank 1.

Go deeper how far down must a document agree to beat a single first place?

Take a document A that is first in one list and absent from the other, so RRF(A) = 1/(k + 1). Take B, ranked r in both lists, so RRF(B) = 2/(k + r). Then B beats A exactly when 2/(k + r) > 1/(k + 1), which simplifies to 2(k + 1) > k + r, that is, r < k + 2.

For k = 60, a document at rank 61 in both lists still beats the first-place document of one list (2/121 = 0.016529 against 1/61 = 0.016393). For k = 1, the document must be in the top 2 of both. So k can be read as a tolerance: how far down both lists a document may sit and still outrank something a single list is certain about. Cormack et al. fixed 60 from preliminary experiments rather than deriving it; it is a reasonable default, not a law, and the right value for your corpus is a parameter to tune on judged queries (but see the next section for how little the results may depend on it).

Weights, depth and ties

  • Weighted RRF. Multiply each list's contribution by a weight (for example 1.0 for dense and 0.5 for a noisier source). The rrf() in the listing above accepts weights.
  • Depth matters. RRF only sees the documents each list returns. A document at rank 150 in one list that both retrievers would agree is relevant contributes nothing if each list is cut at 100. Elasticsearch calls this the rank_window_size. Take each list deep enough that the fused top-n is stable, and measure recall at that depth.
  • Ties happen (t1 and t2 have identical dense scores because their texts contain the same words). Fix a deterministic tie-break, such as document id, or results will flicker between requests.
  • Identity. Fusion adds contributions for the same identifier. If BM25 returns chunk ids and the dense retriever returns parent-document ids, nothing fuses. Decide the unit (chunk or document) before fusing and map both lists onto it (chapter 14).

Fusing scores: normalise, then weight

The other family keeps the scores. Rescale each list to a common range, then take a weighted sum, a convex combination:

score(d) = α · norm(BM25(d)) + (1 − α) · norm(cosine(d))

with a document missing from a list counted as 0 for that list. The weight α between 0 and 1 says how much to trust the lexical side. The normalisation is where the care goes:

  • Min-max maps the list's lowest score to 0 and its highest to 1. It is easy to explain and is the default in several engines, but, as the table showed, one outlier compresses everyone else. It also changes meaning with the list's depth: the "lowest" score of a top-10 list is not the lowest of a top-100 list.
  • Z-score subtracts the list's mean and divides by its standard deviation. It is less tied to the extremes but needs enough scores per list for the mean and the spread to be stable; with ten it is crude.
  • Global statistics (a fixed scale per retriever estimated from many past queries, such as the typical cosine range) avoid per-query instability but need maintaining when the model or the index changes.

What do you get for the extra trouble? Bruch, Gai and Ingber ("An Analysis of Fusion Functions for Hybrid Retrieval", 2022; published 2023) compared the two families and report that a convex combination outperforms RRF both in-domain and out of domain, and that its single parameter can be tuned with a small set of judged examples, while RRF is sensitive to its parameters. That is a research result on specific collections, with scores normalised carefully, and it is worth reading before you decide. It is also why RRF is the safer default when you have no judged queries: it needs no tuning and cannot be wrecked by an unnormalised scale. The next listing sweeps α on our 12 queries, then estimates honestly what tuning α would deliver by choosing it on 11 queries and scoring the 12th.

import statistics
from fusion import *

CACHE = {qid: first_stage(q) for qid, q, _ in QUERIES}

def per_query(alpha, norm):
    return [ndcg(rank(convex(*CACHE[qid], alpha, norm), floor=-99), qr, 10) for qid, _, qr in QUERIES]

alphas = [i / 10 for i in range(11)]
print("alpha (weight on BM25)   " + " ".join(f"{a:>5.1f}" for a in alphas))
for name, norm in (("min-max", min_max), ("z-score", z_score)):
    print(f"mean nDCG@10, {name:<8}" + " ".join(f"{statistics.mean(per_query(a, norm)):>6.3f}" for a in alphas))

# Honest estimate of 'tune alpha on judged queries': pick alpha on 11 queries, score the 12th.

The experiment: does fusion help?

The first listing of this chapter printed the head-to-head. Read it as a whole:

  • Every fusion variant beats both single retrievers on average, by very different margins: min-max at α = 0.5 reached 0.870 against 0.815 for BM25 and 0.764 for dense. RRF with k = 60 reached 0.825, a smaller gain that is within the noise of 12 queries.
  • RRF's value is the floor. On query 11 BM25 alone scored 0.00, dense 0.43, RRF 0.39: it recovered most of the dense retriever's result without anyone telling it which retriever to trust. On query 2 (an error code) it kept BM25's 0.80 while min-max fell to 0.69.
  • RRF barely moves with k here (0.826 at k = 1, 0.825 at 60 and at 200), because both lists are short; on long lists (hundreds of candidates) k matters more.
  • Query 4 shows why no fusion is magic: BM25 gets 0.80, the dense stand-in 0.36, and fused RRF 0.63. A strong retriever diluted by a weak one lands in between. Query-dependent weighting (route identifier-like queries towards lexical, see chapter 3) is how production systems recover that loss.

When fusion hurts

  1. Consensus on a wrong answer. In the stepper, t1 and t2 were top-four in both lists, t1 beat the relevant r1 and t2 almost tied it, because both retrievers liked the words "money" and "back". Fusion rewards agreement; shared mistakes are agreement too. The cure is a reranker (chapter 8) that reads the pair, which is why fusion and reranking are usually stacked.
  2. One retriever is much weaker. Equal-weight RRF gives a poor retriever the same voice as a good one, and noise from the weak list can displace good documents. Measure each retriever alone first. If one is far behind, lower its weight or drop it for that query type.
  3. Highly correlated retrievers. Two embedding models trained similarly produce similar lists, and fusing them adds little. Diversity of method (lexical against dense, or against learned sparse from chapter 9) matters more than count.
  4. A bad normalisation. A min-max on a list of three results, or on a list where one score is an outlier, silently reweights the retrievers. Check the normalised value distributions on a sample of real queries.
  5. Mismatched candidate sets. One retriever filtered by tenant and the other not, lists of very different lengths, or ids that do not line up. A filter must apply to both retrievers before fusion (chapter 12), or a document one retriever was never allowed to return could appear through the other.

Systems connection

Hybrid search is a scatter-gather fan-out to two backends, so the tail-latency arithmetic of tail latency at scale applies: the request is as slow as the slower of the two searches, and the 99th percentile of a maximum is worse than either backend's own. Give each retriever a deadline, and decide what happens when one misses it: returning the other retriever's list alone, and flagging the response as degraded, is usually better than failing. If both run in the same engine (see the dated box below), the engine does the fan-out for you, but the failure modes remain. For the index structures underneath, see specialised stores and the next chapter.

Running hybrid in practice

  1. Start with RRF at k = 60 if you have no judged queries; it is a strong default and needs no calibration.
  2. Build the judged set (chapter 5) and compare, with a paired test over queries, each retriever alone, RRF, and a normalised sum with α chosen on a held-out split. Keep the change only if the gain survives the interval.
  3. Log the per-retriever rank of every returned result. When a user reports a bad result, you can see whether lexical missed it, dense missed it, or fusion buried it.
  4. Pick the candidate depth from recall: measure recall of the fused list at 20, 50 and 100 and pass that depth on to the reranker.
  5. Re-tune after any change to the embedding model, the analyser or the chunking. Score scales and list overlap change with each; a fixed α is a hidden dependency on them.

State of play, October 2026

Hybrid search is a built-in feature of the main search engines. Elasticsearch documents reciprocal rank fusion as generally available, with rank_constant defaulting to 60 (I read the current documentation page). Summaries of the OpenSearch documentation that I found describe two routes: a normalisation processor (min-max or L2 normalisation, with arithmetic, geometric or harmonic mean combination and per-query weights) and a rank-based processor using RRF, introduced in version 2.19. Weaviate's documentation, as summarised by search results, offers a ranked fusion and a relative-score fusion (a min-max normalised weighted sum) and made the latter the default from version 1.24. I did not read the OpenSearch or Weaviate pages directly, and defaults change between releases: check the documentation for the version you run. The comparison between rank-based and score-based fusion is a research finding on particular benchmarks (Bruch et al., 2023), not a rule, so keep both candidates in your evaluation.

Key takeaways

  • Lexical and dense retrievers fail on different queries, so fusing their lists often beats either, and a reranker on top fixes what fusion cannot.
  • Raw scores cannot be added: BM25 is unbounded and query-dependent, cosine is bounded and bunched. Fuse on ranks, or normalise per query.
  • RRF adds 1 / (k + rank) per list. It needs no score calibration and no judged data. A document at rank < k + 2 in both lists beats a first place in one list; k = 60 is a common default, not a law.
  • Min-max is distorted by outliers and list depth; z-scores need enough scores. A convex combination has one parameter, a plateau of good values, and a published result that it can beat RRF when tuned, but tune on held-out queries and report the held-out figure.
  • Fusion rewards agreement, including agreement on a wrong answer; it dilutes a strong retriever with a weak one; and it needs shared document identity, consistent filters and enough candidate depth.
  • Treat hybrid as a fan-out: parallel calls, deadlines, graceful fallback to one retriever, and logging of each retriever's rank for debugging.

Further reading

  • Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual Rank Learning Methods" (SIGIR 2009). The original RRF paper with k = 60.
  • Bruch, Gai and Ingber, "An Analysis of Fusion Functions for Hybrid Retrieval" (2022; ACM TOIS 2023). Compares RRF with convex combinations and reports on tuning and sensitivity.
  • Elasticsearch documentation, "Reciprocal rank fusion". The formula, rank_constant and rank_window_size as implemented.
  • Manning, Raghavan and Schütze, "Introduction to Information Retrieval" (2008). Background on combining evidence and on evaluating a combined ranking.
  • Anthropic, "Introducing Contextual Retrieval" (September 2024). Reports an applied pipeline of embeddings, BM25, rank fusion and reranking and the failure rates at each step; the subject of case study 1.
  • Thakur et al., "BEIR: A Heterogeneous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models" (2021). The benchmark that popularised comparing lexical and neural retrievers on many domains.

Check your understanding

A colleague fuses retrievers with score = bm25 + cosine. For most queries the top five results look dominated by BM25. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. Cosine similarity is more accurate and is being ignored on purpose
  2. BM25 has a bug that doubles its score
  3. BM25 scores are on a much larger and more variable scale than cosines, so they swamp the sum
  4. The inverted index is faster, so its results arrive first
BM25 is an unbounded sum of idf-weighted terms (8.24 for one query above) while cosines sit in a narrow band such as 0.5 to 0.95. Adding them gives BM25 most of the influence. Normalise each list per query, or use rank-based fusion. Speed of arrival plays no part in a score sum.

With k = 60, document A is ranked 1st by BM25 and is absent from the dense list. Document B is ranked 40th by both. Which is fused higher?

  1. B: 2/(60+40) = 0.0200 against 1/61 = 0.0164
  2. A: first place always wins
  3. They tie
  4. It depends on the BM25 scores
B beats A whenever its rank r in both lists is below k + 2 = 62, and 40 is. RRF ignores the raw BM25 scores, so the fourth option is wrong, and first place in one list does not win automatically (that would need a small k such as 1).

Min-max normalisation is applied to a BM25 list whose scores are 41.0, 6.1, 5.9, 5.8, 5.5. What happens to the four lower documents?

  1. They keep their relative advantage over the dense list
  2. They are removed from the list
  3. They become 1.0 each
  4. They are squeezed to values near 0 (about 0.01 to 0.02), so BM25 can barely distinguish between them
The range is 41.0 − 5.5 = 35.5, so 6.1 becomes 0.017 and 5.5 becomes 0. One outlier consumes the scale; the dense side then decides the order of the rest. Z-scores or rank-based fusion are less sensitive.

You tune α for a weighted sum on 20 judged queries and get nDCG@10 of 0.74, up from 0.69 for RRF. You report 0.74 as the expected production gain. What is wrong?

  1. Nothing: tuning on judged queries always generalises
  2. The figure was measured on the same queries used to choose α, so it is optimistic; use a held-out split or cross-validation and report an interval
  3. nDCG@10 cannot be used for fusion
  4. α must always equal 0.5
The sweep in this chapter shows the in-sample best (0.873) above the leave-one-query-out estimate (0.856). With only 20 queries the gap and the noise can both be large. Report the held-out figure with a paired comparison against the RRF baseline.

BM25 returns chunk ids such as doc7#3, while the vector store returns whole-document ids such as doc7. After fusion no document appears in both lists. What went wrong and what is the fix?

  1. The identifiers differ in kind, so contributions never add up; map both lists onto one unit (chunk or document) before fusing
  2. RRF cannot fuse more than one chunk of a document
  3. The value of k is too large
  4. The vector store has no recall
Fusion sums contributions per identifier. If the two retrievers use different identifiers for the same content, the lists do not overlap and the "agreement" effect is lost. Pick the unit, map both lists to it (for example keep the best chunk per document), and then fuse.

In the worked example, the irrelevant passage t1 outranks the relevant r1 after RRF, and t2 almost ties it. What does this show, and what is the usual remedy?

  1. RRF is broken and k must be raised to 1,000
  2. The dense list should be deleted from the pipeline
  3. Fusion rewards documents that several retrievers like, even when they are wrong; add a reranker that reads query and passage together
  4. The judged grades must be incorrect
Both retrievers ranked t1 and t2 highly because they share the words "money" and "back" with the query, so their contributions add up. A larger k would not change which documents appear in both lists. A cross-encoder can notice that the query is about refunds, not transfers (chapter 8).
