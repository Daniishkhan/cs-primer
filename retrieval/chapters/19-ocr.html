OCR: Classical Engines to Vision-Language Models

A scanned page is a grid of pixels, and everything downstream (search, retrieval, citation, extraction) needs characters. This chapter explains how software turns pixels into text, from template matching through Tesseract to models that write the page out like a language model, and why the two families fail in different and equally expensive ways: one makes obvious mistakes, the other makes fluent ones.

Why this matters

Chapter 18 showed that a PDF is often only pictures of text. If the pictures are read wrongly, every later stage inherits the error: the chunker splits garbage, the embedder embeds garbage, and the answer cites a page that says something different from what the index believes. OCR errors are also the most undetectable failure in a RAG system, because the text looks fine. After this chapter you can explain how a recogniser works, measure its error rate in a way that matches what you care about, recognise the failure signatures of classical and vision-language engines, and design a pipeline that routes, checks and audits instead of trusting one engine.

What OCR is for, and when you do not need it

Optical character recognition (OCR) is the task of turning an image of text into the characters it shows. It has been a product category since the 1970s (reading cheques and mail), and it is still the front door of most document pipelines: scanned contracts, photographed receipts, faxed forms, old reports digitised from microfilm, and screenshots.

The first question is whether you need it at all. As chapter 18 explained, a born-digital PDF already carries a text layer: the characters, their fonts and their positions, written by the program that made the file. Reading that layer is exact, instant and free. OCR is for pages where the characters were never stored, only their pictures. A page can also be mixed: a born-digital report with a scanned appendix, or a text layer that was itself produced by a poor OCR run years ago and is now wrong. So the useful habit is the one from chapter 18: check the text layer first, and OCR only what it cannot give you.

What does OCR add to your corpus? Four things, each with its own accuracy question:

  • Characters: the string itself. Wrong characters mean missed matches and wrong numbers.
  • Positions: a bounding box for each word or line. Positions let you highlight a citation on the page image, and they feed layout analysis and table recognition (chapter 20).
  • Order: which text comes before which, in a page with columns, sidebars and footnotes.
  • Confidence: how sure the engine was, per character, word or line. This is the raw material for deciding what to send to a human.

The classical pipeline

Until about 2023 almost all production OCR followed one design: break the problem into stages, and make each stage do one job. Tesseract, the best-known open engine, shows the shape. It was developed at Hewlett-Packard laboratories between 1985 and 1994, released as open source by HP in 2005, and then developed under Google's sponsorship from 2006 to 2017; version 4 added a neural network recogniser, and version 5.0.0 appeared on 30 November 2021 (Tesseract project README, checked October 2026). It ships language data for more than 100 languages.

Stage 1: clean up

Scans are rarely square to the paper. Deskewing estimates the angle of the text lines and rotates the image straight. Binarisation converts greys or colours to pure black and white by choosing a threshold, so that ink and paper separate. A single global threshold fails on a page with a shadow from the scanner lid, so engines use adaptive thresholds computed per neighbourhood (Otsu's method of 1979 picks one global threshold from the histogram; local variants such as Sauvola's do it per window). Further steps remove speckle, remove ruled lines that would join letters, and rescale: recognisers need enough pixels per letter, and 300 dots per inch is the usual advice for body text. A fax at 200 dpi or a phone photo taken from far away is starved of pixels, and no later stage can recover them.

Stage 2: detect the text

Text detection finds where the text is: it outputs regions, usually lines or words, as boxes or polygons. On clean documents this can be classical image processing (connected blobs of ink grouped into lines). On photographs, curved labels and cluttered pages, neural detectors are used instead; EAST (Zhou et al., 2017), CRAFT (Baek et al., 2019) and DB (Liao et al., 2020) are widely cited examples, and PaddleOCR packages a detector, a text-direction classifier and a recogniser as one toolkit. A detection miss is a silent loss: text the detector never boxed is never read, and nothing downstream knows it existed.

Stage 3: recognise

Recognition takes one cropped line or word and outputs its characters. This is the heart of OCR, and it is worth building a small one to see what it can and cannot do.

Recognition by template matching: a toy you can run

The oldest recogniser compares each character image with stored templates and picks the closest. The program below does exactly that on a 5 by 7 pixel font that we define ourselves. It is a stand-in for the first stage of the idea, not a usable OCR engine: real glyphs vary in font, size and weight, and real pages need the segmentation step that we cheat on by knowing every character occupies a fixed cell. Its purpose is to make three effects visible: noise causes errors, errors cluster on look-alike shapes, and the margin between the best and second-best template carries information.

The alphabet deliberately includes the troublemakers of real OCR: the digit 0 and the letter O, the digit 1 and the letters l and I, the digit 8 and the letter B, the digit 5 and the letter S.

"""A toy character recogniser: 5x7 bitmap glyphs, pixel noise, nearest-template matching."""
import random

G = {  # seven rows per glyph, joined by "/"
 "0": ".###./#...#/#..##/#.#.#/##..#/#...#/.###.", "O": ".###./#...#/#...#/#...#/#...#/#...#/.###.",
 "1": "..#../.##../..#../..#../..#../..#../.###.", "l": ".##../..#../..#../..#../..#../..#../.###.",
 "I": ".###./..#../..#../..#../..#../..#../.###.", "2": ".###./#...#/....#/...#./..#../.#.../#####",
 "3": "####./....#/....#/.###./....#/....#/####.", "4": "...#./..##./.#.#./#..#./#####/...#./...#.",
 "5": "#####/#..../####./....#/....#/#...#/.###.", "6": "..##./.#.../#..../####./#...#/#...#/.###.",
 "7": "#####/....#/...#./..#../.#.../.#.../.#...", "8": ".###./#...#/#...#/.###./#...#/#...#/.###.",
 "9": ".###./#...#/#...#/.####/....#/...#./.##..", "S": ".####/#..../#..../.###./....#/....#/####.",
 "B": "####./#...#/#...#/####./#...#/#...#/####.",
}
BITS = {c: [int(ch == "#") for ch in g.replace("/", "")] for c, g in G.items()}

def render(text, flip, rng):
    """One 35-pixel cell per character; each pixel flips with probability `flip`."""
    return [[b ^ (rng.random() < flip) for b in BITS[c]] for c in text]

def recognise(cells, alphabet=None):
    """Nearest template by Hamming distance. Confidence is the gap to the runner-up."""
    out = []
    for cell in cells:
        d = sorted((sum(a != b for a, b in zip(cell, BITS[c])), c) for c in (alphabet or BITS))
        out.append((d[0][1], d[1][0] - d[0][0]))
    return out

def edit_distance(a, b):
    prev = list(range(len(b) + 1))
    for i, x in enumerate(a, 1):
        cur = [i]
        for j, y in enumerate(b, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1, cur[j - 1] + 1, prev[j - 1] + (x != y)))
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def cer(ref, hyp):
    """Character error rate: edit distance divided by the reference length."""
    return edit_distance(ref, hyp) / len(ref)

Now render random 12-character strings with a given probability of flipping each pixel (scanner noise, in the crudest sense) and measure the character error rate (CER). Defined precisely below, it is the minimum number of single-character edits needed to turn the output into the truth, divided by the length of the truth. Run once with the full alphabet, and once with the knowledge that the field is a number.

import random
from collections import Counter
from toyocr import *

rng = random.Random(7)
ALL = list(BITS)
DIGITS = "0123456789"
lines = ["".join(rng.choice(ALL) for _ in range(12)) for _ in range(200)]
nums = ["".join(rng.choice(DIGITS) for _ in range(12)) for _ in range(200)]
print("flip   CER(any text)  CER(digit field, alphabet=0-9)")
for p in (0.0, 0.03, 0.06, 0.10, 0.15):
    r = random.Random(1)
    e1 = sum(edit_distance(t, "".join(c for c, _ in recognise(render(t, p, r)))) for t in lines) / (12 * len(lines))
    r = random.Random(1)
    e2 = sum(edit_distance(t, "".join(c for c, _ in recognise(render(t, p, r), DIGITS))) for t in nums) / (12 * len(nums))
    print(f"{p:.2f}   {e1:6.1%}          {e2:6.1%}")

# which characters get confused at 6% noise?
r = random.Random(2); conf = Counter()
for t in lines:
    for a, (b, _) in zip(t, recognise(render(t, 0.06, r))):
        if a != b: conf[(a, b)] += 1
print("most common confusions at 6% noise:", conf.most_common(6))

0.00     0.0%            0.0%
0.03     1.3%            0.0%
0.06     2.7%            0.1%
0.10     5.3%            0.6%
0.15    10.0%            2.5%
most common confusions at 6% noise: [(('l', '1'), 22), (('I', 'l'), 12), (('l', 'I'), 12), (('B', '8'), 2), (('8', 'B'), 1), (('3', 'B'), 1)]

Three lessons sit in that table. First, errors rise smoothly with noise: at 6% of pixels flipped, about 3 characters in 100 are wrong. Second, the errors are not spread evenly. They concentrate on l, 1 and I, which differ by only a few pixels, with the occasional B and 8. Every OCR engine has such a confusion table, and it is learned from the typeface and the scan quality. Third, and the most useful for engineering: when you know what a field can contain, tell the recogniser. Restricting the alphabet to digits cut the error rate from 2.7% to 0.1% at the same noise level, because the confusable letters were no longer candidates. Real engines expose the same idea as character whitelists, and it is why recognising a postcode field is easier than recognising free text.

Confidence is information, if you keep it

The recogniser also knew when it was unsure. The second number we returned, the gap between the best template and the runner-up, is a crude confidence. Does it predict mistakes?

import random
from toyocr import *

rng = random.Random(7)
ALL = list(BITS)
lines = ["".join(rng.choice(ALL) for _ in range(12)) for _ in range(400)]
r = random.Random(3)
ok, bad = [], []
for t in lines:
    for a, (b, m) in zip(t, recognise(render(t, 0.08, r))):
        (ok if a == b else bad).append(m)
print(f"characters: {len(ok)+len(bad)}, wrong: {len(bad)} ({len(bad)/(len(ok)+len(bad)):.1%})")
print(f"mean margin  correct: {sum(ok)/len(ok):.1f}   wrong: {sum(bad)/len(bad):.1f}")
for thr in (1, 2, 3, 4):
    flagged_bad = sum(m < thr for m in bad); flagged_ok = sum(m < thr for m in ok)
    print(f"flag margin < {thr}: catches {flagged_bad/len(bad):5.1%} of errors, flags {flagged_ok/len(ok):5.1%} of correct characters")
characters: 4800, wrong: 143 (3.0%)
mean margin  correct: 4.6   wrong: 0.5
flag margin < 1: catches 47.6% of errors, flags  1.3% of correct characters
flag margin < 2: catches 99.3% of errors, flags 20.0% of correct characters
flag margin < 3: catches 100.0% of errors, flags 29.4% of correct characters
flag margin < 4: catches 100.0% of errors, flags 52.3% of correct characters

Wrong characters have a mean margin near 0.5; correct ones near 4.6. Flagging every character with a margin below 2 catches 99% of the errors while flagging a fifth of the correct ones. That is the trade a review queue makes: spend human attention on a fifth of the characters to remove almost all errors. A real engine's confidence is not this clean (it can be confidently wrong, and engines differ in how well calibrated they are), but the principle stands. A pipeline that throws away the confidences, as many do by keeping only the text, throws away the cheapest quality control it had.

Analogy: two ways to copy a handwritten letter

A classical engine is a literal typist who copies shapes without understanding the content. Smudged ink becomes a stray symbol or a wrong letter, and the typist often puts a question mark where a letter is hopeless. A vision-language model is an editor who reads the page for meaning and writes it out. A smudged word gets restored from context, usually correctly, and the page reads naturally.

Where it breaks down: a human editor knows when they guessed, and would mark the guess. A model has no such habit: it will restore a smudged word and an unusual but perfectly clear word in the same confident voice, and nothing in the output tells you which one it did.

From templates to learned recognisers: CRNN and CTC

Real text lines cannot be cut into neat equal cells. Letters touch (in cursive they are joined), widths vary, and the same letter appears at many sizes. Modern recognisers therefore skip character segmentation. A convolutional recurrent neural network (CRNN; Shi, Bai and Yao, 2015) reads the whole line image at once: convolutional layers (deep learning, chapter 7) turn the image into a sequence of feature vectors, one per narrow vertical slice; recurrent layers let each slice see its neighbours on both sides, which is how the network decides that a slice belongs to the middle of an m and not the start of an n; and a final layer scores every character class for every slice. Tesseract's version 4 recogniser is a related design built on LSTM layers working along the line.

This raises a puzzle. The training data is a line image and its transcription, hello, but nobody has marked which slices belong to which letter. The network emits, say, nine labels for nine slices, and the text has five letters. Connectionist temporal classification (CTC; Graves et al., 2006) is the device that links the two. It adds one extra class, the blank, meaning "no new character here", and defines decoding by two rules: first merge consecutive identical labels, then delete the blanks. Step through it.

Step through

CTC decoding: nine slices become "hello"

  1. Per-slice labels. The network has scored every class for each of nine slices, and we take the best one in each. The underscore is the blank. Several neighbouring slices see the same letter because letters are wider than a slice.
  2. Merge runs. Consecutive identical labels collapse into one: the two h slices become one h, the two e slices one e. Nothing has been deleted yet.
  3. Drop the blanks. Blanks mean "no new character". Removing them leaves h, e, l, l, o, and the line reads "hello".
  4. Why the blank exists. The word has two l's in a row. If the slices were l, l, l with no separator, the merge rule could not tell one long l from two. The blank in between is how the network says "a new l starts here". It is also how CTC copes with gaps and with the same letter repeated.

The training loss never needs the alignment. For a given transcription there are many label sequences of the right length that decode to it (h h _ e e l _ l o, h _ _ e l _ _ l o, and so on). CTC's loss is the negative log of the total probability of all of them, which a dynamic programme computes in time proportional to slices times transcription length. Training pushes up the probability of the correct text without anyone saying where each letter is.

Go deeper the CTC probability

Let the network output, for slice t, a distribution pt(k) over the character classes plus the blank. A path π picks one class per slice, and its probability is the product ∏t pt(πt) (CTC assumes the slices are independent given the image features). Let B be the rule "merge runs, then delete blanks". The probability of a transcription y is

P(y | image) = ∑π: B(π) = y ∏t=1..T pt(πt)

and the loss is −log P(y | image). The sum has exponentially many terms, but the forward algorithm, the same idea as in hidden Markov models, folds it into a table with one row per prefix of the (blank-padded) transcription and one column per slice. At test time most engines use the greedy decode you just stepped through, or a beam search, optionally combined with a dictionary or a character language model, which is how "recieve" gets pulled towards "receive".

Detection, reading order and the things recognition does not do

A line recogniser reads one line at a time. Everything else is the pipeline's job, and each part has its own failure:

  • Missed or merged lines. A detector may skip faint text, merge two columns into one line, or split a line at a wide gap. Merged columns produce sentences that run from one column into the other, a classic source of nonsense chunks.
  • Wrong reading order. Even with perfect lines, deciding that the sidebar comes after the body, and that a footnote does not interrupt a sentence, is a separate problem handled by layout analysis (chapter 18).
  • Non-text content. Stamps, signatures, logos and chart labels may be read as text, skipped, or turned into junk.
  • Scripts and directions. A model trained on Latin text does not read Arabic or Thai; text running right to left or top to bottom needs the right recogniser. Chapter 22 is about this.

A staged engine reports its output for each stage, usually as a table with one row per word: the page, block, paragraph and line it belongs to, its box (left, top, width, height) and a confidence from 0 to 100. Tesseract's TSV output has exactly these columns. That structure is the reason classical engines remain valuable even when a newer model reads better: you can see where the text is, how sure the engine was, and you can store it for highlighting.

Measuring OCR: error rates and what they hide

To compare engines or catch regressions you need a number. The standard ones come from the edit distance (Levenshtein, 1966): the smallest number of single-character substitutions (S), deletions (D) and insertions (I) that turn the engine's output into the reference text.

CER = (S + D + I) / Nref

where Nref is the number of characters in the reference. The word error rate (WER) is the same calculation on words. The values can exceed 100% (many insertions), and they are not probabilities; they are costs. Try it below, including the presets that show where the number misleads.

Four cautions apply to every error-rate figure you read, including your own.

  1. Normalise before comparing, and say how. Case, whitespace, hyphenation at line ends, ligatures (the single character "fi" versus "f" and "i"), curly versus straight quotes and full-width forms all change CER without changing meaning. Chapter 1's Unicode normalisation (text analysis) is the first step; a published CER without a normalisation policy cannot be compared with another.
  2. Character errors are not equally costly. A wrong letter in "Cab1e" costs one stray miss in search. A wrong digit in an amount is a different invoice. With a 99% character accuracy and independent errors, a 12-digit account number is read correctly with probability 0.9912, about 89%, so one in nine is wrong. For fields that matter, measure field-level exact match and, for numbers, whether the parsed value is equal.
  3. Order contaminates the score. If an engine reads two columns in the wrong order, every character is correct and a page-level CER (computed on the concatenated text) is terrible; if you match lines first, the same output scores perfectly. State which you measured, and score reading order separately.
  4. A mean hides the tail. A pipeline with 1% mean CER may have 95 pages at 0.2% and 5 pages at 16%. Report the distribution and look at the worst pages, as the error-analysis habits of evals chapter 1 recommend.

Common misconception

"Our OCR is 99% accurate, so the text is reliable." The 99% is an average over characters, and the characters that matter (digits, names, units, negations) are a small, high-stakes minority. Errors also correlate: a poor scan, a rare font or a stamp over a figure affects a whole region at once, so they are not spread evenly in the way the arithmetic above assumed. Accuracy that matters is measured on the fields you act on, on pages like the worst ones you will receive.

Vision-language models as OCR

Since about 2023, a second family has joined the first. A vision-language model (VLM; deep learning, chapter 21) cuts an image into patches, encodes them with a vision transformer, projects the result into the language model's embedding space, and lets the language model write text conditioned on the image. Asked to "transcribe this page", it generates the page's text token by token, exactly as it would continue a sentence. Nothing in the architecture separates detection from recognition or recognition from reading order; the model does all of it in one pass, and it can be asked for a different output format each time: plain text, Markdown with tables, HTML, or JSON.

That is the strength. The model reads cursive that defeated line recognisers, because it uses context to resolve ambiguous strokes. It writes a table as a table, a formula as LaTeX, and a two-column article in sensible order, without a hand-built layout stage. A system that needed five components now needs a prompt.

Two design families have emerged, and the difference matters when you evaluate them:

Pipeline-style

A layout detector first finds regions (paragraph, table, formula, figure). A small recogniser model then reads each cropped region. PaddleOCR-VL follows this layout-then-recognition design. Errors are localised, region boxes come for free, and failures are easier to isolate.

End-to-end

One model is given the whole page and writes everything. olmOCR, a 7-billion-parameter vision-language model fine-tuned on about 260,000 pages (Poznanski et al., 2025), is of this kind. It is simpler to deploy, and it can fix reading order using global context, but a mistake can affect the rest of the page, and there are no boxes unless it is trained to output them.

Costs

A language model decoder is expensive per page compared with a CNN-LSTM line recogniser. The olmOCR paper reports converting a million pages for about 176 US dollars with its own pipeline, against more than 6,240 dollars for the same job on GPT-4o by the authors' estimate (both are the paper's own figures at the time of writing, 2025, and depend on prices and hardware). A classical engine on a CPU, for scale, costs only the CPU time. The serving side of VLM-based OCR is ordinary LLM serving: batches of page images, GPU memory held by the KV cache and throughput set by tokens per second, as in LLM training course, chapter 20 and chapter 22. A dense page can run to a few thousand output tokens (an estimate, not a measurement), so a page costs seconds of GPU time where a classical engine costs a fraction of a second on a CPU core.

Systems connection

OCR is a batch workload with a heavy tail. Page time varies by a factor of ten between a clean letter and a dense table, so a queue with a fixed worker pool shows the knee behaviour of queueing theory, and one 400-page scan can occupy a GPU for minutes unless you split it into per-page jobs. Retries on page-level failures need the idempotency habits of failure handling (a page processed twice must not index twice). For the hardware side, see parallelism and GPUs.

When the model writes instead of reads

The key difference between the two families is how they are wrong. A line recogniser maps pixels to characters through a visual comparison, so its errors are visual: a letter O for a zero, a dropped character in a blur. A VLM's decoder is a language model: given an image it is uncertain about, it produces the most plausible continuation. Most of the time plausible is correct, which is why these models score well. When the image is ambiguous, plausible and correct come apart.

All of these are instances of the hallucination described in evals chapter 10, with an unusual feature: the model is not asked to know anything, only to copy. That makes the errors surprising to teams, and it makes them hard to catch by reading the output, because fluent text invites trust. A reviewer proofreading VLM output for sense will approve exactly the errors that matter.

The script below shows the signature in miniature. It uses scripted stand-ins for two engines (the strings are written by hand, not produced by real OCR), applied to a four-line invoice. The "classical" output has visual slips; the "VLM" output is fluent with one plausible digit change; the third has a silently dropped row. We score each against the truth by CER and then run checks that need no ground truth: each row's quantity times price must equal its amount, and the rows must add up to the total.

import re
from toyocr import cer

TRUTH = ["Cable tray 40 x 12.50 = 500.00",
         "Junction box 25 x 8.40 = 210.00",
         "Labour 16 x 55.00 = 880.00",
         "Total 1,590.00"]
# Scripted stand-ins for two engines (NOT real OCR output): each has the failure style typical of its kind.
CLASSICAL = ["Cab1e tray 40 x 12.50 = 500.00",      # letter-level slip in a word
             "Junction box 25 x 8.4O = 21O.OO",      # letter O for zero: not a number any more
             "Labour 16 x 55.00 = 880.00",
             "Total 1,590.00"]
VLM = ["Cable tray 40 x 12.50 = 500.00",
       "Junction box 25 x 8.40 = 210.00",
       "Labour 16 x 55.00 = 830.00",                # fluent, well-formed, wrong
       "Total 1,590.00"]
VLM_DROP = [TRUTH[0], TRUTH[2], TRUTH[3]]          # a silently omitted row

def money(s): return float(s.replace(",", ""))

def validate(lines):
    """Return a list of problems found by checks that need no ground truth."""
    problems, total, subtotal = [], None, 0.0
    for ln in lines:
        m = re.fullmatch(r"(.+?) (\d+) x (\d+\.\d\d) = (\d+\.\d\d)", ln)
        t = re.fullmatch(r"Total ([\d,]+\.\d\d)", ln)
        if m:
            qty, price, amount = int(m[2]), float(m[3]), float(m[4])
            subtotal += amount
            if abs(qty * price - amount) > 0.005:
                problems.append(f"{m[1]!r}: {qty} x {price:.2f} = {qty*price:.2f}, not {amount:.2f}")
        elif t:
            total = money(t[1])
        else:
            problems.append(f"unparseable line: {ln!r}")
    if total is not None and abs(subtotal - total) > 0.005:
        problems.append(f"rows sum to {subtotal:.2f}, total says {total:.2f}")
    return problems

for name, out in [("classical", CLASSICAL), ("vlm", VLM), ("vlm, row dropped", VLM_DROP)]:
    print(f"{name:17s} CER {cer(chr(10).join(TRUTH), chr(10).join(out)):6.2%}  problems: {validate(out) or 'none'}")
classical         CER  4.81%  problems: ["unparseable line: 'Junction box 25 x 8.4O = 21O.OO'", 'rows sum to 1380.00, total says 1590.00']
vlm               CER  0.96%  problems: ["'Labour': 16 x 55.00 = 880.00, not 830.00", 'rows sum to 1540.00, total says 1590.00']
vlm, row dropped  CER 30.77%  problems: ['rows sum to 1380.00, total says 1590.00']

Read the numbers twice. The classical output has a CER of 4.8%, five times the VLM's 0.96%, and it is the easier case: letters in numbers are unparseable, so any numeric validation rejects the line. The VLM's output has a better score and a worse problem: the wrong amount is a perfectly formatted number, and only the internal arithmetic (16 × 55.00 is 880.00, not 830.00) exposes it. The third case has a CER of 31% because nearly a third of the text vanished, but it is the arithmetic check that catches it automatically and names the cause.

Defences that work

  • Redundancy that does not share failure modes. Run a classical engine and a VLM, align them and review only the disagreements. The two make different errors, so agreement is strong evidence; a field where both agree and arithmetic holds is very probably right. A text layer, when present and trusted, is a third voice.
  • Validators on the extracted values. Sums, date plausibility, checksums on identifiers, units and ranges (chapter 20). Validators are the best defence against plausible errors, because they use structure the image does not.
  • Log-probabilities where available. Open VLMs expose token probabilities. Low-probability tokens in digits and names flag likely guesses. They are an imperfect signal, since a model can be confidently wrong, but cheaper than any other.
  • Crop and re-read. For a flagged field, crop the region from the image and ask a second, narrower question ("what is the amount in this box?") with greedy decoding. A small crop leaves little room to invent.
  • Page-level sanity checks. Output length against the number of detected lines, repeated-line detection, language identification against the expected language, and an upper bound on tokens catch omissions, loops and drift.
  • Keep the evidence. Store the page image and, where you have them, boxes, so a human reviewer, or an auditor six months later, can look at the original. A citation that points at OCR text without the image behind it cannot be checked.
  • Treat page text as data. Never let OCR output be interpreted as instructions by a downstream model; delimit it and handle it as untrusted input, as in security chapter 9.

Routing: using each engine where it is strong

The conclusion is not to pick a winner. Each engine has a regime where it is cheap and accurate, and a regime where it is neither. A production pipeline routes between them, and verifies the result before accepting it.

The router can be rule-based (the engine's own confidence is low, the region is a table, the script is handwriting) or learned, and it should be tuned with your own error analysis rather than a published benchmark. The table below is a starting hypothesis.

State of play, October 2026

Document OCR leaderboards turn over every few months, so treat every number here as a dated snapshot of self-reported results, not a ranking to build on.

  • OmniDocBench (Ouyang et al., CVPR 2025) scores whole-page parsing. Its Overall figure averages text accuracy (one minus edit distance), table structure similarity (TEDS, explained in chapter 20) and a formula metric. When read in October 2026 the project README listed specialised document models at the top, from TeleOCR at 96.91 to GLM-OCR at 95.22, general-purpose VLMs such as Gemini 3 Pro at 92.91 and Qwen3-VL-235B at 89.78, and the pipeline tool Marker at 78.44. The README labels its table "v1.6_full" while announcing v1.7, and its text evaluation covers Chinese and English only.
  • olmOCR-Bench (Allen Institute for AI) has more than 7,000 test cases over 1,400 documents. Its README listed Chandra OCR at 83.1, olmOCR v0.4.0 at 82.4, PaddleOCR-VL at 80.0 and Marker at 76.1, with old scans the weakest category for every model (47.7 for olmOCR v0.4.0).
  • Two things to carry away. Small specialised models (under about 2 billion parameters in the OmniDocBench top five) now match or beat much larger general models on these benchmarks, and the rankings differ between benchmarks, so neither is a decision. Neither benchmark covers your documents.

What will not change: cost differences between CPU and GPU, the fluent-failure signature, and the need to measure on your own pages.

Building your own measurement

The closing exercise for any OCR choice is a small, honest test set. Collect 50 to 100 pages from your real intake, chosen to include the worst scans, not the average ones. Transcribe the fields and passages you act on, by hand, with a second person checking a sample (human labels). Run every candidate engine; report CER on normalised text, field-level exact match on the values that matter, the share of pages with any failed validator, the rate of omissions (reference lines absent from output) and a per-page worst case, with confidence intervals as in statistics for evals. Re-run it whenever you change an engine, a model version or a rendering setting. Lab 6 builds the scoring half of this on OCR boxes, and case study 7 examines how two open converters were evaluated.

Key takeaways

  • Use the PDF text layer when it is present and trustworthy. OCR is for pixels with no characters behind them.
  • A staged engine finds lines, recognises each one, and keeps boxes and confidences. A learned line recogniser labels narrow slices, and CTC (merge repeats, drop blanks) turns the labels into text.
  • Character error rate is edit distance over reference length. Normalise first, score the fields you act on, report the tail, and remember that 99% character accuracy leaves one in nine 12-digit numbers wrong.
  • Vision-language models read layout and context at once and write tables and formulas, at a higher cost per page. Their characteristic failure is fluent: plausible substitution, silent omission, autocorrection, invention.
  • Defend with validators, redundancy across engines that fail differently, token probabilities, crop-and-reread, and by keeping the page image with every result.
  • Route by region and difficulty, verify before indexing, send low-confidence results to people, and measure on your own worst pages. Leaderboards are dated and do not cover your documents.

Check your understanding

A mixed scan contains pages that are born-digital with a correct text layer and pages that are photographs of paper. What should the pipeline do first?

  1. Run OCR on every page so that all text has the same provenance
  2. Check each page for a usable text layer and OCR only the pages that lack one
  3. Run the vision-language model on everything, since it is the most accurate engine
  4. Convert every page to an image first to remove inconsistencies between pages
The text layer is exact and free. OCR on a page that already has characters only adds error and cost, and it throws away the font and position information the file stored. The check is per page because a single file can mix both kinds.

A CTC decoder sees the per-slice labels c, c, _, c, a, a, t. What text does it output, and why?

  1. "ccat", because merging only removes blanks
  2. "cat", because all repeated letters merge into one
  3. "ccaat", because each slice is a character
  4. "ccat", because the blank stops the first two c's merging with the third
Runs of identical labels merge first (c c becomes c, a a becomes a), then blanks are deleted. The blank between the second and third c keeps them apart, giving c, c, a, t. Option 2 is the tempting error: without the blank, the c's would merge, which is exactly the problem the blank solves for words with double letters.

Engine A has a CER of 4% and its errors are letters in numbers. Engine B has a CER of 1% and its errors are well-formed but wrong digits. For extracting invoice totals, which statement is most reasonable?

  1. B may be riskier despite its lower CER, because its errors pass format checks and need arithmetic validators to catch
  2. B is better because its CER is lower and CER measures extraction quality
  3. A is useless because 4% is above any acceptable threshold
  4. The two are equivalent because both have at least one wrong character
CER counts characters, not consequences. A's errors break the number format and are caught by parsing; B's pass every format check and change the amount. Option 2 is the tempting one, but the question that matters is how many wrong amounts reach the ledger after your checks, not the mean character error.

A recogniser confuses 0 and O, and the target field is an account number made of digits only. What is the cheapest effective fix?

  1. Retrain the recogniser on a larger set of scans
  2. Upscale all images to a higher resolution
  3. Restrict the recogniser's candidate characters to the digits for that field
  4. Switch to a larger vision-language model
The confusable letter is removed from the candidate set, which the toy experiment showed cutting errors from 2.7% to 0.1% at the same noise. Retraining and larger models may help but cost far more, and a VLM would add fluent failure modes. Resolution helps only if the pixels were the limit.

Which defence is most likely to catch a vision-language model quietly changing 880.00 to 830.00 in one row of an invoice?

  1. Proofreading the output for grammatical sense
  2. Checking that quantity times unit price equals the amount, and that the rows sum to the total
  3. Lowering the temperature to zero
  4. Increasing the image resolution
The altered number is well-formed, so reading for sense passes it. Greedy decoding makes output repeatable but does not make it right, and the model may have been equally wrong at higher resolution. Arithmetic uses structure the image does not and therefore catches the plausible error.

You score two OCR engines on 80 pages. Engine X has mean CER 1.0% with a worst page at 14%; engine Y has mean CER 1.4% with a worst page at 3%. The documents are legal filings where any omitted clause is a serious problem. What is the best next step?

  1. Choose X, since the mean is lower
  2. Choose Y, since the worst case is lower, and stop evaluating
  3. Average the two engines' scores and compare with a published leaderboard
  4. Look at what happens on X's worst pages and measure omissions directly, then decide on the distribution and the omission rate
The mean hides the tail, and CER does not isolate omissions. The right move is error analysis on the worst pages plus a metric aimed at the risk you care about. Choosing on the mean alone ignores the tail; choosing on the worst case alone, with 80 pages, ignores sampling noise; leaderboards do not cover your filings.
