How RAG Fails

A RAG system that gives a wrong answer has failed at one particular stage, and the fix depends on which. This chapter lays out the five ways a retrieval-augmented answer goes wrong (missed retrieval, wrong chunk, ignored context, stale sources and confident synthesis), shows how to tell them apart from a single logged request, and ties each to the repair that works.

Why this matters

The commonest way to waste a month on a RAG system is to treat "the answers are bad" as one problem. Someone swaps the embedding model when the right passage was already being retrieved; someone else rewrites the prompt when the right passage never made it in. Every stage has its own failures and its own tools, and a team that cannot say which stage failed on a given request is guessing. After this chapter you can take a failed request, work out in a few minutes which of five stages broke first, look up the matching fix, and measure whether the fix helped.

A chain fails at its weakest link

You met the whole pipeline in chapter 13: a question is turned into a query, retrieved passages are ranked, some are assembled into a prompt, a model writes an answer, and the answer is checked and cited (chapter 16). The answer is right only if every stage did its job. If, say, the right passage is retrieved 90 per cent of the time, survives reranking and prompt assembly 85 per cent of the time, is used correctly by the model 90 per cent of the time and is cited correctly 95 per cent of the time, then about 65 per cent of requests are fully good (0.90 × 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.95 ≈ 0.65), even though no individual stage looks alarming. The numbers are illustrative assumptions and the stages are not independent in real systems, but the arithmetic is the point: multiplying several "pretty good" stages gives an answer rate that surprises people.

The first systematic account of where those failures happen came from engineering experience. Barnett et al. (2024, "Seven Failure Points When Engineering a Retrieval Augmented Generation System") built RAG systems for three case studies, in research, education and biomedicine, and listed seven failure points. They are a useful checklist, and they line up with the stages as follows.

A word on method. The failure points in that paper come from three projects' experience, not from a measurement of how often each occurs in general, and the paper itself says so. Treat the list as a vocabulary, and measure the frequencies in your system. The rest of this chapter shows how.

Analogy: an open-book exam

RAG is an open-book exam. A student can fail it in five different ways: the book does not contain the answer (missing source); the student opens the wrong chapter (missed retrieval); the right chapter is open but they skim past the line (ignored context); they use last year's edition (stale source); or they cannot find the answer and write something plausible anyway (confident synthesis). A teacher who just says "study harder" fixes none of these. A teacher who looks at where the student's finger was fixes the right one.

Where it breaks down: a student knows when they could not find the answer. A language model has no reliable sense of this, so the "write something plausible anyway" failure is more frequent in systems than in exams, and has to be engineered against rather than trusted to conscience.

The diagnosis discipline: find the first stage that failed

The central skill is failure attribution: for one failed request, identify the first stage at which the pipeline went wrong. "First" matters because later failures are often consequences. If the right passage was never retrieved, the generator's wrong answer is not a generator bug. This is the same move as the "first failure in the trace" of A6 chapter 1, applied to a pipeline whose stages you can inspect.

It needs two things. First, for each test question, a record of which passage contains the answer: the gold passage of the test collection you built in chapter 5. Second, a trace of what the pipeline did: the retrieved list with ranks, the passages that made it into the prompt, and the answer. With both, the diagnosis is a short sequence of yes or no questions, asked in order:

  1. Does the evidence exist in the corpus? If not, the correct behaviour is to abstain. Answering anyway is confident synthesis. If it should exist but does not, that is a content gap, which is a data problem and no retrieval tuning can fix it.
  2. Was the gold passage retrieved? Within the top k that you pass downstream. If not, it is a retrieval miss; note the rank it reached, because rank 4 with k = 3 is a different problem from rank 400.
  3. Did it reach the prompt? Deduplication, token budgets, permission filters and rerank cut-offs can drop a retrieved passage before the model sees it.
  4. Was something conflicting in the prompt too? An older or contradictory passage beside the right one is a stale or conflicting source failure if the answer followed it.
  5. Does the answer contain the fact? If the right passage was alone in the prompt and the answer is still wrong, the model ignored or mis-extracted it.

The first "no" is your diagnosis. To see this with numbers, we need a system whose internals we can read. The listing below, folded away because it is long, is a complete miniature RAG system over 13 passages of a made-up outdoor-gear shop's policies: BM25 and hashed character trigrams (a stand-in for a trained embedder) fused with reciprocal rank fusion (chapter 10), a context of the top k passages, and a rule-based generator that returns the sentence overlapping the question most. The generator is a deliberately simple stand-in for a language model: it is deterministic and inspectable, which a real model is not, but it is enough to show how each stage's failure looks. The corpus includes two versions of the returns policy on purpose.

STOP = set("a an the is are of to in on and or by for with be can do does i you your it my what how many much long there".split())
def words(s): return [w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9.]+", s.lower()) if w not in STOP]
def stem(w): return w.rstrip(".").rstrip("s")

# ---- retrievers: BM25 and hashed character trigrams (a stand-in for a trained embedder)
docs = {d: [stem(w) for w in words(t)] for d, _, _, t in CORPUS}
N = len(docs); avg = sum(map(len, docs.values())) / N
df = Counter(w for ws in docs.values() for w in set(ws))
def bm25(q, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    out = {}
    for d, ws in docs.items():
        tf = Counter(ws); s = 0.0
        for w in set(stem(x) for x in words(q)):
            if tf[w]:
                idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[w] + 0.5) / (df[w] + 0.5))
                s += idf * tf[w] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[w] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(ws) / avg))
        out[d] = s
    return out
def vec(t, dim=512):
    v = [0.0] * dim; t = " " + " ".join(words(t)) + " "
    for i in range(len(t) - 2): v[zlib.crc32(t[i:i+3].encode()) % dim] += 1
    n = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v)) or 1.0
    return [x / n for x in v]
DV = {d: vec(t) for d, _, _, t in CORPUS}
def dense(q):
    qv = vec(q); return {d: sum(a * b for a, b in zip(qv, v)) for d, v in DV.items()}
def rrf(*scorings, k=60):
    fused = Counter()
    for sc in scorings:
        for r, d in enumerate(sorted(sc, key=lambda x: -sc[x]), 1): fused[d] += 1 / (k + r)
    return [d for d, _ in fused.most_common()]

# ---- generator stand-in: returns the context sentence that overlaps the question most
def generate(q, context, abstain_below=None):
    qw = {stem(w) for w in words(q)}; best = (0.0, context[0][1].split(". ")[0])  # never silent: falls back to *something*
    for d, text in context:
        for s in re.split(r"(?<=\.)\s+", text):
            sw = {stem(w) for w in words(s)}
            score = len(qw & sw) / max(1, len(qw))
            if score > best[0]: best = (score, s)
    if abstain_below is not None and best[0] < abstain_below: return "NOT IN SOURCES", best[0]
    return best[1], best[0]

def run(k=3, only_latest=False, abstain_below=None):
    version = {d: v for d, _, v, _ in CORPUS}
    family = {d: f for d, f, _, _ in CORPUS}
    latest = {}
    for d in version: latest[family[d]] = max(latest.get(family[d], 0), version[d])
    text = {d: t for d, _, _, t in CORPUS}
    rows = []
    for q, gold, fact in QUESTIONS:
        ranked = rrf(bm25(q), dense(q))
        if only_latest: ranked = [d for d in ranked if version[d] == latest[family[d]]]
        ctx_ids = ranked[:k]
        ans, conf = generate(q, [(d, text[d]) for d in ctx_ids], abstain_below)
        if gold is None:    stage = "ok (abstained)" if ans == "NOT IN SOURCES" else "confident synthesis"
        elif gold not in ctx_ids: stage = "missed retrieval"
        elif fact.lower() in ans.lower(): stage = "ok"
        elif any(family[d] == family[gold] and d != gold for d in ctx_ids): stage = "stale/conflicting source"
        elif ans == "NOT IN SOURCES": stage = "false abstention"
        else: stage = "answer not extracted"
        rows.append((q, gold, ranked.index(gold) + 1 if gold in ranked else None, ans, stage))
    return rows

Six of twelve requests fail on the baseline, and they fail in five different ways. The overall score (6 of 12) says "bad". The breakdown says what to do: no single change repairs all of these. Notice in the lower table that fixes interact. Raising k from 3 to 5 cut missed retrievals from 2 to 1, but the extra context let the old returns policy into the prompt for a second question, so "stale" rose from 1 to 2. Filtering to the newest version removed every stale failure, which exposed two requests where the right passage was present and the generator extracted the wrong sentence. Each fix moved a failure to a later stage instead of making it vanish. That is normal, and it is why you measure the whole breakdown after every change, not one headline number.

Common misconception

"If the answer is wrong, the model hallucinated." Often the model did exactly what it was asked: it read the passages it was given and summarised them faithfully. The passages were wrong, old or incomplete. Calling every error a hallucination sends the team to the one stage (generation) that may be working perfectly. Attribute first, then name the cause.

Missed retrieval and the wrong chunk

The retrieval stage fails in two related ways. In a retrieval miss the gold passage is not among the results at all. In a ranking miss it is somewhere in the list but below the cut-off you pass to the model, while worse passages sit above it. The run above has one of each. "Can I send a gas cartridge to Hawaii by plane?" has gold rank 9: the policy says "Fuel canisters cannot be shipped by air", and the question shares no content word with it, so BM25 scores nothing and the character n-gram stand-in finds only weak overlap. Dense models trained on meaning do much better on such paraphrases, but they have their own blind spots: product codes, rare names and exact numbers.

"How long do refunds take?" is the ranking miss. The right passage came fourth, and the three above it included "Resoling takes 3 weeks", which shares only the generic word "takes". The generator, reading that context, dutifully answered "Resoling takes 3 weeks". This is the wrong chunk failure: a passage that looks relevant to the retriever, is ranked above the right one, and seeds a wrong answer. Wrong chunks are usually near-misses: another product's warranty, another region's terms, an older version, a paragraph about the same topic that does not answer this question.

The diagnosis for both is the same: measure the retriever on its own with the metrics of chapter 4. Recall@k at the k you actually pass downstream is the number that bounds the system: if the gold passage is in the top 5 for 80 per cent of questions, no generator can answer more than 80 per cent of them from retrieved evidence. The usual remedies, in the order they are usually tried:

  • Fix the analyser and the query. The vocabulary mismatch above is a query-understanding problem (chapter 1, chapter 3) and sometimes best solved by rewriting the query (chapter 15).
  • Combine retrievers. Lexical and dense retrieval miss different things; fusing them is the cheapest large gain (chapter 10).
  • Give chunks their context. A chunk reading "The warranty is 2 years" does not say which product. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval (September 2024) prepended a short LLM-written context to each chunk before embedding and BM25 indexing, and reported that the top-20 retrieval failure rate fell from 5.7 per cent to 3.7 per cent with contextual embeddings, to 2.9 per cent adding contextual BM25, and to 1.9 per cent adding a reranker, on their test sets. Those are vendor-reported numbers on their data; see chapter 14 and case study C1, and measure on your own corpus.
  • Rerank. Retrieve a wider list, k = 50 to 150, and let a cross-encoder choose the best handful (chapter 8). This addresses ranking misses directly.
  • Check filters. A metadata or permission filter applied before ranking can exclude the right passage entirely (chapter 12). If the gold passage is in the index but never appears, test the same query with the filters off.

Right passage, wrong answer: context that is ignored

Suppose retrieval did its job and the right passage is in the prompt. The answer can still go wrong, and in the run it does. "Does the stove warranty cover the burner head?" retrieves the right passage first: "Camping stoves carry a 1 year warranty against manufacturing defects. Burner heads are consumables and are not covered." The stand-in generator returns the first sentence, which overlaps the question on "stove" and "warranty", not the second, which holds the answer. A language model is far better at this than a word-overlap rule, but the failure it makes has the same shape: it answers from the most prominent part of the passage and misses the qualifier. In Barnett et al.'s terms this is FP4, not extracted, and its siblings are the wrong format (a list requested, prose returned), the wrong specificity, and the incomplete answer.

Position and quantity of context

Where the passage sits in the prompt matters. Liu et al. (2023, "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts", published in TACL) tested models on question answering with the relevant document moved around among many distractors, and found performance highest when the relevant information was at the beginning or end of the input and significantly worse when it sat in the middle, even for models built for long contexts. Case study C3 examines the paper closely. A year and more on, practitioners reported related effects: Chroma's technical report "Context Rot" (July 2025) tested 18 models on deliberately simple tasks while varying only the input length, and found performance became less reliable as inputs grew, with distractors making it worse.

State of play, October 2026

The position effects in these papers were measured on models from 2023 to 2025, and newer long-context models usually show smaller dips on simple needle-style tests. Do not assume the effect is gone for your task, model and context length: it is cheap to test by moving the gold passage around in your own prompts and measuring accuracy at each position. The durable lesson is that context is a budget, not a free resource (A4 chapter 10 and A4 chapter 12).

Distractors, passages that are related to the question but do not answer it, are worse than irrelevant noise. Cuconasu et al. (2024, "The Power of Noise") found that highly ranked passages that were related to the query but contained no answer hurt a RAG model's accuracy, while, surprisingly, adding unrelated random passages improved it by up to 35 per cent in their setting. Treat that as a warning that the relationship between context and accuracy is not monotonic, not as a trick to copy. Yoran et al. (2023, "Making Retrieval-Augmented Language Models Robust to Irrelevant Context") showed that irrelevant retrieved passages can lower accuracy, particularly on multi-hop questions, and that a simple NLI filter removes the harm but also throws away relevant passages, whereas fine-tuning on mixed relevant and irrelevant contexts made the model more robust.

Practical consequences: send fewer, better passages after reranking; test the value of k on your own questions rather than copying a default; and consider the ordering. Since models tend to use the edges of the context best, one cheap measure is to place the strongest passages at the start and end and the weakest in the middle.

def edge_order(ranked):
    """Place the best-ranked items at the START and END of the context and the
    weakest in the MIDDLE. ranked[0] is the best passage."""
    front, back = [], []
    for i, item in enumerate(ranked):
        (front if i % 2 == 0 else back).append(item)
    return front + back[::-1]

ranked = ["A", "B", "C", "D", "E", "F", "G"]          # A is the best match
print("relevance order:", ranked)
print("edge order     :", edge_order(ranked))
pos = {x: i + 1 for i, x in enumerate(edge_order(ranked))}
print("slot of the 3 best:", {x: pos[x] for x in "ABC"}, "of", len(ranked))

The deeper fixes target the extraction itself: ask the model to quote the relevant sentence first (chapter 16), split complex questions into steps (chapter 15), return the whole parent section when only a fragment was matched (chapter 14), and, when an answer depends on a qualifier like "not covered", chunk so that the qualifier stays with its claim.

Stale and conflicting sources

The returns policy changed from 30 to 45 days. Both versions are in the index, both match the question "How many days do I have to return an item?", and in the baseline run the answer followed the old one. The retrieval worked. The generation worked. The corpus was wrong for the task, because it held two truths and said nothing about which was current.

Staleness comes from several places. Documents change and the index is not updated (a pipeline lag, or a deletion that never propagates: change data capture is the mechanism for fixing it). Old versions are kept for good reasons, such as audit, and are left searchable. Different sources legitimately disagree (a regional policy versus a global one; a draft versus a published rule). And the model's own training data is an older source still: if the context is silent, models may answer from memory, in confident detail, about a world that has moved on. This is the same problem as cache invalidation, with the extra difficulty that the stale item is paraphrased, not copied.

The fix is metadata applied at retrieval time. Every chunk carries its document family, effective date, version, status and what superseded it, populated at ingestion; retrieval filters to what is in force for the question's date. Below is the rule, including the case of auditing a past decision, where "current" is the wrong question and "what was in force on that date" is the right one.

from datetime import date

# Each chunk carries versioning metadata from ingestion.
CHUNKS = {
  "ret-v3": {"text": "Items may be returned within 30 days of delivery.",
             "effective": date(2024, 1, 1), "superseded_by": "ret-v4"},
  "ret-v4": {"text": "Items may be returned within 45 days of delivery.",
             "effective": date(2025, 6, 1), "superseded_by": None},
  "ret-dr": {"text": "DRAFT: returns window to become 60 days.",
             "effective": date(2027, 1, 1), "superseded_by": None, "status": "draft"},
}

def in_force(cid, on):
    c = CHUNKS[cid]
    if c.get("status") == "draft" or c["effective"] > on:
        return False
    successor = c["superseded_by"]
    return successor is None or CHUNKS[successor]["effective"] > on

for on in [date(2025, 1, 1), date(2026, 10, 14)]:
    print(f"in force on {on}:", [c for c in CHUNKS if in_force(c, on)])
in force on 2025-01-01: ['ret-v3']
in force on 2026-10-14: ['ret-v4']

When two sources of equal standing genuinely disagree, filtering cannot decide for you. The honest behaviour is to say so and cite both. Xie et al. (2023, "Adaptive Chameleon or Stubborn Sloth"; ICLR 2024) found that language models can be quite receptive to external evidence that conflicts with what they learned in training, if the evidence is coherent and convincing, but show a strong confirmation bias when the context mixes information consistent with their memory and information that is not: they tend to favour the consistent part. A prompt that says "if sources conflict, report the conflict and the dates of each" gives the model a legitimate way out; one that says nothing leaves the choice to a bias.

Confident synthesis

The last class is the hardest to see because the answer reads well. In the run, "Do you ship to Brazil?" retrieved the passage "We ship to 24 countries. Customs duties are paid by the recipient." and the stand-in returned "We ship to 24 countries." That sentence is true, relevant-looking and unhelpful: it neither says yes nor no for Brazil, and a hurried reader could take it as "yes". "Is there a student discount?" retrieved nothing relevant and the system answered with a sentence about standard delivery. A real model is more fluent about it, which makes it more dangerous.

Confident synthesis is producing a fluent, assured answer that the evidence does not support. It has several causes: no evidence exists and the model fills the gap from memory; weak evidence exists and the model stretches it; evidence from two passages is combined into a claim neither makes ("members earn points" plus "gift cards cannot be exchanged" becomes "points can't buy gift cards"); or the question was multi-hop and the model skipped a step. Barnett et al.'s incorrect-specificity and incomplete-answer points live here too.

The defences are the machinery of the previous chapter. Give the model explicit permission to say the sources do not contain the answer. Abstain before generation when retrieval scores are weak. Verify claims against the cited spans afterwards. Measure abstention with a risk-coverage curve rather than hoping. Even a crude version helps: in the last row of the table, abstaining when the answer sentence shares no words with the question moved "student discount" from a wrong answer to a correct refusal and lifted the score from 7 to 8 out of 12. It did not fix "Brazil", whose answer overlapped the question on "ship", which tells you precisely what a word-overlap signal cannot see. Closing that gap needs a check that asks whether the passage answers the question, such as the entailment checks of chapter 16.

Systems connection

Most of this chapter is operations practice in new clothes. Per-request traces that record each stage's output are what distributed tracing gives a microservice system, and for model calls specifically A6 chapter 17 covers what to record. Stale data is a caching and replication-lag problem. Treating a failure rate as a per-stage budget is error-budget thinking from reliability engineering. And the reason failures must be attributed to a stage, not just counted, is the same reason you do not debug a latency problem by looking at the average.

    Measuring each stage, and a fix table

    Once you can attribute failures, give each stage its own metric so a change shows up where it should. This is the idea behind RAGAS (Es et al., 2023, "Ragas: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation"), which proposed reference-free metrics along three dimensions: whether the retriever finds relevant, focused context; whether the model uses that context faithfully; and the quality of the answer itself. The table pairs each stage with a question, a metric and what you need to label.

    A technique worth knowing is the gold-context run: feed the generator only the gold passage and see how often it answers correctly. That number is the ceiling for the generation stage. If it is 95 per cent and your full pipeline scores 60 per cent, the missing 35 points are lost upstream, in retrieval and assembly. If it is 70 per cent, even perfect retrieval would leave you at 70. This one experiment is the quickest way to stop arguing about where the problem is.

    Old and new versions both in the prompt; answer follows the old oneStale or conflicting sourceEffective-date metadata, supersession, report conflicts
    Gold alone in the prompt; fact missing from the answerIgnored or mis-extracted contextFewer passages, edge ordering, quote first, parent context (16)
    No gold passage exists; a fluent answer anywayConfident synthesisAllow refusal, abstain on weak scores, verify claims (16)

    Logging so that you can diagnose later

    None of this works if the trace was not kept. The cheapest discipline is to log one structured record per request with enough to replay the pipeline's decisions. A reasonable minimum, shown as a listing (not run), is below. It contains user text, so apply the usual privacy rules: redact, restrict access, and set a retention period.

    {
  "request_id": "r-8841", "ts": "2026-10-14T09:12:03Z", "user_scope": "tenant-17",
  "question": "How many days do I have to return an item?",
  "query_after_rewrite": "return window days delivery",
  "retrieval": [{"chunk": "ret-v3", "rank": 1, "score": 0.0331, "doc_version": 3},
                {"chunk": "ret-v4", "rank": 2, "score": 0.0325, "doc_version": 4}],
  "filters_applied": ["tenant=17", "status!=draft"],
  "prompt_chunks": ["ret-v3", "ret-v4"], "prompt_tokens": 412,
  "answer": "Items may be returned within 30 days of delivery.",
  "citations": [{"chunk": "ret-v3", "gate": "SUPPORTED"}],
  "abstained": false, "model": "model-name-and-version", "latency_ms": 1180
}

    With records like this you can sample failures weekly, label them with the stage taxonomy, and watch the counts move. Add each labelled failure to a regression set so that a fix that later breaks resurfaces in a test, not a complaint. The loop from production traces to labelled failures to regression suites is developed fully in A6 chapter 18.

    Key takeaways

    • A RAG answer is right only if every stage worked, so several decent stages multiply into a mediocre system. Aggregate scores hide which one failed.
    • Attribute each failed request to the first stage that went wrong: evidence missing, not retrieved, not in the prompt, conflicting source, ignored context, or confident synthesis.
    • You need a gold passage for each test question and a trace of each request. The gold-context run gives the ceiling for the generator.
    • Fixes move failures between stages: more context fixes a miss and exposes a conflict. Re-measure the whole breakdown after every change.
    • Context is a budget. Related but non-answering passages can hurt, position can matter, and fewer, better passages are often the cure.
    • Treat freshness as metadata: effective dates, versions, supersession, and an as-of date for audits.
    • Confident synthesis is solved by permission to refuse, abstention on weak evidence and claim verification, not by hoping the model knows when it does not know.

    Check your understanding

    A user asks about a refund deadline. The trace shows the correct policy chunk at rank 1 and in the prompt, nothing conflicting, and an answer that quotes an unrelated delivery time. Where is the first failure?

    1. Retrieval, because the delivery chunk must have been ranked too high.
    2. The corpus, because it must be missing the refund policy.
    3. Generation: the right evidence was present and alone, and the model did not use it.
    4. Staleness, because the answer is wrong.
    The first four checks pass: evidence exists, was retrieved, reached the prompt and had no competitor. So the first failing stage is the model ignoring or mis-extracting. Option 1 is tempting but the gold chunk was at rank 1; a delivery chunk elsewhere in the prompt is a distractor, not the root cause. Wrong does not imply stale.

    You raise k from 3 to 5 and recall@k improves, but the end-to-end score is unchanged and the stale-source failures have doubled. What is the likeliest explanation?

    1. The extra passages included older versions of documents, so a miss became a conflict.
    2. Recall@k is not related to answer quality.
    3. The generator got worse for no reason.
    4. The test set must have changed.
    Fixes shift failures between stages. A bigger k brought the right passage in and also brought in the superseded version, which the generator then followed. Recall@k is an upper bound on the answer rate but not the answer rate. Options 3 and 4 invent causes the evidence does not suggest.

    The generator, given only the gold passage, answers correctly 70 per cent of the time. Your full pipeline answers 55 per cent correctly. What does this tell you?

    1. All 45 points of loss are due to retrieval.
    2. The generator is fine; work on retrieval only.
    3. The corpus must be stale.
    4. The generation stage already caps the system at about 70, and upstream stages lose about 15 more; both need work.
    The gold-context run is the ceiling for the generation stage. 30 points are lost even with perfect context, and roughly 15 more between the ceiling and the full pipeline. Option 1 ignores the 30; option 2 ignores the 15; option 3 is not indicated by these numbers.

    "Do you ship to Brazil?" retrieves a passage saying "We ship to 24 countries" and the system answers with that sentence. Which class and which repair fit best?

    1. Missed retrieval; switch to a larger embedding model.
    2. Confident synthesis; verify that the passage actually answers the question and allow the system to say the sources do not cover it.
    3. Stale source; add effective dates.
    4. Wrong format; ask for yes or no.
    The retriever returned a related passage, which is not what failed. The answer sounds right but does not address the question, and no passage in the corpus does. The remedy is an answer-support check and explicit permission to refuse. A better embedder (1) would only retrieve similar passages, not create the missing one.

    You must show what a policy said on 1 March 2025 for an audit, but the index also holds today's version. Which retrieval rule is right?

    1. Always retrieve the newest version.
    2. Retrieve both and let the model choose.
    3. Filter to the chunks in force on that date: started on or before it, and not yet superseded by then.
    4. Delete old versions from the index.
    Audit questions ask what was in force then, not what is current. The newest-only rule returns the wrong answer for them. Letting the model choose hands the decision to a confirmation bias. Deleting old versions makes this and every future audit impossible.

    Cuconasu et al. found that adding unrelated random passages could raise accuracy in their setting while highly ranked related passages without the answer lowered it. What should you conclude for your system?

    1. Measure on your own questions how related non-answering passages affect accuracy; prefer fewer, better passages, and do not copy the noise trick without testing.
    2. Add random passages to every prompt.
    3. Never retrieve related passages.
    4. Retrieval quality does not matter, since random passages can help.
    The result is a warning that near-miss distractors can be harmful and that context effects are not monotonic, in one setting. It is not a recipe. Options 2 and 4 over-generalise a surprising finding; option 3 would remove the passages that carry the answer.
