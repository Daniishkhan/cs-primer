Dense Retrieval

A dense retriever turns every passage and every query into a vector, so that "get my money back" can land beside "refund policy" although the two share no word. This chapter builds the bi-encoder from first principles: why it separates the work into an offline and an online half, how contrastive training teaches it what "close" should mean, why the choice of negatives matters more than almost anything else, and how to fine-tune one for your own corpus.

Why this matters

BM25 (chapter 2) cannot match a query to a passage that uses different words, and query rewriting (chapter 3) closes only part of the gap. Embedding-based retrieval is the standard answer, and it is the first stage of nearly every RAG pipeline. It is also easy to misuse: people treat an embedding model as a black box that returns "similar" text, then cannot explain why it misses an invoice number or confuses two nearly identical policies. Knowing how the model was trained tells you what it will and will not do, which negatives to mine when you fine-tune it, and why hybrid search (chapter 10) and reranking (chapter 8) exist. It uses the vectors and cosine similarity from A0 chapter 5 and the measurement habits from chapter 4 and chapter 5.

From matching words to comparing vectors

A dense retriever represents text as a list of a few hundred to a few thousand real numbers, an embedding, produced by a neural network. "Dense" contrasts with the sparse term vectors of an inverted index, where almost every entry is zero. Retrieval then has a simple form: embed the query, find the passages whose vectors are closest to it, and return those. Closeness is measured by a similarity function, usually the dot product or its length-normalised form, cosine similarity.

The attraction is that the network can be trained so that meaning, not spelling, decides closeness. Two passages about getting money returned to a customer can sit near each other, and near a question phrased in everyday words, while a passage about holiday calendars sits far away. The cost is that the notion of "close" is learned from data, so it inherits the data's strengths and blind spots, and it is opaque: unlike BM25, you cannot print a formula that explains a score.

The bi-encoder

The architecture used for first-stage dense retrieval is the bi-encoder (also called a dual encoder or two-tower model). One neural network, the query encoder, maps a query to a vector; a second network, the passage encoder, maps a passage to a vector; the score is the dot product of the two. The two networks can share weights or be separate. Karpukhin et al. (2020, "Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering", usually called DPR) used two independent BERT-base encoders, took the output at the [CLS] position as the embedding (768 numbers) and scored with the dot product.

Why this split matters is arithmetic. Reimers and Gurevych (2019, "Sentence-BERT: Sentence Embeddings using Siamese BERT-Networks") reported that finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences took about 65 hours when every pair had to be fed through BERT together, and about 5 seconds once each sentence was encoded once and the vectors compared. With a bi-encoder the expensive neural work grows with the number of passages (done once, offline) and per query it is a single forward pass through a small encoder plus a nearest-neighbour search (chapter 11). Nothing in the passage vector can depend on the query, which is the price: the model must compress each passage into one point without knowing what will be asked of it.

Details that change results

  • Pooling. A transformer outputs one vector per token. To get one vector per text you pool them: take the first token's vector ([CLS]), average all token vectors (mean pooling), or, for decoder-based embedders, use the last token. Each model was trained with a particular choice, and using another gives worse vectors. Follow the model card.
  • Normalisation, dot product and cosine. If vectors are scaled to length 1, the dot product equals cosine similarity and the ranking is the same. Without normalisation, the dot product also rewards longer vectors. DPR found the inner product and L2 distance comparable to each other and better than cosine in its experiments; most modern embedding models are trained with normalised vectors and cosine, and ship that way. Use what the model was trained with. The listing below shows the equivalence on the stand-in embedder.
  • Query and passage prefixes. Some model families (E5, Wang et al., 2022, for example) expect an instruction or prefix such as "query: " and "passage: " added to the text, because they were trained with them. Leaving them off silently degrades results.
  • Maximum input length. Encoders read a fixed number of tokens (commonly 512 for older models, more for newer ones) and truncate the rest. A long passage is represented only by its beginning, which interacts with chunking (chapter 14).
  • Dimension and precision. The memory of the index is passages × dimensions × bytes per number, as computed in the last listing of this chapter.

A stand-in embedder, and what it can and cannot do

To keep the experiments here runnable without downloading a model, the chapter uses small stand-ins that are clearly not trained semantic embedders. The first is a hashed character n-gram embedder: it cuts each word into overlapping three-letter pieces and hashes them into a 512-number vector. It "knows" only which letter sequences two texts share.

import zlib
import numpy as np

DIM = 512
def embed(text, n=3):
    """STAND-IN embedder: hashed character n-grams. Not a trained model; it only
    knows which letter sequences two strings share."""
    v = np.zeros(DIM)
    for word in text.lower().split():
        w = f"<{word}>"
        for i in range(len(w) - n + 1):
            h = zlib.crc32(w[i:i + n].encode())
            v[h % DIM] += 1 if (h >> 9) & 1 else -1      # signed hashing
    norm = np.linalg.norm(v)
    return v / norm if norm else v

Spelling variants of "refund" score 0.55 to 0.77, even the typo "refnd", because they share letter sequences. "reimbursement" scores 0.11 and "money back" exactly 0.00, although a person would call them synonyms. This is the gap a trained model must close, and an n-gram stand-in cannot: it is robust to typos, which BM25 is not, and blind to paraphrase. The last two lines confirm the normalisation point: for unit-length vectors the dot product and cosine are the same number (0.7100), and scaling the vectors changes the dot product (10.65) but not the cosine.

Contrastive training

How do you train two networks so that relevant pairs end up close? You need examples of what should be close, and a loss that rewards closeness for those and distance for everything else. Data are pairs: a query and a passage that answers it, from labelled question-answer data, click logs, or synthetic queries (below). Training then optimises a contrastive loss: pull the positive pair together and push the query away from negatives, passages that should not match.

The loss used by DPR and most successors is a softmax over candidates. For a query q with positive passage p+ and a set of candidates including negatives p1, …, pn, and similarity s(q, p) (a dot product or cosine), the loss is

Plug in numbers. One query, its positive and three negatives, with made-up cosine similarities. The columns are the softmax shares, then the loss.

The idea

  1. sims: positive 0.60, negatives 0.45, 0.20, 0.05; tau in 1, 0.1, 0.02
  2. scaled ← sim / tau; weight ← exp(scaled)
  3. share ← weight ÷ sum of all weights; loss ← −ln(share of positive)
  4. compare with chance: four equal candidates → ln 4
"""InfoNCE for one query by hand: one positive, three negatives, and a few
temperatures. The similarities are made up (cosines, so in -1 to 1)."""
import math

# ── 1. One query, its positive passage, three negatives ──────────────────
SIM = {"positive": 0.60, "neg 1": 0.45, "neg 2": 0.20, "neg 3": 0.05}
TAUS = [1.0, 0.1, 0.02]            # temperatures: large is soft, small sharp


def info_nce(sims, tau):
    """Return (probabilities, loss). Loss = -ln of the positive's share."""
    scaled = [s / tau for s in sims]                   # s(q, p) / tau
    exps = [math.exp(x) for x in scaled]               # exp(...)
    probs = [e / sum(exps) for e in exps]              # softmax over candidates
    return probs, -math.log(probs[0])                  # -ln p(positive)


# ── 2. Same similarities, three temperatures ─────────────────────────────
names = list(SIM)
print("tau   " + "  ".join(f"{n:>8s}" for n in names) + "      loss")
for tau in TAUS:
    probs, loss = info_nce(list(SIM.values()), tau)
    print(f"{tau:<5} " + "  ".join(f"{p:8.3f}" for p in probs)
          + f"  {loss:8.3f}")

# ── 3. The baseline: no signal at all ────────────────────────────────────
print(f"chance, 4 equal candidates: loss = ln 4 = {math.log(4):.3f}")

This is cross-entropy on a classification problem whose classes are the candidate passages and whose correct answer is the positive. It is usually called InfoNCE (after the contrastive predictive coding paper of van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, 2018; DPR calls it the negative log-likelihood of the positive passage). The temperature τ is a positive constant that scales the similarities before the softmax. A small τ (for cosine similarities, values around 0.01 to 0.1 are typical) makes the softmax sharp: a negative that scores slightly below the positive still takes a lot of probability and a lot of gradient. A large τ makes the softmax nearly uniform and the signal weak.

In-batch negatives: B² pairs from B

Where do the negatives come from? One option is to sample extra passages for every query, which costs extra encoder passes. DPR's cheaper trick is in-batch negatives: take a batch of B (query, positive) pairs, encode all queries and all positives once, and compute the full B × B similarity matrix. Row i is query i; the diagonal entry is its positive; the other B − 1 entries in the row are positives of other queries, which serve as negatives for free (Karpukhin et al., 2020, section 3.2). That trains on B² pairs from 2B encoder passes, and, as the paper reports, accuracy keeps improving as the batch gets larger.

Step through

One contrastive batch, with a hard negative

  1. The similarity matrix. Three queries against three in-batch positives and one hard negative h that was mined for q1.
  2. Divide by the temperature. With tau = 0.1 each similarity is multiplied by 10.
  3. Softmax along each row. Each query now has a probability for every candidate, summing to 1.
  4. Take the loss. Minus the log of the probability on the diagonal. Rows that already rank their positive well have small loss.
  5. The hard negative speaks. It looks relevant to q1, so it keeps 0.325 of that row's probability and dominates its loss.

The same numbers are in the next listing, with the effect of the temperature. At τ = 1 the probabilities are close to uniform (about 0.31 to 0.33 for a four-way choice among near-equal candidates) and the loss is large and uninformative; at τ = 0.1 the model is confident on rows 2 and 3 and unsure on row 1; at τ = 0.02 even row 1 is almost solved, so the loss is near zero and there is little left to learn from this batch.

Go deeper optional: the gradient of InfoNCE

Let pj be the softmax probability of candidate j, with candidate 0 the positive c0. Differentiating the loss with respect to the query vector gives

∂L/∂q = (1/τ) ( ∑j pj cj − c0 )

and with respect to a candidate, ∂L/∂cj = (1/τ) (pj − [j = 0]) q. Read these as forces. The query is pulled towards its positive with force 1/τ and pushed away from the probability-weighted average of all the candidates. A negative is pushed away from the query with a force proportional to its own probability pj: a negative that already has probability 0.0001 contributes essentially nothing, and one with probability 0.3 contributes a great deal. That is the whole argument for hard negatives, and it is what the widget below lets you see. (The chapter's NumPy code implements exactly these formulas, with an extra step for the length normalisation, and checks them against numerical differentiation.)

Analogy: training a sorter by contrast

Imagine teaching a new librarian to shelve books by topic. You hold up a question, point to the book that answers it, and then show a pile of other books and say "not these". If the pile is random, the librarian learns quickly that cookbooks do not belong beside tax guides, then stops improving. Progress only continues when you show the near misses: the tax guide for the wrong country, the previous edition of the same manual.

Where it breaks down: a librarian can ask you about an ambiguous case. A model sees only the labels it is given, so if a "near miss" is in fact also a correct answer that nobody labelled, the model is told to push apart two things that belong together, and it cannot ask.

Choosing negatives

The negatives are as important as the positives, because the model learns to separate whatever you show it. There are three families.

Random and in-batch

Passages of other queries. Cheap, plentiful, and mostly easy: a question about refunds against a passage on holidays. They teach the coarse structure of the space and quickly stop contributing gradient.

Hard, from BM25

Passages that score highly lexically but are not relevant. DPR added one per question; they teach that sharing words is not the same as answering.

Hard, from the model itself

The top-ranked wrong passages under the current encoder (ANCE: Xiong et al., 2020, refreshed during training). They target exactly what the model currently confuses.

DPR's own ablation on Natural Questions (Table 3 of the paper, development set) shows the scale of the effect. With a batch of 32 using in-batch gold negatives, top-20 accuracy was 70.8%. Adding one BM25 hard negative per question lifted it to 77.3%, and top-5 accuracy rose from 52.1% to 65.0%. With a batch of 128 the same change moved top-20 from 73.0% to 78.0%. Larger batches helped a little; the right kind of negative helped more. The final DPR model used in-batch negatives plus one BM25 negative per question with batch size 128, and reached 78.4% top-20 accuracy on the NQ test set against 59.1% for BM25 (both from the paper's results).

The false-negative problem

A "hard negative" is mined by taking a high-scoring passage that is not labelled relevant. But labels are incomplete (chapter 5 on unjudged documents). The highest-scoring unlabelled passage is often a correct answer that nobody marked, a false negative. Training the model to push it away from the query damages exactly the behaviour you want. Qu et al. (2021, "RocketQA") treat this as a central issue and denoise their hard negatives with a stronger model before using them, alongside cross-batch negatives and data augmentation. Practical defences:

  • skip the very top ranks when mining (take negatives from, say, ranks 10 to 50, not 1 to 9), because those are the likeliest unlabelled positives;
  • filter candidates with a cross-encoder (chapter 8) and discard any it scores as relevant;
  • deduplicate: near-copies of the positive are not negatives;
  • exclude passages from the same document or section as the positive, unless your task really distinguishes them.

Training a tiny bi-encoder yourself

The following experiments train a real, if tiny, bi-encoder in NumPy, so that you can watch the effects above in numbers. The model is two linear towers: a bag-of-words count vector for the text is multiplied by a learned matrix (one for queries, one for passages) and normalised to unit length. It is a stand-in for a transformer encoder; the loss, the in-batch negatives and the hard-negative mining are exactly the real ones. Because it is so small, the model learns vocabulary relationships directly, which is the same thing a trained encoder learns at much larger scale and with context.

The data are a synthetic support-desk domain. There are 60 families of topics with 4 sibling topics each (240 topics). Passages use "formal" words and queries mostly use "casual" words for the same family, so queries and passages overlap little. Sibling topics share their family's words and differ by one or two cue words, so telling siblings apart is the hard part. The words are abstract tokens (f12_casual2), so this is an illustration of mechanisms, not a claim about any real corpus. First the model, with its loss and hand-derived gradients:

import numpy as np

def normalise(z):
    n = np.linalg.norm(z, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-12
    return z / n, n

def normalise_back(u, n, g):
    """Gradient through u = z / ||z||: (g - u (u . g)) / ||z||."""
    return (g - u * np.sum(u * g, axis=1, keepdims=True)) / n

class BiEncoder:
    """Two linear towers over bag-of-words counts: query -> unit vector, passage -> unit vector."""
    def __init__(self, vocab_size, dim, seed):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.Wq = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (vocab_size, dim))
        self.Wd = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (vocab_size, dim))
        self.m = [np.zeros_like(self.Wq), np.zeros_like(self.Wd)]
        self.v = [np.zeros_like(self.Wq), np.zeros_like(self.Wd)]
        self.t = 0
    def embed_q(self, X): return normalise(X @ self.Wq)[0]
    def embed_d(self, X): return normalise(X @ self.Wd)[0]

    def loss_and_grads(self, Xq, Xc, tau):
        """InfoNCE: query i must pick candidate i out of ALL candidates in Xc
        (its own positive, the other queries' positives, and any extra hard negatives)."""
        q, nq = normalise(Xq @ self.Wq)
        c, nc = normalise(Xc @ self.Wd)
        logits = q @ c.T / tau
        logits -= logits.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        p = np.exp(logits); p /= p.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        B = len(Xq)
        loss = -np.log(p[np.arange(B), np.arange(B)]).mean()
        dl = p.copy(); dl[np.arange(B), np.arange(B)] -= 1; dl /= B       # d loss / d logits
        dq = dl @ c / tau
        dc = dl.T @ q / tau
        gWq = Xq.T @ normalise_back(q, nq, dq)
        gWd = Xc.T @ normalise_back(c, nc, dc)
        return loss, gWq, gWd

    def step(self, grads, lr):
        self.t += 1
        for k, (W, g) in enumerate(zip((self.Wq, self.Wd), grads)):
            self.m[k] = 0.9 * self.m[k] + 0.1 * g
            self.v[k] = 0.999 * self.v[k] + 0.001 * g * g
            mh = self.m[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** self.t); vh = self.v[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** self.t)
            W -= lr * mh / (np.sqrt(vh) + 1e-8)

The largest discrepancy is under 10−9, so the formulas are right. Next the training loop. It samples batches of labelled (query, passage) pairs and, depending on the mode, adds 16 extra negative passages to each batch: none (in-batch only), 16 random ones, 16 mined ones (for each query, the highest-scoring passage from any other topic, under the model as it currently stands), or 16 mined without excluding same-topic passages (the false-negative setting: only the exact positive is excluded). Mining starts after 200 steps, when the model has learned something.

import numpy as np
from toy import make, V, mrr_and_confusion, SIBLINGS
from bi_encoder import BiEncoder

DIM, BATCH = 32, 16
tr_docs, tr_dtopic, tr_q, tr_qtopic = make(100, n_query=12)      # training data
te_docs, te_dtopic, te_q, te_qtopic = make(200, n_query=10)      # held-out: new draws of the same topics

def evaluate(model):
    scores = model.embed_q(te_q) @ model.embed_d(te_docs).T
    return mrr_and_confusion(scores, te_qtopic, te_dtopic)

def train(n_pairs=2880, steps=1500, tau=0.05, negatives="in-batch", seed=0, lr=0.03, warmup=200):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    model = BiEncoder(V, DIM, seed); loss = float("nan")
    pool = rng.permutation(len(tr_q))[:n_pairs]                  # which labelled queries we are allowed to use
    for step in range(steps):
        idx = rng.choice(pool, BATCH, replace=False)
        pos = np.array([rng.choice(np.flatnonzero(tr_dtopic == tr_qtopic[i])) for i in idx])
        Xq, Xc = tr_q[idx], tr_docs[pos]
        if negatives == "random" and step >= warmup:
            Xc = np.vstack([Xc, tr_docs[rng.choice(len(tr_docs), BATCH)]])   # same number of extra negatives, but random
        elif negatives not in ("in-batch", "random") and step >= warmup:
            s = model.embed_q(Xq) @ model.embed_d(tr_docs).T
            hard = []
            for r, i in enumerate(idx):
                bad = tr_dtopic == tr_qtopic[i] if negatives == "hard" else (np.arange(len(tr_docs)) == pos[r])
                s[r, bad] = -np.inf                               # never pick the true topic's passages (or, unfiltered, only the positive itself)
                hard.append(int(np.argmax(s[r])))
            Xc = np.vstack([Xc, tr_docs[hard]])
        loss, gq, gd = model.loss_and_grads(Xq, Xc, tau)
        model.step((gq, gd), lr)
    return model, loss

def bow(ids):
    x = np.zeros(V, dtype=np.float64)
    for i in ids: x[i] += 1
    return x

def passage(rng, f, s):
    ids = list(rng.choice(FORMAL[f], 2, replace=False)) + SIB_DOC[f][s]
    if rng.random() < 0.35: ids.append(rng.choice(CASUAL[f]))          # a little casual wording in the passage
    ids += list(rng.choice(FILL, 6))
    return bow(ids)

def query(rng, f, s):
    ids = list(rng.choice(CASUAL[f], 2, replace=False))
    if rng.random() < 0.2: ids.append(rng.choice(FORMAL[f]))
    if rng.random() < 0.7: ids.append(rng.choice(SIB_Q[f][s]))       # the sibling-specific cue is often present
    ids += list(rng.choice(FILL, 2))
    return bow(ids)

def make(seed, n_pass=6, n_query=10, n_train=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    docs, dtopic = [], []
    for t, (f, s) in enumerate(TOPICS):
        for _ in range(n_pass):
            docs.append(passage(rng, f, s)); dtopic.append(t)
    qs, qtopic = [], []
    for t, (f, s) in enumerate(TOPICS):
        for _ in range(max(n_query, n_train)):
            qs.append(query(rng, f, s)); qtopic.append(t)
    return np.array(docs), np.array(dtopic), np.array(qs), np.array(qtopic)

def mrr_and_confusion(scores, qtopic, dtopic):
    """MRR over queries (a passage of the right topic is relevant) and the share of
    queries whose top result is a passage of a sibling topic (same family, wrong topic)."""
    mrr, sib = 0.0, 0
    for i in range(len(scores)):
        order = np.argsort(-scores[i], kind="stable")
        rel = dtopic[order] == qtopic[i]
        mrr += 1.0 / (int(np.argmax(rel)) + 1)
        top = dtopic[order[0]]
        sib += (top != qtopic[i]) and (top // SIBLINGS == qtopic[i] // SIBLINGS)
    return mrr / len(scores), sib / len(scores)

Reading the table honestly:

  • Training is the big step. The untrained model scores 0.024 and the tf-idf baseline 0.433. Contrastive training takes the toy bi-encoder to 0.674 with nothing but in-batch negatives, because it learns that casual words and formal words of one family belong together. Lexical matching cannot do that.
  • More negatives help, and harder negatives help more. Sixteen random extras raise MRR from 0.674 to 0.722; sixteen mined ones raise it to 0.763, and the share of top-1 results that are siblings falls from 41.8% to 27.1%. The control matters: a comparison with no extras would have credited "hardness" with the whole gain, when about half came from simply having more candidates.
  • The false-negative variant did not hurt here, and I expected it to. In this toy a topic has six interchangeable passages, so a "negative" that is another passage of the same topic is nearly indistinguishable from the positive; pushing it away mostly teaches the model to tell individual passages apart, which does not hurt test performance on new passages. Real corpora behave differently: an unlabelled relevant passage is one you want close to the query. Treat this result as a limit of the toy, not as evidence that false negatives are harmless; the RocketQA authors report the opposite on real data.

The last block of the output asks how many labelled pairs are needed. The answer for this toy depends on the number of topics it has to cover: 60 pairs (about a quarter of a pair per topic) give MRR 0.122, 240 pairs 0.333, 960 pairs 0.582 and 2,880 pairs 0.674. The shape is the useful part: gains continue but slow down, and most of the value arrives early. DPR reports a similar shape on real data: a retriever trained on only 1,000 questions already outperformed BM25 on the Natural Questions development set, and adding more examples (up to 59,000) improved accuracy consistently. How many pairs you need depends on how far your domain is from the base model's training data, which you find out by measuring (chapter 5).

Common misconception

"Embeddings understand meaning, so similarity is relevance." Embedding similarity is the closeness the training pairs taught the model. It is a good proxy for relevance on data like the training data, and a poor one elsewhere (BEIR found DPR the worst generaliser in its comparison, with an average nDCG@10 of 0.237 against BM25's 0.423; see chapter 6). Three corollaries. First, a cosine of 0.82 means nothing on its own: scores are not comparable across models, queries or corpora, so a fixed threshold ("keep results above 0.8") is fragile. Second, embeddings blur what must stay sharp: part numbers, names, amounts and negation ("does not include VAT") often land near their opposites. Third, "bigger dimension is better" is false: dimension buys capacity at the price of memory and speed, and models differ more in training than in size.

Fine-tuning an embedder for your corpus

An off-the-shelf embedding model has been trained on general text. If your documents use specialist vocabulary or your queries look unlike the training queries, fine-tuning on your own pairs can help. The FinMTEB authors (chapter 6) report that domain-adapted models consistently beat general ones on finance tasks, and BEIR's GenQ baseline improved a dense model on specialised collections by fine-tuning it on synthetic queries. Fine-tuning also costs engineering effort and creates a model you must maintain. Try the cheaper levers first: better chunking (chapter 14), hybrid search with BM25 (chapter 10), a reranker (chapter 8) and query rewriting. Fine-tune when measurement on your gold set shows the retriever itself, not the surrounding system, is the bottleneck.

  1. Measure the baseline on the gold set of chapter 5, including slices by query type. A fine-tune should beat both the off-the-shelf model and BM25.
  2. Collect pairs. Sources in order of quality: curated question and answer pairs from your domain experts; click or "this answered my question" feedback (biased towards what was shown, see chapter 4's note on unjudged documents); FAQ entries and their answers; and synthetic queries written by a language model from your passages (the GenQ approach in BEIR). For synthetic queries, apply the overlap check from chapter 5: queries that copy the passage's words teach the model little.
  3. Split by query before anything else. Hold out the gold set's test queries, and also any passage they depend on, from training. Training on test queries makes every later number meaningless (A1 chapter 14 on leakage).
  4. Mine negatives with the baseline model and BM25, drop the top few ranks and cross-encoder-filtered positives (above), and keep one to a few per query.
  5. Train gently. A small learning rate and one to a few epochs, with the largest batch your memory allows, because in-batch negatives improve with batch size. Large steps on small data cause the model to forget general behaviour.
  6. Evaluate on held-out queries, and on general ones. Report gains with an interval, and run a handful of queries unrelated to your domain to catch a model that improved on yours and broke elsewhere.
  7. Re-embed everything. Vectors from the old and new model are not comparable, so the entire corpus must be re-encoded and queries must use the new encoder. Version the index with the model identifier so that a query encoder and an index never mismatch.

With the sentence-transformers library, which implements these losses, the training core is short. This listing is illustrative and was not run here; the library's interface has changed between versions, so check its current documentation.

from datasets import Dataset
from sentence_transformers import (SentenceTransformer, SentenceTransformerTrainer,
                                   losses)

model = SentenceTransformer("your-base-embedding-model")

# one row per training example; the hard negative is optional
train = Dataset.from_dict({
    "anchor":   ["how do i dispute a card charge", "..."],        # the query
    "positive": ["To dispute a charge, contact billing within 30 days...", "..."],
    "negative": ["Payment methods accepted: credit card, bank transfer...", "..."],
})

# softmax over in-batch positives plus the supplied negatives (the loss of this chapter)
loss = losses.MultipleNegativesRankingLoss(model)

trainer = SentenceTransformerTrainer(model=model, train_dataset=train, loss=loss)
trainer.train()
model.save("my-domain-embedder")   # then re-embed the whole corpus with this model

Systems connection

A vector index is derived data whose meaning is fixed by the model that produced it. Changing the embedding model is therefore a migration, like changing a schema (Data Systems 3) or rebuilding a secondary index (Data Systems 7): build the new index beside the old one, evaluate, switch traffic, and keep the old index until you are sure. The memory arithmetic in the listing below is the capacity-planning side: 10 million 768-number float32 vectors need about 31 GB, before any index structure, which is why ANN indexes and compression exist (chapter 11). Embedding calls are also a network dependency with latency and failure modes (Networking 16): timeouts and fallbacks to BM25 belong in the design.

A million 768-number float32 passage vectors take 3.07 GB; halving the precision to float16 halves that, and 8-bit integers quarter it, with some loss of accuracy. A flat (exact) search compares the query against every vector, so its cost grows linearly: 0.77 billion multiply-adds per query at a million passages, 7.7 billion at ten million. Matryoshka representation learning (Kusupati et al., 2022) trains embeddings whose first dimensions form a usable smaller embedding, so the same model can be cut to fit a budget; some embedding model families support this today. Each such shortcut should be re-evaluated on your gold set, not assumed free.

Where dense retrieval fails

  • Exact strings. Identifiers, error codes, product names and amounts. The embedding preserves topic more than spelling, so "invoice 4471-B" can retrieve invoice 4471-C. BM25 is exact on these. This is the main reason production systems combine both.
  • Negation, numbers and qualifiers. "Not covered", "less than 30 days" and "more than 30 days" are close in embedding space.
  • Out-of-domain text. Specialist jargon and unseen formats; the toy's untrained model and BEIR's DPR results are two views of the same effect.
  • Long passages. A single vector for 500 words averages their topics, and text beyond the model's input limit is ignored.
  • Hubness and crowding. In high dimensions some vectors become nearest neighbours of many queries; generic passages can appear for unrelated queries. A reranker or a filter cleans this up.
  • Silent staleness. The index reflects the passages when they were embedded; an updated passage with an old vector is invisible to its new content.

State of play, October 2026

The recipe in this chapter (bi-encoder, InfoNCE with in-batch and mined negatives, fine-tuning on domain pairs) is stable. What changes quickly is which pretrained model to start from. The MMTEB paper (Enevoldsen et al., 2025) reported that large language-model-based embedders reached state-of-the-art results on some language subsets and task categories, while the best publicly available model overall at the time was multilingual-e5-large-instruct with 560 million parameters. Since then new releases appear every few months, from open-weight and hosted providers, with different input limits, prefixes, dimensions and licences. I could not verify a current ranking and give none: use the shortlist procedure of chapter 6, then decide on your gold set. Details such as maximum input length, dimension options and whether a model supports Matryoshka truncation are in each model's card and change with versions.

Key takeaways

  • A bi-encoder embeds queries and passages separately, so passage vectors are built offline and a query needs one forward pass plus a nearest-neighbour search. A cross-encoder reads the pair together: more accurate, too slow for the whole corpus.
  • Training uses a softmax contrastive loss (InfoNCE) over one positive and many negatives, scaled by a temperature. In-batch negatives turn B pairs into B² training comparisons for free.
  • A negative's gradient is proportional to its probability, so easy negatives teach little. Hard negatives, mined from BM25 or from the model itself, supply most of the useful signal (DPR: top-20 accuracy from 70.8% to 77.3% with one BM25 negative per question).
  • Unlabelled relevant passages are false negatives when mined as hard negatives; skip the top ranks, filter with a cross-encoder, deduplicate.
  • Fine-tuning needs pairs, a held-out split by query, gentle training and a full re-embed; compare with cheaper levers first.
  • Use the model as it was trained: pooling, normalisation, prefixes and input length matter. Similarity scores are not comparable across models, and thresholds are fragile.
  • Dense retrieval is weak on exact strings, negation and out-of-domain text, which is why it is usually combined with BM25 and a reranker.

Check your understanding

You train with in-batch negatives and a batch of 128 (query, positive) pairs. How many negative passages does each query see, and what does that cost?

  1. 1 negative, because only the diagonal is a pair
  2. 127 negatives, at no extra encoder cost, since the other positives in the batch are reused
  3. 128 negatives, each needing its own encoder pass
  4. 16,384 negatives, but only after a second pass
The 128 passages are encoded once; for query i, the other 127 passages are negatives, so the B × B matrix gives B² comparisons from 2B encoder passes. Option 4 counts total comparisons in the batch, not negatives per query.

You lower the temperature from 0.1 to 0.01 in a contrastive loss on cosine similarities. What is the most likely consequence?

  1. The loss becomes insensitive to hard negatives
  2. All negatives are weighted equally
  3. Training is guaranteed to converge faster
  4. The softmax becomes sharper: the highest-scoring negatives dominate the gradient, which helps with hard negatives but amplifies the damage from false negatives and can destabilise training
Dividing by a small τ stretches the logits, so the largest few dominate the softmax. A uniform weighting is what a large τ produces. Faster convergence is not guaranteed; very low temperatures often make training harder.

Hard negatives are mined as the top-5 passages from the current model that are not labelled relevant. After fine-tuning, recall on your gold set falls. What is the most plausible cause?

  1. Some of the mined "negatives" were actually relevant but unlabelled, so the model was taught to push them away from the query
  2. The temperature was too high
  3. The batch contained too many easy negatives
  4. The passage encoder was too small
Incomplete labels make the top-ranked non-positive a likely unlabelled positive; RocketQA denoises exactly this. A high temperature or easy negatives would slow learning, not usually reduce recall below baseline. Skipping the top ranks and filtering with a cross-encoder are the fixes.

A colleague sets a rule: "return only results with cosine similarity above 0.8". After swapping the embedding model, almost nothing is returned. Why?

  1. Cosine similarity is undefined for the new model
  2. The new model's index is corrupt
  3. Similarity values are not comparable across models: different models spread their scores over different ranges, so a fixed threshold means something different each time
  4. The new model has fewer dimensions
The scale of similarities depends on the training (temperature, data, normalisation). A threshold calibrated for one model has no meaning for another; if you need a cut-off, calibrate it on judged queries for the model in use, or rank by position instead.

You fine-tune your embedder and evaluate on the held-out gold queries: nDCG@10 improves. You deploy the new query encoder but leave the existing index. What happens?

  1. Nothing: vectors from both models are interchangeable
  2. Quality collapses: the query vectors live in a different space from the stored passage vectors, so the whole corpus must be re-embedded with the new model
  3. Only new documents are affected
  4. Latency doubles but quality is unchanged
Each model defines its own vector space. Mixing query vectors from one model with passage vectors from another compares unrelated coordinates. Version the index with the model identifier and rebuild it.

Users search for "invoice 4471-B" and the dense retriever returns invoices 4471-A and 4471-C first. Which design change addresses this best?

  1. Increase the embedding dimension
  2. Lower the temperature during fine-tuning
  3. Embed the identifier twice
  4. Add lexical retrieval (BM25) alongside the dense retriever and fuse the results, since exact strings are what term matching does well
Embeddings capture topic and approximate meaning; near-identical identifiers land close together. BM25 matches exact tokens. Hybrid search (chapter 10) combines both. A larger dimension or lower temperature does not give the model exact-string matching.
