Chunking & Contextual Retrieval

Documents are too long to embed whole and too long to paste whole, so they are cut into pieces, and the cuts decide what retrieval can find. This chapter compares the ways to cut, shows the damage a bad cut does, and explains the two repairs that matter most: giving each chunk the context it lost, and retrieving small pieces but returning big ones.

Why this matters

Chunking is the least glamorous decision in a RAG system and one of the most expensive to get wrong, because it is made at ingestion time and baked into every vector and every citation. A fact cut in half cannot be retrieved by any embedder. A chunk that says "Revenue grew 7.4%" with no hint of which division or quarter cannot be found by a question that names them. After this chapter you can choose a chunking strategy for a document type, see why a chunk needs the context of the document around it and add that context cheaply, use parent-child retrieval to separate what you match from what you show, and test the choices on your own questions instead of copying a default. It continues the pipeline of chapter 13; the contextual-retrieval technique is the subject of the course's first case study.

Why documents are cut

Three limits force the cut, and they pull in opposite directions.

  • The embedder has an input limit, and one vector has finite capacity. Many embedding models accept only a few hundred tokens, and silently drop the rest (below). Even models with long inputs produce one vector per input, so a 5,000-word chapter is squeezed into the same few hundred numbers as a sentence. The vector becomes an average of many topics, close to none of them.
  • The prompt has a budget. Chapter 13 priced the tokens: every retrieved token costs money and attention. Returning a whole 40-page document to answer a question about one clause is waste.
  • Evidence has to fit in a unit. The answer to a question lives somewhere in the text. If the cut falls through it, the sentence that names the subject and the sentence that gives the number end up in different chunks, and each half is a poor match for the question.

The first two limits favour small chunks; the third favours big ones. A chunk is the unit that is embedded, indexed, retrieved and cited, and the whole chapter is about choosing that unit well.

The standard ways to cut

Fixed-size windows

Split every n tokens (or words, or characters), regardless of content. It is trivial to implement, predictable in size, and works on any text, which is why it is the default in many libraries. Its flaw is that the cut ignores meaning: it falls mid-sentence, mid-table, and between a heading and its first paragraph. Overlap softens this: each window repeats the last few words of the previous one, so a fact near a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk. The price is storage and embedding cost, which grow by size / (size − overlap): a 200-token window with a 40-token overlap stores 1.25 times the text.

Recursive splitting

Try the largest natural separator first (blank lines between paragraphs), and fall back to smaller ones (line breaks, sentence ends, spaces) only for pieces that are still too big. Chunks end at natural boundaries when possible, and the size limit is respected when not. This is a good generic default for prose.

Structural splitting

Use the document's own structure: one chunk per section, per numbered clause, per slide, per function, per FAQ entry, per table row group. The boundaries are meaningful because the author put them there, and each chunk can inherit its heading path as metadata. It requires parsing (chapter 18 explains why that is hard for PDFs) and the chunks are uneven: a section may be three words or three thousand, so structural splitting normally has a maximum size and falls back to recursive splitting inside long sections.

Sentence-window and semantic splitting

Sentence-window methods embed single sentences but remember their neighbours, which is the parent-child idea below. Semantic chunking embeds each sentence and cuts where the similarity between neighbours drops, on the theory that a topic change marks a natural boundary. It adapts chunk size to content, at the cost of an embedding pass at ingestion and a threshold to tune.

Propositions and model-written chunks

Chen et al., "Dense X Retrieval: What Retrieval Granularity Should We Use?" (2023), proposed splitting text into propositions: short, self-contained statements with pronouns replaced by the things they refer to, written by a model. They reported better dense retrieval and downstream question answering than passage or sentence units on the benchmarks they tried. It is a heavier pipeline, and a model rewriting the source can introduce errors, so citations must still point at the original text.

Measuring a chunker

Opinions about chunk size are abundant and often contradictory, because the best size depends on the documents, the questions and the embedder. So measure. The experiment below uses four constructed documents (an annual report with repeated quarterly sections, a lease, API documentation, and a patient leaflet, about 800 words in all) and twelve questions, each with an answer phrase that a retrieved chunk must contain. Retrieval is hybrid, as in chapter 10: BM25 plus a stand-in dense retriever based on hashed character trigrams, fused with reciprocal rank fusion. It is the same machinery as chapter 13, packaged in chunkers.py with the corpus in corpus14.py.

The metric is hit@k: does at least one of the top k returned chunks contain the answer phrase? The last column counts the words returned for the top three, a proxy for what goes into the prompt. Twelve questions are a demonstration of method, not evidence about chunkers in general: one question is 8 points of hit rate.

"""Chunkers, a hybrid retriever and an evaluation loop for chapter 14."""
import math, re, zlib
from collections import Counter
import numpy as np
from corpus14 import DOCS, QUESTIONS

STOP = set("a an the of to and or is are in on for by it be as at from with your you we our can i my do how that this does what why when who much many long".split())
def terms(t):
    out = []
    for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:[.,][0-9]+)*%?", t.lower()):
        if w in STOP: continue
        for suf in ("ing", "ed", "es", "s"):
            if w.endswith(suf) and len(w) - len(suf) >= 4: w = w[:-len(suf)]; break
        out.append(w)
    return out
def words(t): return t.split()
def sentences(t): return [s for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", t.strip()) if s]

# ---- a parsed document: leaf sections with their heading path, and the text in reading order
def sections(doc):
    path, out = [], []
    for line in DOCS[doc].splitlines():
        m = re.match(r"(#+) (.*)", line)
        if m:
            path = path[: len(m.group(1)) - 1] + [m.group(2)]
        elif line.strip():
            out.append({"doc": doc, "path": list(path), "text": line.strip()})
    return out

def heading_before(doc, char_pos):
    """Nearest heading path before a character position (what an LLM would be shown as 'the document')."""
    path, pos = [], 0
    for line in DOCS[doc].splitlines(keepends=True):
        if pos > char_pos: break
        m = re.match(r"(#+) (.*)", line)
        if m: path = path[: len(m.group(1)) - 1] + [m.group(2)]
        pos += len(line)
    return path

# ---- chunkers: each returns dicts with doc, text (what is embedded), shown (what is returned), parent
def fixed(size, overlap=0):
    def run():
        out = []
        for d in DOCS:
            ws, pos = [], 0
            plain = DOCS[d]
            for m in re.finditer(r"\S+", plain): ws.append((m.group(), m.start()))
            step = size - overlap
            for i in range(0, len(ws), step):
                part = ws[i:i + size]
                t = " ".join(w for w, _ in part)
                out.append({"doc": d, "text": t, "shown": t, "start": part[0][1]})
                if i + size >= len(ws): break
        return out
    return run
def structural():
    return [{"doc": s["doc"], "text": s["text"], "shown": s["text"], "start": 0, "path": s["path"]} for d in DOCS for s in sections(d)]
def with_context(chunks):
    """Stand-in for LLM-written chunk context: document title plus the nearest heading path.
    A real system asks a model to write one or two sentences; this is the part of that text a rule can produce."""
    out = []
    for c in chunks:
        path = c.get("path") or heading_before(c["doc"], c["start"])
        ctx = " > ".join(path) if path else ""
        out.append({**c, "text": ctx + ". " + c["text"] if ctx else c["text"]})
    return out

# ---- retrieval: BM25 + hashed-trigram vectors, fused by reciprocal rank fusion
class Index:
    def __init__(self, chunks):
        self.chunks = chunks
        self.tf = [Counter(terms(c["text"])) for c in chunks]
        self.df = Counter(t for tf in self.tf for t in tf)
        self.avg = sum(sum(tf.values()) for tf in self.tf) / len(chunks)
        self.v = np.stack([self.vec(c["text"]) for c in chunks])
    @staticmethod
    def vec(t, dim=512):
        v = np.zeros(dim); s = " " + " ".join(terms(t)) + " "
        for i in range(len(s) - 2): v[zlib.crc32(s[i:i+3].encode()) % dim] += 1
        return v / (np.linalg.norm(v) or 1)
    def bm25(self, q, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
        n_docs, out = len(self.chunks), np.zeros(len(self.chunks))
        for i, tf in enumerate(self.tf):
            n = sum(tf.values())
            for t in terms(q):
                if tf[t]:
                    idf = math.log(1 + (n_docs - self.df[t] + .5) / (self.df[t] + .5))
                    out[i] += idf * tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * n / self.avg))
        return out
    def search(self, q, k=3):
        lex = np.argsort(-self.bm25(q), kind="stable")[:20]; den = np.argsort(-(self.v @ self.vec(q)), kind="stable")[:20]
        score = Counter()
        for lst in (lex, den):
            for r, i in enumerate(lst, 1): score[int(i)] += 1 / (60 + r)
        return [self.chunks[i] for i, _ in score.most_common(k)]

def evaluate(chunks, k=3, shown=lambda c: c["shown"]):
    ix = Index(chunks); hit1 = hitk = 0; ret = 0
    for q, phrase in QUESTIONS:
        res = ix.search(q, k)
        hit1 += phrase in shown(res[0]); hitk += any(phrase in shown(c) for c in res)
        ret += sum(len(words(shown(c))) for c in res)
    n = len(QUESTIONS)
    sizes = [len(words(c["shown"])) for c in chunks]
    return {"chunks": len(chunks), "mean_words": sum(sizes) / len(sizes), "hit1": hit1 / n, "hitk": hitk / n, "returned": ret / n}

rows = [

 ("fixed 25 + heading context", with_context(fixed(25)())),
 ("fixed 60 + heading context", with_context(fixed(60)())),
 ("structural + heading context", with_context(structural())),
]

Read the rows in pairs.

  • Small fixed windows lose facts. With 25-word windows and no overlap, the answer phrase is in the top three only 75% of the time, and top-ranked only half the time. Overlap of 8 words lifts hit@1 to 0.75: the same boundaries, but facts near the cuts now appear whole somewhere.
  • Big chunks are accurate and expensive. The 150-word windows reach hit@1 of 0.92 and hit@3 of 1.00, but return 389 words for the top three, more than four times the roughly 88 words returned by the structural chunks. In this tiny corpus there are only seven chunks, so almost anything big is bound to contain the answer. On a real collection, large chunks would also blur their topics.
  • Structural chunks alone are weak here (hit@1 0.67). Section boundaries are the right cuts, but look at what is embedded: for the report, every quarter's section reads "Revenue grew X% compared with the previous year. The operating margin was Y%...". The words that identify the division and the quarter are in the headings, which were stripped from the chunk text and kept only as metadata. A question that names "Surgical" and "second quarter" has nothing to match.
  • Adding the heading path fixes it. The last row prepends "Acme Robotics annual report 2025 > Surgical division > Second quarter." to each section before indexing. Hit@1 rises from 0.67 to 0.92, hit@3 from 0.75 to 1.00, with about the same 87 words returned. The cut was fine; the chunk had lost its identity.

Common misconception

"Bigger chunks give the model more context, so they are safer." They give the reader more context, but they do not help the retriever: one vector for many topics matches none of them well. And they cost tokens on every request. The chapter's results show the retriever's side of this; the generator's side (distractors in a long context) is what the Lost in the Middle case examines. The better approach is to retrieve small and return big, as below.

Is semantic chunking worth it?

The experiment above did not include semantic chunking, so here it is, using the same stand-in embedder to compare neighbouring sentences and cutting at the lowest 25% or 50% of similarities (with a minimum of two sentences per chunk).

"""Semantic chunking: cut where the similarity of neighbouring sentences drops (stand-in embedder)."""
from chunkers import *

def semantic_chunks(percentile=25, min_sents=2):
    chunks = []
    for d in DOCS:
        sents, starts = [], []
        for m in re.finditer(r"[^\n]+?[.!?](?=\s|$)", re.sub(r"^#+ .*$", "", DOCS[d], flags=re.M)):
            sents.append(m.group().strip()); starts.append(m.start())
        vs = [Index.vec(s) for s in sents]
        sims = [float(vs[i] @ vs[i + 1]) for i in range(len(vs) - 1)]
        cut = np.percentile(sims, percentile)
        group, groups = [0], []
        for i, s in enumerate(sims):
            if s < cut and len(group) >= min_sents:
                groups.append(group); group = []
            group.append(i + 1)
        groups.append(group)
        for g in groups:
            t = " ".join(sents[i] for i in g)
            chunks.append({"doc": d, "text": t, "shown": t, "start": starts[g[0]]})
    return chunks

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{'chunking':<36}{'chunks':>7}{'words/chunk':>13}{'hit@1':>7}{'hit@3':>7}{'words returned (top 3)':>24}")
    for name, ch in (("semantic, cut at lowest 25%", semantic_chunks(25)),
                     ("semantic, cut at lowest 50%", semantic_chunks(50)),
                     ("fixed 60 words (for scale)", fixed(60)())):
        r = evaluate(ch)

On this corpus semantic chunking is worse than a plain fixed window of 60 words at a similar chunk count (hit@1 0.58 against 0.83). The reason is visible in the printed boundaries: the sentences of the quarterly sections are written in the same pattern, so the similarity between "The operating margin was 19%." and the next section's "Revenue grew 7.4%..." is as high as inside a section, and the cuts fall in arbitrary places. Two cautions apply. The stand-in embedder is crude, and a trained embedder would place some boundaries better. And the corpus is tiny. A fair statement of the evidence is the one in Qu, Bao and Tu, "Is Semantic Chunking Worth the Computational Cost?" (2024, Findings of NAACL 2025): across document retrieval, evidence retrieval and answer generation, they found that the extra computation of semantic chunking was not justified by consistent gains over fixed-size chunking. Try it on your own data before paying for it.

The orphan chunk and contextual retrieval

The structural row exposed a more general problem. A chunk is cut out of a document and loses everything the document knew. Pronouns ("it", "this limit"), relative terms ("the following quarter") and shared boilerplate ("Revenue grew...") become ambiguous or identical across chunks. A chunk that has lost the context needed to interpret it is an orphan. The next listing shows one: the query names the division and the quarter, and the right section is nowhere in the top three, because the embedded text does not contain them.

"""The orphan-chunk problem and what chunk context does to it."""
from chunkers import *

q, phrase = "How much did Surgical division revenue grow in the second quarter?", "7.4%"
base = structural()
print("question:", q, "\n")
for name, chunks in (("plain chunks", base), ("chunks with context prepended", with_context(base))):
    ix = Index(chunks)
    top = ix.search(q, 3)
    print(name)
    for r, c in enumerate(top, 1):
        mark = "  <-- answer" if phrase in c["shown"] else ""
        print(f"  {r}. {c['text'][:96]}{'...' if len(c['text']) > 96 else ''}{mark}")
    print()
orphan = next(c for c in base if phrase in c["shown"])
print("the answer chunk as stored, and as embedded after adding context:")
print("  plain  :", orphan["shown"])
print("  context:", with_context([orphan])[0]["text"][:110] + "...")

With plain chunks, the top three are unrelated (the retry-policy circuit breaker, the company overview, the retry defaults) and the answer is absent. With context prepended, the answer chunk is first and the next two results are the closest sibling sections, which is what a good retriever should return. The only change is the text that is embedded and indexed; the text shown to the model is unchanged.

In the listing the "context" is the heading path, which a rule can produce. Many documents have no useful headings, and for them Anthropic's "Introducing Contextual Retrieval" (September 2024) proposes asking a language model to write the context. For every chunk, a model is shown the whole document and the chunk and asked for a short context that situates the chunk within the document, for the purpose of improving search retrieval, answering with only that context. Their example is a chunk reading "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter", which becomes "This chunk is from an SEC filing on ACME corp's performance in Q2 2023; the previous quarter's revenue was $314 million. The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter." The generated text, usually 50 to 100 tokens, is prepended to the chunk before it is embedded (contextual embeddings) and before it is indexed for BM25 (contextual BM25). The model's output is used only for indexing; the user still sees the original chunk.

What it bought, and what it costs

Anthropic reported failure rates for retrieving the right chunk within the top 20 (that is, 1 minus recall@20) averaged over several domains (codebases, fiction, arXiv papers and science papers). The chart plots their published numbers.

Three observations. The big win came from combining contextual embeddings with BM25 (49% fewer failures), the hybrid principle of chapter 10 applied to enriched text. Reranking added more (67% in total), consistent with chapter 8. And passing 20 chunks to the model did better than 10 or 5 in their tests, which is a reminder that the right k depends on the pipeline.

The cost is a model call per chunk. The post estimates a one-time cost of about $1.02 per million document tokens, assuming 800-token chunks, 8,000-token documents, a 50-token instruction, 100 tokens of generated context per chunk, and prompt caching (the provider stores the processed document prefix so that each chunk's call after the first pays a discounted rate for the document). With those assumptions a corpus of 20 million tokens would cost about $20 once, plus the cost of regenerating context for documents that change. Prices change, so recompute with current rates, and note that without caching the whole document would be paid for again with every chunk.

Alternatives and neighbours

  • Heading paths and titles (the listing above) are free and often get most of the benefit for well-structured documents. Add the document title, the section path and, for conversations or tickets, the subject line.
  • Late chunking. Günther et al., "Late Chunking: Contextual Chunk Embeddings Using Long-Context Embedding Models" (2024), run a long-context embedding model over the whole document first and only then split the token embeddings into chunk spans and pool each, so each chunk vector was computed with the surrounding text in view. It needs no model call per chunk, but it needs an embedder that accepts long inputs and exposes token-level outputs, and it changes vectors only, not the text BM25 sees.
  • Summaries and hypothetical questions. Index a one-line summary or generated questions for each chunk alongside the text. These help when users ask in vocabulary the document does not use, but they add the same generation cost and the same risk of errors.

Match small, return big

The size trade-off from the first figure has an escape: use different units for matching and for reading. In parent-child retrieval (also called small-to-big, or sentence-window retrieval), the index contains small child chunks, say sentences, each linked to a larger parent such as its section. A search matches children, which are precise; the system then returns the parent, which has the context. When several children of the same parent match, the parent is returned once.

The listing compares four setups on the chapter's twelve questions: sentences alone, sentences with heading context, the same but returning the parent section, and sections with heading context and no children at all.

"""Small-to-big: match on small child chunks, return the parent section."""
from chunkers import *

def children():
    out = []
    for d in DOCS:
        for sec in sections(d):
            for s in sentences(sec["text"]):
                out.append({"doc": d, "text": s, "shown": s, "path": sec["path"], "parent": sec["text"]})
    return out

def run(name, chunks, returns_parent, k=3):
    ix = Index(chunks); h1 = hk = words_out = 0
    for q, phrase in QUESTIONS:
        seen, texts = set(), []
        for c in ix.search(q, 10):                       # take more children, then de-duplicate parents
            t = c["parent"] if returns_parent else c["shown"]
            if t not in seen: seen.add(t); texts.append(t)
        texts = texts[:k]
        h1 += phrase in texts[0]; hk += any(phrase in t for t in texts)
        words_out += sum(len(words(t)) for t in texts)
    n = len(QUESTIONS)
    print(f"{name:<44}{len(chunks):>7}{h1 / n:>7.2f}{hk / n:>7.2f}{words_out / n:>16.0f}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{'index of':<44}{'chunks':>7}{'hit@1':>7}{'hit@3':>7}{'words returned':>16}")
    ch = children()
    run("sentences, return the sentence", ch, False)
    run("sentences + heading context, return sentence", with_context(ch), False)
    run("sentences + heading context, return section", with_context(ch), True)
    run("sections + heading context (no children)", [{**c, "parent": c["shown"]} for c in with_context(structural())], True)

sentences, return the sentence                   56   0.50   0.75              31
sentences + heading context, return sentence     56   0.83   0.92              30
sentences + heading context, return section      56   1.00   1.00              86
sections + heading context (no children)         20   0.92   1.00              87

Sentences alone are precise but poor orphans: the answer phrase is in the top three for 75% of questions, and for half the time the top result lacks it. Adding the heading context to each sentence lifts hit@1 to 0.83 while returning only 30 words. Returning the parent section makes every question succeed at rank 1 (1.00) for 86 words, against section chunks (0.92 at rank 1, 87 words). The difference between the last two rows is one question out of twelve, and I would not claim parent-child is better than good sections on this evidence. The method's value shows when sections are long: matching a sentence in a 3,000-word section and returning a window of neighbours gives precision that the section vector, averaged over many topics, cannot.

Variants differ in how much the parent returns. Sentence-window retrieval returns the matched sentence plus a few neighbours on each side. Auto-merging retrieval returns the parent only if several of its children match, otherwise just the children. All of them need a data model with explicit links:

Analogy: index cards and the book

To find things in a thick book you make index cards, one per idea, each pointing to a page. A card that says only "it was raised to 55 euros" is useless without the chapter title written on it. That is contextual retrieval: write on every card where it came from. And when you find the right card you do not read the card, you open the book at that page. That is parent-child retrieval: search the cards, read the pages.

Where it breaks down: a human indexer knows which ideas matter. An automatic splitter cuts at fixed places and an LLM writes the context sentence without being able to check it against what the author meant, so some cards will be slightly wrong.

Content that needs special handling

  • Tables. Splitting a table between rows detaches the data from its column headers. Keep a table together where it fits, or repeat the header row in each chunk, or convert rows to sentences ("Plan: Premium; Support: phone and chat"). Chapter 20 covers extraction.
  • Code. Split on functions and classes, not lines, and prepend the file path and the enclosing class name. A function body without its signature is an orphan.
  • Lists and procedures. A numbered procedure is one unit; split it at step 5 and the question "how do I do X" returns half a recipe.
  • Chat, email and tickets. The unit is a thread or an exchange with its subject; a single message ("Yes, that works") has no meaning alone.
  • Slides and scanned pages. Text may have no reading order, or may be in the image: see chapter 18 and visual retrieval, where the page itself is the chunk.
  • Embedder input limits. Many embedding models accept only a few hundred tokens and truncate longer input without an error: the tail of an oversized chunk simply does not contribute to the vector, and a fact in that tail cannot be found by meaning. Check the limit of your model, count tokens with its own tokeniser rather than words, and alert on truncation. Languages and scripts differ in how many tokens a word costs (chapter 22), so a chunk size tuned on English may overflow in another language.

Systems connection

Chunking decisions are storage and compute decisions. Overlap multiplies stored text and embedding calls by size / (size − overlap); contextual retrieval adds one model call per chunk at ingestion and every changed document triggers it again; parent-child doubles the record count but not the vector count. All of these meet the incremental-ingestion discipline of chapter 13 and the change-data-capture pipeline that feeds it: choose stable chunk ids so that editing one paragraph re-embeds one chunk, not the whole file. At scale, memory per vector (chapter 11) turns a 25% overlap into a 25% larger index bill.

Choosing, and testing the choice

There is no universally best chunker, but there is a sensible order of work.

  1. Start from structure. If documents have headings, clauses or entries, cut there, with a maximum size. If they do not, use recursive splitting at paragraph and sentence boundaries.
  2. Pick a size from the embedder and the questions. Stay well below the embedder's input limit. Common starting points are a few hundred tokens with 10 to 20% overlap, but treat them as hypotheses: short factual questions favour smaller chunks, explanations and multi-sentence reasoning favour larger ones.
  3. Add context. Prepend the title and heading path first (free). If recall is still poor on orphan-like chunks, try generated context, and measure whether it pays for its ingestion cost.
  4. Decouple matching from reading. If you see the trade-off biting (precise small chunks lose context, large chunks blur), try parent-child or sentence-window retrieval.
  5. Evaluate on your questions. Build the small judged set of chapter 5, and for each chunker report hit@k or recall@k together with the tokens returned. Look at the failures one by one: a cut through a fact, an orphan, and a blurred long chunk each leave a different signature.

State of play, October 2026

Contextual retrieval is now a common recipe in vendor documentation and open-source frameworks, usually with the generation step done by a small, cheap model. The figures in this chapter (35%, 49% and 67% fewer top-20 failures; about $1.02 per million document tokens) are from Anthropic's September 2024 post and were measured on its own datasets with the models and prices of that date: treat them as an existence proof and a cost model, and rerun the experiment on your data. Evidence on semantic chunking remains mixed, and embedders with very long input windows have made some "just embed the whole page" designs viable. Late chunking and other methods that give each chunk vector awareness of its neighbours are active research; check recent benchmarks before adopting one.

Key takeaways

  • The chunk is the unit that is embedded, retrieved and cited. A cut through a fact makes it unretrievable; an oversized chunk blurs its vector and bloats the prompt.
  • Fixed windows are simple but ignore meaning; overlap rescues boundary facts at a storage cost of size / (size − overlap). Structural and recursive splitting respect the document.
  • Orphan chunks lose their identity. Prepending the title and heading path, or a generated context sentence, to the text that is embedded and indexed (but not to the text shown) repairs it. Anthropic reported 35%, 49% and 67% fewer top-20 failures with contextual embeddings, contextual BM25 and reranking.
  • Semantic chunking is not reliably better than fixed windows; test it before paying for it.
  • Parent-child retrieval matches small units and returns larger ones, escaping the precision versus context trade-off.
  • Store ids, offsets, hashes and inherited metadata with every chunk, and check embedder input limits so nothing is truncated silently.

Check your understanding

A fixed-window chunker with no overlap cuts a contract clause so that "The deposit is returned" ends one chunk and "within 30 days of departure" starts the next. A user asks how quickly the deposit is returned. What is the most direct fix?

  1. Use a better embedding model.
  2. Add overlap (or cut on sentence or clause boundaries) so the whole clause appears in one chunk.
  3. Retrieve more chunks and hope the model joins them.
  4. Add a reranker.
If no chunk contains the whole fact, neither a better embedder nor a reranker can return it. Retrieving more chunks may bring both halves, but neither half matches the question well, and it costs tokens. Repair the cut itself.

Each quarterly section of a report reads "Revenue grew X%. The margin was Y%." with the division and quarter only in the headings, which were stored as metadata. Queries naming a division and quarter retrieve the wrong section. What is the cheapest sensible repair?

  1. Increase the chunk size to a full page.
  2. Switch to semantic chunking.
  3. Fine-tune the embedder on the report.
  4. Prepend the title and heading path to the text that is embedded and indexed (and keep the original for display).
The identifying words are missing from the embedded text. Putting the heading path into that text restores them at almost no cost, as the chapter's experiment showed. Larger chunks blur the quarters together; semantic chunking has no way to see headings that are not in the text; fine-tuning is far more expensive than the problem requires.

In Anthropic's contextual retrieval, what text does the answering model see for a retrieved chunk?

  1. The original chunk. The generated context was used only to build the indexes.
  2. The generated context only.
  3. The generated context plus the chunk, always.
  4. A summary of the whole document.
The context sentence is prepended before embedding and BM25 indexing to make the chunk findable. Showing and citing the original text avoids presenting model-written text, which may contain errors, as if it were the source. Some systems do show the context too, but that is a design choice, not part of the technique.

A team chooses 200-token windows with 50-token overlap. By what factor do stored text and embedding calls grow compared with no overlap?

  1. 1.25
  2. 1.50
  3. About 1.33 (200 / 150)
  4. 2.00
Each window advances by size minus overlap, 150 tokens, so the text is covered 200 / 150, about 1.33 times. An overlap of 40 tokens would give 1.25 (200 / 160), and 1.50 would need an overlap of about 67 tokens.

Parent-child retrieval indexes sentences and returns their sections. When does it help most?

  1. When sections are very short, because then children and parents are the same.
  2. When sections are long: sentence-level matching is precise, while the section supplies the context the model needs to read it.
  3. When the embedder has a very small input limit and cannot embed sections at all, which makes sentences unnecessary.
  4. When documents have no structure and no sections exist.
With long sections a single vector blurs many topics, so matching on sentences is far more precise, while returning the section keeps the surrounding information. With short sections there is little to gain, and without any section or window there is no parent to return (though a sliding window of neighbours can play that role).

Your embedder silently truncates input after 512 tokens, and your chunker produces 900-token chunks. What is the likely symptom?

  1. An error at indexing time.
  2. Slower search only.
  3. Questions about facts in the second half of each chunk fail to retrieve it by meaning, while BM25 (which sees all the text) still can.
  4. Duplicate results.
The vector represents only the first 512 tokens, so semantic matching for later content is blind to it; BM25 is unaffected because it indexes the whole text, which makes the fault look like a dense-retrieval weakness on some queries. Many models do not raise an error. Count tokens with the model's own tokeniser and alert when input is truncated.
