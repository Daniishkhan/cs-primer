Building a Test Collection

The metrics of chapter 4 are only as good as the judgements they are computed from. This chapter shows how to build a set of queries and relevance judgements for your own corpus that you can trust: where queries come from, how to write labelling guidelines, how pooling finds candidates without judging everything, how to measure whether your judges agree, and how to do it in about a week.

Why this matters

Public benchmarks (chapter 6) tell you which models are worth trying. Only a test collection built from your documents, your users' questions and your definition of "relevant" tells you which one to ship. Teams that skip it tune by looking at a handful of queries they remember, and discover regressions from customers. Teams that build it get a number they can defend, a way to catch regressions on every change, and a dataset that later chapters (fine-tuning an embedder, evaluating a RAG pipeline) reuse. After this chapter you can plan, run and document a small test collection, and know which of its flaws will bias your comparisons.

The Cranfield recipe

Computer-based retrieval evaluation begins with the Cranfield experiments of the 1950s and 1960s, led by Cyril Cleverdon in the UK. Their lasting contribution is a recipe: fix a collection of documents, fix a set of questions, have people judge which documents answer which question once, and then compare any retrieval system by running it on the fixed questions and scoring its output against the fixed judgements. The cost of judging is paid once; every later experiment is cheap and repeatable. Since 1992 the Text REtrieval Conference (TREC), run by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, has applied the recipe at scale, and nearly every retrieval benchmark since, including BEIR, descends from it.

A test collection therefore has three parts:

  • a document set: the corpus the system searches (or a frozen snapshot of it);
  • a set of topics or queries, each with the information need behind it written down;
  • a set of relevance judgements (qrels): labels for query and document pairs, from a defined scale.

Analogy: an exam paper

A test collection is an exam for retrieval systems. The questions are fixed, the mark scheme is written by someone who has read the material, and candidates are compared by their marks. A good exam covers the syllabus, has unambiguous marking and has not been seen in advance by the candidates.

Where it breaks down: an exam setter knows the right answers before the exam. In retrieval, the mark scheme is built by looking at what candidate systems return, so the scheme can be biased towards the candidates that were used to build it. Pooling, below, is about that problem.

Step 1: define the task and the unit of retrieval

Before collecting anything, write two sentences. Who asks, and what do they want? What counts as a result? The first fixes the information need; the second fixes the retrieval unit: whole documents, pages, sections or chunks. These choices change the collection. If the system answers support questions by passing chunks to a language model, then relevance should be judged at chunk or section level, and the judgement should say whether the chunk contains what is needed to answer, not merely whether it is about the topic. If it is an enterprise search for whole files, the unit is the file.

Two traps are worth naming now. First, topical versus useful: a page that mentions "late payment fees" is on topic for the query "what is the late payment fee", but only the page stating the figure is useful. Judge usefulness for the stated need. Second, chunking changes the collection: if you re-chunk the corpus (chapter 14) after judging chunks, the judgements no longer apply. Judge against something chunking-independent when you can (a document and a text span that holds the answer), and map chunks to those spans automatically.

Step 2: queries

Where queries come from

The best source is real queries from the people who will use the system: search logs, support tickets, chat transcripts, emails to a help desk. They carry real vocabulary, typos, abbreviations and the odd shape of what people want. If the system is new and no logs exist, there are three fallbacks, in descending order of quality:

  1. Interview and shadow users. Ask subject-matter experts for the questions they are asked most often and the ones they find hardest. Ask them to phrase these as the user would, not as the document would.
  2. Mine adjacent sources. Support-ticket titles, forum posts, FAQ entries, and the questions in training material often come close to real user language.
  3. Generate queries with a language model from documents: give it a passage and ask what question a user would ask that this passage answers. This is cheap and scales, and it is how many public benchmarks and fine-tuning sets are built (BEIR's GenQ baseline does it for training). It has a well-known bias, discussed next.

Common misconception

"Queries generated from a document are as good as real ones." A model asked to write a question from a passage tends to copy the passage's own words, so the query and its source document share vocabulary far more than real queries do. That favours lexical matching: BM25 looks better than it will on real traffic, and an embedder that was good at paraphrase looks less impressive. Check for it: compute the fraction of query words that occur in the relevant passage, and compare with the same figure on a few dozen real queries. If generated queries overlap much more, instruct the generator to paraphrase and avoid copying, or use generated queries for training and real ones for the test set. A second error nearby: "the gold set is the truth". It is one team's defensible, documented answer, and chapter 4's metrics are only comparisons against it.

Which queries, and how many

Do not sample uniformly from the log and stop. Search logs are dominated by a few popular queries (the head), while most distinct queries appear once or twice (the long tail; chapter 3). Build the set in strata so that every important kind of query is represented:

  • head queries (frequent), torso, and tail (rare), sampled in proportion to the traffic you care about and also boosted for the tail, which is where retrieval fails;
  • query types: lookups of an identifier ("invoice 4471-B"), questions in natural language, short keywords, multi-part questions;
  • queries known to be hard: those with a recent complaint, a zero-result history, or an abandoned search;
  • a small share of unanswerable queries whose answer is not in the corpus. Keep them in a separate slice: they test whether the system abstains rather than retrieving something plausible, which matters for RAG (chapter 17) and cannot be scored by recall.

How many? Statistics decides, not habit. TREC tracks traditionally use 50 topics, and that number appears in many papers because it was affordable, not because it is enough for small differences. The widget and the listing below show the arithmetic. Let sd be the standard deviation of the per-query difference in score between two systems, and n the number of queries. The mean difference has standard error sd/√n; a 95% interval is about 1.96 times that, and a difference can be reliably detected (80% power, 5% significance) only if it exceeds about 2.8 times that. The standard error shrinks with the square root: four times as many queries halve the interval.

Try it

How many queries do I need?

Set the spread of the per-query difference between your two systems (0.106 is the value from the listing; real systems can be noisier) and the gain you hope to detect. Then see how many queries you need and what a given set can resolve.

Finally, split the queries into a development set that you tune against and a test set that you consult rarely. Every time you inspect the test queries to decide what to change, you spend some of their independence. The split must be by query (and by topic if queries cluster), never by judgement row; chapter 7 adds a third reason, because fine-tuning an embedder on queries that also appear in the test set makes every number meaningless.

Step 3: guidelines and a relevance scale

Two careful people given the same query and document will often disagree unless told how to decide. The remedy is a short written guideline: the information need, the scale with definitions, a decision rule for the awkward cases and worked examples. Draft it before judging, and revise it after a pilot (below). A scale of four grades works well because it is fine enough to separate "answers the question" from "related", and coarse enough that judges rarely agonise.

GradeNameDefinitionExample for "what is the late payment fee?"
3PerfectStates the answer clearly and could be used on its own"The late payment fee is 2 percent per month."
2GoodContains the answer, but with extra material or needing a little inferenceA terms page where the fee is one clause among many
1MarginalOn topic and helps find the answer, but does not state itA page saying "late fees may apply; see the pricing schedule"
0Not relevantDoes not help, or is about a different thing with similar wordsAn annual report that mentions "late in the year" and "payment platform"

Add rules for the cases that cause arguments. A few common ones: duplicates (a document identical to a higher-grade one gets the same grade); outdated versions (an old policy that has been superseded is grade 0 for a query about the current policy, whatever its content); partial answers (a document that answers half of a two-part question is at most grade 2); and documents the judge could not understand (a separate label, "cannot judge", rather than a guess). Write the information need beside each query ("the user wants the percentage and the period, not the policy history"); a query string alone is ambiguous, and different judges will resolve the ambiguity differently.

Step 4: finding candidates by pooling

Judging every document for every query is impossible: 100 queries over a corpus of 50,000 passages would be 5 million judgements. The Cranfield approach, and TREC's, is pooling: run several different retrieval systems on every query, take the top d results from each (the pool depth), merge them into one set with duplicates removed, and judge only that set. Everything outside the pool is treated as non-relevant. The wager is that relevant documents, if they exist, are ranked high by at least one system, so judging the union of the top results finds most of them (Spärck Jones and van Rijsbergen, 1975, proposed the idea; Zobel, 1998, studied how reliable the results are).

Step through

Pooling, and what it misses

  1. Two existing systems. A (BM25) and B (a tuned variant) each return their top five for one query.
  2. Merge into a pool. The union has seven distinct documents; duplicates are judged once.
  3. Judge the pool. People grade only these seven. d3, d1 and d2 are relevant; every document outside the pool is implicitly 0.
  4. A new system arrives. A dense retriever C ranks d5, d3, d6, d1, d12. Three of its five results were never in the pool (red).
  5. The unfairness. Unjudged results count as non-relevant, so C is under-scored, because it finds relevant documents the lexical systems did not.
  6. The repair. Judge the holes, then re-score. Add a dense or otherwise different system to the pool from the start.

Pooling is cheap and effective, and it has one built-in weakness: the judged set reflects the systems that built the pool. A system that behaves like them is scored fairly. A system that retrieves differently finds relevant documents nobody judged, which count against it. The BEIR authors measured exactly this on the TREC-COVID collection (Thakur et al., 2021, section 6): the share of each system's top-10 results that no judge had seen (Hole@10) was 6.4% for BM25 but 14.4% for the dense retriever ANCE and 31.8% for TAS-B. After 980 missing judgements were added, BM25's nDCG@10 moved from 0.656 to 0.668 while ANCE moved from 0.654 to 0.735, a change that turned "slightly below BM25" into "about seven points above". The pool, even with many contributing systems, was biased towards lexical retrieval.

To see the mechanism in numbers, the next listing simulates a collection (60 queries, 600 documents each, 12 relevant per query). Two lexical systems build the pool; a dense system is held out. The assumption that makes the bias visible, and which you should recognise from real corpora with vocabulary mismatch, is that 45% of the relevant documents share no words with the query, so lexical systems cannot find them. This is an illustrative simulation, not a measurement of any real system.

"""A small synthetic test collection used by chapter 5 (all numbers are simulated).
Each query has 600 documents; 12 are relevant. Every document has two hidden
signals: 'lex' (word overlap with the query) and 'sem' (meaning match)."""
import random, math

def make_query(rng, n_docs=600, n_rel=12):
    docs = []
    for i in range(n_docs):
        rel = i < n_rel
        if rel:
            sem = rng.gauss(2.0, 0.8)
            lex = rng.gauss(1.6, 0.9) if rng.random() < 0.55 else rng.gauss(0.0, 0.9)  # 45% share no words
        else:
            sem = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            lex = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        docs.append((rel, lex, sem))
    return docs

def run(rng, docs, kind):
    if kind == "bm25":
        key = lambda d: d[1] + rng.gauss(0, 0.3)
    elif kind == "bm25-tuned":
        key = lambda d: d[1] + rng.gauss(0, 0.5)
    else:
        key = lambda d: d[2] + rng.gauss(0, 0.5)
    order = sorted(range(len(docs)), key=lambda i: -key(docs[i]))
    return order

def ndcg_binary(order, rel_set, judged, k=10, only_judged=True):
    gains = [1 if (i in rel_set and (i in judged or not only_judged)) else 0 for i in order[:k]]
    dcg = sum(g / math.log2(r + 2) for r, g in enumerate(gains))
    n_ideal = len(rel_set & judged) if only_judged else len(rel_set)
    ideal = sum(1 / math.log2(r + 2) for r in range(min(k, n_ideal)))
    return dcg / ideal if ideal else 0.0

def collection(n_queries=60, seed=11):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    data = []
    for q in range(n_queries):
        docs = make_query(rng)
        runs = {k: run(rng, docs, k) for k in ("bm25", "bm25-tuned", "dense")}
        data.append((docs, runs, {i for i, d in enumerate(docs) if d[0]}))
    return data

In the true column the dense system is much better than BM25 (0.398 against 0.167). With a pool built only from lexical runs and depth 5 or 10, the measured scores say the opposite: BM25 0.43, dense 0.11 to 0.15. A deeper pool narrows the gap (at depth 100 the two are about equal, 0.222 against 0.228) but never closes it, because the documents that only the dense system can find stay unjudged. When the pool includes the dense run too (last line), the ordering is right again even at depth 10 and a pool of 24.5 documents per query. The absolute numbers are inflated, because the ideal ranking in nDCG is built from the relevant documents the pool happened to find (chapter 4); what matters for decisions is that the order of the systems is correct.

Systems connection

A judgement file is derived data, like a search index: it is a function of a corpus snapshot, a guideline version and the set of systems that built the pool. If the corpus changes, some judged documents disappear or change and some new ones are unjudged. Treat it like any other versioned artefact (Data Systems 19 on keeping derived data consistent): snapshot the corpus, store judgements with the document version and the guideline version, and record which runs contributed to the pool. If the test queries are real user queries, they may contain personal data; see Security 17 for minimisation and retention before you copy them into a shared spreadsheet.

Practical pooling advice

  • Diversify the contributors. BM25, at least one dense retriever, and if you have one a hybrid or reranked run (chapter 10). Diversity matters more than depth.
  • Depth 10 to 20 per system is usually enough for a small collection scored with cut-off-10 metrics. Judging deeper helps recall-oriented measures but costs proportionally more.
  • Add a few random or low-ranked documents to a sample of queries. If judges find relevant documents among them, the pool is missing things you will want to know about.
  • Judge holes continuously. Each time you evaluate a new system, count its Hole@10 (share of top-10 results with no judgement) and judge them before trusting the score. Buckley and Voorhees (2004) developed measures such as bpref that tolerate incomplete judgements, but the cheaper fix is to complete them.
  • Keep the judge blind. Show the pool in a shuffled order without saying which system returned which result, so that a system's reputation does not leak into labels.

Step 5: judging, agreement and adjudication

Who judges, and how long it takes

The best judges are people who understand the domain and the user's need: a support engineer for support tickets, a paralegal for contracts. Crowd workers are acceptable for general-language tasks and weak for specialist corpora. Start with a pilot: two people independently judge the same 15 to 20 queries (a few hundred pairs), you compare their labels, and you rewrite the guidelines wherever they disagreed. Most guideline failures show up in the pilot.

Budget the work with an explicit assumption, then measure it in the pilot. Suppose a judge needs about 45 seconds per query and passage pair (assumption: short passages, a clear guideline). For 100 queries with a pool of 20 documents each, that is 2,000 judgements, about 25 hours of one person's time; double-labelling a quarter of them adds six more. That is a feasible week for two part-time judges, and it is why pool depth and query count are the dials: each doubling of either doubles the bill.

Measuring agreement

Raw percentage agreement overstates reliability, because two judges who both say "not relevant" most of the time will agree often by chance. Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) corrects for that: it is the observed agreement minus the agreement expected by chance, divided by the maximum possible improvement over chance. A value of 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is what chance would give. Common rough bands (from Landis and Koch, 1977) call 0.6 to 0.8 substantial and above 0.8 almost perfect; these are conventions, not laws. For graded labels, weighted kappa counts a disagreement between grades 2 and 3 as less serious than one between 0 and 3. Evals chapter 5 treats agreement and adjudication in general; here is the retrieval-specific computation.

import random
from collections import Counter

# Two annotators grade 80 query-document pairs on a 0-3 scale. We simulate
# them: each sees the same 'true' grade but misreads it by one level some of
# the time (annotator B a little more often, and biased towards generosity).
rng = random.Random(5)
truth = rng.choices([0, 1, 2, 3], weights=[45, 25, 20, 10], k=80)
def annotate(g, slip, up_bias):
    if rng.random() < slip:
        step = 1 if rng.random() < up_bias else -1
        return min(3, max(0, g + step))
    return g
A = [annotate(g, 0.18, 0.5) for g in truth]
B = [annotate(g, 0.30, 0.7) for g in truth]

def kappa(a, b, weight=lambda i, j: 0 if i == j else 1):
    labels = sorted(set(a) | set(b)); n = len(a)
    ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    obs = sum(weight(x, y) for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    exp = sum(weight(i, j) * ca[i] * cb[j] for i in labels for j in labels) / (n * n)
    return 1 - obs / exp

for i in range(4):

agree = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(A, B)) / 80
print(f"\nraw agreement                      : {agree:.2f}")
print(f"Cohen's kappa, 4 grades            : {kappa(A, B):.2f}")
print(f"weighted kappa (quadratic weights) : {kappa(A, B, lambda i, j: (i - j) ** 2):.2f}")
bin_a = [int(x >= 1) for x in A]; bin_b = [int(y >= 1) for y in B]
print(f"Cohen's kappa, relevant vs not     : {kappa(bin_a, bin_b):.2f}   (agreement {sum(x == y for x, y in zip(bin_a, bin_b)) / 80:.2f})")

The two simulated judges agree on 71% of the pairs on the four-grade scale, with kappa 0.60. The confusion matrix shows where: nearly all errors are by one grade, and most are between 0 and 1 (the boundary of "marginal"). The weighted kappa of 0.89 says that the disagreements are mostly small ones. Collapsing to relevant-or-not gives agreement 0.85 and kappa 0.69. Your reaction to a number like this should not be to blame a judge. If the 0/1 boundary is where people disagree, make the definition of "marginal" sharper, or merge the two grades.

Adjudication settles disagreements. For each pair where the judges differ by more than one grade (or differ on relevance), a third person, or the two judges together, decide the final label and note the reason; the reasons feed back into the guidelines. Use the adjudicated label in the gold set and keep the originals, so that the disagreement rate is recorded.

How much does label noise matter?

Less than people fear, for comparing systems, and more than they hope, for reporting absolute numbers. Voorhees (2000, "Variations in relevance judgments and the measurement of retrieval effectiveness") compared system rankings made from different assessors' judgements of the same TREC topics and found them highly correlated, even though individual documents were often judged differently. The simulation below damages the gold labels on purpose: judges miss a share of the relevant documents and add a few false positives. The scores fall, but the order of the systems does not change.

The dense system's large lead survives every noise level. The tiny gap between the two BM25 variants is a different matter: it is already within a hundredth or two of zero and moves around as the noise changes, so noisy labels would be a poor basis for choosing between them. That is the practical cost of label noise: it hides small differences while leaving large ones visible, and it is one more reason the agreement check matters most when you tune between close variants.

Language-model judges

A language model can label query and passage pairs far faster than a person. Thomas, Spielman, Craswell and Mitra (2023, "Large language models can accurately predict searcher preferences") report, for a prompt developed at Bing and tested against labels from real searchers, accuracy comparable to or better than third-party human labellers at a fraction of the cost, while also finding that prompt wording changes accuracy noticeably. Faggioli et al. (2023, "Perspectives on Large Language Models for Relevance Judgment") discuss the open questions, including circularity: if a system under test uses the same model family that produced the labels, it may be favoured. A sound practice is to use a model to propose labels, have people judge a stratified sample of the same pairs, report the model's agreement with people (kappa again), and send low-confidence or disputed pairs to people. Evals chapter 3 covers calibration, bias and the checks in more detail.

A gold set in a week

Putting the steps together, a small team can produce a defensible first collection in five working days. The numbers are a plan to adapt, not a standard.

On day 5 write a short data card (Evals chapter 6 has a template): where queries came from, how they were sampled, the scale and guideline version, how many judges, the kappa, the pool (which systems, which depth), known gaps, the corpus snapshot, and the intended use ("comparing retrievers for the support corpus; not a measure of end-to-end answer quality"). Then freeze the test split. It will need maintenance: add production queries as the product changes (the evaluation lab and Evals chapter 18 show the loop), judge holes when new systems are tried, and re-check agreement when the guideline changes.

State of play, October 2026

Language-model-assisted labelling is now routine in industry practice and an active research topic in the evaluation community, but no single prompt, model or validation method has settled the questions of bias and circularity. Treat any claim that "model labels replace human labels" with the same scepticism as a leaderboard number: ask what the agreement with people was, on which kinds of query, and who ran the check. Labelling tools change quickly; a spreadsheet with a locked column of document text and a drop-down for the grade is adequate for a first collection, and the discipline above matters more than the tool.

Key takeaways

  • A test collection is documents, queries with their information needs, and graded judgements. Judge once, compare many systems, and never let the retriever see the judgements.
  • Prefer real queries, stratified by frequency and type, with an unanswerable slice; generated queries copy the source document's words and flatter lexical search.
  • The number of queries sets the resolution: the interval shrinks as 1/√n, so 50 queries separate a keyword baseline from an embedder but not two close BM25 settings.
  • Write the relevance scale with definitions, the information need per query and rules for duplicates, outdated versions and partial answers; pilot with two judges first.
  • Pooling judges the union of several systems' top results. It biases against systems unlike the contributors; diversify the pool and judge each new system's holes.
  • Measure agreement with kappa, fix guidelines where judges disagree, adjudicate, and keep both the originals and the final labels.
  • Label noise lowers absolute scores and blurs small differences but preserves large ones; model-assisted judging needs a human-checked sample.
  • Freeze a test split, version the collection with a data card, and keep maintaining it.

Check your understanding

You evaluate a new embedding model against judgements pooled from BM25 runs only. It scores below BM25 on nDCG@10, but its top results look good when you read them. What is the most likely explanation and the right next step?

  1. The embedder is worse; stop
  2. The queries are too few; add 100 more and rerun
  3. Many of its top results were never judged and count as non-relevant; judge its holes (or add it to the pool) and re-score
  4. nDCG cannot compare lexical and dense systems
Pooling only from lexical systems leaves a retriever that finds different relevant documents with many unjudged results (BEIR measured Hole@10 of 14% to 32% for dense models against 6% for BM25 on TREC-COVID). More queries would not fix a systematic bias. nDCG can compare any ranked lists given complete enough judgements.

Two judges agree on 90% of binary relevance labels, but 92% of all pairs are non-relevant. What should you conclude about the reliability of the labels?

  1. Not yet much: with so many easy non-relevant pairs, chance agreement is high, so compute kappa on the relevant-heavy part or overall
  2. Excellent: 90% is above any threshold
  3. Poor: 90% is below 95%
  4. Nothing: agreement cannot be measured with binary labels
If both judges say "not relevant" for nearly everything, they will agree on most pairs by chance (here about 85% would match by chance), so 90% is little better than chance. Kappa corrects for this and can be low even when raw agreement looks high. Binary labels can be compared by kappa.

The paired difference in nDCG@10 between two retrievers has a standard deviation of 0.10 across queries. Roughly how many queries are needed to reliably detect a true improvement of 0.03 (80% power, 5% significance)?

  1. About 10
  2. About 30
  3. About 50
  4. About 90
n = (2.8 × sd / delta)2 = (2.8 × 0.10 / 0.03)2 = 9.332 ≈ 87, so about 90. Fifty queries would detect only differences above about 0.04, and ten would detect only gains near 0.09.

A team writes all test queries by asking a language model to produce a question from each document in the corpus. Which risk should they check for first?

  1. The queries will be too difficult for any system
  2. The queries copy the document's vocabulary, which inflates lexical retrieval and understates the value of semantic matching
  3. The queries will all be unanswerable
  4. The model will refuse to write queries
Generated questions tend to reuse the source passage's own words, so word overlap is far higher than in real queries. That makes BM25 look better than it would on real traffic. Check overlap against real queries, and keep real queries for the test set.

Your gold set has chunk-level judgements. A month later you change the chunk size from 400 to 250 tokens. What happens to the judgements?

  1. They remain valid because the documents are the same
  2. They are valid for the queries but not the documents
  3. They no longer apply to the new chunks; judge against a chunking-independent unit (document plus answer span) and map chunks to it
  4. They become valid after normalisation
A label attached to a chunk id says nothing about new chunks with different boundaries. Judging a stable unit, such as a document and the span containing the answer, lets you map any chunking onto the same truth.

The pilot shows judges disagree mostly between grades 0 and 1. What is the best response?

  1. Replace the judge who gave more 1s
  2. Average the two labels and continue
  3. Collect more queries before judging further
  4. Sharpen the definition of "marginal" with examples (or merge the two grades), then re-pilot
Systematic disagreement at a boundary is a guideline problem, not a judge problem. Averaging hides it and adds nothing to reliability; more queries do not change the ambiguity. Clarifying the boundary is cheap and raises agreement.
