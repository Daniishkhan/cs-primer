Query Understanding

Documents are written carefully; queries are typed in a hurry. They are short, misspelled, ambiguous and different from the vocabulary of the answer. This chapter covers what a search system does to a query before it reaches the index: correcting spelling, expanding with synonyms, working out what the person wants, relaxing when nothing matches, and coping with the long tail of queries nobody has asked before.

Why this matters

The best ranking function cannot rescue a query that does not say what the user meant. Most retrieval failures that users notice start here: "recipt" returns nothing, "car" misses every page about automobiles, a question phrased as a sentence matches the wrong documents because of its small words. Retrieval for language models inherits all of this, because the "query" is often a user's chat message or a question rewritten by another model. After this chapter you will be able to add the cheapest, highest-value fixes in the right order, and to see when a fix does more harm than good.

Queries are not documents

Three properties make queries awkward compared with the documents they search.

  • They are short and underspecified. Few words must stand for a whole need. "Jaguar" could be an animal, a car or a software product, and nothing in the query says which.
  • They contain errors. Typing on a phone, speaking to an assistant and copying from a screenshot all introduce mistakes that a published document would not contain.
  • They use the searcher's words, not the author's. The person who writes "get my money back" and the author of the "Refunds" page do not share a vocabulary. Chapter 0 showed the zero-result failure this causes.

There is also the matter of novelty. Google reported in 2019 that about 15 percent of the searches it sees each day are ones it has never seen before. Whatever your own figure is, a large share of real queries will be unique strings, which means you cannot fix queries one by one; you need methods that generalise. The next section measures this shape.

The long tail of queries

When you count how often each distinct query appears in a log, a few queries are extremely common and a vast number appear once or twice. This shape is described by Zipf's law: the frequency of the r-th most common item is roughly proportional to 1/r. Search people call the three regions the head (the few very frequent queries), the torso (a middle band) and the tail (the great many rare ones). Let us simulate a log from a Zipf distribution. The exponent and sizes are illustrative assumptions, not measurements of any real system, but the qualitative shape is robust.

import random, itertools
from collections import Counter

random.seed(11)
VOCAB_Q = 300_000            # possible distinct queries
S = 1.0                      # Zipf exponent: an illustrative assumption, not a measurement
weights = [1 / (r ** S) for r in range(1, VOCAB_Q + 1)]
cum = list(itertools.accumulate(weights))
log = Counter(random.choices(range(VOCAB_Q), cum_weights=cum, k=1_000_000))

total = sum(log.values()); distinct = len(log)
ranked = sorted(log.values(), reverse=True)
print(f"{total:,} searches, {distinct:,} distinct queries")
for label, lo, hi in [("head: top 100 queries", 0, 100), ("torso: ranks 101-10,000", 100, 10_000), ("tail: everything else", 10_000, distinct)]:
    part = sum(ranked[lo:hi])
    print(f"  {label:<26} {part / total:6.1%} of volume   ({(min(hi, distinct) - lo):,} distinct queries)")
once = sum(1 for c in log.values() if c == 1)
print(f"queries seen exactly once: {once / distinct:.1%} of distinct, {once / total:.1%} of volume")
print(f"a cache holding the top 10,000 answers would hit {sum(ranked[:10_000]) / total:.1%} of searches")
1,000,000 searches, 145,590 distinct queries

  torso: ranks 101-10,000     35.3% of volume   (9,900 distinct queries)
  tail: everything else       25.3% of volume   (135,590 distinct queries)
queries seen exactly once: 53.8% of distinct, 7.8% of volume
a cache holding the top 10,000 answers would hit 74.7% of searches

Two consequences follow. Different parts need different treatment. The head can be handled by hand: a team can review the top hundred queries, curate their best results, and cache the answers. In this simulation a cache of the 10,000 most frequent queries would answer three quarters of the traffic. The tail cannot be handled by hand at all (over half of the distinct queries here occur exactly once), so it depends on general mechanisms: spelling correction, expansion, relaxation and, later, dense retrieval. Averages hide the tail. A metric averaged over traffic is dominated by the head, while one averaged over distinct queries is dominated by the tail. Report both when you evaluate (chapter 4), and when you build a gold set sample from the whole range (chapter 5), not only from the popular queries.

The query pipeline

Between the search box and the index sits a short pipeline. Each step is optional and independently measurable, and the order matters: correct before you expand, otherwise you expand a typo.

Spelling correction

The task: given a query word that is probably wrong, find the intended word. Two ideas combine.

How close is a candidate? Edit distance

The edit distance between two strings is the smallest number of single-character edits that turns one into the other. Levenshtein (1966) counted insertions, deletions and substitutions. Damerau (1964), studying real typing errors, noted that swapping two neighbouring letters ("recieve" for "receive") is common enough to count as one edit; the combined measure is called Damerau-Levenshtein distance. Most misspellings in practice are one or two edits from the intended word.

Which candidate is likely? The noisy-channel view

Many words may be within one or two edits of a typo. "recipt" is one edit from "receipt" and one from "recipe". Which was meant? The noisy-channel model (Kernighan, Church and Gale, 1990; Brill and Moore, 2000) frames it as Bayes' rule: pick the word w that maximises P(w) × P(typed | w), the prior probability of the word times the probability that someone meaning w would have typed what we see. Norvig (2007), in a famous short essay, showed a working corrector in about twenty lines of Python using exactly this idea, with word counts as the prior and "fewer edits are more probable" as the error model. Our version takes the same shape: nearest first, ties broken by frequency.

from collections import Counter

def edit_distance(a, b):
    """Damerau-Levenshtein (optimal string alignment): insert, delete, substitute, swap neighbours."""
    d = [[0] * (len(b) + 1) for _ in range(len(a) + 1)]
    for i in range(len(a) + 1): d[i][0] = i
    for j in range(len(b) + 1): d[0][j] = j
    for i in range(1, len(a) + 1):
        for j in range(1, len(b) + 1):
            cost = a[i - 1] != b[j - 1]
            d[i][j] = min(d[i-1][j] + 1, d[i][j-1] + 1, d[i-1][j-1] + cost)
            if i > 1 and j > 1 and a[i-1] == b[j-2] and a[i-2] == b[j-1]:
                d[i][j] = min(d[i][j], d[i-2][j-2] + 1)
    return d[-1][-1]

# Vocabulary with counts: in a real system, from your own documents and query log.
VOCAB = Counter({"invoice": 900, "invoices": 300, "voice": 40, "notice": 120, "payment": 1500, "payments": 400,
                 "refund": 700, "refunds": 250, "receipt": 330, "recipe": 15, "password": 800, "passport": 200,
                 "from": 5000, "form": 600, "tax": 1200, "text": 150, "late": 450, "plate": 30})

def correct(word, max_dist=2):
    if word in VOCAB: return word, 0, 'known'
    cands = [(edit_distance(word, w), -VOCAB[w], w) for w in VOCAB if abs(len(w) - len(word)) <= max_dist]
    cands = [c for c in cands if c[0] <= max_dist]
    if not cands: return word, None, 'no candidate'
    dist, negc, w = min(cands)          # nearest first; ties go to the most frequent word
    return w, dist, 'corrected'

print("edit_distance('recieve','receive') =", edit_distance("recieve", "receive"), "(one swap)")
for w in ["invoce", "paymnet", "refnud", "pasword", "recipt", "taxx", "zzzz"]:
    print(f"{w:<9} -> {correct(w)}")
print("real words are never touched:", correct("from"), correct("form"))   # even if the user meant the other

The prior is the point. "recipt" resolves to "receipt" (count 330) rather than "recipe" (15), because among equally close candidates, the commoner one is more likely. Where do the counts come from? From your own data. A general English dictionary would happily "correct" product names, drug names, account codes and surnames to common words. A vocabulary built from your documents and your query log knows your domain. Notice also the last line: a word that is itself valid is never corrected, so the typo "form" for "from" slips through. These are called real-word errors, and they need context.

Using context

Look at the neighbouring word. "Refund form" is a common query and "refund from" is not; "email from" is common and "email form" is rare. A bigram count from past queries captures that. The next listing mixes the prior of each candidate word with how often it follows the previous word.

from collections import Counter
# Bigram counts from (pretend) past queries: the context a single-word corrector lacks.
BIGRAMS = Counter({("refund", "form"): 220, ("refund", "from"): 3, ("tax", "form"): 400,
                   ("email", "from"): 310, ("email", "form"): 4, ("late", "payment"): 380})
UNI = Counter({"form": 600, "from": 5000})

def best_in_context(prev, observed, alternatives, lam=0.5):
    """Score each candidate by P(word) mixed with P(word | previous word); keep the observed word unless clearly beaten."""
    def score(w):
        p_uni = UNI[w] / sum(UNI.values())
        p_bi = BIGRAMS[(prev, w)] / (sum(c for (p, _), c in BIGRAMS.items() if p == prev) or 1)
        return lam * p_uni + (1 - lam) * p_bi
    ranked = sorted(alternatives, key=score, reverse=True)
    return ranked[0], {w: round(score(w), 3) for w in alternatives}

for prev, word in [("refund", "from"), ("email", "from"), ("tax", "from")]:
    print(f"{prev} {word!r:<7} ->", best_in_context(prev, word, ["from", "form"]))
refund 'from'  -> ('form', {'from': 0.453, 'form': 0.547})
email 'from'  -> ('from', {'from': 0.94, 'form': 0.06})
tax 'from'  -> ('form', {'from': 0.446, 'form': 0.554})

"refund from" is changed to "form"; "email from" is left alone; "tax from" is changed. Cucerzan and Brill (2004) built the idea into a web-scale corrector that iterates over whole queries and uses query-log statistics. The practical lessons are simple: a corrector is only as good as its data; context beats words in isolation; and the decision to act needs a threshold.

Auto-correct or suggest?

There are three behaviours, in rising order of risk. Suggest ("Did you mean receipt?") leaves the choice to the user and costs a click. Correct and show ("Showing results for receipt; search instead for recipt") acts when confidence is high but allows an undo. Silently correct is acceptable only for very confident cases. Correcting the wrong thing is worse than not correcting: product codes, names and deliberate rare words ("Zoë", a medicine called "Lamictal") look like errors to a naive corrector. Two mitigations are routine: never correct tokens found in your index with a decent document frequency, and fall back to the original query when the corrected one returns fewer results.

Efficiency is a practical concern. Computing the edit distance from the query word to every word in a vocabulary of millions is too slow. Systems generate only candidates within one or two edits (Norvig's trick: enumerate every deletion, swap, replacement and insertion of the word, which for a word of length n is a few hundred strings, and keep those in the vocabulary), or use index structures built for it. Lucene's fuzzy matching, for example, uses automata to find all terms within a given edit distance (at most two) without scanning the dictionary.

Systems connection

Query logs power spelling correction, synonym mining and evaluation, but they are also personal data: people type names, symptoms, account numbers and legal worries into search boxes. Treat logs like any sensitive store: redact, restrict access, set retention limits, and aggregate before analysing. The head of the distribution is naturally cacheable, but a cache keyed on the query text must also key on anything that changes the answer, such as the user's permissions (chapter 12).

Synonyms and expansion

Query expansion adds terms to the query so that documents using different words can match. The words may come from a hand-built synonym list ("car" and "automobile"), from a thesaurus, from mining the query log or, as in chapter 15 later, from a language model. The aim is to attack vocabulary mismatch directly.

Where to apply it is a design choice already met in chapter 1. At index time, each document is stored under the extra terms too, so queries stay simple but changing the list needs a reindex and the index grows. At query time, the query is rewritten, so the list can change daily, at the cost of larger queries. Most systems expand at query time.

Whatever the mechanism, expansion has three kinds of risk:

  • Direction. Some relations go one way. A search for "smartphone" should probably match pages about particular phone models; a search for one specific model should not match every phone. Hypernyms ("animal" for "dog") and hyponyms are not synonyms.
  • Ambiguity. "Java" expands to "coffee" in one sense and to nothing useful in another. Expansion that ignores context injects the wrong sense.
  • Query drift. Each added term moves the query further from what was typed, and the added terms compete with the original ones. The remedy is weights: give expansion terms less weight than the user's own words.

The next listing runs these three cases through the BM25 scorer of chapter 2 (bm25.py-style scoring, with query-term weights added) over nine small documents about heart attacks, Java and cars.

import math, re
from collections import Counter

DOCS = {
 "m1": "Treatment of myocardial infarction in adults: aspirin and urgent angioplasty.",
 "m2": "Heart attack warning signs include chest pain and shortness of breath.",
 "m3": "Myocardial infarction guidelines for emergency departments.",
 "m4": "Seasonal flu vaccines and heart health in older adults.",
 "c1": "Java garbage collection tuning for low latency services.",
 "c2": "Java coffee beans from the highlands, roasted fresh.",
 "c3": "Coffee brewing guide: grind size, water temperature and time.",
 "c4": "Used car buying checklist: inspect the automobile history report.",
 "c5": "Vehicle insurance quotes for a new car.",
}
tok = lambda s: re.findall(r"[a-z]+", s.lower())
tfs = {d: Counter(tok(t)) for d, t in DOCS.items()}
N = len(tfs); avg = sum(sum(c.values()) for c in tfs.values()) / N
df = Counter(t for c in tfs.values() for t in c)

def bm25(weighted_terms, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    out = {}
    for d, c in tfs.items():
        dl = sum(c.values()); s = 0.0
        for t, w in weighted_terms.items():
            if c[t]:
                idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + .5) / (df[t] + .5))
                s += w * idf * c[t] * (k1 + 1) / (c[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * dl / avg))
        if s: out[d] = round(s, 2)
    return sorted(out.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])

SYN = {"heart": ["myocardial"], "attack": ["infarction"], "java": ["coffee"], "car": ["automobile", "vehicle"]}
def expand(q, weight):
    terms = {t: 1.0 for t in tok(q)}
    for t in list(terms):
        for s in SYN.get(t, []): terms.setdefault(s, weight)
    return terms

for q, w in [("heart attack", None), ("heart attack", 1.0), ("heart attack", 0.4),
             ("java latency", None), ("java latency", 1.0), ("car", None), ("car", 0.5)]:
    terms = expand(q, w) if w else {t: 1.0 for t in tok(q)}
    print(f"{q!r:<16} syn_weight={w!s:<5} ->", bm25(terms)[:5])
'heart attack'   syn_weight=None  -> [('m2', 2.94), ('m4', 1.36)]
'heart attack'   syn_weight=1.0   -> [('m3', 3.16), ('m2', 2.94), ('m1', 2.59), ('m4', 1.36)]
'heart attack'   syn_weight=0.4   -> [('m2', 2.94), ('m4', 1.36), ('m3', 1.26), ('m1', 1.04)]
'java latency'   syn_weight=None  -> [('c1', 3.37), ('c2', 1.42)]
'java latency'   syn_weight=1.0   -> [('c1', 3.37), ('c2', 2.85), ('c3', 1.36)]
'car'            syn_weight=None  -> [('c5', 1.5), ('c4', 1.36)]
'car'            syn_weight=0.5   -> [('c5', 2.52), ('c4', 2.29)]

Read the three groups. For "heart attack", the unexpanded query finds only m2 (which uses those exact words) and m4 (the flu vaccine page that mentions "heart"). Expanding at full weight brings in m3 and m1, the two pages that say "myocardial infarction", and m3 even tops the ranking. That is the benefit. Expanding at weight 0.4 also brings them in, but now m4, which only matches "heart", still ranks above both, so in this tiny corpus the weighting was too timid to help the ranking even though it helped recall. The right weight is an empirical question for your data. For "java latency", full-weight expansion pulls in c2 (coffee beans) and c3 (brewing): the query drifted. For "car", expansion helps: c4 ("automobile") rises from 1.36 to 2.29 and nearly ties c5.

Pseudo-relevance feedback

Another source of expansion terms needs no thesaurus. Pseudo-relevance feedback assumes the top few results of the first search are relevant, extracts terms that are unusually frequent in them, and adds those to the query for a second search. It comes from Rocchio's relevance-feedback work in the 1960s and 1970s, and variants such as RM3 are standard baselines in research. It often raises recall and it can wreck a query: if the top results were wrong, the added terms reinforce the wrong sense. It also doubles the retrieval work.

Common misconception

"Auto-correct and synonyms are always improvements." Both trade precision for recall. They help on the queries that were failing and can hurt the ones that were fine: a corrected product code, a doubled-up synonym on a clear query, a drifted ambiguous word. Evaluate them against the whole query set, not just the failures you went looking for, and keep a switch to turn each off.

Intent: what is the person doing?

Broder (2002), in "A taxonomy of web search", split web queries into three kinds. Navigational queries aim at a particular site or page ("acme invoice portal"). Informational queries seek information on a topic ("how long do refunds take"). Transactional queries want to do something ("download invoice template"). The taxonomy has been refined many times, but the idea stands: the same ranking is not right for all intents. A navigational query wants one correct answer at position one; an informational one wants a spread of good pages.

Inside a company the types look different but the principle is the same. A box that accepts "INV-20931" (an identifier lookup), "late payment fee" (keywords), a quoted phrase, and "how long do refunds take" (a question) should not treat them identically. An identifier should match exactly and skip stemming and spelling correction; a quoted phrase should use positions; a question can drop its question words and often benefits from semantic retrieval. Intent detection can be rules, as below, or a small classifier trained on labelled queries, or a language model prompt.

When nothing matches: relaxation

By default most engines treat a multi-word query as AND for precision, or OR for recall. AND is crisp and often returns nothing for long queries; OR returns too much. A good compromise is a threshold: require at least m of the n terms (Elasticsearch calls this minimum_should_match). Query relaxation automates the decision: try the strict query, and if it returns too few results, retry with a looser one. The listing combines relaxation with a rule-based intent router. It reuses the BM25 scorer from chapter 2.

import re
from bm25 import docs, df, tok, bm25_term   # the scorer from chapter 2

def search_msm(query, msm):
    """Rank docs that contain at least `msm` of the query terms."""
    terms = [t for t in dict.fromkeys(tok(query)) if df[t]]      # unknown words cannot match anything
    out = []
    for d in docs:
        hit = [t for t in terms if docs[d][t]]
        if len(hit) >= msm:
            out.append((round(sum(bm25_term(t, d) for t in hit), 2), d, len(hit)))
    return sorted(out, reverse=True)

def search_relaxing(query):
    words = list(dict.fromkeys(tok(query)))
    unknown = [t for t in words if not df[t]]
    n = len(words) - len(unknown)
    for msm in range(n, 0, -1):
        r = search_msm(query, msm)
        if r: return {"need": f"{msm} of {len(words)}", "not in index": unknown, "top": r[:2]}
    return {"need": "none matched", "not in index": unknown, "top": []}

for q in ["late payment fee", "late payment penalty invoice", "refund tax invoice template", "zzz qqq"]:
    print(f"{q!r}\n    {search_relaxing(q)}")

def classify(q):
    q = q.strip().lower()
    if re.fullmatch(r"[a-z]{1,3}-?\d{3,}", q) or re.fullmatch(r"inv-\d+", q): return "lookup (exact id)"
    if q.startswith(('"', "'")): return "phrase"
    if re.match(r"(how|what|why|when|can|does|do|is)\b", q): return "question"
    if re.search(r"\b(buy|price|cheap|order|download)\b", q): return "transactional"
    return "keywords"
print()
for q in ["INV-20931", '"late payment fee"', "how long do refunds take", "download invoice template", "tax invoice"]:
    print(f"{q!r:<30} -> {classify(q)}")

'refund tax invoice template'

"late payment penalty invoice" contains a word that is not in the index at all ("penalty"), so no document can contain all four terms; relaxation drops to three of four and finds d1. "refund tax invoice template" has no document with all four terms either, but d6 matches three of them and is returned. "zzz qqq" matches nothing, and the system says so rather than returning something: an honest empty result is an important output, and a retrieval-augmented system should pass it to the generator as "nothing found" instead of padding the context with whatever scored highest. Relaxation also shows its risk: after relaxing, a result matching three of four terms may be missing the one word that mattered most. Show the user what was relaxed, or order the dropping by term importance (lowest idf first).

Analogy: the librarian's reference interview

A good reference librarian does not hand over whatever matches the literal words. They hear "something about money back", ask what kind of purchase, notice you have the wrong spelling of an author, think of the other word for what you want, and, if the shelf is empty, suggest the nearest shelf. Each of those is a stage in the pipeline above: classification, spelling, expansion, relaxation.

Where it breaks down: the librarian can ask you a question and wait for the reply. Most search boxes cannot, so each guess must be made silently from the text alone, and a wrong guess costs the user a failed search rather than a short conversation. Conversational and agentic systems (chapter 15) bring the clarifying question back.

Measuring query understanding

Each stage should have its own measurement so you know which one helped:

  • Zero-result rate and low-result rate, before and after each stage, on a sample of real queries. These are cheap and do not need judgements.
  • Correction precision: of the queries the corrector changed, what fraction did a person agree with? Sample a few hundred and label them.
  • Reformulation rate: how often a user immediately retypes a query, a sign that the first answer failed.
  • Ranking metrics on a judged set that deliberately includes head, torso and tail queries (chapter 4, chapter 5), compared with and without each stage.

State of play, October 2026

Language models now do much of the work that rules and thesauri did: rewriting a query into a cleaner one, generating several paraphrases, or turning a conversation turn into a standalone search (chapter 15). The dense retrievers of Part 3 also absorb part of the spelling and synonym problem, because related words land near each other in the embedding space. These help, but they add latency and cost per query and can introduce their own errors (a rewrite that changes the meaning), so the measurements above still apply. For the effect of a neural language model on web search queries, see the case study BERT Comes to Web Search. The 15 percent figure for never-seen queries is Google's statement from its October 2019 announcement about BERT in search; your own rate will differ.

Key takeaways

  • Queries are short, misspelled and phrased in the searcher's words; a large share are unique strings, so fixes must generalise.
  • Query frequency follows a Zipf-like long tail: curate and cache the head, build general mechanisms for the tail, and evaluate on both.
  • Spelling correction combines edit distance (candidates) with a prior from your own vocabulary (choice) and, ideally, neighbouring words; never correct valid terms with real document frequency, and prefer suggestions when unsure.
  • Expansion attacks vocabulary mismatch but risks direction, ambiguity and drift; weight the added terms and measure.
  • Intent changes what a good result is: route identifiers, phrases, keywords and questions differently.
  • Relax strict queries when nothing matches, say what was relaxed, and report honestly when there is nothing relevant.
  • Measure each stage on real queries: zero-result rate, correction precision, ranking metrics by head, torso and tail.

Check your understanding

The typo "recipt" is one edit from both "receipt" (count 330) and "recipe" (count 15) in your vocabulary. What should a noisy-channel corrector do, and why?

  1. Return "recipe", because shorter words are likelier
  2. Refuse to correct, because the candidates tie on distance
  3. Return "receipt": with equal edit distance, the candidate with the higher prior probability is the more likely intended word
  4. Return both and let BM25 decide
The model multiplies a prior (how common the word is) by an error likelihood (how plausible the typo is). Equal edit distances give equal error likelihoods, so the prior decides. Returning both is a valid product choice but is not what the noisy-channel decision rule does; length plays no part.

Users of an HR portal frequently search "form" when they mean "from", in queries such as "emails form payroll". A single-word dictionary corrector does nothing. What addition helps most?

  1. Context: counts of word pairs from past queries, so "emails from" beats "emails form"
  2. A larger edit-distance limit
  3. Stemming at index time
  4. A longer stop-word list
Both strings are valid words, so no edit-distance method will flag them; only the neighbouring word makes one more probable. A larger edit distance would add more wrong candidates, and stemming and stop lists address different problems.

Your team reviews the 200 most frequent queries every month and tunes them by hand. Quality on those queries is excellent, yet overall user satisfaction barely moves. Which explanation is most plausible given a Zipf-like log?

  1. Hand-tuning always makes other queries worse
  2. The head queries are cached, so tuning is ignored
  3. Zipf's law does not apply to enterprise search
  4. The head covers only a fraction of traffic (in the simulation, 100 queries gave 39 percent), while the large tail is untouched by manual tuning
The tail is where many searches live and where general mechanisms (spelling, expansion, relaxation, dense retrieval) are needed. The head is easy to curate but bounded in what it can improve. The figures come from a simulation under an assumed exponent, so check the shape on your own log.

After adding a synonym list, "java memory leak" starts returning coffee-farming pages. What is the best fix?

  1. Delete the entire synonym list
  2. Give expansion terms lower weight than the user's words and make ambiguous synonyms conditional on context (such as the other query terms or the field)
  3. Expand at index time instead of query time
  4. Increase the BM25 parameter k1
This is query drift from an ambiguous entry. Weights and context conditions keep the useful expansions and limit the harm. Moving expansion to index time does not remove the ambiguity, and k1 has no relation to the problem; deleting everything throws away the benefit on queries like "car".

A query returns zero results under AND. Which is the most defensible next step in a customer-facing search?

  1. Silently switch to OR and show the top ten with no comment
  2. Show an empty page and nothing else
  3. Retry requiring fewer terms (and spelling suggestions), and tell the user which words were dropped or corrected; if still nothing relevant, say so
  4. Return the most popular documents on the site
Relaxation recovers many failures, but silently changing the meaning hides risk, and popular but unrelated results mislead. Showing what was relaxed keeps the user in control, and an honest empty result is better than a confident wrong one.

You add a spelling corrector and zero-result rate drops from 8 percent to 3 percent. What should you check before celebrating?

  1. Whether the corrector is written in the same language as the index
  2. Nothing: fewer empty pages is the goal
  3. Whether the stop list changed
  4. Whether the corrected queries were what people meant: sample the changed queries and label them, and check that queries that already worked did not get worse
Zero-result rate only measures one failure. A corrector that rewrites everything into something that matches will lower it while lowering quality. Correction precision and ranking metrics on the whole query set show whether the change helped.
