Public Benchmarks & Their Limits

BEIR, MTEB and a growing family of domain benchmarks let you compare retrievers and embedding models without labelling anything yourself. They are good at one job: deciding which few candidates deserve your time. This chapter explains what they measure, shows with the benchmark papers' own numbers where averages mislead, and ends with a procedure for turning a public leaderboard into a shortlist and your own gold set into a decision.

Why this matters

The first question when choosing a retriever or an embedding model is "which one is best?", and the quickest answer is a leaderboard. Leaderboards are useful and also a reliable source of bad decisions: the model at the top of a general average can lose to a cheaper one on your documents, and a model strong on a benchmark may have trained on it. After this chapter you can read a benchmark paper for what it actually measured, spot the usual ways its numbers mislead, and use it for what it is good at without letting it make the final call. It builds on chapter 4 (the metrics) and chapter 5 (the collection that makes the final call).

What a benchmark is for

A benchmark is a test collection that many people agree to use, so that results from different papers and products can be compared. Retrieval benchmarks follow the recipe of chapter 5: a corpus, queries, judgements and a metric, almost always nDCG@10. Their value is that someone else paid for the judgements and everyone runs the same test.

Their limit is built in. A benchmark samples some corpora, some query styles and some definitions of relevant, and your system faces a different sample. It is a measure of a model's behaviour on those tasks. Whether that predicts behaviour on your tasks is an empirical question that the benchmark cannot answer by itself. The right role is therefore a shortlist filter, not a decision: it can rule out models that are clearly weak, flag the ones worth testing, and suggest where a model might fail. The decision comes from your own collection.

Analogy: the fuel-economy figure

A car's published fuel economy comes from a standard test cycle. It lets you compare two cars fairly and rules out a thirsty one. It does not tell you what you will get on your commute, with your load, in your weather. If your drive is mostly short city trips, a car with a slightly worse figure may suit you better.

Where it breaks down: a car's test cycle is a fixed, public procedure that manufacturers tune for, but the car itself cannot see the test. A machine-learning model can be trained on data that overlaps the benchmark, which is a different kind of problem and a harder one to detect.

BEIR: retrieval without training on the target

Before BEIR, neural retrievers were mostly evaluated on the dataset they were trained on (usually MS MARCO). Thakur, Reimers, Rücklé, Srivastava and Gurevych (2021, "BEIR: A Heterogeneous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models") asked a different question: how well does a retriever do on data it has not been trained on? Such zero-shot evaluation is closer to what you face when you deploy a general model on your own documents. BEIR collects 18 English datasets from 9 retrieval tasks, including question answering (NQ, HotpotQA, FiQA), fact checking (FEVER, SciFact), duplicate-question retrieval (Quora, CQADupStack), argument retrieval (ArguAna, Touché-2020) and biomedical, news and scientific-citation retrieval (TREC-COVID, BioASQ, NFCorpus, TREC-NEWS, SCIDOCS). The metric is nDCG@10. The paper evaluated ten systems from five families: lexical (BM25), sparse neural, dense, late interaction and cross-encoder reranking.

Its best-known finding is that BM25 is a "robust baseline", in the authors' words. Models trained on MS MARCO, where BM25 scored 0.228, beat it comfortably in-domain, yet many fell behind it on other datasets. The listing below uses the paper's Table 2 (copied into beir_data.py with the paper's rounded values) to recompute the zero-shot averages and count wins against BM25.

from beir_data import MODELS, TABLE, MS_MARCO

n = len(TABLE)
avg = {m: sum(row[i] for row in TABLE.values()) / n for i, m in enumerate(MODELS)}
print(f"zero-shot average nDCG@10 over {n} datasets, best to worst")
for m in sorted(avg, key=avg.get, reverse=True):
    wins = sum(row[MODELS.index(m)] > row[0] for row in TABLE.values())
    print(f"  {m:<11} {avg[m]:.3f}   beats BM25 on {wins:>2} of {n} datasets   (MS MARCO, in-domain: {MS_MARCO[MODELS.index(m)]:.3f})")

print("\nBest model per dataset (the average hides how often the leader changes):")
leaders = {}
for ds, row in TABLE.items():
    best = MODELS[row.index(max(row))]
    leaders.setdefault(best, []).append(ds)
for m, ds in sorted(leaders.items(), key=lambda kv: -len(kv[1])):
    print(f"  {m:<11} {len(ds):>2}: {', '.join(ds)}")

print("\nDense retrievers against BM25, relative change on selected datasets (ANCE / TAS-B):")
for ds in ("MS MARCO", "Quora", "ArguAna", "FiQA-2018", "BioASQ", "Touche-2020", "SciFact"):
    row = TABLE[ds] if ds in TABLE else MS_MARCO
    b = row[0]
    print(f"  {ds:<12} BM25 {b:.3f}  ANCE {100*(row[5]-b)/b:+5.0f}%  TAS-B {100*(row[6]-b)/b:+5.0f}%")

Read the output as the paper did. The reranker pipeline (BM25 followed by a cross-encoder, chapter 8) leads the average (0.476) and beats BM25 on 16 of 18 datasets. The dense retrievers ANCE, TAS-B and GenQ have averages below BM25's 0.423, even though they score 70% to 79% better on MS MARCO, the dataset they learned from. The per-dataset view shows why one number is not enough: ANCE is 36% better than BM25 on NQ and 32% on ArguAna, and 34% worse on BioASQ and 35% worse on Touché-2020.

Two further BEIR lessons carry into this chapter. First, the paper states that in-domain performance is "not a good indicator" of out-of-domain generalisation, which is the reason to be wary of any benchmark whose training split resembles its test split. Second, Thakur et al. report that many datasets show a lexical bias because their judgements were collected from systems that rely on term matching; the TREC-COVID study in the next section quantifies it.

MTEB and MMTEB: embeddings across many tasks

BEIR evaluates retrieval. Embedding models are used for more than retrieval, so Muennighoff, Tazi, Magne and Reimers (2022, "MTEB: Massive Text Embedding Benchmark") built a benchmark for the whole range. The abstract reports 8 embedding tasks (bitext mining, classification, clustering, pair classification, reranking, retrieval, semantic textual similarity and summarisation), 58 datasets covering 112 languages, and 33 models tested. Its headline finding is that no single text embedding method performed best on all tasks. For retrieval MTEB reuses BEIR datasets and evaluation, with nDCG@10 as the main metric, so the retrieval column of MTEB is partly the same exam as BEIR.

Two design choices matter when you read an MTEB result.

  • The overall score is an average across task categories. A model can rank high overall because of strength in classification or semantic similarity, which you may not need, and be mediocre at retrieval. For a retrieval system, read the retrieval column, and within it the datasets nearest your own.
  • Contamination was set aside by design. The MTEB authors wrote that controlling for overlap between model training data and benchmark datasets is hard at their scale, so they ignored it in the scores, believing the effect insignificant "as long as enough datasets are averaged". They also noted one clear case: some models did unusually well on one reranking dataset, likely because part of it was in their training data. Since 2022 the benchmark has become a target that models are trained against. The public leaderboard has tried to show which scores are zero-shot, meaning the model's authors state that its training data did not include the benchmark's tasks (check its current documentation; see the dated box below).

MMTEB (Enevoldsen et al., 2025, "MMTEB: Massive Multilingual Text Embedding Benchmark") extends the approach to over 500 quality-controlled tasks in more than 250 languages. Its authors report that billion-parameter language-model-based embedders reached state-of-the-art results on some language subsets and task categories, while the best publicly available model overall was multilingual-e5-large-instruct, with only 560 million parameters, and they propose a correlation-based downsampling that keeps the ranking of models while cutting the evaluation cost. The takeaway for a practitioner is that big benchmarks have become expensive to run, and cheaper subsets exist; check that a subset's ranking has been validated before relying on it.

State of play, October 2026

Facts here are dated and will change. The BEIR paper is from 2021 and its datasets are now widely used in training mixtures or as development sets, so a high BEIR score no longer demonstrates zero-shot generalisation unless the model's authors document that BEIR train splits and queries were excluded. MTEB's original paper (2022) has been followed by MMTEB (arXiv, February 2025) and by changes to the public leaderboard: its layout, task sets and the way "zero-shot" is flagged have evolved, and many scores shown are submitted by model authors, not re-run by the maintainers. I could not verify the current leaderboard ranking in October 2026 and do not give one. Before relying on any figure, open the live leaderboard, note its date and version, find out which scores are self-reported, and look for the model's training data statement.

Domain and specialised benchmarks

General benchmarks are broad on purpose. If your documents are finance filings, source code, legal contracts or clinical notes, look for a benchmark in that domain, and treat its existence as evidence about how much general results transfer. Two examples with checkable findings:

  • FinMTEB (Tang and Yang, 2025, "FinMTEB: Finance Massive Text Embedding Benchmark") has 64 finance-specific datasets over 7 tasks in English and Chinese, from news to annual reports and regulatory filings. Its authors report that performance on general benchmarks correlates only weakly with performance on financial tasks, that domain-adapted models consistently beat general-purpose ones, and, surprisingly, that a bag-of-words baseline beat dense embeddings on financial semantic textual similarity. Those are the authors' claims about their benchmark; they support running a domain test, not a specific model choice.
  • BRIGHT (Su et al., 2024, "BRIGHT: A Realistic and Challenging Benchmark for Reasoning-Intensive Retrieval") has 1,384 real-world queries in areas such as economics, psychology, mathematics and coding, where relevance needs reasoning, not matching words or topics. The authors report that the then top model on the MTEB leaderboard scored 59.0 nDCG@10 on MTEB and only 18.3 on BRIGHT. A good score on one benchmark says little about another task type.

Domain benchmarks reduce one gap and leave others. They still use someone else's queries, someone else's definition of relevant, and a corpus that is not yours.

How benchmark numbers mislead

The average hides who wins where

An average treats all datasets as equally important to you, which is almost never true. The listing below takes the BEIR table, splits it into two groups of six datasets (web-and-Wikipedia-style versus science-and-finance), and ranks seven retrievers within each group. It then resamples the 18 datasets with replacement 2,000 times and asks which system comes first on the average each time. Note that the choice of the two groups is mine; the point is only how much the ranking depends on which datasets you weight.

import random
from beir_data import MODELS, TABLE

# Compare four embedding-style systems only (drop the reranker, which is not a first-stage retriever)
use = ["BM25", "DPR", "ANCE", "TAS-B", "GenQ", "ColBERT", "docT5query"]
idx = [MODELS.index(m) for m in use]

def mean_scores(datasets):
    return {m: sum(TABLE[d][i] for d in datasets) / len(datasets) for m, i in zip(use, idx)}

def order(scores):
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)

def spearman(a, b):
    ra = {m: r for r, m in enumerate(a)}; rb = {m: r for r, m in enumerate(b)}
    n = len(a); d2 = sum((ra[m] - rb[m]) ** 2 for m in a)
    return 1 - 6 * d2 / (n * (n * n - 1))

web = ["NQ", "HotpotQA", "DBPedia", "FEVER", "Climate-FEVER", "Quora"]
science = ["TREC-COVID", "BioASQ", "NFCorpus", "SciFact", "SCIDOCS", "FiQA-2018"]
print("ranking on web-and-Wikipedia-style datasets :", " > ".join(order(mean_scores(web))))
print("ranking on science-and-finance datasets     :", " > ".join(order(mean_scores(science))))
print(f"Spearman correlation between the two rankings: {spearman(order(mean_scores(web)), order(mean_scores(science))):+.2f}")

rng = random.Random(0)
alld = list(TABLE)
wins = {m: 0 for m in use}
for _ in range(2000):
    sample = [rng.choice(alld) for _ in alld]            # resample the 18 datasets with replacement
    wins[order(mean_scores(sample))[0]] += 1
print("\nwho ranks first by average nDCG@10 when the 18 datasets are resampled (2,000 draws):")
for m, c in sorted(wins.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    if c: print(f"  {m:<11} {c/20:5.1f}% of draws")

The two rankings differ in their top places (ColBERT leads on web-style datasets, docT5query on science and finance) and agree only moderately (Spearman correlation +0.64 on seven systems; 1.0 would be identical). The bootstrap is sobering: by re-drawing the dataset sample, the first place by average goes to docT5query in 48% of draws and to ColBERT in 39%, with three other systems taking the remaining 13%. The benchmark contains too few datasets to give a stable "winner" among close contenders. Taking the average at face value to pick one of the top systems is choosing on noise. (A small caution: this uses the paper's rounded published scores and ignores how uncertain each per-dataset score is, so the real instability is larger.)

Incomplete judgements favour the systems that built them

Chapter 5 described pooling and its holes. BEIR's TREC-COVID study is the cleanest public evidence of how large the effect can be, and the listing reproduces the paper's Table 4: the share of each system's top 10 that no assessor had judged (Hole@10), and the nDCG@10 before and after the authors judged the 980 missing pairs.

# Table 4 of Thakur et al. (2021): TREC-COVID, nDCG@10 before and after judging the
# top-10 results that no assessor had seen (980 query-document pairs in total).
rows = {  # model: (hole@10 %, original, annotated)
    "BM25": (6.4, 0.656, 0.668), "docT5query": (2.8, 0.713, 0.714), "ColBERT": (12.4, 0.677, 0.735),
    "ANCE": (14.4, 0.654, 0.735), "TAS-B": (31.8, 0.481, 0.555), "DPR": (30.6, 0.332, 0.445),
    "BM25+CE": (1.6, 0.757, 0.760),
}
def ranks(idx):
    order = sorted(rows, key=lambda m: -rows[m][idx]); return {m: order.index(m) + 1 for m in rows}
before, after = ranks(1), ranks(2)
print(f"{'model':<11}{'hole@10':>8}{'before':>8}{'after':>8}{'gain':>8}{'rank':>8}")
for m in sorted(rows, key=lambda m: rows[m][0]):
    h, a, b = rows[m]
    print(f"{m:<11}{h:>7.1f}%{a:>8.3f}{b:>8.3f}{b - a:>+8.3f}{before[m]:>5} -> {after[m]}")

On the original judgements BM25 (0.656) edges ANCE (0.654); on the completed ones ANCE reaches 0.735, ahead of BM25's 0.668. ColBERT and ANCE finish tied at 0.735 (the listing breaks the tie by order of appearance). A public benchmark conclusion of "dense does not beat BM25 here" would have been an artefact of who contributed to the pool. That caution applies to every benchmark built by pooling, including the retrieval parts of MTEB that inherit BEIR's judgements.

Common misconception

"The model at the top of the leaderboard is the best model for my system." The leaderboard ranks models on an average over tasks that are not yours, with a few points of margin that are often within the noise of the benchmark itself, and possibly with training overlap. Another common error: "if it beats BM25 on a public benchmark it will beat BM25 on mine". The BEIR numbers above show dense retrievers losing to BM25 on 14 of 18 datasets zero-shot (for ANCE), and FinMTEB's authors report a bag-of-words baseline beating dense embeddings on one finance task. Measure on your own queries.

From leaderboard to decision

The workable procedure has four filters, from cheap and coarse to expensive and exact. Each stage removes candidates, so the expensive stage sees only a few.

  1. Write down the constraints. Languages and scripts, maximum document length in tokens, licence terms (can you use it commercially), vector size (storage and speed, see chapter 11), whether it can run in your environment, and a latency budget.
  2. Pick the benchmark slices that resemble your task. Retrieval column, not the overall average; datasets with similar query and document shape and language; a domain benchmark if one exists.
  3. Shortlist three to five, including a baseline. Always include BM25 and last year's choice; if a new model cannot beat them on your data, it is not an improvement.
  4. Evaluate the shortlist on your gold set with the metrics of chapter 4 and intervals from chapter 5, sliced by query type. Check how the private ranking compares with the public one; a large disagreement is information about the benchmark's relevance to you.
  5. Decide with cost in view. When two candidates are within the interval, choose the cheaper, faster or simpler.

Systems connection

Choosing a model from a leaderboard is like choosing a database from a vendor's benchmark page. A vendor's result tells you the system can be fast on some workload; only a test with your data shape, concurrency and failure conditions tells you whether it will be fast for you. Reliability chapter 10 makes the same argument for load testing, and Evals chapter 9 gives a general list of questions to ask of any leaderboard number; Evals chapter 7 covers how benchmarks leak into training data and decay.

Key takeaways

  • A public benchmark is a shared test collection. Use it to shortlist candidates, then decide on your own queries and judgements.
  • BEIR (18 English datasets, 9 tasks, nDCG@10, zero-shot) showed that BM25 is a robust baseline and that in-domain scores do not predict out-of-domain scores: ANCE beat BM25 on 4 of 18 datasets.
  • MTEB spans 8 task types, 58 datasets and 112 languages; its retrieval part reuses BEIR. No single method wins everywhere; read the retrieval column, not the overall average.
  • Averages over a few datasets are unstable among close contenders: resampling BEIR's datasets changed the first-place system in about half of draws.
  • Pooled judgements favour systems like those that built the pool: dense retrievers gained up to 0.11 nDCG@10 on TREC-COVID once unjudged results were judged.
  • Training overlap, query and document shape, language, near-ties, prompts and self-reported scores all separate a leaderboard from your result.
  • Filter by hard constraints, then benchmark slices, then your gold set with intervals, then cost.

Check your understanding

A model ranks first on the overall MTEB average but only fifteenth on the retrieval tasks. You are building a retrieval system. What should you conclude?

  1. Use it: the overall average is the most reliable summary
  2. Look at the retrieval column and the datasets nearest your domain; the overall average blends task types you do not need
  3. Ignore MTEB completely
  4. Retrain the model on MTEB's retrieval datasets
The overall score averages eight different task types; strength in classification or similarity can lift it. The retrieval slice is the relevant evidence, and it should then be tested on your data. Ignoring the benchmark wastes a useful filter, and training on its datasets is the contamination problem.

On BEIR, a dense retriever trained on MS MARCO scores far above BM25 on MS MARCO but below it on many other datasets. Which lesson do the authors draw?

  1. Dense retrieval is inherently worse than BM25
  2. MS MARCO is a bad dataset
  3. BM25 should always be used alone
  4. In-domain performance is not a good indicator of out-of-domain generalisation, so evaluation must cover varied data
The paper reports this finding directly. It does not show that dense retrieval is inherently worse (late-interaction and reranking models did well, and dense models won on several datasets); it shows that strength on the training distribution does not transfer automatically.

A dataset's judgements were pooled from lexical systems only. A new dense model scores lower than BM25 on it. What is the most careful reading?

  1. The comparison may be biased against the dense model, whose relevant results are less likely to have been judged; check Hole@10 and judge the unseen results
  2. The dense model is definitely worse
  3. The metric is wrong for dense models
  4. BM25 has memorised the dataset
BEIR's TREC-COVID study found Hole@10 of 6.4% for BM25 against 14.4% for ANCE and 31.8% for TAS-B, and ANCE moved from 0.654 to 0.735 once the holes were judged. Definitely worse is too strong, and BM25 does not memorise anything; the bias is in the judgements.

Resampling BEIR's 18 datasets with replacement and averaging, the first-place system among seven close contenders changes in roughly half of the draws. What does this imply?

  1. The benchmark's scores are fabricated
  2. The system with most wins is always best
  3. A small set of datasets cannot give a stable winner among close systems, so averages should not be used to pick between near-equals
  4. Bootstrapping is invalid for benchmarks
The first place depends on which datasets are drawn, and 18 datasets are few. This calls for choosing by performance on data like yours. The numbers are real (from the paper), and the bootstrap is a reasonable way to see sensitivity to dataset choice.

The MTEB authors chose to ignore overlap between model training data and benchmark datasets. Which statement describes their reasoning and its present-day weakness?

  1. They proved there was no overlap
  2. They judged it hard to control at their scale and thought the effect insignificant when many datasets are averaged; once models are trained to do well on the benchmark, that assumption weakens
  3. They removed all overlapping models
  4. They used only private datasets
The paper says controlling for contamination is challenging and that effects should be small if enough datasets are averaged. That was reasonable in 2022. After the benchmark became a public target, deliberate or accidental overlap can affect a model's position, which is why training-data statements and zero-shot flags matter.

You have shortlisted three embedders from public benchmarks and evaluated them on your own 120-query gold set. Their nDCG@10 differ by less than the 95% interval. Which is the best next step?

  1. Pick the one that is top of the public leaderboard
  2. Average the three
  3. Add random queries until one wins
  4. Treat them as tied on quality and choose on cost, latency, vector size and operational simplicity, or collect more queries if the gap matters
When differences lie within the interval, the evidence does not separate them, so a public leaderboard rank (which is itself noisy) should not break the tie. Adding queries until a winner appears is a form of peeking; decide in advance how many queries you need (chapter 5).
