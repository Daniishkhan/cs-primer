Design a Document-Processing Pipeline

Design a pipeline that turns three million scanned and digital invoices and forms a month into structured records a finance system can trust. The core problems are routing each page to the cheapest engine that can read it, catching errors with checks a model cannot fake, and spending human attention only where it changes the outcome.

How a design studio works

📐 The brief

A finance director says: "We receive about three million supplier invoices and customer forms a month, by email, file transfer and paper that is scanned by a bureau. Today a team of people types them into our ERP. We want the machine to read them, and people to look only at what the machine is unsure about. A wrong amount or a wrong bank account is a real loss, so I need to know the error rate, not a promise."

  • Some documents are clean PDFs from accounting software. Others are skewed photocopies, faxes, handwritten delivery notes, and forms in several languages.
  • Month-end is four times busier than an ordinary day. Payment terms mean late processing costs money.
  • Records go into a system that pays people. Duplicates and wrong bank details are fraud vectors as well as mistakes.
  • Builds on PDFs and layout, OCR and tables, forms and key-value extraction, with the pipeline you built in lab 6.
  • You have 60 to 75 minutes. Work through the stages in order.

2Success metrics and evaluation plan

How do you measure "accurate" for a field? What is the unit, what do you compare against, and how do you build the gold set? What does exact string match hide?

Compare with a worked answer

Accuracy needs a definition per field type before it can be a number. A character error rate is the right measure for the OCR stage (OCR); the business cares about field accuracy after normalisation: is the extracted total the same amount as the true total? The script scores twelve extractions against gold values both ways.

"""Scoring extraction against a gold set: compare normalised values, not raw strings, and count errors by type."""
import re
from decimal import Decimal, InvalidOperation
from datetime import datetime

def n_amount(s):
    s = re.sub(r"[^\d,.\-]", "", s)
    if "," in s and "." in s:
        s = s.replace(",", "") if s.rfind(".") > s.rfind(",") else s.replace(".", "").replace(",", ".")
    elif "," in s:
        s = s.replace(",", ".") if len(s.split(",")[-1]) == 2 else s.replace(",", "")
    try: return Decimal(s)
    except InvalidOperation: return None

  • Exact match says 1 of 12; normalised says 7 of 12. "1.234,50" and "1,234.50" are the same amount; "30/09/2026" and "2026-09-30" are the same date. A metric that punishes formatting differences measures the wrong thing and sends engineers off fixing what finance does not care about. The normalisers must be written once, tested, and shared by the evaluator and the production validators.
  • Normalisation choices are decisions. The accent-folded vendor name ("Muller" for "Müller") fails above because accents were not folded; whether that counts as an error depends on how the downstream system matches vendors. Decide per field and write it in the labelling guide. The date "09/30/2026" is rejected because no day-first format fits it, which is the correct outcome for an ambiguous or impossible date.
  • The errors that matter are small. "78060" for "780.60" is a hundredfold error from one lost character, "Acne" for "Acme" is one letter, and "1O42" for "1042" is a letter O for a zero. All are tiny in character error rate and large in consequence. Report accuracy for the critical fields separately, and report silent errors (wrong and not flagged) separately from caught ones. Silent errors are the number the director asked for.
  • Build the gold set from the real stream. 2,000 documents drawn at random across types, sources, languages and scan quality (building a test collection; the same discipline applies), labelled twice by experienced clerks and adjudicated, with the page image, the true value and a reason code for disagreements. Add 200 deliberately hard documents (faxes, handwriting, rotated pages, stamps over text) and report them as a separate slice, never mixed into the headline figure.
  • Confidence intervals, always. Field accuracy of 99.5% measured on 2,000 documents with six critical fields each is 12,000 fields, but the fields of one document are correlated (same scan, same vendor template), so the effective sample is smaller; use a bootstrap that resamples documents, not fields (metrics and the interval approach in lab 2).
  • Slices decide. Report by document type, source (email versus bureau scans), language, vendor (top 50 vendors cover most volume and each has a layout), and page quality bucket. A system that is 99.8% on average and 90% on the biggest vendor's faxes is a system with one problem, found by slicing.
  • The end-to-end metric is cost-weighted: reviewer minutes per 1,000 documents at the required final accuracy (stage 8), because accuracy can always be bought with more human review.

  • Machine cost: under a cent per document. Seven million pages a month through a mix of text-layer extraction (cheap), OCR plus layout analysis (middling) and a vision-language model (expensive, used for about one scanned page in eight) cost about $27,000. The prices are assumptions for the shape; a real quote or your own GPU cost per page replaces them (cost, latency and quality).
  • Human review costs about eight times the machine. At 28% of documents needing a person and about 31 seconds each, review needs about 55 full-time people and about $200,000 a month. This is why the next stages obsess over what reaches a person. One percentage point of review rate is worth about $7,000 a month; a one-point reduction in machine cost is a rounding error by comparison.
  • The VLM re-read is cheap in aggregate if rare. At $0.02 a page it adds about $10,000 a month when 12% of scanned pages use it. If routing were lazy and sent every scanned page to it, the machine bill would roughly quadruple and latency would rise too, so triage is a cost lever as well as an accuracy one.
  • Compute is modest: about 6 worker-seconds per document means roughly 95 busy slots at the peak day, 133 with headroom. The shape is bursty, so use a queue with autoscaling and a deadline-aware priority (invoices near their payment date first; overload and load shedding). Month-end needs a plan, not just a bigger number (queues and backpressure).

  • Schema per document type, validated at the boundary.   • Idempotency end to end. The same document arrives twice (a vendor resends, email retries). The document id is the hash of the bytes; the record key is business content. Processing the same input twice must produce one record (logs and exactly-once).
  • Versioned everything. Engine version, model version, normaliser version and validator version on each field. When an OCR engine is upgraded you can compare old and new on the stored images and know which records to re-check.

5High-level architecture

Draw the pipeline. Where is the decision that most affects cost? Where is the last chance to stop a wrong record?

Compare with a worked answer

Life of a document:

  1. Intake stores the original bytes, computes the hash, drops duplicates and enqueues a job with a deadline.
  2. Triage classifies the document type and checks each page: is there a text layer, is it complete (not a scanned image with a hidden bad OCR layer), the language, rotation and skew, resolution and blur. It records a quality score.
  3. Each page goes to the cheapest engine that can read it: the text layer with layout analysis for born-digital pages, an OCR engine with layout analysis for scans, and a vision-language model for pages whose OCR confidence or quality score is poor (stage 6).
  4. Extraction produces key-value pairs and tables into the type's schema, keeping text, box and confidence for each value.
  5. Validation applies rules the model cannot fake: sums, checksums, formats, ranges, vendor master and duplicate checks (stage 7).
  6. The router compares each critical field's confidence and validator results with thresholds (stage 8). Clean records are accepted; others show a clerk only the flagged fields, with the page region highlighted.
  7. Corrections and a random sample of accepted records feed the audit and gold set (stage 9).

The design principle: use the model for reading, and use deterministic code for judging. Models are good at turning pixels into candidate values; arithmetic and checksums are better at deciding whether a candidate is plausible.

6Deep dive 1: choosing how to read each page

Compare a text layer, a classical OCR engine and a vision-language model for these pages. What does each get wrong? How do you route, and how do you stop a model from writing text that is not on the page?

  • Route on evidence. Triage rules: a page whose text layer decodes to normal words in the right language goes to the text layer; an image page goes to OCR; a page whose OCR mean confidence is below a threshold, whose layout analysis found tables the OCR text garbled, or whose critical fields failed validation goes to the VLM. A reasonable starting split, under the assumptions of stage 3, is 45% text layer, 48% OCR, 7% VLM; measure it, then tune the thresholds on the gold set (routing: using each engine where it is strong).
  • Do not fall for the hidden bad text layer. Many scanned PDFs carry an invisible OCR text layer from the bureau's scanner, sometimes poor. Check that the text layer and a fresh OCR of a few regions agree before trusting it; otherwise you read the bureau's mistakes with full confidence.
  • Constrain the VLM. Ask for the values in the type's schema, with a quote of the text region each came from, and compare the quote with an independent OCR of that box. If the VLM says the total is "780.60" and OCR of the same box reads "78O.6O", a post-OCR comparison with character-level normalisation passes, while a model that changed the digits fails. This is the same quote check as in the policy studio, applied to pixels. Report the rate of such disagreements as a metric of VLM trustworthiness.
  • Self-hosting versus calling an API. Invoices contain bank details and personal data. A hosted model may be unacceptable, or acceptable only with a data processing agreement and region guarantees; a self-hosted open model removes the question at a cost in operations. Decide this early, and design the router so that the engine is a configuration choice (security reviews for AI systems).
  • Layout before extraction. Reading order and table structure matter more than character accuracy for invoices: a perfect OCR of cells in the wrong order yields the wrong price for the wrong line (tables, forms and key-value extraction). Evaluate table structure separately.
  • Vendor templates. The top 200 vendors send 60% of volume, each in a stable layout. A cheap, strong trick is a per-vendor template or a few-shot example for the extraction step, selected by the vendor id, falling back to the general model for the long tail.

7Deep dive 2: validators that catch what models cannot

List the checks you can apply to an extracted invoice without any model. For each of seven realistic OCR faults, say which check would catch it.

Compare with a worked answer

An extracted record is a set of claims that must be consistent with each other and with the world. The script below applies four classes of check to seven records, each with one realistic fault: an IBAN with a letter read for a digit, a dropped digit in a line item, a lost decimal point, a misread year, a resent invoice, and transposed digits.

"""Validators turn 'looks plausible' into checks the extraction can fail. Each extracted record below has one realistic fault."""
import re, datetime as dt
from decimal import Decimal

def iban_ok(s):                                    # ISO 13616: move 4 chars to the end, letters -> numbers, mod 97 == 1
    s = re.sub(r"\s", "", s).upper()
    if not re.fullmatch(r"[A-Z]{2}\d{2}[A-Z0-9]{11,30}", s): return False
    t = s[4:] + s[:4]
    return int("".join(str(int(c, 36)) for c in t)) % 97 == 1

def check(rec, seen):
    errs = []
    if not iban_ok(rec["iban"]): errs.append("IBAN checksum")
    lines = sum(Decimal(x) for x in rec["lines"])
    if lines != Decimal(rec["net"]): errs.append(f"lines sum {lines} != net {rec['net']}")
    if Decimal(rec["net"]) + Decimal(rec["tax"]) != Decimal(rec["total"]): errs.append("net + tax != total")
    d = dt.date.fromisoformat(rec["date"])
    if not (dt.date(2026, 10, 14) - dt.timedelta(days=400) <= d <= dt.date(2026, 10, 14) + dt.timedelta(days=1)): errs.append(f"date {rec['date']} out of range")
    key = (rec["vendor"], rec["number"])
    if key in seen: errs.append("duplicate of an invoice already paid")
    seen.add(key)
    return errs

good = dict(vendor="Acme Ltd", number="INV-1042", date="2026-09-30", iban="GB82 WEST 1234 5698 7654 32",
            lines=["400.00", "250.50"], net="650.50", tax="130.10", total="780.60")
cases = [("clean", {}),
         ("O read for 0 in the IBAN", dict(iban="GB82 WEST 1234 5698 7654 3Z")),
         ("one digit dropped in a line", dict(lines=["400.00", "25.50"])),
         ("decimal point lost in total", dict(total="78060")),
         ("7 read for 1 in the year", dict(date="2028-09-30")),
         ("same invoice sent twice", dict()),
         ("digits transposed in net and total", dict(net="605.50", total="735.60"))]
seen = set()
for i, (name, patch) in enumerate(cases):
    rec = {**good, **patch, "number": "INV-1041" if name.startswith("same") else f"INV-{1041 + i}"}
    errs = check(rec, seen)
    print(f"{name:36} -> {'ok' if not errs else '; '.join(errs)}")

  • Checksums and formats catch the characters a recogniser confuses. An IBAN carries a mod-97 check (ISO 13616), so a single wrong character is caught with certainty and two with very high probability. Tax identifiers, card numbers and many account formats have similar checks. These are the cheapest, strongest validators you have; list the ones that apply in every country you serve.
  • Arithmetic catches wrong numbers anywhere in the arithmetic: lines must sum to net, net plus tax must equal total, tax must equal rate times net. A single misread digit breaks at least one equation, and the equations also tell you which field to suspect (if lines sum to net but net plus tax is not total, look at tax and total). Caveat: rounding, discounts and multi-rate tax need explicit rules or you will flood reviewers with false alarms; start from the vendor's historical behaviour.
  • Range and plausibility catch the misread year (2028 for 2026), an invoice date in the future, a total ten times the vendor's largest earlier invoice. Cheap and surprisingly effective; set bounds per vendor from history.
  • Master data and duplicates catch the fraud-shaped errors. A bank account that differs from the vendor's account on file is the single most important flag in the system, a classic payment-fraud pattern (changed bank details by a forged invoice), and must always go to a person and a call-back procedure, however confident the model is. The duplicate check on (vendor, number) caught the resent invoice.
  • What validators cannot catch. The transposed digits in the last case were caught only because the line items disagreed with the net; if the supplier's whole invoice had been consistently misread, nothing would fire. A wrong but internally consistent record (the right invoice from the wrong vendor) passes arithmetic. Validators lower the silent-error rate; they do not make it zero, which is why stage 9 samples what was accepted.
  • Use validator outcomes as features in the router. A field that passed a checksum is far more trustworthy than its model confidence alone says. Combine them (stage 8) rather than treating them as separate gates.
  • Write validators as pure, versioned functions, with a test per fault class and a table of the false alarm rate on the gold set, so a new rule can be rolled out only if it catches errors without drowning reviewers.

8Deep dive 3: confidence, thresholds and human review

Choose the threshold that decides which fields a person sees. What happens to the final error rate and the reviewer workload as you raise it? Is the score trustworthy?

Compare with a worked answer

The simulation below stands in for real extraction results: 40,000 documents with six critical fields each, from three quality classes (55% clean, 33% ordinary scans, 12% poor). The confidence scores are informative but not calibrated, and a tail of wrong fields still scores high, as in real systems. A reviewer is assumed to be right 99.5% of the time on the fields they are shown, and review time is 20 seconds to open a document plus 6 seconds per flagged field.

"""Confidence routing: a person checks the fields the system is unsure about. Synthetic, seeded data standing in for
extraction results: scores are informative but not calibrated, and a tail of errors still scores high."""
import random
rng = random.Random(5)
DOCS, FIELDS = 40_000, 6      # the 6 critical fields: vendor, invoice number, date, total, tax id, IBAN
# document quality classes: share, per-field accuracy
CLASSES = [("clean born-digital", 0.55, 0.997, 60), ("ordinary scan", 0.33, 0.975, 25), ("poor scan / handwriting", 0.12, 0.85, 10)]

def make_doc():
    r, acc, sharp = rng.random(), None, None
    for name, share, a, k in CLASSES:
        if r < share: acc, sharp = a, k; break
        r -= share
    fields = []
    for _ in range(FIELDS):
        ok = rng.random() < acc
        c = rng.betavariate(sharp, 1.0) if ok else rng.betavariate(4.5, 2.2)
        fields.append((min(c, 0.9995), ok))
    return fields

docs = [make_doc() for _ in range(DOCS)]
total = DOCS * FIELDS
raw_err = sum(not ok for d in docs for _, ok in d) / total
print(f"{DOCS:,} documents, {total:,} fields; raw critical-field error {raw_err:.2%}\n")
print("threshold | fields flagged | docs needing a person | final field error | review seconds/doc (20 + 6 per flagged field)")
for th in (0.70, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95, 0.98):
    flagged = needs = 0; wrong_after = 0.0; secs = 0
    for d in docs:
        f = [(c, ok) for c, ok in d if c < th]
        flagged += len(f)
        if f: needs += 1; secs += 20 + 6 * len(f)
        wrong_after += sum(not ok for c, ok in d if c >= th) + 0.005 * len(f)   # a reviewer is right 99.5% of the time
    print(f"   {th:.2f}   |    {flagged/total:6.1%}      |        {needs/DOCS:6.1%}         |      {wrong_after/total:6.3%}      | {secs/DOCS:5.1f}")

  • The raw critical-field error is 2.8%. With a threshold of 0.90, 8.7% of fields are flagged, 28% of documents need a person, and the final error falls to 0.22%, under the 0.3% target, at about 31 seconds per reviewed document (8.8 seconds averaged over all documents).
  • Raising the threshold stops helping, and then hurts. At 0.95 the final error is 0.15%, but 56% of documents need a person; at 0.98 almost every document is reviewed and the error rises to 0.24%, because the reviewer's own 0.5% error rate is applied to many more fields. Human review is not free of error. This is the argument for choosing the threshold on the cost-versus-error curve, not by pushing it to the top.
  • The knee is at 0.90 to 0.95. Going from 0.90 to 0.95 buys 0.07 points of accuracy for 27 more points of document review rate. At $7,000 a month per point, that is about $190,000 a month. Whether that is worth it depends on the cost of an undetected error: tie the threshold to the money (cost models and return on investment).
  • Different thresholds for different fields. A wrong bank account costs far more than a wrong address line. Set a high threshold, or always-review, for the bank account and total; a lower one for low-stakes fields. A per-field cost matrix beats one global number.
  • Calibrate before you threshold. Raw model scores often mean different things at different times. Fit a mapping from score to the observed probability of being correct on a held-out set (grouped bins or isotonic regression), refit it whenever the model or the document mix changes, and choose the threshold on a calibration half, then check on a test half. Do not reuse the same data to choose and to report.
  • Validators move fields across the line. A field with 0.85 model confidence whose checksum passes can be accepted; one with 0.97 whose arithmetic fails must be flagged. Build the router as a small model or rule table over confidence, validator results, field type, vendor history and page quality, trained on reviewer outcomes.
  • Design the review screen for speed and accuracy. Show the cropped region beside the extracted value, one field at a time, with keyboard shortcuts, and the likely alternative candidates. Do not show the model's guess first for fields where people anchor on it; consider a double-blind check for the highest-risk fields (two people, or a person and the machine, must agree). Measure time per field, accept and edit rates, and disagreement between reviewers.
  • Staffing is queueing. A 24-hour service level with Monday and month-end peaks needs capacity above the average; with 55 reviewers on average, plan for 70% to 80% utilisation and cross-train; at 95% the wait explodes (queueing theory). Prioritise by payment due date.

9Deep dive 4: auditing what nobody looked at

Records the machine accepted are never reviewed. How do you know their error rate is still what you think, and how fast will you notice a regression, for example after an OCR engine update or a new vendor template?

Compare with a worked answer

Sample the accepted records. A reviewer re-reads a random sample against the page image, blind to the extracted value, and the audit raises an alarm if the sample has at least K wrong documents. The script computes, for a baseline error rate of 0.4% of auto-accepted documents and a regression to 1.2%, how likely each daily sample size is to raise the alarm, and how often it cries wolf in normal times.

"""Auditing what the system accepted without a person: can a daily sample notice that quality has slipped?
Alarm rule: raise it when the sample contains at least K wrong documents. Exact binomial probabilities."""
from math import comb
def p_alarm(n, p, k):
    return 1 - sum(comb(n, i) * p**i * (1 - p)**(n - i) for i in range(k))
base, bad = 0.004, 0.012        # doc-level error rate among auto-accepted documents: normal vs after a silent regression
print("daily sample n | alarm if wrong >= K | false alarms/month (normal) | chance to catch the regression within 1 day / 3 days")
for n, k in [(200, 3), (500, 5), (1000, 8), (2000, 14), (4000, 24)]:
    fa = p_alarm(n, base, k) * 22
    d1 = p_alarm(n, bad, k); d3 = 1 - (1 - d1) ** 3
    print(f"   {n:5d}       |        {k:2d}           |           {fa:6.2f}            |   {d1:6.1%} / {d3:6.1%}")
print("\nreviewing 1,000 documents a day at ~25 s each costs about", round(1000 * 25 / 3600, 1), "reviewer-hours a day")

  • 1,000 a day catches a tripled error rate within a day nine times in ten (91%), and within three days almost certainly (99.9%), with about one false alarm a month. That costs under seven reviewer-hours a day: about 2% of the review budget for a safety net on 70% of the volume.
  • 200 a day is not enough: it catches the regression on day one only 43% of the time. Small samples tempt teams because they are cheap; the table shows what they buy.
  • Stratify the sample, not just random: oversample new vendors, new document types, the VLM route, low-margin confidences just above the threshold, and the days after any deployment. A uniform sample under-represents exactly the places regressions start. Report the error rate with an interval per stratum.
  • Blind, independent and recorded. The auditor must not see the extracted value, or they will agree with it. Their reading becomes new gold labels, so the audit is also how the gold set stays current as vendors and layouts change.
  • Drift signals that need no labels: shifts in the distribution of confidence scores, the validator failure rate, the VLM route share, the average number of flagged fields, new vendor ids, and the share of pages with low image quality. A change in any of them after a deployment is an early warning ahead of the audit.
  • Close the loop. Reviewer corrections and audit findings update the gold set weekly, retrain or recalibrate the router, and add validators. A model update must pass the gold set (including the hard slice) and a shadow run on the previous week's real documents before it carries traffic (deployment strategies; the improvement flywheel).

Common misconception

"The model is 99% accurate, so 99% of invoices are right." Accuracy is per character or per field, and an invoice has many fields. At 99% per field and six critical fields, only about 94% of invoices are fully right. Also, the errors that cost money are not spread evenly: they concentrate in a few vendors, a few scan sources and a few fields. Measure the unit the business cares about, and slice.

Degraded modes. If the VLM service is down, pages that would go there are queued for review with a note, not read by a worse engine silently. If validators cannot load vendor master data, nothing is auto-accepted. If the ERP rejects records, they wait in a dead-letter queue with the reason, and a daily count goes to the owner. Each degraded mode has a visible state and a stated decision, which is much of the difference between an engineered system and a demo.

11Rollout, monitoring and trade-offs

How do you move from people typing to people checking without a bad month? What did you give up? What changes for handwriting, a new country, or ten times the volume?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Shadow (4 weeks). The pipeline processes every document; clerks continue as usual. Compare field by field; build the first gold set from disagreements, and measure silent errors and the time a clerk takes to enter versus verify.
  2. Assist. Pre-fill the clerks' screens from the pipeline, with every field verified. Measure edit rate by field and vendor; use it to set the thresholds of stage 8.
  3. Partial automation by slice. Switch on auto-accept for the cleanest slice (born-digital, known vendors, passing validators), keeping 100% audit at first, then sampling. Expand by slice once it meets the error target with an interval.
  4. Operate: weekly gold-set refresh, monthly threshold review, quarterly full re-evaluation of engines.

  • Handwriting (delivery notes, signatures, forms): OCR confidence collapses; route to the VLM or to review by rule, lower expectations, and keep it out of the auto-accept slice. Handwritten digits in amounts always go to a person.
  • A new country brings new number and date formats, tax identifiers with their own checksums, new scripts and often right-to-left text; each needs its own normalisers, validators and gold-set slice before launch (multilingual documents).
  • Ten times the volume: machine cost stays small; review cost does not. The investments that pay are more vendor templates, tighter validators, better calibration, and straight-through processing for trusted vendors. Compute scales out; the queue and the ERP import rate become the next limits.
  • Retrieval over the archive. Once documents are structured and stored, people will want to search them: "all invoices from this vendor for this project". That is a search problem with a schema: index the structured fields for filtering and the text for lexical and dense retrieval, and consider page-image retrieval for the documents that OCR serves poorly (visual document retrieval).

Say your design aloud in five minutes, then compare with the model summary.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"Three million documents a month is seven million pages and eighteen million critical fields. The machine side is cheap, under a cent a document; the human side is where the money is, about eight times more. So the design question is how few fields a person has to look at for a given final error. I define accuracy on normalised values per field, report silent errors separately, and slice by vendor, source, language and scan quality, with intervals. The pipeline routes each page to the cheapest engine that can read it: text layer for digital pages, OCR with layout analysis for scans, and a vision-language model only for the few pages that fail quality or validation, with its output checked against an independent OCR of the same box, because models write plausible text. Every field stores the raw string, the normalised value, the box and the engine version. Then deterministic validators do the judging: checksums on bank accounts and tax ids, line items summing to net, net plus tax equal to total, date ranges, duplicates, and a comparison with the vendor master. A changed bank account always goes to a person. A router combines model confidence with validator results; in the simulation a threshold of 0.90 flags 9% of fields, sends 28% of documents to a clerk for about half a minute, and takes the critical-field error from 2.8% to 0.22%. Pushing it higher costs a lot and eventually makes errors worse, because reviewers make mistakes too. A thousand blind audits a day of accepted records catch a regression within a day nine times in ten. Rollout is shadow, then assist, then auto-accept by slice. If we added handwriting or a new country, new slices, normalisers and validators first."

Notice the shape: review cost dominates → cheapest reader per page → validators judge → thresholds at the knee → audit the unseen → slice by slice rollout.

Key takeaways

  • At this scale the machine is cheap and people are expensive. Design around how few fields a human must look at for a target final error.
  • Define accuracy on normalised field values, report silent errors apart from caught ones, and slice by vendor, source, language and quality.
  • Use the cheapest engine that can read each page. Use a vision-language model sparingly, and check what it writes against an independent read.
  • Models read; deterministic checks judge. Checksums, sums, ranges, master data and duplicate checks catch what recognisers confuse.
  • Pick the review threshold at the knee of the cost-versus-error curve. Past it, cost grows and reviewer errors can make the result worse.
  • Audit what nobody looked at, blind and stratified. A thousand a day detects a tripled error rate within a day nine times in ten.
  • Roll out in shadow, then assist, then automatic acceptance by slice, with a gold set that keeps growing.
