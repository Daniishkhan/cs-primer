Design an Experiment That Can Fail

A team has changed how its support assistant retrieves and writes, and the new version scores higher on an internal test. You are asked to decide whether it really helped. The skill on trial is designing a test that could come back saying no, with the metric, the sample size, the stopping rule and the decision thresholds all written down before anyone sees a result.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock design session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong design with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the order an experiment is really designed in: clarify the decision, choose the metric, screen offline, size the test, fix the unit and the assignment, pick a stopping rule, plan the analysis, write the decision table, list what can still go wrong, and say it aloud. Every number on this page is either computed by a script you can read and run, or an assumption labelled as one. The scripts need only Python and NumPy; the data are simulated, because the point is the method, not a particular product.

📐 The brief

Your product lead says: "We rebuilt the reply assistant in the helpdesk. It now retrieves from the policy library before it drafts, and the prompt is new. On our 500-question internal set it goes from 76.0% to 78.4%. I would like to ship on Friday. Can you tell me it is better?"

  • The assistant drafts replies to customer tickets at a retailer's returns helpdesk (about 3,000 tickets a day); a human agent reviews and sends each draft.
  • The new version costs about 3 cents more per ticket (a larger model and a retrieval call). Assume it is worth that if it lifts the share of tickets resolved at first contact by one percentage point or more.
  • The change sits behind a feature flag, so it can be switched off in minutes. Customers and agents can be assigned to a version, but a customer must never see two versions of the assistant in one case.
  • Builds on Statistics for Experiments, Reproducible Experiments, Lab 5 and Case 5 (peeking). Returns in Statistics for Evals, From Model to Value and Deployment Strategies.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes. Output: a one-page experiment plan that someone else could run without you.

1Clarify the decision

What is the decision, who makes it, and what does each kind of mistake cost? Write six questions with assumed answers, and state the claim in one sentence that could be false.

Compare with a worked answer

An experiment is a tool for a decision. Until the decision is written down, "did it help?" has no answer, because "help" has no threshold. These are the questions that change the design:

What exactly is decided?Ship the new assistant to everyone, keep the old one, or keep testingThree outcomes need three regions on the result axis (stage 8)

The claim, in a form that can fail: "Among customers who open a returns ticket during the test, the new assistant raises the share of tickets resolved at first contact by at least one percentage point, without raising escalations to a human by more than one point." Notice that it names a population, a metric, a minimum size and a harm limit. "The new version is better" names none of them.

Out of scope: evaluating the retrieval component on its own (that is a separate offline question, see relevance metrics), changing the agent interface, and any change to the model provider.

2Choose the metric

What is the primary metric, what are the guardrails, and which tempting metric would you refuse to use as the headline? Say how long each takes to observe.

Compare with a worked answer

A metric is a proxy for what you want: a number you can measure standing in for an outcome you care about but cannot observe directly. The closer the proxy is to the outcome, the less room there is for it to improve while the outcome does not.

RoleMetricDefinitionWhen it is known
PrimaryResolved at first contactTicket closed with no reopen and no new ticket from the same customer about the same order within 7 days7 days after the ticket
GuardrailEscalation rateShare of tickets passed to a human specialist or a managerAt close
GuardrailHarmful-reply rateShare of a random sample of sent replies that a reviewer marks as wrong policy, unsafe or rudeAfter review; sample 200 per arm per week
GuardrailCost and latencyModel cost per ticket; time from ticket arrival to draft shownImmediately
DiagnosticDraft acceptance, edit distanceHow much agents change the draft before sendingAt send
  • The primary metric is the outcome, not the activity. "Agents accepted the draft" is quick and easy to move, because a more fluent or more confident draft is accepted more often whether or not it is right. That is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure (see reward models and reward hacking for the same effect inside training). Keep acceptance as a diagnostic that helps explain a result, never as the thing you win on.
  • One primary metric. With one, the false-positive rate is the 5% you chose. With five, "something improved" is nearly guaranteed (stage 7).
  • The window creates a lag. A ticket opened on the last enrolment day is only scored a week later. Plan for enrolment time plus 7 days, and do not read the primary metric on recent tickets, whose 7-day window is still open: they look better than they are, because reopens have not happened yet.
  • Guardrails are tested for harm, with a margin. A guardrail is a metric you do not want to get worse. You decide in advance how much worse counts as a breach (here, escalations rising by more than one point), and you test against that margin rather than against zero. Stage 8 returns to this.

Common misconception

"If the headline metric is up and nothing else is significantly worse, we are fine." A guardrail that is merely not significant may simply be underpowered: with a few thousand tickets, a real one-point rise in escalations is often invisible. The guardrail needs its own sample size and its own margin. In this design it ends up needing more tickets than the primary metric.

3Screen it offline first

The 500-question set shows 78.4% against 76.0%. How much does that tell you? How would you check whether two runs of the same system differ by as much?

Compare with a worked answer

An offline test is a cheap screen that decides what is worth the cost of a real experiment. It is not the experiment, because the questions are not the tickets, and the grading is not the customer. But it can still fail to impress, and you should check whether it does. The simulation below builds per-question outcomes matching the team's numbers (the models disagree on 40 of 500 questions: B right on 26, A right on 14), then asks three things: how wide is the interval, how many questions would a convincing result need, and how much does the same system move between two runs?

"""Offline gold set: is 78.4% vs 76.0% on 500 questions a real gain? (simulated per-question results)"""
import math
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(101)
n = 500
# Per-question outcomes built to match the team's report: A 76.0%, B 78.4%.
both_right, only_a, only_b = 366, 14, 26
a = np.array([1] * both_right + [1] * only_a + [0] * only_b + [0] * (n - both_right - only_a - only_b))
b = np.array([1] * both_right + [0] * only_a + [1] * only_b + [0] * (n - both_right - only_a - only_b))
print(f"A {a.mean():.1%}  B {b.mean():.1%}  difference {b.mean() - a.mean():+.1%}")
print(f"questions where the systems disagree: {only_a + only_b} of {n} ({(only_a + only_b) / n:.1%}); B wins {only_b}, A wins {only_a}")

# Paired bootstrap: resample QUESTIONS, keep each question's (A, B) pair together.
idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=(20_000, n))
d = b[idx].mean(axis=1) - a[idx].mean(axis=1)
lo, hi = np.percentile(d, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"paired bootstrap 95% interval for the gain: [{lo:+.1%}, {hi:+.1%}]")

# Exact sign test on the disagreements (McNemar): if no real difference, each disagreement is a fair coin.
k, m = only_b, only_a + only_b
tail = sum(math.comb(m, i) for i in range(k, m + 1)) / 2 ** m
print(f"sign test on {m} disagreements: P(B wins >= {k}) = {tail:.3f} one-sided, {min(1, 2 * tail):.3f} two-sided")

# How many questions would we need? Paired sample size: n = (z_a + z_b)^2 * q / delta^2,
# where q = share of questions on which the systems disagree and delta = the true gain.
za, zb = 1.96, 0.8416
for delta in (0.024, 0.02, 0.01):
    q = 0.08
    print(f"  to detect a true gain of {delta:.1%} with 80% power when 8% of questions disagree: {(za + zb) ** 2 * q / delta ** 2:,.0f} questions")

# Run-to-run noise: the SAME system, sampled twice at temperature > 0, on the same 500 questions.
# Assumption: 65% of questions are easy (96% pass rate when sampled), 10% hard (5%), 25% unstable (20% to 80%).
kind = rng.choice(3, size=n, p=[0.65, 0.10, 0.25])
p = np.where(kind == 0, 0.96, np.where(kind == 1, 0.05, rng.uniform(0.2, 0.8, n)))
diffs = []
for _ in range(2_000):
    r1 = rng.random(n) < p
    r2 = rng.random(n) < p
    diffs.append(r2.mean() - r1.mean())
diffs = np.array(diffs)
print(f"same system, two runs: accuracy {p.mean():.1%}; the gap between two runs has std {diffs.std():.2%}, "
      f"and exceeds 2.4 points in {np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= 0.024):.1%} of repeats")
A 76.0%  B 78.4%  difference +2.4%
questions where the systems disagree: 40 of 500 (8.0%); B wins 26, A wins 14
paired bootstrap 95% interval for the gain: [+0.0%, +4.8%]
sign test on 40 disagreements: P(B wins >= 26) = 0.040 one-sided, 0.081 two-sided
  to detect a true gain of 2.4% with 80% power when 8% of questions disagree: 1,090 questions
  to detect a true gain of 2.0% with 80% power when 8% of questions disagree: 1,570 questions
  to detect a true gain of 1.0% with 80% power when 8% of questions disagree: 6,279 questions
same system, two runs: accuracy 74.8%; the gap between two runs has std 1.79%, and exceeds 2.4 points in 19.7% of repeats
  • The paired interval just touches zero. Resampling questions (keeping each question's A and B results together, as in chapter 15) gives a 95% interval of 0.0 to +4.8 points. The sign test on the 40 disagreements agrees: two-sided p = 0.081. The team has weak evidence of a gain, not strong evidence.
  • Only the 40 disagreements carry information. The other 460 questions have the same outcome in both systems. This is why a paired analysis is much tighter than comparing two separate accuracies, and why the question count that matters is the number of disagreements.
  • A convincing offline result needs about 1,100 questions for a true 2.4-point gain at 80% power, 1,570 for a 2-point gain, and over 6,000 for 1 point (the formula is the paired version of chapter 15's sample-size rule). A 500-question set cannot tell apart gains smaller than about 3 points.
  • Run-to-run noise is of the same size as the claim. Under the stated assumptions about how many questions are unstable at temperature above zero, two runs of one unchanged system differ by 2.4 points or more about one time in five. So run the old system twice before you believe the gap between old and new: an A/A comparison (the same system against itself) tells you the noise floor you must beat. Fix the seeds where the provider allows it, and report the spread over several runs.

What the offline result is good for: deciding that the change is promising enough to test online, and finding the questions on which it is worse (read the 14 where A wins; they are the first thing to look at, see Statistics for Evals). It is not good enough to ship on, and the plan should say so.

4Size the test

How many tickets, how many customers and how many days do you need to detect a one-to-two-point lift on a 68% baseline? What assumption in your calculation would you check first?

Compare with a worked answer

Sizing starts from the smallest effect you would act on. Here the minimal worthwhile effect is 1 point (the break-even from the brief), but to be safe the test is sized to detect 2 points with 80% power: a test with 80% power finds a true effect of that size four times in five. If the true lift is exactly 1 point, a 2-point design will usually return an interval too wide to say so, which is a real risk to name (stage 8). The script uses the formula of chapter 15 and then corrects it for the fact that tickets from one customer are not independent.

"""How many tickets, how many customers, how many days? Every input is an assumption to challenge."""
import math
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(202)

def n_per_arm(p1, p2, za=1.96, zb=0.8416):          # z for a 5% two-sided test and 80% power
    return (za + zb) ** 2 * (p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2)) / (p2 - p1) ** 2

base, mde = 0.68, 0.02
n = n_per_arm(base, base + mde)
print(f"if tickets were independent: {n:,.0f} tickets per arm to see +{mde:.0%} on a {base:.0%} baseline")

# Tickets are not independent: one customer sends several, and a customer's tickets resemble each other.
tickets = 1 + rng.negative_binomial(0.6, 0.35, size=200_000)      # skewed: mean about 2.1 tickets per customer
rho = 1 / (2.72 + 1.28 + 1)                                        # intra-customer correlation if pass rate ~ Beta(2.72, 1.28)
m_eff = (tickets ** 2).mean() / tickets.mean()                     # clusters are unequal, so big customers weigh more
deff = 1 + (m_eff - 1) * rho
print(f"mean {tickets.mean():.2f} tickets per customer, size-weighted {m_eff:.2f}, correlation {rho:.2f}")
print(f"design effect = 1 + (m - 1) * rho = {deff:.2f} -> {n * deff:,.0f} tickets per arm = {n * deff / tickets.mean():,.0f} customers per arm")

per_day = 3_000
for label, tickets_needed in (("primary alone", n * deff), ("guardrail on escalations (stage 8)", 16_577 * deff)):
    days = 2 * tickets_needed / per_day
    print(f"{label:<36}{tickets_needed:>8,.0f} tickets per arm -> {days:4.1f} days of traffic -> {math.ceil(days / 7)} whole weeks")
if tickets were independent: 8,391 tickets per arm to see +2% on a 68% baseline
mean 2.12 tickets per customer, size-weighted 3.63, correlation 0.20
design effect = 1 + (m - 1) * rho = 1.53 -> 12,800 tickets per arm = 6,046 customers per arm
primary alone                         12,800 tickets per arm ->  8.5 days of traffic -> 2 whole weeks
guardrail on escalations (stage 8)    25,289 tickets per arm -> 16.9 days of traffic -> 3 whole weeks
  • The textbook number is a floor. 8,391 tickets per arm assumes every ticket is an independent coin flip. Customers send several tickets, and a customer's tickets resemble one another. The design effect, 1 + (m − 1)ρ, multiplies the required sample by the cost of that dependence: m is the average cluster size (here the size-weighted 3.63, because a few heavy users contribute many tickets) and ρ is the correlation between tickets of one customer (assumed 0.2). The factor is 1.53, so the test needs about 12,800 tickets per arm, from about 6,000 customers.
  • The correlation is the assumption to check first. Nobody knows ρ in advance. Estimate it from last month's tickets (the share of variance that lies between customers) before you commit, and rerun the script. If it were 0.4 the design effect would be 2.0 and the test would need about a third more traffic.
  • Weeks, not days. 8.5 days of traffic becomes two whole weeks, because ticket behaviour changes by day of week and the test should contain complete cycles (chapter 15). The guardrail on escalations, sized in stage 8, needs three weeks, so that is the planned enrolment, followed by the 7-day outcome window: a month from start to answer.
  • If the baseline were 90% instead of 68% the same 2-point lift would need fewer tickets (the variance p(1 − p) is smaller), and if the effect were 1 point you would need about four times as many. Halving the effect quadruples the sample; this is the lever to explain to the product lead.

5Fix the unit and the assignment

What gets randomised, how, and what checks tell you assignment worked before you look at any metric? What happens if you randomise tickets but the customers repeat?

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Randomise customers, not tickets. The brief says a customer must never see two versions in one case, and the maths says the same thing: if you assign per ticket, the same customer's tickets land in both arms and the arms contaminate each other (a customer who got a poor draft last week writes more angrily this week). Assign by hashing the customer id with a per-experiment salt, a random string that makes this experiment's split independent of every other experiment's.
  2. Analyse at the unit you randomised. If you randomise customers but compute the standard error as if each ticket were independent, the error bars are too small. The simulation below runs 3,000 experiments in which the two arms are identical (an A/A test), so every "significant" result is a false positive.
"""A/A tests with customers randomised: how often does a test with NO real difference say 'significant'?"""
import math
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(202)
E, cust = 3_000, 2_000                                         # simulated experiments, customers per arm
naive_fp = robust_fp = 0
for _ in range(E):
    tk = 1 + rng.negative_binomial(0.6, 0.35, size=2 * cust)  # tickets per customer
    pass_rate = rng.beta(2.72, 1.28, size=2 * cust)            # each customer's own chance of a good outcome
    resolved = rng.binomial(tk, pass_rate)                     # resolved tickets per customer
    arm_a = np.arange(2 * cust) < cust
    est = []
    for sel in (arm_a, ~arm_a):
        T, K = tk[sel].sum(), resolved[sel].sum()
        p = K / T
        naive = p * (1 - p) / T                                # treats every ticket as an independent coin flip
        resid = resolved[sel] - p * tk[sel]
        robust = (resid ** 2).sum() / T ** 2                   # sums residuals per customer, then squares
        est.append((p, naive, robust))
    d = est[0][0] - est[1][0]
    naive_fp += abs(d) / math.sqrt(est[0][1] + est[1][1]) > 1.96
    robust_fp += abs(d) / math.sqrt(est[0][2] + est[1][2]) > 1.96
print(f"{E:,} A/A experiments, {cust:,} customers per arm")
print(f"  tickets treated as independent : {naive_fp / E:.1%} false positives (promised 5%)")
print(f"  errors computed per customer   : {robust_fp / E:.1%} false positives")
3,000 A/A experiments, 2,000 customers per arm
  tickets treated as independent : 10.5% false positives (promised 5%)
  errors computed per customer   : 4.4% false positives

Treating clustered tickets as independent produces false positives at roughly twice the promised rate (10.5% against 5%): the test looks twice as eager as it claims. Computing the error from per-customer sums restores it (4.4%, within simulation noise of 5%). The same mistake in the other direction, randomising tickets and analysing customers, is no better: the clean fix is to randomise and analyse at the same unit.

  1. Check the split before the metrics. A sample ratio mismatch (SRM) is a split that differs from the planned one by more than chance allows (chapter 15 has the chi-square check). If you planned 50/50 customers and got 51.5/48.5 of 20,000, something upstream is broken: a crash in one arm, a bot filter applied to one arm, a redirect that loses users. An SRM means the comparison is biased in unknown ways; fix it before you read a single metric.
  2. Keep everything else fixed. Same agents, same queues, same hours, one flag. If the new assistant also changes which tickets get an automatic reply, the arms differ in two ways and you will not know which one moved the number.
  3. Interference. Agents work both arms. A tired agent who spends longer on a hard new-arm draft has less time for old-arm tickets. If agents can see which arm they are in, they can change behaviour. Either hide the arm or randomise by agent-shift blocks, and say in the plan which you chose and why.

6Choose the stopping rule

The dashboard updates daily and your product lead will ask every morning. What is your rule for when the test ends, and what would you do if someone insists on an early look?

Compare with a worked answer

The guarantees in the previous stages assume one analysis of a sample whose size was fixed in advance. Anything that lets the data influence when you stop breaks them (the peeking problem; see Case 5). There are three honest options, and the choice is worth writing down.

  • Fixed horizon. Enrol for three weeks, wait for the window, analyse once. Simple, valid, wasteful if the answer is obvious early. Nobody looks at the primary metric before the end; they may look at SRM and guardrail dashboards, which are checks on the experiment, not on the result.
  • Group sequential. Plan a few looks (here four, at the end of each week) and use a stricter bar at each so that the total false-positive rate stays at 5%. You may stop early if the bar is crossed.
  • Always-valid methods. Intervals that stay correct at any time (Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, 2017). Convenient on platforms that support them; the price is a little power.

The script compares four rules on the same simulated experiments: a world with no effect (to count false positives) and one where the true effect is exactly the size the test was designed to detect (to count power and how much data each rule uses).

"""Stopping rules for a 4-look experiment: false positives, power and how early each rule stops."""
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(303)
E, K = 200_000, 4
steps = rng.standard_normal((E, K))
def paths(theta):
    # z at look k, when the final (k = K) z has mean theta; the information grows linearly with k.
    s = np.cumsum(steps + theta / np.sqrt(K), axis=1)
    return s / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, K + 1))
null, alt = paths(0.0), paths(2.8)                     # 2.8 = 1.96 + 0.84: an effect a fixed test finds 80% of the time
def calibrate(shape):
    lo, hi = 1.5, 5.0
    for _ in range(40):
        c = (lo + hi) / 2
        if np.mean((np.abs(null) > c * shape).any(axis=1)) > 0.05: lo = c
        else: hi = c
    return c
rules = {
    "fixed horizon, look only at the end": np.array([np.inf, np.inf, np.inf, 1.96]),
    "peek at every look, z > 1.96": np.full(K, 1.96),
    "Pocock: same bar every look": np.full(K, calibrate(np.ones(K))),
    "O'Brien-Fleming style: strict early": calibrate(np.sqrt(K / np.arange(1, K + 1))) * np.sqrt(K / np.arange(1, K + 1)),
}
print(f"{'rule':<38} {'bars at looks 1-4':<26} {'false pos':>9} {'power':>7} {'mean data used if effect real':>30}")
for name, bar in rules.items():
    fp = np.mean((np.abs(null) > bar).any(axis=1))
    cross = np.abs(alt) > bar
    pw = np.mean(cross.any(axis=1))
    first = np.where(cross.any(axis=1), cross.argmax(axis=1) + 1, K)
    used = np.mean(first) / K
    bars = " ".join("  - " if np.isinf(b) else f"{b:.2f}" for b in bar)
    print(f"{name:<38} {bars:<26} {fp:>9.1%} {pw:>7.1%} {used:>29.0%}")
rule                                   bars at looks 1-4          false pos   power  mean data used if effect real
fixed horizon, look only at the end      -    -    -  1.96             5.0%   79.8%                          100%
peek at every look, z > 1.96           1.96 1.96 1.96 1.96            12.7%   84.2%                           61%
Pocock: same bar every look            2.36 2.36 2.36 2.36             5.0%   71.7%                           72%
O'Brien-Fleming style: strict early    4.04 2.86 2.33 2.02             5.0%   79.0%                           82%
  • Peeking at four looks with the usual 1.96 bar turns a 5% test into a 12.7% test: roughly one world in eight with no effect at all declares a winner. It also looks slightly more powerful (84%), which is not a benefit: that power is bought with false positives.
  • Pocock uses the same stricter bar (2.36) at every look; it gives up some power (71.7%) in exchange for stopping early more often. O'Brien-Fleming is very strict early (4.04 at the first look, 2.02 at the last) and so nearly matches the fixed test in power (79%) while still allowing an early stop when the effect is large. The bars here agree with the published values for four looks (Pocock 1977; O'Brien and Fleming 1979) to within simulation noise.
  • The recommendation for this test: fixed horizon on the primary metric, with the SRM and guardrail checks running daily. A one-month decision on a reversible feature does not justify a more complicated procedure. Record the rule before the test starts. If the product lead asks for an early look, the answer is: "You may have it, and it will be labelled exploratory, and it cannot change the decision rule."

Systems connection

A canary release is a small experiment with a stopping rule: you watch a few metrics while exposure ramps up and halt on a breach (Deployment Strategies). The difference is the question. A canary asks "is anything broken?" and may stop at the first sign; an A/B test asks "how much better is it?" and must not stop at the first good sign.

7Plan the analysis

Write the analysis plan in five lines: the estimate, the interval, the test, the segments, and how many things you are allowed to look at. What is the rule for the question "did it work for mobile users in Germany?"

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Estimate and interval. The difference in resolved-at-first-contact between arms, in percentage points, with a 95% interval computed from per-customer sums (stage 5). Report the estimate and interval first; the p-value is secondary.
  2. Population. Everyone assigned (intention to treat), including customers whose ticket the assistant could not draft for. Dropping them after the fact selects on a post-treatment variable and can bias the comparison.
  3. One primary test, one decision. Everything else is a guardrail (tested for harm against a margin) or exploratory (reported with the number of things examined).
  4. Segments are exploratory. Pre-register at most three (channel, customer tier, ticket type). Any other cut is a hypothesis for the next experiment. With 20 independent cuts and no true effect anywhere, the chance that at least one shows p < 0.05 is 1 − 0.9520 = 64% (chapter 15 simulates it). "It worked for mobile users in Germany" is what that 64% looks like in a meeting.
  5. Corrections where they apply. Primary: none needed (one test). Segments: Benjamini-Hochberg at a false discovery rate of 5% (the expected share of declared findings that are false). Guardrails: each is one-sided against its margin; there are three, so use a Holm adjustment or declare which one is the gate.

Also write down the checks that can void the test: an SRM; a logging outage in one arm; a change to the policy library during the test (it would move both arms differently); and a major incident or promotion that changes the ticket mix. A voided test is rerun, not rescued.

Common misconception

"We can adjust for imbalance afterwards, so randomisation details matter less." Adjusting for covariates (customer tier, ticket type) can tighten the interval a little, which is useful, but it cannot repair a broken assignment or a contaminated unit. Choose the unit and the check first; use adjustment only to gain precision, and pre-specify the covariates.

8Write down what would change your mind

Before the test starts, fill in a table: for each region the result could fall in, what do you do? Make sure one of the rows is "no".

Compare with a worked answer

A test that cannot come out against the change is not a test. The way to guarantee that it can is to commit, in writing and in advance, to what each result means. Two thresholds do the work: zero (is there any effect?) and the minimal worthwhile effect (is it big enough to pay for the 3 cents? here +1 point). The decision depends on where the whole 95% interval sits relative to those lines, not on whether it excludes zero.

The guardrail needs its own sample, because showing "no worse than a margin" is a stricter claim than showing "better than zero". The script sizes it and applies the table to six imagined results (the letters match the figure).

"""Pre-registered decision rule: what each possible result would make us do. All thresholds are assumptions."""
za, zb = 1.96, 0.8416
DEFF = 1.5256        # design effect from the sizing script (printed there as 1.53)

  • The guardrail sets the size of the experiment. Showing that escalations rose by less than one point, if they truly did not change, needs 16,577 independent tickets per arm. That is twice the primary metric's requirement, and it turns the design-effect-corrected total into about 25,300 tickets per arm: 17 days of traffic, three whole weeks. Many teams discover this after the test, when the guardrail comes back "inconclusive".
  • Row A ships. The whole interval is above the worthwhile effect and the guardrail holds. Row D is a useful "no": the interval is narrow and lies inside the range where any effect is too small to pay for the extra cost. A test that is able to say that is better than one that can only say "yes" or "not enough data".
  • Row C is the honest hard case. The interval stretches from slightly harmful to clearly worthwhile; the data are compatible with a good change and with no change. "Not significant" must not be reported as "no effect" (chapter 15). The pre-agreed action is to extend only if the planned extension is affordable, otherwise to take the cheaper option and revisit.
  • Row E stops regardless of the primary metric, which is what a guardrail is for. The decision table should say who has the authority to stop the test, because that is what you will need at 2 a.m.

Write the table with names and thresholds, date it, and send it to the stakeholders before the start. People are much better at accepting "no" from a rule they agreed to than from an analyst after the fact.

Key takeaways

  • Write the claim and the decision table before the data exist. If no result could make you say no, you have not designed an experiment.
  • Pick one primary outcome metric; keep easy proxies as diagnostics. Guardrails need margins and their own sample size, and often set the size of the whole test.
  • An offline score is a screen. Count the questions on which the systems disagree, and run the old system twice to see the noise.
  • Sample size follows the smallest effect worth acting on, and halving that effect quadruples it. Clustered units multiply it by the design effect.
  • Randomise and analyse at the same unit. Ignoring the clustering doubled the false-positive rate in the simulation.
  • Fix the stopping rule in advance. Four peeks turn a 5% test into a 13% test; group sequential bars or always-valid methods keep early looks honest.
  • The interval's position relative to zero and to the worthwhile effect decides the action, and "inconclusive" is a legitimate outcome that is not "no effect".
