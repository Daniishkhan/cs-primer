Budget the Compute for a Project

A research plan says "we will continue pretraining a 7B model on 40 billion tokens, then fine-tune it". A funder asks what that costs. You turn the plan into GPU-hours, memory, storage and a bill, say how wrong the bill could be, and name the three assumptions that decide whether the request is honest.

How a design studio works

This page is a mock planning session of 60 to 90 minutes, not a chapter. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer is one strong plan with its reasoning, not the only right one. The stages follow the way a budget is built: clarify what the number is for, list the work, convert it to GPU-hours, check that it fits in memory, convert hours to calendar time, add storage, assemble the bill, find the risky assumptions, plan the gates that limit the damage, and write the request. All the scripts are short, use only Python and NumPy, and share one small module (budget.py, in stage 3) so you can change an assumption and watch the bill move. Every price and efficiency figure is an assumption you should replace.

📐 The brief

A small public-sector research group wants to adapt an open-weights 7B model to its own documents. A published paper (imagined for this studio) reports that continued pretraining on 40B tokens of domain text, followed by supervised fine-tuning, beats the base model on a domain benchmark. The funder says: "Tell us what it costs to reproduce this and try our own variants. We will approve a number, not a hope."

  • Plan: tune the data mixture with six small proxy runs; continue pretraining the 7B model once on 40B tokens; run a sweep of supervised fine-tuning (full and LoRA); evaluate six checkpoints on four suites.
  • Hardware: rented H100 80 GB GPUs in 8-GPU nodes. The group has no hardware of its own and no spare cluster time.
  • You may use only what you can derive or measure: the rules of GPUs, Clouds & What Compute Costs and the habits of Reading Papers Efficiently.
  • Builds on those two chapters, Floating Point & Numerical Stability (bf16) and Gradient Descent & Optimisers (Adam state). Returns in GPU Memory, Parallel Training, Checkpoints, Failures & Long Runs and The Economics of a Token.
  • You have 60 to 90 minutes. Output: a one-page budget request with a range and the assumptions that move it.

1Clarify what the number is for

Who reads the number, what do they do with it, and what does the paper tell you about its own cost? Write six questions with assumed answers, and list what a paper's compute section usually leaves out.

What the paper tells you, and what it does not. Reading the compute section is a skill of its own (see chapter 16). Papers usually give the cost of the final run. The DeepSeek-V3 technical report (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) states 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours priced at an assumed $2 an hour, $5.576 million, and says plainly that this covers the official training run, not the research, ablations or failed experiments before it. The Llama 3 paper (Llama Team, 2024) reports about 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs for the 405B model, which the rule 6ND reproduces (chapter 17). So expect to find, for a paper like this one:

  • The tokens and parameters of the main run, from which you can derive FLOPs yourself, and often a GPU count and a duration.
  • Rarely: the utilisation achieved, the number of restarts, the proxy and ablation runs, evaluation cost, storage, or what the hardware cost per hour.
  • Therefore your budget must add an overhead multiplier to the paper's figure, and label it as your assumption.

The honest framing: "We estimate X for the plan as described, with a range of Y to Z, and here are the assumptions that decide where in that range we land." A single number with no range invites the funder to hold you to the cheapest case.

The table is the single most reusable artefact of the exercise: a reviewer can challenge any row without touching the maths. Three habits make it reliable:

  • Put tokens, not epochs, in the plan. "Two epochs of a 600M-token set" is 1.2B tokens processed; compute cares about tokens processed, so write that number.
  • Separate the tokens you have from the tokens you wish for. The paper's 40B tokens may be a curated, filtered set. If your own corpus is 25B after deduplication, either the main run shrinks or you repeat data for more than one epoch, which has its own cost in quality (see pretraining data).
  • Mark the elastic rows. They are where the plan grows when results are interesting, and where the budget needs a policy (stage 9).

Not in the plan, and said so: preprocessing on CPUs (cleaning, deduplication, tokenisation), human annotation, any model API calls (for example an LLM judge or synthetic data), serving the finished model and staff time. Each is a separate line in a real proposal.

3Turn work into GPU-hours

Write the formula that converts a phase to GPU-hours. Which two inputs would you least trust, and what do you do about evaluation, where FLOPs are the wrong tool?

Compare with a worked answer

For a dense transformer, training costs about 6 × N × D FLOPs (2ND forward, 4ND backward; chapter 17). Divide by what the chip can really deliver, peak FLOP/s times the MFU (model FLOPs utilisation, the fraction of peak the useful arithmetic reaches), and by 3,600 for hours. Three refinements go into the module below: small models run at lower MFU than large ones; LoRA skips the weight gradients of frozen weights, so it costs nearer 4ND than 6ND; and evaluation is generation, which is memory-bound, so it is estimated from a measured tokens-per-second rate rather than from FLOPs. The assumptions sit at the top, in one place.

"""budget.py: a compute budget as named inputs and small functions. Every number here is an assumption you can change."""
from dataclasses import dataclass

H100_PEAK = 989e12          # dense bf16 FLOP/s (NVIDIA datasheet; the 1,979 headline assumes sparsity)
PRICE = 2.50                # $ per GPU-hour, on-demand, specialist cloud (assumption; see the dated box)

@dataclass
class Phase:
    name: str
    params: float           # N: parameters (active ones, for a mixture-of-experts)
    tokens: float           # D: tokens processed per run
    runs: int               # how many times we plan to do it
    mfu: float              # model FLOPs utilisation we expect
    flops_per_param_token: float = 6.0     # 6 for full training; about 4 for LoRA (no weight-gradient for frozen weights)

    def flops(self):
        return self.flops_per_param_token * self.params * self.tokens * self.runs

    def gpu_hours(self):
        return self.flops() / (H100_PEAK * self.mfu) / 3600

PLAN = [
    Phase("Proxy runs for the data mixture (1B, 10B tokens)", 1e9, 10e9, 6, 0.30),
    Phase("Continued pretraining (7B, 40B tokens)",           7e9, 40e9, 1, 0.40),
    Phase("SFT sweep, full fine-tune (7B, 1.2B tokens)",      7e9, 1.2e9, 6, 0.35),
    Phase("SFT sweep, LoRA (7B, 1.2B tokens)",                7e9, 1.2e9, 10, 0.30, 4.0),
]

def eval_gpu_hours(checkpoints=6, suites=4, prompts=2000, out_tokens=500, tok_per_s_per_gpu=3000):
    """Generation is memory-bound, so use a measured throughput, not FLOPs."""
    return checkpoints * suites * prompts * out_tokens / tok_per_s_per_gpu / 3600

SMOKE_GPU_HOURS = 8 * 3     # one 8-GPU node for 3 hours of smoke tests and a throughput measurement

def overhead(rework=0.20, idle=0.25):
    """Billed time per useful GPU-hour: lost work is repeated, and idle rented time is paid for."""
    return (1 + rework) / (1 - idle)

def total_gpu_hours(plan=PLAN, **kw):
    useful = sum(p.gpu_hours() for p in plan) + eval_gpu_hours() + SMOKE_GPU_HOURS
    return useful, useful * overhead(**kw)

  • Continued pretraining is 56% of the useful compute (1,180 of 2,100 GPU-hours). The 6ND rule for it is solid; what is uncertain is the MFU. Planning at 40% is a reasonable assumption for a tuned sharded run, not a promise. A first attempt on a new code base is often well below.
  • The "per GPU" column is a free sanity check. 9,419 tokens per second per GPU for the 7B run is a number you can measure in ten minutes. If your smoke test shows 4,000, the plan's biggest line almost doubles. This is why the smoke test is Gate 0 (stage 9).
  • Evaluation is cheap here (2 GPU-hours) because it is only generation at an assumed 3,000 output tokens per second per GPU. It stops being cheap when an LLM judge scores every output, or when long chain-of-thought answers multiply the tokens. Those are separate lines.
  • The overhead multiplier, 1.60, is a model of two things: lost work (a failed run repeats hours that were not checkpointed, plus runs you rerun after a bug: assume 20% extra) and idle rented time (nodes allocated while you debug, wait for data or sit between runs: assume 25% of rented time). The multiplier is (1 + 0.20) / (1 − 0.25). It is an assumption, and it is the line a funder should question.

4Check that it fits in memory

Will full fine-tuning of the 7B model fit on one 80 GB card? On eight? What about LoRA? What do the answers do to the shape of the cluster you rent?

Compare with a worked answer

A job that does not fit does not run slowly, it stops with an out-of-memory error. The budget therefore needs a fit check for each phase before its cost means anything. Mixed-precision Adam holds 16 bytes per parameter (bf16 weights and gradients, fp32 master copy and two fp32 moments; chapter 17), so the 7B model has 112 GB of training state before any activations. The script adds an activation estimate: roughly 34 × sequence × batch × hidden bytes per layer without recomputation (Korthikanti et al., 2022, with the attention-score term dropped on the assumption of a fused attention kernel), or about 2 × sequence × batch × hidden per layer if each layer's activations are recomputed in the backward pass.

"""Will it fit? Per-GPU memory for three configurations of the 7B model (GB, 1 GB = 1e9 bytes). Activations are an estimate."""
N, layers, hidden = 7e9, 32, 4096
GB, HBM = 1e9, 80

def activations_gb(seq, micro_batch, recompute):
    """Per layer about 34 * seq * batch * hidden bytes in 16-bit without recomputation (Korthikanti et al., 2022,
    attention-score term dropped because we assume a fused attention kernel); with full recomputation keep
    only each layer's input: 2 * seq * batch * hidden bytes."""
    per_layer = (2 if recompute else 34) * seq * micro_batch * hidden
    return per_layer * layers / GB

def report(name, state_gb, act_gb, extra=0.0):
    total = state_gb + act_gb + extra
    print(f"{name:<46}{state_gb:>8.1f}{act_gb:>8.1f}{extra:>8.1f}{total:>9.1f}   {'fits' if total < HBM * 0.9 else 'DOES NOT FIT'}")

print(f"{'configuration (7B, sequence 4096)':<46}{'state':>8}{'acts':>8}{'other':>8}{'total':>9}   80 GB card")
report("full fine-tune, 1 GPU, no sharding",       N * 16 / GB, activations_gb(4096, 1, True))
report("full fine-tune, 8 GPUs sharded (FSDP), mb 2", N * 16 / GB / 8, activations_gb(4096, 2, True), extra=4)
report("same, but no activation recomputation",    N * 16 / GB / 8, activations_gb(4096, 2, False), extra=4)
lora = 4.2e7                                         # rank-16 adapters on all linear layers: about 42M parameters
report("LoRA, 1 GPU (frozen bf16 weights), mb 4",   N * 2 / GB + lora * 16 / GB, activations_gb(4096, 4, True))
print("\n'other' = communication buffers and allocator slack, a rough 4 GB. State = 16 bytes/parameter for full training.")

  • One GPU cannot full-fine-tune a 7B model. The state alone (112 GB) exceeds the 80 GB card; no batch-size change helps. You need to shard the state across GPUs (FSDP or ZeRO; Parallel Training) or avoid training every weight.
  • Eight GPUs make it comfortable. Each holds 14 GB of state; with activations and slack the job needs about 20 GB of 80 GB. That headroom means you may skip activation recomputation (54 GB per card), which saves the extra forward pass in the backward step and raises MFU. A real run is the only way to confirm, so keep the MFU assumption wide.
  • LoRA fits on a single card (about 19 GB with 14 GB of frozen bf16 weights, about 40M adapter parameters and their optimiser state), so each LoRA run can use one GPU, and ten of them can run side by side on a node. That is a scheduling gain, not a compute gain: the GPU-hours of the sweep are the same however you pack them.
  • This sets the unit you rent. You rent whole 8-GPU nodes for the main run; the smaller runs share nodes or use single GPUs. Because GPUs are billed while allocated, the packing of small runs onto nodes is part of the idle-time assumption.

Common misconception

"If the weights fit, the job fits." For inference that is nearly true (add the KV cache). For training the weights are one-eighth of the state: 14 GB of bf16 weights against 112 GB of weights, gradients, master copy and optimiser moments. A model you can run on a laptop GPU is not one you can train on it.

5Convert hours to calendar time

If the main run is 1,180 GPU-hours, how long does it take on one node, four nodes and eight? Does using more GPUs make it cheaper? What else limits when you can start?

Compare with a worked answer

GPU-hours is a cost; wall-clock time is a schedule. They are related by the number of GPUs and by scaling efficiency, the fraction of ideal speed-up that survives the communication between GPUs. The efficiencies below are assumptions in the typical range for a model of this size on a good interconnect, not measurements; measure your own in the first hundred steps.

from budget import *
cpt = PLAN[1]
per_gpu = cpt.mfu * H100_PEAK / 42e9          # tokens per second per GPU at the planned MFU (6 * 7e9 = 42e9 FLOPs per token)
print(f"single-GPU speed at {cpt.mfu:.0%} MFU: {per_gpu:,.0f} tokens/s; 40B tokens = {cpt.tokens / per_gpu / 3600:,.0f} GPU-hours if scaling were perfect")
print(f"{'nodes':>5} {'GPUs':>5} {'scaling eff.':>13} {'wall-clock':>11} {'GPU-hours billed':>17} {'cost at $2.50':>14}")
for nodes, eff in ((1, 1.00), (2, 0.95), (4, 0.90), (8, 0.80)):
    gpus = 8 * nodes
    hours = cpt.tokens / (gpus * per_gpu * eff) / 3600
    print(f"{nodes:>5} {gpus:>5} {eff:>13.0%} {hours / 24:>8.1f} d {hours * gpus:>17,.0f} {hours * gpus * PRICE:>14,.0f}")

    1     8          100%      6.1 d             1,180          2,949

  • Speed costs money, slowly. Going from one node (6.1 days) to eight (1.0 day) raises the cost from $2,949 to $3,686, because efficiency drops from 100% to 80% in this model. Choose the node count by the deadline you have, not by the lowest price; the price difference is smaller than the uncertainty in the MFU.
  • Failure rates scale with the number of GPUs. More GPUs means more chances of a hardware fault in a given day, and a bigger loss on each restart unless checkpoints are frequent. This is where the lost-work part of the overhead multiplier comes from (Checkpoints, Failures & Long Runs).
  • Calendar time also includes waiting. Cloud accounts have GPU quotas that new accounts often have to request, and a node may not be available on the day you want it. Budget a week of lead time in the schedule, and ask for quota at the start. None of that appears in the GPU-hours.
  • The schedule is sequential. Proxy runs decide the mixture, the main run needs the mixture, the SFT sweeps need the main checkpoint. The total calendar time is the sum of the stages plus gaps, not the maximum.

6Count storage and data movement

List everything that must be stored, with sizes. What is the largest item? What does a checkpoint cost in GPU time as well as in dollars?

Compare with a worked answer

Storage is rarely the big line in a bill, but it is the one people forget to size and, more often, to delete. A checkpoint is a saved snapshot of a training run, from which it can restart. For the main run it holds the bf16 weights (14 GB) plus the fp32 master copy and Adam moments (84 GB), 98 GB in total (gradients are not saved). The weights-only snapshot that you evaluate and share is 14 GB.

"""Storage and data movement for the plan. Prices are list-price style assumptions, not a quote."""
GB = 1e9
STORE_PER_GB_MONTH = 0.023     # $ per GB-month, object storage, standard tier (assumption)
EGRESS_PER_GB = 0.09           # $ per GB leaving the provider (assumption; some providers charge nothing)
months = 2

tokens, bytes_per_token_text, bytes_per_token_ids = 40e9, 4.2, 2     # 2-byte ids need a vocabulary under 65,536
raw = tokens * bytes_per_token_text / GB
tokenised = tokens * bytes_per_token_ids / GB
ckpt_weights = 7e9 * 2 / GB                                          # bf16 weights only
ckpt_full = 7e9 * (2 + 4 + 4 + 4) / GB                               # + fp32 master weights and Adam m, v (gradients are not saved)
items = {
    "raw text, cleaned and deduplicated (2 copies)": 2 * raw,
    "tokenised corpus (uint16 ids)": tokenised,
    "CPT: last 3 full checkpoints (weights + optimiser)": 3 * ckpt_full,
    "CPT: weights-only snapshots every 2,500 steps (x4)": 4 * ckpt_weights,
    "SFT full sweep: 6 runs x 2 weights-only checkpoints": 6 * 2 * ckpt_weights,
    "LoRA sweep: 10 runs x ~170 MB adapters x 3": 10 * 3 * 0.17,

  • The corpus and the full-state checkpoints dominate (about 336 + 294 GB of the 959 GB). Keep the last three full checkpoints and delete older ones; keep weights-only snapshots of milestones.
  • The money is small; the time is not. At an assumed $0.023 per GB-month, two months of storage is about $44. But writing a 98 GB checkpoint at an assumed 2 GB/s stalls an 8-GPU job for 49 seconds, and 20 saves cost 2.2 GPU-hours. Saving more often reduces lost work after a failure and increases the stall; the right frequency balances the two.
  • Egress (leaving the provider) is the surprise charge. Moving 47 GB out once costs about $4 at an assumed $0.09 per GB, but copying a full checkpoint out repeatedly, or serving a model from another cloud, would not. Some providers charge nothing for egress. Check the line in the provider's price list before you choose where the data lives.
  • Data prep is not in these numbers. Tokenising 40B tokens takes CPU time and disk, and the raw corpus has to come from somewhere. If it is bought or licensed, that is another line.

  • GPU time is 99% of the bill. Storage and egress together are under $50. Scrutinise GPU assumptions; do not spend a meeting on object-storage tiers.
  • The base case is $8,449. Of that, $5,250 is useful compute and $3,150 is the cost of lost work and idle time. The overhead is 37% of the bill, which is why the multiplier needs defending.
  • Spot is cheaper per hour and not free. Interruptible capacity at an assumed $1.20 per GPU-hour, with rework rising from 20% to 50% because of interruptions, gives about $5,089. It only works if checkpoints are frequent enough that an interruption costs minutes (stage 6) and if the job restarts without a human. A run that needs a person to restart it is not a spot run.
  • What is left out matters more than what is in. A real request adds staff time, annotation, CPU preprocessing, API calls for judging, and a contingency line. Present them separately so the funder can see the GPU number is a floor on the project cost, not the project cost.

The $2.50 per GPU-hour used here is an assumption, not a quote. Public roundups I could read in 2026 (May 2026 is the latest I found) put H100 on-demand rental from about $1.25 to well over $10 per GPU-hour depending on provider, variant and billing model: specialist clouds mostly between about $2 and $3.50, hyperscalers higher, spot capacity much lower, and a market average near $3.60 reported by one roundup. Several of these pages are published by GPU sellers and the sources disagree, so treat them as leads, not benchmarks. Newer generations keep pushing older prices down. Read the provider's own pricing page on the day you write the request, record the date beside the price, and keep it as an input you can change (see also the dated box in chapter 17).

8Name the risky assumptions

Pick a best and a worst plausible value for each uncertain input, before running anything. Which assumption moves the bill most? If you vary all of them at once, where does your base case fall in the range?

Compare with a worked answer

A sensitivity analysis changes one input at a time and records the effect on the answer; it tells you where to spend your checking time. A Monte Carlo estimate draws all the uncertain inputs at random from their ranges together, many times, and reports the spread of the answer. The ranges below are written down as a best and a worst case, with the base case as the most likely value, before the script runs. They are judgements, and the script makes them visible so that someone can argue with them.

import numpy as np
from dataclasses import replace
from budget import *

BASE = dict(mfu_factor=1.0, price=2.50, cpt_tokens=40e9, rework=0.20, idle=0.25, sweep_scale=1.0, proxy_runs=6)
RANGE = {                    # (low, high): a plausible best and worst case, written down BEFORE the run
    "mfu_factor": (1.25, 0.65), "price": (1.80, 4.00), "cpt_tokens": (30e9, 60e9),
    "rework": (0.10, 0.60), "idle": (0.10, 0.45), "sweep_scale": (0.5, 2.0), "proxy_runs": (3, 12),
}
def bill(mfu_factor, price, cpt_tokens, rework, idle, sweep_scale, proxy_runs):
    plan = []
    for p in PLAN:
        runs = p.runs
        if p.name.startswith("Proxy"): runs = proxy_runs
        elif p.name.startswith("SFT"): runs = p.runs * sweep_scale
        tokens = cpt_tokens if p.name.startswith("Continued") else p.tokens
        plan.append(replace(p, runs=runs, tokens=tokens, mfu=p.mfu * mfu_factor))
    useful = sum(p.gpu_hours() for p in plan) + eval_gpu_hours() + SMOKE_GPU_HOURS
    return useful * overhead(rework, idle) * price + 959 * 0.023 * 2 + 47 * 0.09

base = bill(**BASE)
print(f"base case ${base:,.0f}\n")
print(f"{'assumption':<12}{'best case':>12}{'worst case':>12}{'bill at best':>14}{'bill at worst':>15}{'swing':>9}")
rows = []
for k, (lo, hi) in RANGE.items():
    b_lo, b_hi = bill(**{**BASE, k: lo}), bill(**{**BASE, k: hi})
    rows.append((b_hi - b_lo, k, lo, hi, b_lo, b_hi))
for swing, k, lo, hi, b_lo, b_hi in sorted(rows, reverse=True):

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
D = 20_000
draws = {k: rng.triangular(min(lo, hi), BASE[k], max(lo, hi), D) for k, (lo, hi) in RANGE.items()}
draws["proxy_runs"] = np.round(draws["proxy_runs"])
out = np.array([bill(**{k: v[i] for k, v in draws.items()}) for i in range(D)])
p10, p50, p90 = np.percentile(out, [10, 50, 90])
print(f"\nMonte Carlo over all seven at once ({D:,} draws, triangular): P10 ${p10:,.0f}  P50 ${p50:,.0f}  P90 ${p90:,.0f}")
print(f"base case is at the {np.mean(out < base):.0%} percentile; chance the bill exceeds 2x base: {np.mean(out > 2 * base):.1%}")
rank = lambda x: np.argsort(np.argsort(x))
print("rank correlation with the bill:", ", ".join(f"{k} {np.corrcoef(rank(v), rank(out))[0, 1]:+.2f}" for k, v in draws.items()))

  • The base case is optimistic. 9Plan the gates

Design three points at which you stop and decide whether to spend more. For each, what do you measure, what is the stop rule, and how much money is at risk at that point? Is there a cheaper plan that still answers the question?

Compare with a worked answer

A budget is not only a forecast; it is also a control. The cheapest way to cut the risk of an expensive run is to arrange the plan so that the early, cheap stages produce the information that decides the expensive ones. Each gate has a measurement, a rule and a cost at the point of decision.

from budget import *

  • Test small first. Run everything at 1B on a few million tokens before the large run. The smoke test belongs to this: the same code path, smaller sizes, a few cents (chapter 17).
  • Checkpoint, and let the machine restart itself. An unattended restart is what makes spot capacity viable, and shortens every failure.
  • Turn it off. Idle GPUs are the largest source of unplanned spend. Tag every instance with an owner and an expiry time, and have a scheduled job that lists what is running.
  • Log cost with the run. GPU-hours and dollars go into the same experiment record as the seed and the configuration (chapter 4), so a later comparison knows what each result cost.

Systems connection

This is capacity planning with an error bar. Load Testing & Capacity Planning asks you to measure a small system and extrapolate; Cost & Efficiency asks you to keep utilisation high and idle capacity low; Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation asks you to state assumptions and check the biggest one first. All three apply here, with GPUs in place of web servers.

Rubric

Tick what your own budget (before reading the answers) covered. The first items are what a solid answer has; the later ones separate a senior answer.

  • Said what the number is for, what is in scope and what is not (staff, annotation, serving, preprocessing).
  • Wrote the plan as a table of phases with model size, tokens processed (not epochs), runs and elasticity.
  • Used 6ND for dense training, noted LoRA's lower cost, and used measured throughput for generation.
  • Turned FLOPs into hours with peak times MFU, stated the MFU, and said how it will be measured.
  • Did the memory fit check (16 bytes per parameter for Adam training plus activations) before costing a phase.
  • Chose the node shape from the fit check, and noticed that whole nodes are billed.
  • Separated GPU-hours from calendar time, with a scaling-efficiency assumption and a note on quota and lead time.
  • Sized storage and checkpoints, including the GPU time lost to writing them, and checked egress charges.
  • Added an explicit overhead multiplier for lost work and idle time, labelled as an assumption, and read the paper's figure as the cost of the final run only.
  • Kept the price as a dated, changeable input, noting that sources disagree.
  • Ran a sensitivity analysis, ranked the assumptions, and noticed that the base case is optimistic relative to the combined range.
  • Gave a range with percentiles and asked for a ceiling above the base case, ideally released in steps.
  • Designed gates with a measurement, a stop rule and money at risk, and offered a cheaper alternative plan.
  • Listed cost controls: smoke tests, unattended restarts, expiry tags, spend alerts, cost logged with each run.

Key takeaways

  • A budget is a chain: tokens and parameters, FLOPs (6ND), GPU-hours (peak × MFU), overhead, price, plus storage. Write each link as a named input.
  • One phase usually dominates, and one multiplier (overhead) is usually the least defended number. Defend it with a measurement or label it as an assumption.
  • Check memory before cost: a job that does not fit does not run. Training state is about 16 bytes per parameter, eight times the bf16 weights.
  • GPU-hours and calendar time are different. More GPUs finish sooner at a slightly higher cost, and quota and failures add waiting and rework.
  • Storage and egress are small in dollars here; checkpoint writes cost GPU time, and forgotten data and idle instances cost real money.
  • A base case built from everything going well sits low in the distribution. Ask for a ceiling from the median or higher, released in steps, with gates.
  • Rank assumptions by swing and spend your time on the top two. Price and utilisation are the cheapest to narrow and the most valuable to narrow.
