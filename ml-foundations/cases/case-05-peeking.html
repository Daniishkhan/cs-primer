Peeking at A/B Tests

An online experiment runs for two weeks, and a dashboard shows a "chance to beat baseline" that updates with every visitor. It is natural to look every day and stop when the number crosses 95%. That habit quietly breaks the guarantee the number was built on. This case follows the problem from Evan Miller's 2010 warning "How Not To Run an A/B Test" to Johari, Pekelis and Walsh's "Always Valid Inference: Bringing Sequential Analysis to A/B Testing" (arXiv 2015, published at KDD 2017 as "Peeking at A/B Tests: Why It Matters, and What to Do About It"), whose methods run inside a commercial experimentation platform. I read the sources and then reproduced the effect in a simulation of my own.

The pattern in one sentence

A significance test is valid only for the stopping rule it assumed, so decide in advance when you will stop, or use a method whose guarantee holds at whatever moment you choose to stop.

Context: a dashboard that invites a mistake

Online experiments produce data continuously. A web shop splits visitors at random between the current page (control) and a new one (treatment) and counts purchases in each group. Unlike a clinical trial, nothing stops anyone looking at the running totals, and looking is cheap: a longer experiment delays the next idea, so there is a strong incentive to stop as soon as the answer seems clear. Johari, Pekelis and Walsh say as much: the opportunity cost of longer experiments is large, and most users lack a good prior sense of the effect size they are after, so they cannot pick the run-time well in advance.

The trouble is that the p-values and confidence intervals on the dashboard were designed for a single look at a sample size fixed beforehand. Chapter 15 showed the arithmetic: with no real difference, a test has a 5% chance of crossing the 1.96 line at the planned end, but a path that is allowed to be inspected many times has many more chances to cross it. Figure 1 of the paper shows exactly this on a real platform dashboard: an A/A test (both arms identical) whose "chance to beat baseline", which is 1 − pn, rises above the 95% line for a user who watches continuously, producing a false positive.

A/B test
A randomised comparison of two versions of something (control and treatment) on a metric such as conversion rate.
A/A test
An experiment where both arms are identical. Any "winner" it declares is a false positive, which makes it a clean way to measure a procedure's error rate.
Type I error
Declaring an effect when there is none. A test at level α = 0.05 promises this happens at most 5% of the time under its assumed stopping rule.
Peeking
Checking significance repeatedly during a run and using what you see to decide whether to stop.
Fixed-horizon test
A test whose sample size is chosen before data arrive, analysed once at the end.
Sequential test
A test designed so that its error guarantee survives repeated looks or a data-dependent stopping time.
Always valid p-value
A p-value process (p1, p2, ...) whose guarantee P(pT ≤ s) ≤ s holds for every stopping time T, so you may stop whenever you like (Johari et al.'s Definition 1).

The problem, in the sources' numbers

Three sources quantify the damage, with different set-ups, so the numbers are not interchangeable.

  • Miller (2010) simulates a test with a 50% baseline conversion rate in which an experimenter stops either at 5% significance or after 150 observations, with a logo change that does nothing. He reports a false positive rate of 26.1%, "more than five times" the nominal 5%, and calls this a worst case because significance is checked after every observation, though "not unheard-of". He also reports that to keep a true 5% level after ten peeks the reported threshold must be about 1%, and criticises one testing framework that stops experiments automatically on significance.
  • Johari, Pekelis and Walsh state that with 10,000 samples, "a sample size which is quite common in online A/B testing", Type I error "can easily increase fivefold" when users monitor continuously.
  • The Optimizely post (2015) reports simulated A/A tests with 5,000 visitors and a 5% target: looking after every visitor gave a false winner or loser at least once in 57% of tests; looking every 500 visitors, 26%; every 1,000 visitors, 20%. These are the company's own simulations.

Why peeking inflates false positives

Take one A/A test and watch its z statistic, the difference between the arms divided by its standard error. Early on, with few visitors, the z statistic swings widely; as data accumulate it settles towards zero, but it never stops moving. The planned-end rule asks one question, "is |z| above 1.96 on day 14?", and noise answers yes 5% of the time. The peeking rule asks fourteen questions and stops at the first yes. The events are positively correlated (today's z is mostly yesterday's z), so the false positive rate is not 14 × 5%, but it is far above 5%.

Common misconception

"If I stop at p < 0.05 the result is still 95% reliable, because p < 0.05 is what I saw." The p-value is a statement about a procedure: how often a rule of the form "run, then test once" would fire by chance. A rule of the form "keep going until it fires" is a different procedure with a different error rate, and the dashboard number does not know which one you are using. Miller's formulation is the clearest: the reported significance assumes the sample size was fixed in advance.

The three ways out

1. Fix the horizon and keep to it

Miller's advice for experimenters is to fix the sample size in advance (his rule of thumb is n = 16σ2/δ2 per group, for a minimum effect δ and variance σ2; see chapter 15 for power), to ignore the dashboard's verdict until the end, and, if you do look, never to stop early. This is valid and simple, and it is what you should do when you can. Its cost is flexibility: if the effect is huge you wait anyway, and if the effect is smaller than you planned for, you cannot extend the test without paying for it statistically.

2. Group sequential tests: plan the looks

Medicine faced this problem first, because trial monitoring committees must look at accumulating data for ethical reasons. Group sequential designs choose, in advance, K looks and a stricter threshold at each so that the overall chance of a false positive is still 5%. Miller names this route (sequential experiment design, citing Pocock, 1977). Three simple boundaries for 14 daily looks, calibrated by my own simulation of null random walks:

  • Bonferroni: use α/14 at every look (|z| above about 2.91). Valid, but wasteful: it spends less than the full 5%.
  • Pocock-style: one constant boundary chosen so the total is 5% (about 2.62 for 14 looks).
  • O'Brien–Fleming-style: a boundary proportional to 1/√t, very strict early and close to 1.96 at the end (here 2.11/√t where t is the fraction of the planned sample seen). It costs little power if the test runs to the end.

These need the number and spacing of looks fixed in advance. Lan and DeMets (1983) relaxed that with alpha-spending functions, as chapter 15 mentions; I did not read that paper.

3. Always valid inference: let the user stop whenever

Johari, Pekelis and Walsh start from a different requirement: the platform's users are not statisticians, they have different priorities, and they will look when they like. They want to keep the p-value "interface" (threshold p at your own α) but make it survive any stopping rule. Their Definition 1 does exactly that: a sequence of p-values (pn) is an always valid p-value process if for any stopping time T (any rule that decides, from the data seen so far, whether to stop) and every s between 0 and 1, Pθ0(pT ≤ s) ≤ s. Their Theorem 1 says such processes correspond one to one with sequential tests, so any good sequential test gives you one.

The construction they use is the mixture sequential probability ratio test (mSPRT). For each possible true effect θ, the likelihood ratio compares how well θ explains the data against no effect. You do not know θ, so you average this ratio over a prior-like mixing distribution H on θ, giving a single number Λn after n observations: the accumulated evidence against the null. The test rejects the first time Λn reaches 1/α, and the paper notes that the threshold ensures Type I error control via standard martingale techniques (Siegmund, 1985). The resulting p-value is the smallest threshold level the evidence has ever cleared: pn = min(1, 1/Λ1, ..., 1/Λn), a number that can only fall. (This running-minimum form is my reading of Theorem 1 applied to the mSPRT, not a formula quoted from the paper.)

For normal data with variance V per observation and a Normal(0, τ2) mixing distribution, the average has a closed form, with m the running mean of the (treatment minus control) differences:

Λn = √( V / (V + nτ2) ) × exp( n2τ2m2 / (2V(V + nτ2)) )

I derived this for the toy below (it is the standard Gaussian integral; the paper treats a more general case and a two-stream version with unknown means) and checked it against brute-force numerical integration of the mixture: both give 0.664143 for n = 5,000 pairs and a sum of 25 (the check is in the second script below).

What does this cost? The paper makes three points. First, mSPRTs are "tests of power one": a user who can wait forever will eventually detect any real effect. Second, the choice of mixing distribution matters mainly in the ballpark: in their simulations with a normal prior, a mixing variance wrong by about one order of magnitude lost under 5% of average power and added no more than 10% to average run-length, but missing by two orders of magnitude could lose 20% of power and add 40% to run-length. Third, the Type I error guarantee does not depend on the mixing choice at all, only efficiency does. On 10,000 real experiments from the platform, split by customer tier, the authors compared the sample size at which their two-stream mSPRT stopped with that of a fixed-horizon test chosen for 80% average power under a fitted prior over effect sizes; for every tier the ratio fell below one with high probability. They also report that a fixed-horizon analyst would need to know the true effect to within a relative error of about 50% to beat the mSPRT's run-time. These are the authors' own results on their own platform's data, and I could not check them independently.

Try it yourself: peeking, bought back by stricter rules

The script simulates 8,000 two-arm tests for each of two worlds: no real difference, and a 10% relative lift on a 10% conversion rate. Each test runs 14 days with 1,000 visitors per arm per day (14,000 per arm), and every rule below sees the same data. For each rule it counts the share of tests declared significant. In the null world that share is the false positive rate; in the lift world it is the power. The mSPRT uses the closed form above with τ = 0.01 and is read only at the daily looks. It uses NumPy.

"""Peeking at A/B tests: 14 days, 1,000 visitors per arm per day, 10% base conversion rate."""
import numpy as np

P, DAYS, PER_DAY, SIMS = 0.10, 14, 1_000, 8_000
V = 2 * P * (1 - P)                    # variance of one (treatment - control) pair
TAU2 = 0.01 ** 2                       # mSPRT: prior variance of the true difference
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

def daily_sums(lift):
    """Cumulative sum of (treatment - control) pairs, read at the end of each day."""
    n = DAYS * PER_DAY
    x = rng.random((SIMS, n)) < P * (1 + lift)
    y = rng.random((SIMS, n)) < P
    cs = np.cumsum(x.astype(np.int8) - y.astype(np.int8), axis=1, dtype=np.int32)
    idx = np.arange(1, DAYS + 1) * PER_DAY
    return cs[:, idx - 1], idx         # shape (SIMS, DAYS), pairs seen at each look

def boundaries():
    """Critical |z| per look. Constants calibrated on 1,000,000 simulated null walks."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(7)
    z = np.abs(np.cumsum(r.standard_normal((1_000_000, DAYS)), axis=1) / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, DAYS + 1)))
    t = np.arange(1, DAYS + 1) / DAYS
    pocock = np.quantile(z.max(axis=1), 0.95)
    obf = np.quantile((z * np.sqrt(t)).max(axis=1), 0.95)
    return {"fixed 1.96": np.full(DAYS, 1.96), "Bonferroni": np.full(DAYS, 2.91),
            "Pocock-style": np.full(DAYS, pocock), "O'Brien-Fleming-style": obf / np.sqrt(t)}

def msprt_p(s, n):
    """Always-valid p-value: running minimum of 1 / (mixture likelihood ratio), Normal(0, TAU2) mixing."""
    m = s / n
    lam = np.sqrt(V / (V + n * TAU2)) * np.exp(n * n * TAU2 * m * m / (2 * V * (V + n * TAU2)))
    return np.minimum.accumulate(np.minimum(1.0, 1.0 / lam), axis=1)

bounds = boundaries()
for lift in (0.0, 0.10):
    s, n = daily_sums(lift)
    z = np.abs(s / np.sqrt(V * n))
    print(f"true relative lift {lift:.0%}: share of {SIMS} tests declared significant")
    print(f"  {'one look at day 14':38s}{(z[:, -1] > 1.96).mean():7.1%}")
    print(f"  {'peek daily, stop at 1.96':38s}{(z > 1.96).any(axis=1).mean():7.1%}")
    for name in ("Bonferroni", "Pocock-style", "O'Brien-Fleming-style"):
        print(f"  {name + ', daily looks':38s}{(z > bounds[name]).any(axis=1).mean():7.1%}")
    print(f"  {'mSPRT, daily looks':38s}{(msprt_p(s, n) <= 0.05).any(axis=1).mean():7.1%}")
true relative lift 0%: share of 8000 tests declared significant
  one look at day 14                       5.1%
  peek daily, stop at 1.96                22.4%
  Bonferroni, daily looks                  2.3%
  Pocock-style, daily looks                5.1%
  O'Brien-Fleming-style, daily looks       5.1%
  mSPRT, daily looks                       1.1%
true relative lift 10%: share of 8000 tests declared significant
  one look at day 14                      78.5%
  peek daily, stop at 1.96                86.3%
  Bonferroni, daily looks                 53.4%
  Pocock-style, daily looks               65.7%
  O'Brien-Fleming-style, daily looks      77.1%
  mSPRT, daily looks                      47.6%

Read the first block. One look at the planned end gives 5.1%, as promised. Peeking daily and stopping at 1.96 gives 22.4%, more than four times the promise, so more than one A/A test in five produces a winner. The three planned-look boundaries bring the rate back to about 5% (Pocock-style and O'Brien–Fleming-style, which I calibrated to hit it) or below it (Bonferroni, 2.3%). The mSPRT lands at 1.1%, well under 5%: its guarantee is an upper bound, and with this τ and only 14,000 pairs it is conservative.

The second block is the price. Peeking looks like the most powerful rule (86.3% against 78.5% for one look), but its extra "power" is bought partly with the same false positives. Among the honest rules the O'Brien–Fleming-style boundary loses almost nothing (77.1%), the Pocock-style constant loses more (65.7%), Bonferroni more (53.4%), and the mSPRT at this sample size loses the most (47.6%), because it holds no particular sample size in mind and cannot use the fact that the test will be stopped at day 14 anyway. In exchange you may stop on any day, or not at all.

A second script asks two questions the first cannot: when does the false win happen, and does stopping early distort the estimate?

import numpy as np
from math import sqrt, exp
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
P, N, LOOKS, SIMS = 0.10, 14_000, 14, 8_000
V, TAU2 = 2*P*(1-P), 0.01**2

# 1. closed form of the mixture likelihood ratio vs brute-force numeric integration
n, s = 5000, 25.0                       # n pairs, sum of differences
m = s / n
theta = np.linspace(-0.2, 0.2, 400_001)
lr = np.exp((n/V) * (theta*m - theta**2/2))              # N(theta, V/n) over N(0, V/n) density ratio at mean m
prior = np.exp(-theta**2/(2*TAU2)) / sqrt(2*np.pi*TAU2)
numeric = np.trapezoid(lr*prior, theta)
closed = sqrt(V/(V+n*TAU2)) * exp(n*n*TAU2*m*m/(2*V*(V+n*TAU2)))
print("closed form", round(closed, 6), " numeric", round(float(numeric), 6))

# 2. which day does a null peeker declare a win, and what does a stopped real winner estimate?
def sims(lift):
    x = rng.random((SIMS, N)) < P*(1+lift); y = rng.random((SIMS, N)) < P
    cs = np.cumsum(x.astype(np.int8)-y.astype(np.int8), axis=1, dtype=np.int32)
    day = np.arange(1, LOOKS+1)*N//LOOKS - 1
    z = cs[:, day]/np.sqrt(V*(day+1))
    est = cs[:, day]/(day+1)/P                              # relative lift estimate
    return z, est
z, est = sims(0.0)
hit = (np.abs(z) > 1.96).any(axis=1)
first = (np.abs(z) > 1.96).argmax(axis=1) + 1
print("A/A tests ever 'significant':", f"{hit.mean():.1%}")
print("day of first false win (share of all A/A tests):", {d: f"{((first==d)&hit).mean():.1%}" for d in (1,2,3,5,7,14)})
print("A/A tests that were 'significant' on day 1 alone:", f"{(np.abs(z[:,0])>1.96).mean():.1%}")
z, est = sims(0.10)
win = (z > 1.96).any(axis=1)
f = (z > 1.96).argmax(axis=1)
stop_est = est[np.arange(SIMS), f]
print("true lift 10%; peeker stops on a positive win in", f"{win.mean():.1%}", "of tests; mean estimated lift at stopping", f"{stop_est[win].mean():.1%}")
print("same tests read at day 14 (no peeking): mean estimate", f"{est[:, -1].mean():.1%}")

A/A tests ever 'significant': 21.7%
day of first false win (share of all A/A tests): {1: '4.8%', 2: '3.0%', 3: '2.6%', 5: '1.3%', 7: '1.3%', 14: '0.6%'}

(This run uses a different random draw from the first script, so the "ever significant" share is 21.7% rather than 22.4%; the gap is simulation noise.) Almost a quarter of the false alarms arrive on day 1 alone, when one look is already a 4.8% chance, and the share of tests that fire for the first time falls steadily with the day, because each new look has fewer chances left to add. And the estimates are biased: when the true lift is 10% and the peeker stops on the first positive significant look, the mean estimated lift at the stopping time is 16.6%, while the same tests read at day 14 average 10.0%. Stopping when the data look good selects the lucky moments, so early-stopped winners overstate the effect, whichever method flagged them. Section "Limits and criticism" returns to this.

Try it yourself

  1. In peeking_toy.py change DAYS to 2, then 5, then 28 (keep PER_DAY at 1,000) and watch the "peek daily" false positive rate on the null world. Chapter 15 reports 8.3% for 2 looks and 14.0% for 5 under a different set-up; do you get similar values? Note that the boundary function needs no change because it recomputes for any DAYS.
  2. Change TAU2 to 0.001 ** 2 and then 0.05 ** 2. How do the mSPRT's false positive rate and power move? The paper says the Type I error guarantee does not depend on τ; check that your numbers agree and that only the power changes.
  3. Build the "watch every visitor" version: compute msprt_p on the full cumulative sum rather than on the daily readings. Does the false positive rate stay under 5%? Compare with the daily version.

What the vendor did with it

The Optimizely post says that its Stats Engine, built with "a team of Stanford statisticians" (unnamed in the post), began powering results for all its customers on 21 January 2015. Its methods are sequential testing (an average likelihood ratio updated as each visitor arrives, which the post describes as a test of power one) plus control of the false discovery rate across many goals and variations. The vendor reports that the new engine reduces incorrect winner or loser calls "from 30% to 5%" without sacrificing speed, that in its simulated A/A tests with checks after every visitor the sequential error fell to 3% (bounded by 5%), and that it can be slower or faster than a fixed-horizon test depending on how far the true lift is from the effect size you had planned for. As I read the post, it says that if the experimenter had set the minimum detectable effect to within 5% of the actual lift, sequential testing was on average 60% slower. I report these as the company's claims: I did not see the data behind the headline figures and I did not read Optimizely's current documentation.

State of play, October 2026

The two sources that supply numbers for this case date from 2010 and 2015, and the paper was revised last in 2019. A web search I ran in October 2026 returned Optimizely documentation pages describing sequential testing combined with a tiered Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for false discovery rate across metrics and variations, but I did not fetch or verify them. I did not survey how other experimentation platforms handle peeking today (some use fixed-horizon tests with warnings, others sequential or Bayesian methods), and I make no claim about any specific vendor's current default. If you adopt a platform, read its documentation for what its p-value or interval guarantees and under which stopping rules.

Limits and criticism

  • Validity is not the same as efficiency. An always valid method protects the error rate but is not free. In my toy it had the lowest power at 14,000 pairs; the paper's argument is that the cost is modest when the mixing distribution is in the right ballpark and the user's stopping preferences are unknown. If your test has a well-estimated effect and a fixed budget, a fixed-horizon test or an O'Brien–Fleming plan can be more efficient.
  • Estimates after stopping are biased. My second script shows a 16.6% estimate for a true 10% lift among early-stopped winners. The error rate is controlled; the effect size you report is still inflated. I did not research corrections (conditioning or shrinkage); treat early-stopped estimates as upper-ish bounds and confirm large wins with a holdout or replication (chapter 15 calls this the winner's curse).
  • The statistics are not the only reason to wait. A day-of-week pattern, a novelty effect that fades, or users who return a week later can all make the first days unrepresentative. That is general experimental practice rather than anything these papers test, and it is a reason to prefer whole weeks even if the maths allows an earlier stop.
  • Peeking is one of several ways to spend your error budget. The paper extends the idea to many hypotheses by combining always valid p-values with multiple-testing control, and Optimizely's post stresses false discovery rate control for the same reason. Ten metrics and five variations produce many chances for a false win even with a fixed horizon.
  • Bayesian alternatives. Miller suggests Bayesian designs, which in his words allow stopping at any time while keeping inferences valid, as the likely direction. Whether a given Bayesian stopping rule controls frequentist error is a separate question that I did not research here.

Analogy: checking the oven door

A recipe says "bake for 40 minutes". Each time you open the door the temperature drops a little and the result changes, and a cook who opens it every five minutes and takes the cake out the instant it looks done is following a different recipe from the tested one. A fixed-horizon test is the original recipe. A group sequential design is a recipe that says "you may check at minutes 10, 20 and 30, and here is what to look for at each time". An always valid method is a recipe written for a cook who will open the door whenever they like.

Where it breaks down: opening an oven changes the cake, but looking at a dashboard changes nothing about the data. The damage is entirely in the decision rule, which is why the same data can be fine for one analyst and misleading for another.

Lessons, and where they return

  • Write the stopping rule down before the first visitor. The rule is part of the test. In Studio 1 you pick one and defend it, and Lab 5 has you demonstrate peeking in code. The whole idea reappears in evaluation of language models as soon as you re-run a benchmark until the new prompt wins: see A6, chapter 4.
  • Search over many variants is a form of peeking. An evolutionary search that keeps the best of many candidates according to a noisy score is choosing a stopping point (or a winner) after seeing the data, and needs a held-out test for the same reason: A7, chapter 16.
  • Online experiments are how models earn their place. A new ranker or prompt is judged by an A/B test after offline metrics; a fixed horizon, a pre-agreed minimum effect and a guardrail metric are the defaults. See A1, chapter 21.
  • The same trade-off sits inside operations. Automated canary analysis decides, on a short sample, whether a new release is worse than the old one; that is a sequential test with a power problem, and multi-window burn-rate alerts are another way of trading speed against false alarms.
  • Always ask who chose the stopping time. A result is only as trustworthy as the process that decided to stop. A report that says "we ran until significant" tells you the error rate is not the one on the label.

Key takeaways

  • A p-value is valid for a stopping rule. Peeking daily and stopping at the first |z| above 1.96 turned a 5% error rate into 22.4% in my 14-look simulation; Miller reported 26.1% in his, and the Optimizely post 57% for checks after every visitor.
  • Three remedies: fix the horizon (Miller), plan the looks with stricter boundaries (group sequential, Pocock and O'Brien–Fleming styles), or use always valid p-values from an mSPRT so any stopping time is safe (Johari, Pekelis and Walsh).
  • Validity costs power or flexibility. In my toy at 14,000 pairs per arm, power for a 10% lift was 78.5% for one look, 77.1% for an O'Brien–Fleming-style plan and 47.6% for the mSPRT.
  • Early-stopped winners overstate the effect (16.6% against a true 10% in my simulation).
  • The same logic applies to any search or monitoring loop that decides when to stop after seeing the data.

Check your understanding

In an A/A test the team checks a 5% significance test every day for 14 days and stops at the first significant result. Which false positive rate is the best estimate?

  1. 5%, because each daily test is at the 5% level
  2. About 70% (14 × 5%)
  3. Roughly 20%, well above 5% but far below 14 × 5%
  4. Below 5%, because stopping early saves data
The simulation gives 22.4%. It is not 5%, because the rule is a different procedure from "test once". It is not 14 × 5%, because daily z statistics share most of their data and are strongly correlated, so the chances overlap.

What does Definition 1 of Johari, Pekelis and Walsh require of an always valid p-value process?

  1. P(pT ≤ s) ≤ s under the null for every stopping time T
  2. That pn is below 0.05 at the planned sample size
  3. That the experimenter looks at most once per day
  4. That the test has 80% power at every sample size
The guarantee must hold at any data-dependent stopping time, including infinite ones. Option 2 is just a fixed-horizon test; option 3 limits looking, which the method does not need to; option 4 confuses validity (Type I error) with power.

An experiment is stopped early on a significant positive result, and the true lift is 10%. What does the simulation suggest about the reported lift?

  1. It is unbiased if a sequential method flagged the result
  2. It is biased downwards because early data are noisy
  3. It averages about 10% because the p-value is valid
  4. It is biased upwards, about 16.6% in the simulation, because stopping selects lucky moments
Controlling the Type I error rate says nothing about the estimate at a data-dependent stopping time. Tests that stop on good-looking data are selected for upward noise. Option 1 is the tempting one: a valid error rate does not make the estimate unbiased.

In the toy, the mSPRT had the lowest power (47.6%) and a false positive rate of 1.1%. What is the right reading?

  1. The mSPRT is broken because it is below 5%
  2. The Type I guarantee is an upper bound, and its flexibility to stop at any time is paid for with power at this fixed, modest sample size
  3. Larger τ would raise the false positive rate above 5%
  4. It would beat a fixed-horizon test at every sample size
The paper says validity does not depend on the mixing distribution; τ affects efficiency only. The rule is conservative at small n and gives up some power compared with a plan for one fixed length. Option 3 contradicts the validity result; option 4 overstates the paper's claim, which compares run-times under assumptions about the effect-size distribution.
