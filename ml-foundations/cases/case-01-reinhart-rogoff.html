A Spreadsheet Error That Shaped Policy

In 2010 two economists published a table showing that rich countries with public debt above 90% of GDP had grown by an average of −0.1% a year. In 2013 a team at the University of Massachusetts Amherst asked them for their working spreadsheet, rebuilt the calculation, and got +2.2%. The gap came from three choices, one of them a plain spreadsheet range error, and none of them visible in the paper. This case is about what an analysis hides when only its output is published.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the full text of the primary source: Herndon, Ash and Pollin, "Does high public debt consistently stifle economic growth? A critique of Reinhart and Rogoff", the peer-reviewed version in the Cambridge Journal of Economics (advance access, December 2013). It quotes Reinhart and Rogoff (RR) and reprints their published table, but I did not read RR's own papers, their New York Times replies or their errata memorandum; where I describe them, I am relying on the critique's quotations and on one fetched news piece (Retraction Watch, 18 April 2013). The story of how the error was found (a doctoral student failing to reproduce the table in a class exercise) comes only from search-result summaries of NPR and Chronicle of Higher Education coverage; my attempt to fetch the NPR transcript timed out, so treat that detail as secondary. I did not survey the later economics literature on debt and growth: this case is about the calculation, not about whether high debt harms growth. The re-computation in the "Try it yourself" section uses the country figures printed in the critique's tables, not the original spreadsheet.

The pattern in one sentence

A published number is the end of a chain of choices (which rows, which averaging, which formula range), so an analysis is only checkable if the chain, not just the result, is shared, written down and tested.

Context: a clean claim that arrived at the right moment

After the 2007 to 2009 financial crisis, many governments faced large deficits and an argument about whether to cut spending quickly. Reinhart and Rogoff's paper "Growth in a Time of Debt" (2010) gave that argument a number. It sorted 20 advanced economies' years into four bands of public debt as a share of GDP and compared average growth in each band. As reprinted in the critique, the bands and the results for 1946 to 2009 were:

Public debt / GDPMean real GDP growth, per year (as published)
30% or below4.1%
30% to 60%2.8%
60% to 90%2.8%
above 90%−0.1%

The pattern was a "cliff" at 90%. The critique quotes RR's summary: "median growth rates for countries with public debt over roughly 90 percent of GDP are about one percent lower than otherwise; average (mean) growth rates are several percent lower." The method is appealingly simple (sort, then average), which is part of why the result travelled.

It travelled far. The critique documents, from RR's own website and other sources, that the findings underpinned testimony by Reinhart to a US House committee in March 2011, were the only evidence on debt and growth cited in a 2013 US federal budget plan proposed by Congressman Paul Ryan and passed by the House, and were frequently cited by the UK Chancellor and the European Commission's economic commissioner. It quotes the economist Paul Krugman writing in June 2013 that the paper "may have had more immediate influence on public debate than any previous paper in the history of economics". (That is Krugman's judgement as reported by the critique; I did not read the original.) The critique's authors add that the paper's influence on austerity policy is "an indisputable fact" regardless of RR's intentions, and say they do not try to judge those intentions. How much the paper caused any particular policy is a separate, contested question that I do not try to settle.

Replication
Re-running a published analysis, ideally from the original data and code, to see whether the same result comes out. A critical replication also asks whether the choices made were sound.
Working spreadsheet
The file in which the numbers were actually computed. Public data files and the working file can differ in exactly the ways that matter.
Country-year
One country in one year: the unit of observation in this dataset. Ten countries spent 110 country-years above 90% debt.
Weighting
How much each observation counts when you average. Equal weight per country-year is one choice; equal weight per country is another.
Stylised fact
An economist's term for a robust empirical regularity that later theory must explain. The critique's complaint is that this one was not robust.

The mechanism: how one cell is made

To produce the "above 90%" figure, someone has to decide which rows enter, how to combine them and what formula to type. Each decision is small and each has a defensible version and an indefensible one. The paper reported none of them. The critique says RR publicly posted country-level spreadsheets with source documentation, but those files did not say which series, years and methods were used in the paper, so the authors could not reproduce the table from them. They asked RR for the working file, and RR sent it on 4 April 2013. With it, they say, "we were able to approximate closely the published RR results" and so locate the three problems.

Problem A: years left out, with no explanation

RR's 1946 to 2009 sample leaves out the early post-war years for three countries that were above 90% debt then: Australia (1946 to 1950), Canada (1946 to 1950) and New Zealand (1946 to 1949). The critique reports that the paper never says so. The closest passage it can find is RR's remark that Australia and New Zealand showed "no growth deterioration at very high debt levels" but that "those high-growth high-debt observations are clustered in the years following World War II". The critique's objection is one of consistency: the same sample keeps the four post-war years for the United States, in three of which the US economy shrank while debt was above 90%. For New Zealand, the omitted years had growth of +7.7%, +11.9%, −9.9% and +10.8%; once they were removed, New Zealand contributed a single year (1951, growth −7.6%) to the top band.

Problem B: a range that stopped five rows short

The working spreadsheet had a plain coding error. To average growth by debt band, RR's formulas covered rows 30 to 44 of a block that runs to row 49 (and rows 5 to 19 instead of 5 to 24 in the 1790 to 2009 sheet). The countries are listed alphabetically, so the five missing rows were Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada and Denmark. They were dropped from every average, mean and median, in both samples. The critique notes that the omission is clearly accidental and says RR have since acknowledged it. It matters most for the top band because Belgium alone spent 25 years above 90%.

Problem C: one number per country

RR did not average all the country-years in a band. They first averaged within each country, then averaged those country averages. The critique calls this "means of country means". The consequences are easy to state in numbers: the UK spent 19 years above 90%, with average growth of 2.4%. New Zealand, after Problem A, spent one year there (−7.6%). Under RR's method each of the seven countries left in the cell counts for 1/7, or 14.3%, so New Zealand's single year weighs as much as the UK's nineteen. The critique found no argument for this in either version of the paper and notes RR's later reply that "It is the accusation that our weighting procedure is unconventional that is itself unconventional". It also grants that serial correlation within a country could justify down-weighting long runs, but says RR never made that case.

A fourth, smaller error

The critique also found a transcription slip when a country average was copied from a country sheet to the summary sheet: New Zealand's −7.6% became −7.9%. With only seven equal weights it moves the final figure by 0.1 percentage point.

Putting the pieces back together

The three problems interact, so the order in which you fix them does not matter but their combined size does. The critique's Table 5 switches each choice on and off. Everything needed to redo it for the top band is in its Table 4: the number of years each country spent above 90% and its average growth there. The script below uses those printed figures and nothing else. It is a re-computation of the top-band cell only, from the critique's tables; it is not RR's code or spreadsheet.

"""Rebuild the >90% debt/GDP cell of Reinhart-Rogoff from the country figures in
Herndon, Ash and Pollin (2013), Table 4, then switch each choice on and off."""
from itertools import product

# country: (years above 90%, mean growth in those years, %). Source: HAP Table 2 and Table 4.
full = {
    "Australia": (5, 3.8), "Belgium": (25, 2.6), "Canada": (5, 3.0), "Greece": (19, 2.9),
    "Ireland": (7, 2.4), "Italy": (10, 1.0), "Japan": (11, 0.7),
    "New Zealand": (5, 2.6), "UK": (19, 2.4), "USA": (4, -2.0),
}
NZ_ONE_YEAR = (1, -7.6)         # New Zealand after dropping 1946-49: only 1951 left, growth -7.6%
NZ_TRANSCRIBED = (1, -7.9)      # the figure typed into RR's summary sheet

def cell(spreadsheet_error, drop_years, country_weights, transcription=False):
    d = dict(full)
    if spreadsheet_error:                       # sum range stopped short: Aus, Aut, Bel, Can, Den vanish
        for c in ("Australia", "Belgium", "Canada"): d.pop(c)
    if drop_years:                              # 1946-50 for Aus, Can and 1946-49 for NZ left out
        d.pop("Australia", None); d.pop("Canada", None)
        d["New Zealand"] = NZ_TRANSCRIBED if transcription else NZ_ONE_YEAR
    if country_weights:                         # mean of country means
        return sum(g for _, g in d.values()) / len(d)
    return sum(n * g for n, g in d.values()) / sum(n for n, _ in d.values())

print(f"{'spreadsheet':>11} {'years':>6} {'weights':>13} | mean growth, debt > 90%")
for err, drop, cw in product([0, 1], [0, 1], [0, 1]):
    print(f"{'error' if err else 'ok':>11} {'drop' if drop else 'keep':>6} "
          f"{'per country' if cw else 'per year':>13} | {round(cell(err, drop, cw), 1) + 0.0:5.1f}%")
print()
print(f"RR as published (all three choices + typo): {cell(1, 1, 1, transcription=True):5.2f}%  (published: -0.1%)")
print(f"country-year weighting, all data          : {cell(0, 0, 0):5.2f}%  (HAP: 2.2%)")

print(f"In the published version, one year of New Zealand ({NZ_TRANSCRIBED[1]}%) carries weight 1/7 = {1/7:.1%};")
print(f"under per-year weighting, 1 of {sum(n for n, _ in full.values())} country-years is {1/110:.1%}.")

# A guard that would have caught the range error: the formula must see every country.
def checked_mean(values, expected_rows):
    assert len(values) == expected_rows, f"averaged {len(values)} rows, table has {expected_rows}"
    return sum(values) / len(values)

try:
    checked_mean([0.0] * 15, expected_rows=20)      # RR summed rows 30-44 of a block that ends at row 49
except AssertionError as e:
    print("guard fired:", e)

         ok   drop   per country |   0.3%

Every row of the table matches the corresponding row of the critique's Table 5 for the top band (2.2, 1.9, 1.9, 1.9, 1.7, 1.4, 0.3, 0.0, and −0.1 with the typo). The unrounded result for the published combination is −0.07, which rounds to the published −0.1. Notice what the grid shows: no single choice is dramatic (each moves the answer by about 0.3 percentage points), but the choices compound. Dropping years while weighting by country takes the cell from 2.2 to 0.3, because it leaves New Zealand's one bad year standing alone.

The other bands barely move: the critique finds the lower three stay around 4%, 3% and 3% with or without RR's choices. The corrected top-band mean of 2.2% leaves a drop between the 60–90% band (about 3.2%) and the top band of one percentage point, instead of the 2.9 points the published table implied (2.8 down to −0.1).

What the replication did and did not show

The critique's headline is that over 1946 to 2009, countries above 90% averaged 2.2% growth, "not −0.1% as published". Its further findings are worth separating from the arithmetic:

  • Medians, which RR emphasised in their reply, had the same problems. RR's published medians were 4.2, 3.0, 2.9 and 1.6 for the four bands. After correcting exclusions and errors and weighting by country-year, the critique gets 4.1, 3.1, 2.9 and 2.3. Even keeping RR's own country weighting, RR's errata (which I did not read; I rely on the critique's Table 7) give 2.5 for the top band, so the drop from the 60–90% band is 0.4 points, not 1.3.
  • The 1790 to 2009 sample had the same distortions. RR's means were 3.7, 3.0, 3.4 and 1.7; the critique's recalculation gives 3.7, 3.2, 2.5 and 2.1.
  • There is no sharp threshold at 90%. Adding a 90–120% band, the critique finds mean growth of 2.4% against 3.2% for 60–90% in the post-war sample, and a smooth scatter plot with no visible kink. It does see a steeper drop between 0% and 30% debt, which it notes is not the range relevant to the policy debate.
  • The relationship varies by country and period. For 2000 to 2009 the critique finds no drop in growth above 90% relative to the 30 to 90% range.

The critique is also careful about its limits. It describes itself as "a narrowly gauged critical replication", follows RR in assuming debt causes lower growth (while noting RR themselves acknowledge elsewhere that recessions can raise debt, the reverse direction), and does not claim that high debt is harmless. In its own words, correcting the errors turns "the reality of modestly diminished average GDP growth rates for countries carrying high public debt levels" into something very different from "a false image that high public debt ratios inevitably entail sharp declines in GDP growth".

What the authors of the original said

Fairness requires the other side. According to the fetched Retraction Watch piece, RR's first public response, dated 16 April 2013, said they had "literally just received" the draft and would review it, observed that the critics' results were similar to theirs, and maintained that the evidence remained "entirely consistent with our original interpretation". The critique reports that RR then replied twice in the New York Times and posted an errata memorandum on 5 May 2013, and that they acknowledged the spreadsheet range error. The critique also says RR argued the medians were what they had always emphasised: "We have never used anything but the conservative median estimate in our public discussions, where we stated that the difference between growth associated with debt under 90 percent of GDP and debt over 90 percent of GDP is about 1 percentage point". The critique's response is that the medians were affected by the same errors, and that RR had not addressed the selective exclusion of the three countries, the country-and-period variation or the weighting rationale. I have only the critique's account of those exchanges, so the dispute here is described from one side's published summary plus one short fetched statement from the other.

Analogy: a recipe with a missing line

Imagine a cookbook that prints the ingredients and the finished dish, but not the oven temperature or the fact that the cook always discards the first batch. Readers who follow the printed recipe get a different dish, and cannot tell whether they or the book are wrong. The working spreadsheet is the cook's notebook: until it is shared, nobody can compare.

Where it breaks down: a recipe can be wrong on a clean kitchen and still be a good recipe. Here the discarded batch (the early post-war years) was a defensible-looking choice that cut against the claim being made, which is why it needed saying, not only the typo.

Common misconception

"This is a story about Excel, so use code instead and the problem goes away." The range error was a spreadsheet bug, but it explains only about 0.3 of the 2.3 percentage points. The exclusions and the country weighting would have run identically in Python. What a script buys you is that every choice is a line someone can read, diff and test; it does not make the choices correct. The lesson is about visibility and checks, not the tool.

The lessons, and where this course uses them

  • Share the chain, not only the result. The public spreadsheets were not enough; the working file was. In your own work, that means the exact code or notebook, the data snapshot and the environment that produced each figure: chapter 4, Reproducible experiments. A result you cannot regenerate from a command is a claim.
  • Count rows at every step. The range error is a row-count error. After every join, filter or aggregation, check how many rows and groups you expect and print how many you got; chapter 3, Data wrangling, makes this a habit. A one-line guard (the last lines of the script above) would have caught it; so would plotting the per-country values before averaging them.
  • Write down every exclusion and its reason. Chapter 3's cleaning log is the fix for Problem A: if "dropped 1946 to 1950 for three countries because ..." had been a line in the paper, a reader could judge it.
  • Ask what the unit of averaging is. Problem C is a macro-average (mean of group means) where a pooled average was expected. The same trap appears in model evaluation. Suppose an eval has three categories with 500, 480 and 4 questions, and a model scores 90%, 88% and 0% on them (illustrative numbers, not real data). The pooled accuracy is 88.7%; the mean of the three category scores is 59.3%, driven by a category of four questions. Neither is wrong, but each answers a different question, and the report must say which. See chapter 15, Statistics for experiments, and Evals chapter 4, Statistics for evals on clustered questions and error bars.
  • Check a surprising result before you build on it. A cliff at a round number, from a simple method, with big policy implications, is the profile of a result worth replicating. Chapter 16, Reading papers efficiently, gives the checks; A1, chapter 14 on leakage shows the same habit applied to model scores.

Try it yourself

Edit rr_replication.py three ways and predict the result before you run it.

  1. Change NZ_ONE_YEAR from −7.6 to 0.0. By how much does the “all three choices” row change? Compare with the effect of the transcription typo (0.1 points).
  2. Add a fictional eleventh country that spent exactly one year above 90% with growth of −10%. What does it do to the per-country mean and to the per-year mean? Which method is more fragile to one bad year?
  3. Plot or print each country's average growth next to its number of years. Would you have trusted a per-country average based on 1, 4 and 25 years equally?

Key takeaways

  • The published −0.1% and the corrected +2.2% come from the same data; the difference is three undocumented choices (years dropped, a range error, one-number-per-country weighting) plus a typo.
  • Each choice moved the result by about 0.3 points alone; together they changed its sign. Check interactions, not only single changes.
  • The data were public; the working file was not. Reproducibility means sharing the chain of choices, with row-count checks at each step.
  • Say what you average over (rows or groups), and report both when they disagree.
  • The dispute did not end with "high debt is harmless": the critique's own claim is narrower, and the originals' medians were closer than their means. Describe a replication by what it shows.

Check your understanding

Which statement best describes why the authors of the critique could not reproduce the table from the public spreadsheets?

  1. The public spreadsheets were full of typing errors
  2. The data were secret and could not be requested
  3. The public files lacked the choices of series, years and method that produced the table, which lived in the working file
  4. Excel cannot compute a mean over twenty countries
The critique says the posted files did not include the exact series, years and methods used; RR sent the working spreadsheet on request, and with it the published numbers could be approximated. The data were public, so the second option is wrong, and the first and fourth misplace the problem: the missing information was the analysis choices.

In the top debt band, the published method gave New Zealand's single year (−7.6% growth) the same weight as the UK's 19 years. Why?

  1. It averaged within each country first, then averaged the country figures, so every country counted once
  2. It weighted observations by population
  3. It used medians instead of means
  4. A formula range excluded the UK's early years
Means of country means give each of the seven remaining countries 1/7 of the weight. Population weighting was not used. Medians had the same issues, so they are not the cause, and the range error dropped other countries (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark), not UK years.

A colleague says, "We use Python scripts, so a bug like this cannot happen to us." What is the best reply?

  1. Correct: scripts never have range errors
  2. Only partly: scripts fix the weighting problem but not the exclusions
  3. Incorrect: scripts and spreadsheets are equally opaque
  4. Scripts make the choices readable and testable, but a wrong slice or a questionable exclusion can still be written; add row-count checks and review
The range error is a slice-length bug that code can contain too (values[:15] instead of values[:20]). The exclusion and weighting decisions were not spreadsheet faults at all. Scripts help because the choices are visible; they do not make them right.

An evaluation has three categories of 500, 480 and 4 questions. A model scores 90%, 88% and 0%. The report states "mean accuracy 59.3%". What is the most important question to ask?

  1. Whether the model was trained with float16
  2. Whether the figure is a mean of category scores or pooled over questions, since a tiny category dominates the first and the two give very different answers (59.3% against 88.7%)
  3. Whether three categories is enough
  4. Whether accuracy should be replaced by loss
This is the same trap as Problem C: an average of group averages gives four questions as much weight as 500. Neither number is wrong, but the report has to say which one it gives and why. The numbers are illustrative, not from a real evaluation.
