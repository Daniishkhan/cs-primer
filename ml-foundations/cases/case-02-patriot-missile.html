The Patriot Missile Clock

On 25 February 1991 a Patriot battery at Dhahran, Saudi Arabia, did not engage an incoming Scud missile, which hit a barracks and killed 28 American soldiers. The US General Accounting Office traced the failure to a software problem: a clock stored in tenths of a second was converted to a real number in a 24-bit register, 0.1 cannot be written exactly in binary, and the tiny error grew with every hour the battery stayed switched on. This case follows that error from one rounded number to a radar looking in the wrong place.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the full GAO report, Patriot Missile Defense: Software Problem Led to System Failure at Dhahran, Saudi Arabia (IMTEC-92-26, 4 February 1992). It is a scanned document, so some numerals were uncertain in the text layer; I used the figures that agree with each other and with the second source. I also read Robert Skeel's short article "Roundoff Error and the Patriot Missile" (SIAM News, July 1992), through a university mirror. Skeel was working from the GAO report and from further inquiries, and his explanation of why the error did not cancel is his, so I label it as his. I did not read the Army's own investigation, the Science article he was responding to, or the long debate about how well Patriots performed against Scuds in the 1991 war; the GAO report does not address that question, and Skeel mentions it only in his last sentence. The reconstruction of the arithmetic in the "Try it yourself" section is mine; it reproduces the GAO's published table exactly, which supports Skeel's inference about the conversion but is not the Army's code.

The pattern in one sentence

A rounding error that is harmless in one calculation can grow without bound if it accumulates with uptime or iterations, so ask how large the error is after the longest run the system will ever see, not after the first.

Context: a system used in a way it was not built for

The Patriot is a mobile surface-to-air missile system. The GAO report says the system has evolved since the mid-1960s, and was originally designed to operate in Europe against Soviet medium- to high-altitude aircraft and cruise missiles flying at up to about Mach 2 (1,500 mph). To avoid detection it was designed to be mobile and to operate for only a few hours at one location. In 1991 it was being used against Scud ballistic missiles, which the report says fly at about Mach 5 (3,750 mph, roughly 1,676 metres a second). It was also being run as a fixed defence of key sites: the report says battalions were generally placed in permanent positions. The computer at its centre, the weapons control computer, is described by the GAO as "based on a 1970s design with relatively limited capability to perform high precision calculations".

Both mismatches matter. A faster target magnifies any timing error, and a longer run magnifies any error that accumulates. The Army, says the report, "had much to learn" about tracking Scuds, and it adapted the software six times between August 1990 and February 1991. Each update required the battery to shut down for at least one to two hours. The system had no built-in data recorder, and US commanders chose not to use the available external recorders because they feared they might cause an unplanned shutdown. Israeli units did use recorders, and their data became the first clue.

Range gate
The patch of sky where the radar expects a tracked object to reappear. The computer predicts it from the target's velocity and the time of the last detection, and ignores returns outside it.
Register
A small, fast storage location inside a processor in which arithmetic happens. The Patriot's were 24 bits wide.
Fixed-point number
A number stored as a whole-number count of equal steps (for example, of 2−23), with the binary point at a fixed position. Unlike floating point, its steps do not change with size.
Truncation (chopping)
Rounding by discarding the bits that do not fit, which always moves a positive number towards zero. Rounding to nearest is more accurate on average.
Uptime
How long a system has been running since its last restart. Here it is the quantity the error was proportional to.

The mechanism: a clock in tenths of seconds

The GAO explains the chain in four steps. The range gate's prediction is "a function of the Scud's known velocity and the time of the last radar detection". The system's internal clock keeps time "in tenths of seconds but is expressed as an integer", so 3,600,000 means 100 hours. To predict where the Scud will next appear, both time and velocity must be real numbers, so the integer must be converted. Because of how the computer performs its calculations and "the fact that its registers are only 24 bits long", the conversion "cannot be any more precise than 24 bits". The report concludes that the effect of this on the range gate "is directly proportional to the target's velocity and the length of time the system has been running".

The GAO does not say which number was cut short. Skeel infers it from the report's numbers: the error figures of 0.0275 seconds after eight hours and 0.3433 seconds after 100 hours are both very nearly 2−20 (about 0.0001%) of the elapsed time, which is what you get if each tick of 0.1 seconds was multiplied by a 24-bit value of 0.1 chopped after the 23rd binary place. In binary 0.1 is 0.0001100110011… and never ends, exactly as 1/3 never ends in decimal. Chopped to 23 places it is 0.0999999046…, short by about 9.5 × 10−8. Every tick of the clock therefore adds a time that is a hair too small.

One tick's error is nothing. But the clock ticks ten times a second, 36,000 times an hour. The drift, in seconds, is simply the error per tick times the number of ticks, and it is linear in uptime. The script below reproduces the GAO's Appendix II from that one idea.

The part that confuses everyone: why did it not cancel?

Skeel raised the obvious objection himself. Tracking depends on the difference between two radar times, not on the absolute clock reading. If every conversion were short by the same fraction, 2−20, an interval of half a second would be wrong by half a microsecond, which is irrelevant. So why did a 0.34-second error matter? His answer, from "further inquiries", is that the software had been modified several times to cope with the speed of ballistic missiles, and that at least one modification introduced a subroutine that converted clock time more accurately. This conversion was needed in about half a dozen places, but "the call to the subroutine was not inserted at every point where it was needed". The result was that "a less accurate truncated time of one radar pulse" was being subtracted from "a more accurate time of another radar pulse", so that, in his words, "the error no longer cancelled". I have not seen the code; this is Skeel's account, which he says came from further inquiries. It does fit the GAO's own account that a software change compensating for the inaccuracy existed by 16 February. I treat it as the best available explanation and I flag it as such.

What happened: February 1991

The GAO sets out the sequence. On 11 February the Patriot Project Office received Israeli data showing a 20% shift of the range gate after the system had run for eight consecutive hours. The Project Office said the system will not track a Scud once the shift reaches 50%. Since the shift is proportional to time, extrapolating 20% at eight hours gives 50% at about 20 hours: after that the radar looks in the wrong place and does not track. The GAO notes that the Army did not use the Israeli data to work out how long the system could safely run.

Meanwhile an interim remedy existed. The report says significant shifts "could be eliminated by rebooting the system", which takes about 60 to 90 seconds and resets the clock to zero. The Army, however, "believed the Israeli experience was atypical": they assumed other Patriot users were not running the system for eight or more hours at a time. A software change compensating for the time calculation was included in a version released on 16 February. On 21 February the Project Office sent users a message saying very long run times could shift the range gate and that a change to improve targeting was coming. The message did not say what counted as a very long run, because, according to Army officials quoted by the GAO, they presumed the users would not run the batteries continuously for periods long enough to make the Patriot fail to track.

On 25 February, Alpha Battery, protecting the Dhahran air base, had run for over 100 consecutive hours. The report says the resulting clock inaccuracy shifted the range gate so far that "the system could not track the incoming Scud", Alpha Battery did not engage, and the Scud struck an Army barracks, killing 28. The GAO's appendix gives the shift at about 100 hours as 687 metres. The modified software reached Dhahran on 26 February.

The GAO's own method deserves a note. It did not rely on the Army's word: it reviewed the Army's analyses, then independently examined the system's architecture description, the assembly language programs for surveillance, tracking and target engagement, and the specific machine instructions responsible, and checked the Army's correction by redoing the key calculations. It did not give a draft to the Department of Defense; it discussed the facts with officials, who "generally agreed with the facts as presented".

Try it yourself: rebuild the table

The script chops 0.1 to 23 binary places using exact fractions, multiplies the error by the number of ticks, and compares with the GAO's printed table. It then shows the cancellation failure with two radar pulses half a second apart after 100 hours. The last line is a contrast, not part of the Patriot story: ordinary float32 addition of 0.1 a million times, which drifts by a different mechanism (rounding in accumulation, not truncation of a constant).

"""Patriot clock drift: 0.1 s chopped to 24 bits, accumulated over uptime.
My reconstruction from Skeel (1992) and the GAO report (IMTEC-92-26), not Army code."""
from fractions import Fraction

TENTH_EXACT = Fraction(1, 10)
# 0.1 in binary is 0.0001100110011...; chop after 23 bits past the point (24-bit register,
# the leading zero already used), as Skeel infers.
BITS = 23
TENTH_CHOPPED = Fraction(int(TENTH_EXACT * 2**BITS), 2**BITS)   # round toward zero

err_per_tick = TENTH_EXACT - TENTH_CHOPPED
print(f"0.1 chopped to {BITS} fractional bits = {float(TENTH_CHOPPED):.12f}")
print(f"error per tick = {float(err_per_tick):.3e} s  (relative {float(err_per_tick/TENTH_EXACT):.3e}, 2^-20 = {2**-20:.3e})")

print(f"\n{'hours':>6} {'ticks':>10} {'clock error (s)':>16} {'GAO table (s)':>14} {'shift @1676 m/s':>16}")
gao = {1: 0.0034, 8: 0.0275, 20: 0.0687, 48: 0.1648, 72: 0.2472, 100: 0.3433}
for h in (1, 8, 20, 48, 72, 100):
    ticks = h * 3600 * 10
    e = float(ticks * err_per_tick)
    print(f"{h:>6} {ticks:>10,} {e:>16.4f} {gao[h]:>14.4f} {e*1676:>14.0f} m")

# Why the error did not cancel: tracking uses the DIFFERENCE of two times.
t1, t2 = 100*36000, 100*36000 + 5            # two radar pulses half a second apart (5 ticks)
true_dt = float((t2 - t1) * TENTH_EXACT)
both_old = float(t2*TENTH_CHOPPED - t1*TENTH_CHOPPED)          # same truncation on both
mixed = float(t2*TENTH_EXACT - t1*TENTH_CHOPPED)               # one converted accurately, one not
print(f"\ntrue interval            : {true_dt:.4f} s")
print(f"both times chopped       : {both_old:.4f} s  (error {both_old-true_dt:+.2e} s)")
print(f"one fixed, one not fixed : {mixed:.4f} s  (error {mixed-true_dt:+.4f} s)")

# A fix that avoids the problem: keep integer ticks, convert once, exactly.
print("\ninteger ticks, exact conversion at the end:", float(Fraction(t2 - t1) * TENTH_EXACT), "s")
# Same drift in ordinary float32: add 0.1 a million times.
import numpy as np
s = np.float32(0)
for _ in range(10**6): s += np.float32(0.1)
print(f"float32 sum of 0.1, 10^6 times: {s:.1f} (exact 100000.0)")

Every row of the clock-error column matches the GAO's table to four decimal places, for 1, 8, 20, 48, 72 and 100 hours. That agreement is evidence for Skeel's inference about the 23-bit chop, though not proof that the Army's code did exactly this. The last column of my output uses the Scud speed the report states (1,676 m/s) and gives 575 metres at 100 hours, consistent with Skeel's "more than half a kilometre". The GAO's own table gives 687 metres; the report does not say what velocity it assumed, and 687 metres divided by 0.3433 seconds implies about 2,000 m/s, plausibly a different closing speed. I do not know the reason for the difference and have not tried to resolve it.

Analogy: a tape measure that is a hair short

A tape measure that reads 0.0001% too short does no harm marking out a bookshelf. Use it to lay a pipeline end to end, restarting nowhere, and the error compounds with every tape length. The danger is not the size of one error but the number of times it is applied without being reset.

Where it breaks down: a tape measure's error is the same each time, so a surveyor can calibrate against it. The Patriot's problem was inconsistency: the error was present in some conversions and absent in others, so it could not be calibrated away by a constant.

Common misconception

"Floating-point error is a numerical-analysis footnote that only matters for scientific computing." The Patriot did not even use floating point for this step; it used a fixed 24-bit conversion. The general point survives the detail: any finite representation of 0.1 has an error, and what decides whether it matters is how it accumulates, how it interacts with other errors and how long the system runs. Chapter 11 shows the same pattern in sums of float16 values and in unstable softmax.

What the report concluded and what it left open

The GAO's finding is specific: "a software problem in the system's weapons control computer" led to "an inaccurate tracking calculation that became worse the longer the system operated", and the modified software which fixed the calculation arrived a day late. It also records what the Army did afterwards: the Project Office said in September 1991 that the Patriot algorithms would continue to be re-examined, and that an endurance test had been run to make sure extended run times did not cause other difficulties.

The report does not claim that the clock error was the only reason Patriots missed Scuds. Skeel closes his article with a sentence worth repeating: "serious doubts have been expressed about the ability of Patriot missiles to hit Scuds." The GAO report was asked a narrow question (was there a software problem in this incident, and what was it?) and answered it. How well Patriots worked overall in the 1991 war is a separate debate that I did not research here.

Lessons, and where they return

  • Bound the error at the longest run, not the typical one. The design assumption was a few hours per site. Whenever a quantity grows with time, iterations, batch count or model depth, compute it at the worst case. Chapter 11 does this with machine epsilon and accumulation; chapter 1 introduces why 0.1 is not exact.
  • Keep time as integers, convert once at the end. The script's last lines keep integer ticks and multiply by an exact 1/10 only when a real number is needed, with no drift. The same advice applies to money (count cents) and to elapsed time in logs; see also the Systems cases on the leap second that broke DNS and the chapter on time and clocks.
  • A partial fix can be worse than none. Improving the conversion in some places but not all made the mismatch worse than the uniform old bias. When a bug affects several call sites, search for all of them, test the fix against the whole path, and prefer a single shared function over six copies. In ML terms: if you change a normalisation or a tokeniser in training, change it identically in serving.
  • A warning needs a number. The 21 February message said "very long run times" and gave no hours, although the Army had already computed that tracking would degrade near 20 hours. Operators cannot act on an unquantified warning. See incident response and postmortems for how to turn a known hazard into a runbook line with a threshold.
  • Record data from the field. The only evidence that the system drifted came from the Israelis, who used recorders. Without recorded performance, the Army learned by anecdote.
  • The cheap mitigation existed. Rebooting every few hours was a 60 to 90 second workaround. The failure was in distributing the knowledge that it was needed.

Systems connection

Long-running services show uptime-dependent bugs: counters that overflow, caches that grow, clocks that step. Postmortems record such contributing factors, and alerting is how a threshold like "20 hours" reaches a human before it matters.

Try it yourself (again)

  1. Change BITS in the script from 23 to 31 and rerun. How long before the clock error reaches 0.34 seconds? Predict first: each extra bit halves the error.
  2. Replace the chop with rounding to nearest (use round instead of int). How large is the error per tick, and which way does it go?
  3. Find the uptime after which the error exceeds one tenth of a second with the 23-bit chop. Compare with the GAO's table.

Key takeaways

  • The Patriot clock counted tenths of a second; converting each tick used a 24-bit value of 0.1 that was short by about one part in a million. After 100 hours the clock was off by 0.3433 seconds (GAO, Appendix II).
  • Error proportional to uptime means the right question is what the error is after the longest run, and the design assumed hours while the use became days.
  • Per Skeel, an incomplete fix left one conversion accurate and another not, so the error stopped cancelling in time differences. Partial fixes need checking end to end.
  • The Army knew on 11 February about a drift and had a 60 to 90 second workaround, but the warning on 21 February gave no number. A hazard is useful only if the people running the system are told its threshold.
  • Describe the failure by what the report shows: a software problem in this incident, not the whole story of Patriot performance.

Check your understanding

Why did the Patriot's clock error grow, rather than stay at a fixed small size?

  1. The radar sent more pulses the longer it ran
  2. Each clock tick (0.1 s) was converted using a slightly short value of 0.1, so the errors added up in proportion to the number of ticks
  3. The 24-bit register overflowed after 20 hours
  4. The Scud's speed changed during the flight
The GAO says the effect is proportional to the length of time the system had been running. A constant error per tick, summed over 3.6 million ticks in 100 hours, gives 0.3433 seconds. There was no register overflow; the 20-hour figure is where the resulting range-gate shift reached 50%.

Skeel points out that if every time conversion had the same relative error, the damage would be tiny. Why?

  1. Radar tracking uses absolute clock time, which would be accurate
  2. The error would be smaller than the radar's resolution
  3. Rebooting would hide it
  4. Tracking depends on the difference between two times, and an identical relative error scales both, leaving the interval wrong by only about 0.0001% of itself
An interval of half a second would be off by roughly half a microsecond. The error only mattered because, in Skeel's account, one time was converted with the improved routine and the other with the old one, so the error did not cancel.

A system stores elapsed time as a count of 10 ms ticks and needs seconds for a calculation. Which design avoids Patriot-style drift?

  1. Keep the integer tick count and convert once, exactly, at the point of use; compute differences on the integers first
  2. Convert every tick to seconds and add them as they arrive
  3. Use a smaller register
  4. Restart the system every day
Integers are exact; converting once per use gives a result with one rounding error that does not accumulate. Adding a converted constant on every tick accumulates its error (the Patriot's problem, and the float32 sum in the script). Restarts only bound the drift, as the Army's reboot workaround did.

The Army knew a fix existed on 16 February and a reboot workaround existed from 11 February. According to the GAO, what was missing on the day of the attack?

  1. Any way to measure the clock error
  2. A budget for the new software
  3. A quantified instruction to users (how long they could run) and the fix reaching Dhahran, which arrived 26 February
  4. A radar that could see Scuds
The 21 February message gave no figure for "very long run times", and the GAO says the delivery of the modified software took time because of wartime transport. The error could be measured (the Israeli data did), and the report does not discuss funding or radar capability as causes.
