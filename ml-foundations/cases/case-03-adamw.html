AdamW: Weight Decay Done Right

For plain gradient descent, adding an L2 penalty to the loss and shrinking the weights a little each step are the same thing. Inside Adam they are not, and the common libraries of the time implemented the first and called it the second. Loshchilov and Hutter's paper "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization" (2017, ICLR 2019) pointed out the difference, moved one line of the algorithm, and gave the optimiser its name: AdamW. This case shows exactly which line, why it matters and what the evidence was.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read the full text of the paper (arXiv:1711.05101, version 3, 4 January 2019, the ICLR 2019 version), including its algorithms, propositions and experimental section. I read the current PyTorch documentation pages for Adam and AdamW (version 2.14, via the documentation site, October 2026), and the arXiv abstract page of Wang and Aitchison (2024). I did not read the appendix proofs in detail, Aitchison's (2018) Bayesian-filtering paper that the authors cite, Hanson and Pratt (1988), or the authors' code. The paper's results come from large image-classification runs I did not rerun. The two scripts in this case are my own toy experiments on small synthetic problems: they illustrate the mechanism, and they are not a replication of the paper's numbers.

The pattern in one sentence

Two techniques that are identical under one optimiser can differ under another, so when you move a hyperparameter between optimisers, check what the library actually computes and not only what the argument is called.

Context: a regulariser with two names

A model with many parameters can fit its training data by making some weights very large, and large weights often generalise badly. A regulariser discourages that. Two versions were in everyday use by 2017. An L2 penalty adds ½λ′∥w∥² to the loss, so the gradient gains a term λ′w. Weight decay, in the form Hanson and Pratt described in 1988 (as the paper cites), shrinks the weights directly on every step: w ← (1 − λ)w − α∇f, where α is the learning rate and ∇f the loss gradient. Chapter 10 of this course introduced both and the trap between them (Gradient descent and optimisers); this case tells the story and weighs the evidence. The penalty also appears in A1, chapter 6 as ridge regression.

The paper's observation is that the two are interchangeable only for SGD. Its abstract says: L2 regularisation and weight decay are "equivalent for standard stochastic gradient descent (when rescaled by the learning rate), but as we demonstrate this is not the case for adaptive gradient algorithms, such as Adam", and that "common implementations of these algorithms employ L2 regularization (often calling it 'weight decay' in what may be misleading due to the inequivalence we expose)". The authors' motivation was a puzzle of the time: on image classification (CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100), the best published results still used SGD with momentum, and the paper cites Wilson et al. (2017) for evidence that adaptive methods generalised worse. Their hypothesis was that part of the gap came from how Adam was regularised.

Adaptive optimiser
An optimiser (Adam, RMSProp, AdaGrad) that rescales each weight's update by a running estimate of that weight's typical gradient size.
Preconditioner
The rescaling applied to the gradient before the step. In Adam it is the diagonal matrix with entries 1 / (√v̂ + ε).
Coupled and decoupled
With an L2 penalty the regulariser's gradient is added to the loss gradient and passes through the optimiser's machinery (coupled). With decoupled weight decay it is applied separately to the weights.
Weight decay factor
The fraction λ of each weight removed per step (times the schedule multiplier). Not the same number as the L2 coefficient λ′ except for SGD.

The mechanism: why SGD cannot tell the difference

Take SGD and add the penalty. The update on a batch with gradient g is

w ← w − α(g + λ′w) = (1 − αλ′) w − αg.

Compare with weight decay, w ← (1 − λ)w − αg. They are the same update when λ = αλ′. The paper states this as its Proposition 1 (with λ′ = λ/α, "the L2 regularizer has to be set to λ/α"). The practical consequence is already interesting: for SGD with an L2 penalty, the best penalty strength depends on the learning rate. Change α and you must change λ′ to keep the same shrinkage. The authors argue that decoupling removes that coupling, and that this "coupling of initial learning rate and L2 regularization factor might have contributed to SGD's reputation of being very sensitive to its hyperparameter settings".

The mechanism: what Adam does to the penalty

Adam keeps a running average of the gradient (m) and of its square (v) and steps by α m̂ / (√v̂ + ε), where hats mark bias correction (chapter 10). If the L2 penalty is added to the gradient first, the quantity fed to m and v is g + λ′w, and everything the optimiser does to g it also does to the penalty term. In particular the penalty is divided by √v̂, which is roughly the typical size s of the weight's gradient. The shrinkage that the penalty produces per step is therefore about αλ′w / s.

That has a surprising consequence. A weight with a large typical gradient (s large) is shrunk less; a weight with a tiny typical gradient is shrunk more. The regulariser's strength is set by the gradient scale, not by the number you chose. The paper puts it this way: with an L2 penalty "weights with large historic parameter and/or gradient amplitudes being regularized less than they would be when using weight decay". Its Proposition 2 says that for an optimiser with a non-scalar preconditioner there is no L2 coefficient that reproduces weight decay, and Proposition 3 gives the picture for a fixed preconditioner diag(s)−1: weight decay on the loss is equivalent to an L2 penalty of λ′/(2α) ∥√s ⊙ θ∥², scaled by the gradient size. (The paper adds that this does not apply directly to Adam, because Adam's preconditioner changes every step, but it gives the intuition.)

AdamW's fix is to leave Adam's moment machinery alone and apply the decay to the weights outside it. In the paper's Algorithm 2, line 12 reads θt ← θt−1 − ηt ( α m̂t / (√v̂t + ε) + λθt−1 ), and the L2 variant instead puts λθt−1 into the gradient on line 6. Here ηt is a schedule multiplier shared by both terms, so decay follows the learning-rate schedule.

Go deeper optional

A one-weight check of the 1/s effect. Suppose a weight has no useful signal, so its loss gradient is noise of typical size s, and its value is w. With an L2 penalty, Adam sees g + λ′w. If λ′|w| is much larger than s, then m / √v is close to sign(λ′w), whatever the size of λ′: the normalised step is capped at about α, so the weight loses about α per step, whatever the size of λ′. If s is much larger than λ′|w|, the penalty is a small tilt on noise and the weight shrinks by about αλ′w / s per step. In both regimes the shrinkage depends on s. With decoupled decay it is αλw per step, a constant fraction, for every weight, as the first script below shows.

Why the paper normalises weight decay. Appendix B.1 of the paper notes that the best weight decay depends on how many weight updates the run takes ("the larger the runtime/number of batch passes to be performed, the smaller the optimal weight decay"), and proposes setting λ = λnorm √(b / (B T)), where b is the batch size, B the number of training points and T the number of epochs. The authors call it "merely one possibility informed by few experiments".

Seeing it in numbers

The first script checks the SGD equivalence and then looks at the Adam case in the cleanest possible setting: four weights with no signal (the loss gradients are pure noise with typical sizes 0.01, 0.1, 1 and 10), each starting at 1.0, trained for 2,000 steps with the same nominal strength 0.1. A good regulariser should shrink them all by the same factor. This is my toy; it is meant to display the 1/s effect, not to mimic a real model.

import numpy as np

# --- 1. SGD: L2 penalty and weight decay are the same update (lambda' = lambda / alpha) ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
A = rng.normal(size=(50, 5)); b = rng.normal(size=50)
grad = lambda w: A.T @ (A @ w - b) / len(b)
alpha, lam = 0.1, 0.05
w_l2 = w_wd = np.ones(5)
for _ in range(100):
    w_l2 = w_l2 - alpha * (grad(w_l2) + (lam / alpha) * w_l2)      # L2 coefficient lambda/alpha
    w_wd = (1 - lam) * w_wd - alpha * grad(w_wd)                    # decay (1 - lambda) w
print("SGD  max |w_L2 - w_decay| after 100 steps:", np.abs(w_l2 - w_wd).max())

# --- 2. Adam: what the penalty does to one update, for weights with different gradient scales ---
def adam_step(w, g, m, v, t, lr=1e-3, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g; v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g
    return w - lr * (m / (1 - b1**t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - b2**t)) + eps), m, v

wd = 0.1; lr = 1e-3
scales = np.array([0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0])        # typical size of the loss gradient for 4 weights
runs_a, runs_w = [], []
for seed in range(20):                            # average 20 seeds: the loss gradients here are pure noise
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    m = np.zeros(4); v = np.zeros(4); mW = np.zeros(4); vW = np.zeros(4)
    w_a = np.ones(4); w_w = np.ones(4)
    for t in range(1, 2001):
        g = scales * rng.normal(size=4)                      # mean-zero loss gradient, no signal to learn
        w_a, m, v = adam_step(w_a, g + wd * w_a, m, v, t)    # Adam + L2: penalty added to the gradient
        w_w, mW, vW = adam_step(w_w, g, mW, vW, t)           # AdamW: adaptive step uses the loss gradient only
        w_w = w_w - lr * wd * w_w                            # decay applied directly to the weight
    runs_a.append(w_a); runs_w.append(w_w)
print("\nfour weights, start at 1.0, loss-gradient scales", scales, "; lr 1e-3, decay 0.1, 2000 steps")
print("Adam + L2 penalty 0.1 :", np.round(np.mean(runs_a, axis=0), 3))
print("AdamW decay 0.1       :", np.round(np.mean(runs_w, axis=0), 3))
print("pure decay (1-lr*wd)^2000 :", np.round((1 - lr * wd) ** 2000, 3))

The SGD forms agree to rounding error (about 10−17), as Proposition 1 says. In Adam with an L2 penalty, the shrinkage depends on the gradient scale exactly as the argument predicts: the weight with the quietest gradients is pulled almost all the way to zero (0.023), and the loudest barely moves (0.979), a spread of more than forty to one. With decoupled decay every weight ends within 0.02 of the 0.819 that pure decay, (1 − 10−4)2000, predicts. (The small differences are noise in the averaged runs.) The quiet weight is over-regularised and the loud one under-regularised, relative to the setting you asked for.

What the paper reports

The paper's experiments use ResNets (26 layers, "2x64d" with 11.6 million parameters and "2x96d" with 25.6 million) on CIFAR-10 and on ImageNet32x32 (a downsampled ImageNet with 1.2 million images). Its main claims, in its own words and numbers:

  • L2 is not effective in Adam. In a grid over learning rate and regularisation strength for a 26 2x64d ResNet on CIFAR-10 (100 epochs), Adam's best results with non-zero L2 were "comparable to the best ones obtained without the L2 regularization, i.e., when λ = 0", whereas for SGD the penalty helped.
  • Decoupling separates the two hyperparameters. For L2 (in SGD and Adam) the good region of the grid lay along a diagonal, so the learning rate and the penalty had to be tuned together. With decoupled decay (SGDW and AdamW) the good region was more nearly aligned with the axes: "even if the learning rate is not well tuned yet ... leaving it fixed and only optimizing the weight decay factor would yield a good value".
  • Better test error. Over longer runs (up to 1,800 epochs), AdamW reached lower training loss and lower test error than Adam. The paper says AdamW "achieved 15% relative improvement in test error compared to Adam both on CIFAR-10 ... and ImageNet32x32" at the default learning rate of 0.001, and that this closed most of the gap to SGD with momentum on CIFAR-10.
  • Schedules matter for Adam too. A cosine-annealing learning-rate multiplier improved Adam "substantially", which the authors say is easy to overlook because Adam is "adaptive".

Those are the authors' reports from their own experiments; I have not reproduced them. The paper also lists, as a limit, that its normalised weight decay is one choice informed by few experiments.

Try it on a toy: a grid over learning rate and strength

To see the "coupling" claim in miniature I ran a grid of my own. The task is a small linear regression (80 training points, 60 features of different scales, 3,000 test points) trained with minibatch Adam for 2,000 steps, with either an L2 penalty or decoupled decay, over three learning rates and seven strengths, averaged over five minibatch seeds. It is far from the paper's setting (no deep network, no image data), so read it as an illustration, not as evidence about ResNets.

import numpy as np
n, d, n_test = 80, 60, 3000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
scale = np.exp(rng.uniform(np.log(0.3), np.log(3), d))        # features on very different scales
w_true = rng.normal(size=d) / scale                             # so each feature matters equally
X = rng.normal(size=(n, d)) * scale; y = X @ w_true + 1.0 * rng.normal(size=n)
Xt = rng.normal(size=(n_test, d)) * scale; yt = Xt @ w_true

def run(variant, lr, lam, seeds=range(5)):
    return float(np.mean([run1(variant, lr, lam, seed=s) for s in seeds]))

def run1(variant, lr, lam, steps=2000, bs=24, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); w = np.zeros(d); m = np.zeros(d); v = np.zeros(d)
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        i = r.integers(0, n, bs); g = X[i].T @ (X[i] @ w - y[i]) / bs
        if variant == "L2": g = g + lam * w
        m = 0.9 * m + 0.1 * g; v = 0.999 * v + 0.001 * g * g
        w = w - lr * (m / (1 - 0.9**t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - 0.999**t)) + 1e-8)
        if variant == "decoupled": w = w - lr * lam * w
    return float(np.mean((Xt @ w - yt) ** 2))

lrs = [0.003, 0.01, 0.03]
lams = [0, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1]
for variant in ("L2", "decoupled"):
    print(variant)
    print("  lr \\ lam " + "".join(f"{l:>9g}" for l in lams))
    for lr in lrs:
        print(f"  {lr:<9g}" + "".join(f"{run(variant, lr, l):9.2f}" for l in lams))

print("predict-zero test MSE:", round(float(np.mean(yt**2)), 1))

Common misconception

"AdamW is just Adam with a different name for the same regularisation." The name does hide the difference, which is the point of the paper. L2 regularisation and weight decay are the same operation only for SGD, and only after you rescale the coefficient by the learning rate. In Adam the two apply different amounts of shrinkage to different weights. Code that uses weight_decay=0.01 with an optimiser that implements an L2 penalty is not running the algorithm you read about.

What came after

State of play, October 2026

The paper says that decoupled weight decay "has already been adopted by many researchers" and implemented in TensorFlow and PyTorch. The current PyTorch documentation (version 2.14) lists torch.optim.AdamW with defaults learning rate 1e-3, betas (0.9, 0.999), eps 1e-8 and weight_decay 1e-2, and describes its decay step as applied directly to the parameters, so that "decay doesn't accumulate in the momentum or variance". The page for torch.optim.Adam describes its weight_decay argument as an L2 penalty with default 0, added to the gradient before the moment updates, and mentions a decoupled_weight_decay option which makes it behave like AdamW. These are version-specific facts: check the documentation for the version you use.

On how to set the strength, Wang and Aitchison (ICML 2025; first posted May 2024) argue that AdamW's weights behave like an exponential moving average of recent updates, so the quantity to hold fixed when you scale a model or dataset is that averaging timescale (measured in epochs), not the decay value itself. Their abstract reports that, for a fixed timescale, the optimal weight decay should fall as the dataset grows and rise as the model grows. This is a refinement of the paper's own observation that the best decay depends on the number of updates. I read only the abstract page.

Lessons, and where they return

  • Read what the library computes. The same argument name can mean different mathematics in different optimisers. Before reusing a recipe (for example "AdamW, weight decay 0.1") confirm the implementation matches the one the recipe came from; Lab 3 has you build Adam and AdamW and compare them.
  • Hyperparameters interact. Tune learning rate and strength jointly at least once, or you may mistake a coupled pair for two independent knobs. Chapter 10 covers schedules, and A2, chapter 3 returns to training dynamics.
  • Check a claim against a small case. The first script took a few lines and made the paper's central argument visible. That habit is chapter 16's advice on reproducing a result.
  • Optimiser state is memory. Adam stores two extra numbers per weight; decoupled decay adds no new state (see chapter 10 and A3, chapter 2).
  • Normalised and scheduled decay. Decay follows the learning-rate multiplier in Algorithm 2, which matters when you use warmup and cosine schedules, as in the training recipes of A2, chapter 17.

Systems connection

A parameter whose name stays the same while its meaning changes is a classic API-design hazard: the interface promises one thing and the implementation does another. See API design on contracts, and note how the PyTorch documentation now spells the semantics out in the algorithm box.

Key takeaways

  • For SGD, an L2 penalty with coefficient λ/α and weight decay λ produce the same update; the best penalty therefore depends on the learning rate.
  • In Adam the penalty is divided by √v̂ like any gradient, so weights with large gradients are regularised less and weights with small gradients more.
  • AdamW moves the decay outside the moment machinery (line 12 of Algorithm 2). The paper reports better generalisation (15% relative test-error improvement on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32x32 at the default learning rate) and less coupling between learning rate and decay.
  • My toy runs show the mechanism clearly and the paper's qualitative claim weakly; they do not reproduce its numbers.
  • Check what a library's weight_decay argument computes in the version you use.

Check your understanding

For SGD with learning rate 0.1, which L2 coefficient λ′ gives the same update as weight decay with λ = 0.01?

  1. 0.01, since they are the same thing
  2. 0.001
  3. 0.1
  4. 1.0
The SGD update with the penalty is (1 − αλ′)w − αg, so λ = αλ′, giving λ′ = λ/α = 0.01 / 0.1 = 0.1. Using 0.01 (the tempting answer) would shrink the weights ten times too little.

In Adam with an L2 penalty, two weights have the same value but the first has a typical loss gradient ten times larger than the second. Which is regularised more strongly, and why?

  1. The second, because the penalty term is divided by √v̂, which is smaller for the weight with smaller gradients
  2. The first, because larger gradients mean larger penalties
  3. Both equally, because the penalty is the same for equal weights
  4. Neither, because Adam ignores penalties
The penalty gradient λ′w is the same for equal weights, but the update divides by √v̂, which tracks the gradient scale, so the quiet weight gets a bigger effective push towards zero. That is why the third option, which looks at the penalty alone, is wrong. The script's output (0.023 against 0.979) shows the size of the effect.

Which statement about the paper's evidence is accurate?

  1. It proved AdamW beats SGD on every task
  2. It showed decoupled decay helps SGD more than Adam
  3. It reported only theory, with no experiments
  4. It reported, on image classification with ResNets, that AdamW generalised better than Adam with L2 (including a 15% relative improvement in test error at the default learning rate) and that learning rate and decay were less coupled
The paper has propositions and experiments, on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32x32. It says AdamW closed most of the gap to SGD with momentum on CIFAR-10, not that it won everywhere, and it says the L2 penalty was effective for SGD while Adam gained nothing from it.

You copy a training recipe that says "AdamW, weight decay 0.1" into a framework whose Adam optimiser has a weight_decay argument that adds an L2 penalty to the gradient. What is the best course of action?

  1. Pass weight_decay=0.1 to Adam, because the argument has the same name
  2. Check the framework's documentation or source for a decoupled option, or use its AdamW, and verify that the decay is applied outside the moment estimates
  3. Divide 0.1 by the learning rate and use Adam
  4. Remove weight decay, since Adam adapts anyway
Dividing by the learning rate is the SGD conversion and does not fix Adam, which has no equivalent L2 coefficient (Proposition 2). Removing regularisation changes the recipe. The right step is to confirm that the implementation matches the algorithm the recipe assumes.
