Learning with AI Assistants

Coding assistants can write a working function in seconds, and that is exactly the danger when your aim is to understand it. This chapter separates getting the output from building the skill, summarises what early studies found, and gives you a short routine for using an assistant to learn faster instead of to learn less.

Why this matters

Everything in this course, from autograd to KL divergence, can be pasted into an assistant and returned as working code. That makes it tempting to skip the understanding, and the cost shows up later, when a loss turns to nan, a metric looks too good, or an interviewer asks why. Your future work will use these tools constantly (Working with AI Coding Tools covers professional practice). What you can control now is whether you come out of each session knowing more. After this chapter you will have a routine for that.

Where the full treatment lives

Agents chapter 22 is the full treatment of working with AI coding tools. This chapter covers only the learning side: how to use an assistant without losing the skill.

Output is not understanding

When you ask an assistant to write code, you receive an artefact: something that runs. When you work through a problem yourself, you build a skill: the ability to produce, debug and explain it next time without help. An assistant is excellent at the first and, by default, does nothing for the second. Mixing the two up is the central risk. You can finish a lab with every check passing and be unable to reproduce a line of it.

Two pieces of evidence, both small and both worth reading with their caveats, make the point from different directions.

State of play, October 2026

Speed. In 2025 METR ran a randomised trial with 16 experienced open-source developers working on 246 real issues in repositories they knew well. Each issue was randomly assigned to allow or forbid AI tools. With AI allowed, developers took 19% longer, although they had predicted a 24% speed-up and afterwards still believed AI had sped them up by about 20%. METR lists its own limits: a small, specialised group; early-2025 tools (mainly Cursor with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet); and no claim that this holds for other developers or settings. METR says the result is out of date and has published a February 2026 update on later tools, which I have not read. Learning. Anthropic's January 2026 randomised trial gave 52 mostly junior Python developers a task using a library (Trio) they had not met, with or without an AI chat assistant, then a quiz. The AI group averaged 50% and the hand-coding group 67% (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.01), with the largest gap on debugging questions; the AI group's time saving of about two minutes was not statistically significant. The authors stress the small sample, a quiz taken straight after the task, a chat-style assistant rather than an agentic tool, and that the interaction patterns are associations, not causes. Both findings are about particular tools, tasks and people at a particular time, and are likely to be revised.

Neither study says "assistants are bad". The second one is more useful for us because it also looked at how people used the assistant. Their quiz scores split sharply by behaviour.

The pattern is not subtle. People who handed over the writing (AI delegation, progressive reliance, relying on the assistant to debug) learned least. People who used it for conceptual inquiry, asking why and how, or who asked for explanations alongside code, learned most. The best habit in the group was to ask only conceptual questions and write the code themselves, and that group was also the second fastest overall.

A routine that keeps the understanding

Turn those findings into a loop you run on every non-trivial task. It is five steps, and the middle three are what the study's best performers did.

  1. Predict. Before asking, write down in a comment what you expect: the shapes, the rough size of the answer, the main steps. A prediction turns the answer from something to copy into something to compare, and any mismatch shows you what you did not know.
  2. Ask for the why. Request explanations alongside code: "Why subtract the maximum?" "What would break if the axis were 0?" Ask conceptual questions first and write the first version yourself when the idea is the point of the exercise.
  3. Read every line. Trace the code with a tiny input, shape by shape (NumPy, Arrays & Broadcasting is your guide). Any line you cannot explain is a question for the assistant or for the documentation.
  4. Verify independently. This is independent verification: checks the author of the code did not write. Do not use the assistant to check the assistant. Use a hand-computed value, a property, an edge case, a numerical gradient check (Derivatives & the Chain Rule), or the primary source (Reading Papers Efficiently).
  5. Explain back. Close the chat and explain the solution aloud or in a paragraph without looking. If you cannot, ask for a simpler example, then try again.

Verification, concretely

Assistants write fluent, plausible code that is sometimes wrong in ways that pass a casual check. Here is a softmax of the kind an assistant will give you, with three checks run on it. It is correct on the textbook example, and it is invariant to shifts on small inputs, so two of three checks pass. Only the third, an edge case with realistic logits, exposes it. This is the overflow problem from Floating Point & Numerical Stability.

import numpy as np

def softmax_suggested(z):
    """A plausible assistant answer: correct on the textbook example."""
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

def softmax_checked(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())          # shift-invariance: subtract the maximum
    return e / e.sum()

def check(fn, name):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    print(name)
    # 1. a hand-computed value: softmax([0, 0]) = [0.5, 0.5]
    print("  hand value  ", fn(np.array([0.0, 0.0])))
    # 2. a property: adding a constant to all logits changes nothing
    z = rng.normal(size=4)
    print("  shift ok    ", np.allclose(fn(z), fn(z + 5.0)))
    # 3. an edge case: large logits, as real models produce
    with np.errstate(all="ignore"):
        out = fn(np.array([1000.0, 1001.0, 999.0]))
    print("  large logits", out)

check(softmax_suggested, "suggested:")
check(softmax_checked, "after checking:")
suggested:
  hand value   [0.5 0.5]
  shift ok     True
  large logits [nan nan nan]
after checking:
  hand value   [0.5 0.5]
  shift ok     True
  large logits [0.24472847 0.66524096 0.09003057]

Had you only asked "does it match the formula?" you would have shipped nan. The habit is to pick checks the author did not think of: a hand value, a property, and an extreme.

Common misconception

"If the tests pass and the assistant is confident, the code is right." Confidence in a model's tone is not evidence. The tests may be the assistant's own, written to pass its own code, and fluent explanations can be wrong. An assistant can also cite functions, flags or papers that do not exist. Treat its output like a fast colleague's first draft: useful, worth reading, and never exempt from your own checks. The same goes for explanations of ideas: confirm key facts in the documentation or the paper.

When to use it, and when to switch it off

Use the assistant freely for work you already understand and want done faster: boilerplate, unfamiliar syntax of a language you know, a regular expression you can test, a first plot, a refactor you will review. Use it with care for explaining unfamiliar code, summarising a long document, or suggesting what to try next. Switch it off, or limit it to conceptual questions, when the goal is to learn the idea itself: the labs in this course, the first implementation of a new algorithm, a derivation you are meant to be able to reproduce. That split follows the evidence above, which saw large time savings on familiar tasks elsewhere and weaker learning on a new library.

  • Protect what you paste. Do not send secrets, credentials, personal data or confidential code to a tool unless your organisation has approved it. Prompts may be logged or retained under the provider's terms; see Data Protection & Privacy.
  • Keep ownership of the spec. Write the tests or the acceptance criteria yourself. The person who defines "correct" decides what the assistant optimises for.
  • Keep a log. Note what you accepted, what you changed and why. Reading it a week later shows which habits help.
  • Mind your skills. Every so often, solve a problem of the same type with no assistant. If it feels much harder than it used to, that tells you something.

Try it yourself

Pick one exercise from the course (a small function from Python for Numerical Work will do). First write your prediction and an attempt without help for 15 minutes. Then ask an assistant for a solution and for the three most likely bugs in your attempt. Run verify.py-style checks on both versions: a hand value, a property and an edge case. Finally close the chat and explain what you learned in two sentences. Repeat the exercise a week later without help and see how far you get.

Key takeaways

  • An assistant gives you an artefact; only your own effort builds a skill. Keep the two apart.
  • Early randomised evidence is small and tool-specific. METR (2025) found experienced developers slower with AI on their own repositories while believing they were faster; Anthropic (2026) found lower quiz scores for novices who learned a new library with an assistant, with the largest gap in debugging.
  • How you use the tool matters most. Asking conceptual questions and writing the code yourself did well; delegating the writing or the debugging did badly.
  • Run the loop: predict, ask for the why, read every line, verify with checks the assistant did not write, then explain back.
  • Use the tool freely on familiar work, sparingly on new ideas, and not at all on the labs, where the struggle is the point.
  • Protect secrets and personal data, and own the specification.

Further reading

  • Anthropic, "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills" (January 2026). The randomised trial and the interaction patterns summarised above; read the limitations.
  • METR, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity" (July 2025). The randomised study of 16 developers and 246 issues, with a clear list of what it does not show and a link to the 2026 update.
  • H. Bastani et al., "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning" (PNAS, 2025). A field experiment with nearly a thousand high school mathematics students: an unguarded chat tutor helped during practice but left students worse off once it was removed, while a tutor designed to protect learning largely avoided the harm. I know it from the abstract and secondary coverage, not a full reading, so check the paper for numbers.

Check your understanding

You are working through a lab whose goal is to implement backpropagation yourself. Which use of an assistant best fits the evidence in this chapter?

  1. Ask it to write the whole solution, then read it afterwards.
  2. Ask it to run the lab checks for you and fix anything that fails.
  3. Write the code yourself, and use the assistant for conceptual questions such as why the chain rule applies here.
  4. Ask it to rewrite your code until all the checks pass, without reading the changes.
In Anthropic's study, the participants who asked only conceptual questions and wrote the code themselves scored well, and delegation or leaning on the assistant to debug scored worst. In a lab the struggle is the point, so handing over the writing defeats it even if the checks pass.

An assistant gives you a function, and its own unit tests pass. What is the most valuable next step?

  1. Add checks it did not write: a hand-computed value, a property that must hold, and an extreme input.
  2. Ask the same assistant whether the code is correct, because it knows best.
  3. Accept it, since passing tests is conclusive.
  4. Delete the tests and ship, since tests were written by a machine.
Tests written by the same source as the code often share its blind spots, as the softmax example shows: it passes on ordinary inputs and fails on large logits. Asking the assistant to approve itself is not independent. Independent checks catch what its tests missed.

Which reading of the METR 2025 study is best supported?

  1. AI tools slow down all programmers.
  2. AI tools speed up all programmers by about 20%.
  3. Developers' self-reports of speed-up are reliable, since they know their work best.
  4. In that setting, with those tools and those experienced developers on their own repositories, tasks took longer with AI, and the developers' sense of speed did not match the measurement.
The study's own caveats limit the claim to a particular group, tool generation and kind of work. The 20% figure is what developers believed, not what was measured. The first two options overgeneralise in opposite directions; the third is contradicted by the gap between belief and measurement.

What is the purpose of the "explain back" step?

  1. To give the assistant feedback so it improves.
  2. To test whether you can reproduce and justify the solution without help, which exposes understanding you only borrowed.
  3. To produce documentation for the repository.
  4. To check that the code runs.
Explaining without looking is retrieval practice: you find out what you really know. It is not feedback for the tool, documentation, or a test of the code, which are the job of other steps.
