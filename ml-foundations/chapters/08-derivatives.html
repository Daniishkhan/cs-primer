Derivatives & the Chain Rule

Training a model means changing numbers to make a score better, and a derivative tells you which way to change each number and how hard to push. This chapter builds the derivative from a slope you can measure, extends it to many inputs as the gradient, and ends with the one rule, the chain rule, that lets the gradient of a huge composed function be assembled from tiny local pieces.

Why this matters

Every learning method in this path has the same inner loop: compute a number that says how wrong the model is (the loss), work out how that number would change if each parameter were nudged, then nudge them. The middle step is differentiation. Without it you cannot read the backpropagation chapter (A2, chapter 2), cannot see why a gradient-descent run explodes or crawls (chapter 10), and cannot follow the policy-gradient derivation that underlies RLHF (A3, chapter 7). After this chapter you will be able to differentiate by hand the functions that appear in neural networks, check any gradient numerically, read a gradient as a direction, and track the shapes of derivatives through a layer. The next chapter then turns the chain rule into a program.

A slope you can measure

Suppose a function f takes a number x and returns a number f(x): the loss as a function of one weight, say. If you change x by a small amount h, the output changes by f(x+h) − f(x). The ratio of the two,

(f(x+h) − f(x)) / h,

is the average rate of change over that small stretch: output change per unit of input change. It is the slope of the straight line (a chord) through the two points (x, f(x)) and (x+h, f(x+h)). The derivative f′(x) (also written df/dx, "d f by d x") is what this slope settles to as h shrinks towards zero. Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent, the line that touches the curve at x and has the same direction as the curve there. Practically it is a sensitivity: if f′(x) = 2, a small nudge δ in x changes f by about 2δ.

For f(x) = x² at x = 1, the chord slope is ((1+h)² − 1) / h = (2h + h²) / h = 2 + h. That is 3.5 for h = 1.5, 2.1 for h = 0.1, and it tends to 2 as h goes to 0. So f′(1) = 2, which agrees with the rule you may remember, d(x²)/dx = 2x. The word limit in textbooks is this "what the chord slope settles to" made precise.

Measuring it with code

Because the definition is a recipe, you can follow it numerically. This is called a finite difference: pick a small h and compute the ratio. Two versions matter. The forward difference uses the point and one neighbour, (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h. The central difference uses both neighbours, (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h, which cancels the leading error term and is far more accurate for the same h. Here they are tried on f(x) = ex, whose derivative is ex again, so at x = 1 the true answer is e = 2.71828…

Read the table from the top. Shrinking h from 0.1 to 10−5 makes both errors fall, the forward error by a factor of 10 per decade and the central error by a factor of 100. That is truncation error: the chord is not yet the tangent. But below roughly h = 10−8 the errors turn round and climb, to 0.39 at h = 10−15 and to 2.7 (as large as the answer itself, so no digits are right) at 10−16, where x+h rounds back to x and the numerator is exactly zero. The cause is the floating-point arithmetic from chapter 1: f(x+h) and f(x) agree in their first many digits, so their difference keeps only a few significant ones, and dividing by a tiny h magnifies that noise. Common misconception

"A smaller step gives a better numerical derivative." It does only down to a point. With 64-bit floats the sweet spot for a central difference is around h = 10−5 to 10−6 (about the cube root of machine epsilon, which is where truncation and rounding errors balance). With 32-bit floats it is far coarser, around 10−2, which is why gradient checks are always run in float64.

Finite differences are slow for real models, because they need two evaluations of the whole function per parameter (a model with a billion weights would need two billion forward passes for one gradient). They are the right tool for something else: checking a gradient that you computed some cleverer way. Hold on to that, because the whole of the next chapter is the cleverer way.

Rules for differentiating by hand

You do not derive every derivative from the limit. A short list of facts and rules covers almost everything in machine learning. The facts first.

The sigmoid entry is worth deriving once, because the answer is so tidy. Write σ(x) = (1 + e−x)−1. The chain rule (below) gives σ′(x) = e−x / (1 + e−x)2. Split that as [1 / (1 + e−x)] × [e−x / (1 + e−x)] = σ(x) × (1 − σ(x)), because 1 − σ(x) = e−x / (1 + e−x). The derivative is computed from the output σ(x), which is already in memory after the forward pass. That reuse of forward values is a recurring theme.

Then the rules for combining functions. Let u and v be functions of x.

  • Sum rule. (u + v)′ = u′ + v′. Derivatives are linear, so the gradient of a sum of per-example losses is the sum of per-example gradients. This is why a minibatch gradient is an average of per-example gradients.
  • Constant multiple. (c u)′ = c u′.
  • Product rule. (u v)′ = u′ v + u v′. Each factor takes a turn being differentiated while the other is held still.
  • Chain rule. (u(v(x)))′ = u′(v(x)) · v′(x). The next section.

Every row of the table can be verified with the finite-difference recipe. Do that at a few arbitrary points, once, so that you trust both the table and the checker:

Go deeper why the product rule holds

Start from the definition for u(x)v(x): [u(x+h)v(x+h) − u(x)v(x)] / h. Add and subtract u(x+h)v(x) in the numerator to get u(x+h) [v(x+h) − v(x)] / h + v(x) [u(x+h) − u(x)] / h. As h goes to 0, u(x+h) tends to u(x) (differentiable functions are continuous), the first bracket tends to v′(x) and the second to u′(x). The result is u v′ + v u′. Geometrically: a rectangle with sides u and v grows two thin strips when each side grows, and the strips have areas v du and u dv. The corner du dv is second order and disappears in the limit.

Kinks: where the derivative does not exist

ReLU has a corner at 0: the slope is 0 to the left and 1 to the right, and no single number fits. Strictly the derivative does not exist there. Frameworks pick a convention (PyTorch returns 0 at exactly 0) and move on, because an input landing exactly on 0.0 is rare in floating point and either choice gives a valid subgradient, a slope of some line that stays below the function. Kinks matter more for what they imply about regions: for a ReLU unit with a negative input, the local slope is exactly 0, so the unit passes no learning signal back, and a unit that is negative for every training example is a "dead" unit that never recovers. Functions like argmax or rounding are worse: they are flat almost everywhere and jump elsewhere, so their derivative is zero or undefined and gradients cannot flow through them. Chapter 9 returns to this when it asks what an autodiff system can and cannot differentiate.

The chain rule

Networks are compositions: the output of one function is the input of the next. A single sigmoid neuron with a squared error already has the shape loss(σ(w x + b)). How does the loss respond to w? The change in w moves z = w x + b, which moves σ(z), which moves the loss. Each link has its own sensitivity, and the sensitivities multiply.

if y = f(u) and u = g(x), then dy/dx = (dy/du) × (du/dx).

Analogy: a train of gears

Three gears mesh in a line. If gear A turns 3 times for each turn of B, and B turns 2 times for each turn of C, then A turns 3 × 2 = 6 times for each turn of C. The chain rule is the same statement about rates: dA/dC = (dA/dB)(dB/dC). You never need to know the whole mechanism, only each pair of neighbouring gears. That is exactly what makes it possible to write libraries where each operation knows only its own local derivative.

Where it breaks down: gears are rigid and linear, so the ratio is the same at every position. Real derivatives are local and change with the operating point: the ratio for a sigmoid is 0.25 near z = 0 and nearly 0 when |z| is large. The chain rule multiplies the ratios at the current point, so you must have the forward values on hand.

Why it works

Near a point, a differentiable function looks like a straight line: f(u + δ) ≈ f(u) + f′(u) δ. Push a small change δx through g first: u changes by ≈ g′(x) δx. Push that through f: y changes by ≈ f′(u) × g′(x) δx. The slope of the whole composition is the product. The approximation errors are second order in δx and vanish in the limit, which is the whole proof.

A worked example: one neuron, one example

Take an input x = 2 with label y = 1, and parameters w = 0.3, b = −0.5. The neuron computes z = w x + b, then p = σ(z), and the loss is the cross-entropy L = −[y ln p + (1−y) ln(1−p)] (the reason for this loss is chapter 13; for now it is a function that is small when p is close to y). The graph of the computation is a chain, L ← p ← z ← w, and the derivative of L with respect to w is the product of the four local derivatives along it:

dL/dw = (dL/dp) × (dp/dz) × (dz/dw)

Each factor is something from the table: dL/dp = −y/p + (1−y)/(1−p), dp/dz = p(1−p), and dz/dw = x. Step through the numbers: first the forward pass that fills in every intermediate value, then the backward pass that multiplies local derivatives from the loss back towards the parameters.

Step through

One neuron: forward values, then the chain rule backwards

  1. The inputs. The data (x = 2, label y = 1, green) and the parameters we want gradients for (w and b, purple). The goal is dL/dw and dL/db.
  2. Multiply. w · x = 0.3 × 2 = 0.6. Keep this value; the backward pass needs the forward values.
  3. Add the bias. z = 0.6 + (−0.5) = 0.1.
  4. Sigmoid. p = σ(0.1) = 0.5250. The model says "52.5% likely to be class 1".
  5. Loss. With y = 1 the cross-entropy is −ln(0.525) = 0.6444. The forward pass is done.
  6. Start at the loss. dL/dL = 1 by definition. One step back, dL/dp = −y/p = −1/0.525 = −1.905: raising p would lower the loss steeply.
  7. Through the sigmoid. Multiply by the local derivative p(1−p) = 0.2494: dL/dz = −1.905 × 0.2494 = −0.475. The addition passes this value unchanged to its inputs (the local derivative of a sum is 1), so the product w·x gets −0.475 too.
  8. To the parameters. dL/db = −0.475 × 1. For w the local derivative of w·x with respect to w is x = 2, so dL/dw = −0.475 × 2 = −0.950. Both are negative: increasing w or b lowers the loss.

The same computation in code, with every factor named, next to the famous shortcut and a finite-difference check:

import math

# One neuron, one example:  z = w*x + b,  p = sigmoid(z),  L = -(y*log p + (1-y)*log(1-p))
x, y = 2.0, 1.0
w, b = 0.3, -0.5

def loss(w, b):
    p = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-(w * x + b)))
    return -(y * math.log(p) + (1 - y) * math.log(1 - p))

# Forward pass, keeping every intermediate value
z = w * x + b
p = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))
L = loss(w, b)

# Backward: multiply local derivatives along the chain  L <- p <- z <- w
dL_dp = -(y / p) + (1 - y) / (1 - p)
dp_dz = p * (1 - p)
dz_dw = x
dz_db = 1.0
dL_dw = dL_dp * dp_dz * dz_dw
dL_db = dL_dp * dp_dz * dz_db

print(f"z = {z:.4f}, p = {p:.4f}, L = {L:.4f}")
print(f"dL/dp = {dL_dp:.4f}, dp/dz = {dp_dz:.4f}, dz/dw = {dz_dw}")
print(f"chain rule : dL/dw = {dL_dw:.6f}, dL/db = {dL_db:.6f}")
print(f"shortcut   : (p - y) * x = {(p - y) * x:.6f}, (p - y) = {p - y:.6f}")
h = 1e-6
print(f"numeric    : dL/dw = {(loss(w + h, b) - loss(w - h, b)) / (2 * h):.6f}, "
      f"dL/db = {(loss(w, b + h) - loss(w, b - h)) / (2 * h):.6f}")

Three results agree to six decimals. Notice the shortcut: after multiplying the chain out, dL/dz collapses to p − y, so dL/dw = (p − y) x. The ugly −y/p and p(1−p) cancel. This is not luck: the cross-entropy loss and the sigmoid are made for each other, as chapter 13 shows, and the same cancellation turns up for softmax. The gradient says "how wrong the prediction was, times the input that caused it".

Common misconception

"The chain rule is something done by hand in a derivation." In practice no one differentiates a network on paper. The chain rule is the algorithm: every operation stores its inputs on the forward pass, and on the backward pass multiplies the gradient arriving from above by its own local derivative. The bookkeeping, not the calculus, is what a framework provides.

Many inputs: partial derivatives and the gradient

A model has many parameters, so the loss is a function of many numbers, f(x, y) in the smallest interesting case. A partial derivative ∂f/∂x is the ordinary derivative in x with y frozen as if it were a constant; likewise ∂f/∂y. For f(x, y) = x² + 3y² + xy,

∂f/∂x = 2x + y,    ∂f/∂y = 6y + x.

Collect the partial derivatives into one vector and you have the gradient, written ∇f (read "nabla f" or "grad f"):

∇f(x, y) = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y).

For n parameters the gradient is an n-vector with the same shape as the parameter vector, one slope per parameter. That shape match is not a coincidence: it is what lets the update "w ← w − step × ∇L" make sense. At (1, 2) the example's gradient is (4, 13): increasing y is worth more than three times as much per unit as increasing x.

The gradient is the steepest direction

The partial derivatives give slopes along the two axes. What about a slope along some other direction, a unit vector u? Walk a distance t from the point p in direction u. For small t the function behaves like its tangent plane: f(p + t u) ≈ f(p) + t (∇f · u). So the slope in direction u is the directional derivative, the dot product ∇f · u. From chapter 5, a dot product is |∇f| |u| cosθ = |∇f| cosθ, where θ is the angle between the gradient and u. That is biggest (equal to |∇f|) when θ = 0, i.e. when u points along the gradient; zero when u is perpendicular (walking along a contour keeps f constant); and most negative when u points opposite. Three consequences:

  • ∇f points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that steepest slope.
  • −∇f is the direction of steepest descent, which is why gradient descent subtracts the gradient.
  • The gradient is perpendicular to the contour lines (level sets) of f.

Check it on f = x² + 3y² + xy at (1, 2), measuring the slope in many directions by finite differences:

import math

def f(x, y):
    return x * x + 3 * y * y + x * y

def grad_analytic(x, y):
    return (2 * x + y, 6 * y + x)        # partial derivatives, worked by hand

    return ((f(x + h, y) - f(x - h, y)) / (2 * h),
            (f(x, y + h) - f(x, y - h)) / (2 * h))

print("analytic gradient:", grad_analytic(x, y))

Common misconception

"The negative gradient points at the minimum." It points in the direction of steepest local descent, which equals the direction to the minimum only when the contours are circles. On an elongated bowl the two differ, which is why plain gradient descent zigzags across a narrow valley instead of sliding down it. Fixing this is the job of momentum and adaptive optimisers, chapter 10.

Vectors in, vectors out: Jacobians and shapes

The functions inside a network take vectors and return vectors: a linear layer maps an input of length 4 to an output of length 3. For f taking an n-vector to an m-vector, there are m × n partial derivatives, ∂fi/∂xj, one for each (output i, input j) pair. Arranged as a matrix they form the Jacobian J of shape (m, n): row i is the gradient of output i. The Jacobian is the best linear approximation to f near a point: f(x + δ) ≈ f(x) + J δ. In the language of chapter 6, it is "the matrix that describes how f acts on small nudges".

The chain rule for vector functions is then matrix multiplication. If y = g(x) with Jacobian Jg of shape (m, n) and z = f(y) with Jacobian Jf of shape (p, m), then the Jacobian of the composition is Jf Jg with shape (p, m)(m, n) = (p, n). The inner sizes match exactly because one function's output is the next one's input. Shapes are a contract, and the chain rule is where the contract earns its keep: if your derivative has the wrong shape you have made an algebra mistake.

Training uses a special case: the final output is a scalar loss L, so the Jacobian of L with respect to anything is a single row, which we store as the gradient vector of the same shape as that thing. Going backwards, the gradient with respect to a layer's input is the Jacobian transposed times the gradient with respect to its output:

∇xL = JT ∇yL.

The linear layer, derived

Take the layer y = W x with W of shape (out, in) = (3, 4) and x of shape (4,), and some downstream loss L. Suppose the backward pass has delivered r = ∇yL, shape (3,). Entry yi = ∑j Wij xj, so Wij influences only yi, and with strength xj. Hence

∂L/∂Wij = ri xj   ⇒   ∇WL = r xT (an outer product, shape (3, 4)).

And xj influences every yi with strength Wij, so ∂L/∂xj = ∑i ri Wij, that is ∇xL = WT r, shape (4,). Shapes did all the checking: the only way to build a (3, 4) from a (3,) and a (4,) is the outer product, and the only way to build a (4,) from W (3, 4) and r (3,) is WT r. For a batch X of shape (B, in) and Y = X WT of shape (B, out), the same logic gives ∇WL = (∇YL)T X, shape (out, in), which sums the per-example outer products, and ∇XL = (∇YL) W, shape (B, in).

Here is that, for L = ½ |W x − t|², checked by a reusable gradient check: perturb each entry, recompute the loss, compare. The script also verifies the softmax-plus-cross-entropy gradient that the derivation below promises.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W = rng.standard_normal((3, 4))      # (out, in)
x = rng.standard_normal(4)           # (in,)
t = rng.standard_normal(3)           # target, (out,)

def loss(W):
    r = W @ x - t
    return 0.5 * r @ r               # L = 1/2 |Wx - t|^2

# By hand: r = Wx - t, dL/dr = r, and W[i,j] only touches r[i] through x[j],
# so dL/dW[i,j] = r[i] * x[j]: an outer product, shape (3, 4) = shape of W.
r = W @ x - t
grad_W = np.outer(r, x)
grad_x = W.T @ r                     # dL/dx, shape (4,)

def grad_check(f, A, eps=1e-6):
    """Central-difference gradient of scalar f at array A, one entry at a time."""
    G = np.zeros_like(A)
    for idx in np.ndindex(A.shape):
        Ap, Am = A.copy(), A.copy()
        Ap[idx] += eps
        Am[idx] -= eps
        G[idx] = (f(Ap) - f(Am)) / (2 * eps)
    return G

num = grad_check(loss, W)
rel = np.linalg.norm(grad_W - num) / (np.linalg.norm(grad_W) + np.linalg.norm(num))
print("grad_W shape:", grad_W.shape, " grad_x shape:", grad_x.shape)
print("relative error vs numeric:", f"{rel:.2e}")

# Softmax + cross-entropy: the gradient with respect to the logits is p - onehot
logits = rng.standard_normal(5)
target = 2
def ce(l):
    e = np.exp(l - l.max())
    return -np.log(e[target] / e.sum())
p = np.exp(logits - logits.max()); p /= p.sum()
analytic = p.copy(); analytic[target] -= 1
numeric = grad_check(ce, logits)
print("softmax-CE gradient, analytic:", np.round(analytic, 4))
print("softmax-CE gradient, numeric :", np.round(numeric, 4))

# Batched version: X is (B, in), Y = X W^T is (B, out); the loss sums over the batch
B = 5
X = rng.standard_normal((B, 4))
T = rng.standard_normal((B, 3))
def batch_loss(W):
    R = X @ W.T - T
    return 0.5 * (R * R).sum()
R = X @ W.T - T                      # (B, out)
dW = R.T @ X                         # (out, B) @ (B, in) = (out, in)
dX = R @ W                           # (B, out) @ (out, in) = (B, in)
num_b = grad_check(batch_loss, W)
rel_b = np.linalg.norm(dW - num_b) / (np.linalg.norm(dW) + np.linalg.norm(num_b))
print("batched: dW", dW.shape, "dX", dX.shape, "relative error", f"{rel_b:.2e}")

batched: dW (3, 4) dX (5, 4) relative error 1.99e-10

Relative errors near 10−10 to 10−11 mean the analytic and numeric gradients agree. The rule of thumb for float64 is that anything under about 10−6 is a pass, and anything above 10−3 is a bug. A relative error is the right yardstick, because gradients can be tiny or huge; comparing absolute differences hides bugs when gradients are small.

Go deeper the softmax cross-entropy gradient

Logits z1..K, probabilities pi = ezi / ∑j ezj, and the loss for true class t is L = −ln pt. Expand: L = −zt + ln ∑j ezj. Differentiate with respect to zi: the first term gives −1 if i = t and 0 otherwise; the second, by the chain rule on ln, gives ezi / ∑j ezj = pi. So ∂L/∂zi = pi − [i = t]: the predicted probabilities, with 1 subtracted at the true class. The gradient is bounded between −1 and 1 and vanishes as the model becomes confidently right. The same derivation for the sigmoid neuron above gave p − y.

Curvature in one paragraph

The derivative of the derivative, f″(x), measures how the slope itself changes: curvature. For f(x) = ½ λ x² the slope is λ x and the curvature is the constant λ. A steep, narrow bowl has a large λ; a wide shallow one a small λ. For several variables the matrix of second partial derivatives is the Hessian, and its largest eigenvalue (chapter 7) plays the role of λ in the steepest direction. You do not need to compute Hessians in practice, but one fact will drive the next chapter: gradient descent with step size η on the quadratic above updates x ← x − η λ x = (1 − ηλ) x, which shrinks x only if |1 − ηλ| < 1, i.e. η < 2/λ. Curvature caps the usable step size.

What goes wrong

Vanishing and exploding products

The chain rule multiplies one local derivative per layer. A product of many numbers below 1 collapses, and a product of many numbers above 1 blows up. The sigmoid derivative is at most 0.25, so the best case through a stack of sigmoid layers is 0.25 per layer:

import math

sigmoid = lambda z: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))
d = lambda z: sigmoid(z) * (1 - sigmoid(z))

print("max of sigmoid'(z):", d(0.0))
g = 1.0
for layer in range(1, 21):
    g *= d(0.0)                       # best case: every layer sits at z = 0
    if layer in (1, 2, 5, 10, 20):
        print(f"after {layer:2d} sigmoid layers the gradient is scaled by at most {g:.2e}")

# ReLU: derivative is exactly 1 on the active side, so the product does not shrink
print("ReLU, 20 active layers:", 1.0 ** 20)
max of sigmoid'(z): 0.25
after  1 sigmoid layers the gradient is scaled by at most 2.50e-01
after  2 sigmoid layers the gradient is scaled by at most 6.25e-02
after  5 sigmoid layers the gradient is scaled by at most 9.77e-04
after 10 sigmoid layers the gradient is scaled by at most 9.54e-07
after 20 sigmoid layers the gradient is scaled by at most 9.09e-13
ReLU, 20 active layers: 1.0

After ten sigmoid layers the signal is below 10−6 of its original size, and that is the best case (and ignores the weight matrices, which multiply in too). This is the vanishing-gradient problem, and it is why ReLU (derivative exactly 1 where active), careful initialisation and residual connections were such important advances. A2 chapter 3 (Initialisation, Normalisation & Residuals) picks it up. The mirror problem, exploding gradients, arises when the factors exceed 1, and is handled by gradient clipping (chapter 10).

Other traps

  • Non-differentiable steps. Thresholding, argmax, sampling and rounding block gradients. Methods such as policy gradients (A3) and straight-through estimators exist to get around exactly this.
  • Wrong shape, silently right numbers. A gradient with shape (4,) when it should be (4, 1) can broadcast (chapter 2) into a wrong but plausible result. Always assert shapes.
  • Forgetting the sum over uses. If one value feeds two places, its gradient is the sum of the contributions from both. Chapter 9 makes this explicit.
  • Trusting a check that is too loose. A gradient check done in float32, or with a step of 10−15, tells you nothing; use float64 and h around 10−5 to 10−6.

Systems connection

Derivatives are sensitivities, and you have used them already without the name. In Reliability, chapter 1 a request that must pass through three services in series succeeds with probability A = A1 A2 A3. The product rule gives ∂A/∂A1 = A2 A3: the value of improving service 1 by one point is the availability of the others combined, so the weakest service has the largest payoff per unit of effort. And the performance method of Systems, chapter 22 is a finite-difference gradient: change one thing, measure, divide.

Key takeaways

  • The derivative is the slope that chord slopes settle to: the sensitivity of the output to a small change in the input. A central finite difference measures it to about 10−10, with a sweet-spot step near 10−5 because rounding error grows as h shrinks.
  • The chain rule multiplies local derivatives along a composition. Backpropagation is nothing more than this rule applied systematically, with forward values saved for reuse.
  • The gradient collects the partial derivatives into a vector with the same shape as the parameters. It points in the direction of steepest ascent, its length is that slope, and it is perpendicular to contours. It is a local direction, not an arrow to the minimum.
  • For vector functions the derivative is a Jacobian, and the chain rule is a product of Jacobians. Reading shapes is the fastest check: for y = W x, ∇WL = (∇yL) xT and ∇xL = WT ∇yL.
  • Always check an analytic gradient against finite differences, in float64, using relative error.
  • Long products of small factors vanish (sigmoid: at most 0.25 per layer) and of large factors explode. Both resurface in chapters 9 to 11.

Check your understanding

You estimate a derivative with the central difference in float64. You shrink h from 10−5 to 10−14 and the error gets much worse. Why?

  1. The central difference is only accurate for h larger than 0.1
  2. The truncation error grows as h shrinks
  3. f(x+h) and f(x−h) agree in almost all their digits, so rounding error dominates the difference, and dividing by a tiny h magnifies it
  4. Python's exp function is less accurate for arguments close to each other
Truncation error falls as h shrinks (option 2 has it backwards), but each function value carries a rounding error of about 10−16 relative, and the subtraction of nearly equal numbers leaves few correct digits. Dividing by 2h = 2×10−14 turns a 10−16 noise into roughly 10−2 error. The central difference is fine for moderate h (option 1 is false).

A network has 10 sigmoid layers. In the best case every sigmoid sits at z = 0. Ignoring the weights, about how much is the gradient scaled by passing back through all ten?

  1. About 0.25
  2. About 10−6
  3. About 2.5
  4. About 10−12
The chain rule multiplies one factor per layer, and the largest value of σ′ is 0.25. So the best case is 0.2510 ≈ 9.5×10−7. The value 0.25 (option 0) would be the answer for a single layer; 10−12 is roughly the value after twenty layers.

For f(x, y) = x² + 3y² the gradient at (1, 2) is (2, 12). Which statement is correct?

  1. Moving in direction (2, 12) leaves f unchanged to first order
  2. Moving in direction (−2, −12) is the quickest way to reach the minimum at the origin, in a straight line
  3. The slope of f along the x-axis at this point is 12
  4. Moving a small step along (−2, −12) decreases f faster than any other direction of the same length, though it does not point at the origin
The negative gradient is the steepest local descent direction (option 3). Directions that leave f unchanged are perpendicular to the gradient, not along it (option 0). The origin lies in direction (−1, −2), not (−2, −12), so option 1 is the tempting misconception. The slope along x is the partial derivative ∂f/∂x = 2, not 12.

A layer computes y = W x with W of shape (5, 8) and x of shape (8,). The backward pass delivers r = ∇yL of shape (5,). What are the correct gradients and shapes?

  1. ∇WL = r xT with shape (5, 8), and ∇xL = WT r with shape (8,)
  2. ∇WL = x rT with shape (8, 5), and ∇xL = W r with shape (5,)
  3. ∇WL = r · x (a single number), and ∇xL = r
  4. ∇WL = WT r with shape (8,), and ∇xL = r xT with shape (5, 8)
The gradient of a thing always has the shape of that thing: (5, 8) for W and (8,) for x. Only r xT builds a (5, 8) from (5,) and (8,), and only WT r maps (5,) to (8,). Option 1 has the right ingredients with transposed shapes, and the matrix product W r does not even exist for (5, 8) and (5,).

A hidden ReLU unit receives a negative pre-activation for every example in the training set. What happens to the gradient reaching that unit's incoming weights, and why?

  1. It is large, because the unit is far from zero
  2. It is undefined, so training crashes
  3. It is exactly zero, because the local derivative of ReLU is 0 for negative inputs and the chain rule multiplies it in
  4. It equals the loss gradient, because ReLU passes gradients through unchanged
The chain rule multiplies by the local derivative, which is 0 in the negative region, so nothing flows back to those weights and they never update: a dead unit. The derivative is undefined only at exactly 0, which frameworks resolve by a convention. ReLU passes gradients unchanged only on its active side.

You compare your analytic gradient with a float64 central-difference check (h = 10−6) and get a relative error of 0.02. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. h is too small; try 10−12
  2. There is a bug in the analytic gradient (a wrong factor, a missing term or a transposed shape)
  3. Float64 is not precise enough to check gradients
  4. Finite differences always disagree with analytic gradients by a few per cent
With float64 and h around 10−6, a correct gradient agrees to 10−8 or better; 0.02 is a large discrepancy. Making h smaller would make the numeric side worse, not better (the V-shaped error curve). The fix is to look for the bug, typically by checking one layer or one parameter at a time.
