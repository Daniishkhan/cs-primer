Reading Papers Efficiently

Almost everything in the rest of this path was first written down in a research paper, and new papers appear faster than anyone can read them. This chapter gives you a three-pass method for deciding what deserves your time, a map of what to skip, a checklist for testing whether a claim is believable, and a procedure for reproducing a result when it matters.

Why this matters

The later courses lean on primary sources: the Transformer paper, the LoRA paper, the DPO paper, technical reports for open models, benchmark papers whose numbers your team will quote in a launch review. An engineer who cannot read them is limited to blog-post summaries of blog-post summaries, and summaries drop exactly the caveats you need. An engineer who reads every word of every paper never ships anything. After this chapter you will be able to triage a paper in ten minutes, extract its claim in one sentence, list the reasons you might not believe it, and decide whether a reproduction is worth a day of your time. It builds directly on Reproducible Experiments and Statistics for Experiments.

A paper is a claim with evidence

A research paper is an argument: the authors claim something, show evidence, and ask you to update your beliefs. Reading well means separating those three parts. The claim is the sentence the authors would defend if you could ask them one question ("our method reaches the same accuracy with a third of the compute"). The evidence is what they show in support: experiments, proofs, measurements. Everything else is packaging: motivation, related work, polish.

Your reason for reading decides how deep to go, so decide it first. An engineer usually has one of four reasons:

  • Keeping up. You want to know that a technique exists and roughly what it does. A first pass is enough.
  • Using an idea. You may adopt it in your system. You need the mechanism, the conditions it was tested under, and the failure cases: a careful second pass plus a claim check.
  • Betting on a number. A headline result will drive a decision (switch model, buy hardware, change a metric). Check the evidence as if you were the sceptical reviewer, and reproduce it if the stakes justify it.
  • Learning a field. You read several papers around one topic and want the map, not one paper's details. This is a literature survey, covered near the end.

Papers also come in kinds, and the kind tells you what to look for. A new-method paper (Vaswani et al., 2017, "Attention Is All You Need") proposes something and shows it works. An analysis paper studies something that already exists. A benchmark or dataset paper defines a test others will use, so its main risk is that the test measures the wrong thing. A technical report describes a model or system a lab built, often with little detail about what was tried and discarded. A survey organises a field. A position paper argues for a view. Each calls for different suspicion: a new-method paper will flatter its method, a benchmark paper will flatter its benchmark, and a technical report will omit what did not work.

The three-pass method

S. Keshav's "How to Read a Paper" (ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 2007) is the short, durable answer to reading efficiently. Its central idea is to read in up to three passes instead of starting at the first word and pushing on to the last. Each pass has a goal and an optional stopping point.

Pass 1: a bird's-eye view (five to ten minutes)

Keshav's first pass has five steps: read the title, abstract and introduction carefully; read the section and sub-section headings and ignore the rest; read the conclusions; glance over the references and tick off ones you know. Then try to answer his five "Cs":

Category
What type of paper is this: a new method, a measurement, an analysis, a system description?
Context
Which other papers is it related to, and which problem formulation does it build on?
Correctness
Do the assumptions look reasonable at first glance?
Contributions
What does the paper say is new, in the authors' own list?
Clarity
Is it well enough written that a second pass will be productive?

Write the claim as one sentence before you move on. If you cannot, the paper is unclear or you lack background, and either is a good reason to stop. For ML papers add two questions of your own: what is the headline number, and against what baseline? A first pass that ends with "the paper reports 84.1 on a reasoning benchmark, against 81.7 for the previous best" is a good first pass.

Pass 2: grasp the content, skip the details (up to an hour)

Now read with a pencil. Keshav's advice is to ignore details such as proofs and to jot down key points in the margins. For ML papers the second pass has a specific order that works better than front to back:

  1. Look at the figures and tables first. Are axes labelled? Are there error bars or several seeds? Which rows are the authors' method and which are baselines?
  2. Read the method section for the idea, not the notation. Redraw the pipeline on paper: inputs, what is learned, outputs, loss. If you can draw it you understand it.
  3. Read the experimental setup. Datasets, splits, metrics, model sizes, training budget, hyperparameter search. This is where fair comparisons are made or lost.
  4. Read the ablations. An ablation removes one component to see what the result depends on. If there are none, the paper has shown that a bundle works, not which part matters.
  5. Read the limitations and failure cases (often a short paragraph near the end, or the appendix).
  6. Mark unread references that the argument leans on.

At the end you should be able to explain the paper to a colleague in two minutes, including one thing that worries you about it. Keshav notes that sometimes you will not understand a paper even after two passes; your options are to set it aside, to come back after reading background material, or to persevere into a third pass.

Pass 3: re-create it (hours, for the few that matter)

The third pass is for papers you will build on, review, or bet money on. Keshav's key move is to virtually re-implement the paper: make the same assumptions as the authors and re-create the work in your head or on paper, then compare your version with theirs. Where they differ, you have found either an innovation you can learn from or a hidden assumption they did not state. For an ML paper this often means working through the key equation with small numbers, as we did for softmax in Floating Point & Numerical Stability, or implementing the core component in 30 lines and checking its shapes and gradients (Automatic Differentiation showed how). Keshav gives beginners several hours for this pass; an expert is faster because they already hold the background.

Analogy: inspecting a house before you buy

Pass 1 is walking round the outside with the estate agent's brochure: roof, garden, which street, does it match the photos. Pass 2 is going through every room with a notepad and reading the survey. Pass 3 is hiring someone to open the walls and test the wiring. You would not open the walls of every house you view, and you would not buy one after only walking round the outside.

Where it breaks down: a house sits still, but a paper's claims depend on code and data you often cannot see, and a paper has no surveyor of its own. Peer review checks that the argument reads as sound; it does not rerun the experiments.

What to skip, and in what order

Papers are written in the order a reader of a finished argument wants, not in the order you need information. The map below shows where the value usually is for an engineer deciding whether to use a result.

Some specific things you can safely leave on the first pass:

  • Proofs and derivations in the appendix. Read the statement of a theorem and the one-line intuition; check the proof only if your use depends on it.
  • The related-work section. Skim it for the field's map and for names to search, but do not try to follow every comparison.
  • Hyperparameter tables. Skip on first read, and keep them for the day you reproduce the result.
  • Pages and pages of qualitative samples. Look at three, as you would for any exploratory look at data, then move on. Authors choose samples that flatter.

Things you should not skip, however long the paper: the exact definition of the metric, how the test set was built, what the baselines were given (tuning budget, model size, data), and the limitations paragraph. Technical reports for large models can run to a hundred pages. Read the data, evaluation and safety or limitation sections for what you need and treat the rest as a reference to search.

Checking whether a claim is believable

Most published ML improvements are small, measured once, on a benchmark that the community has been tuning against for years. Reasonable scepticism is a skill, not a mood. Lipton and Steinhardt ("Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship", 2018) catalogued recurring problems: failure to separate explanation from speculation, mathiness, and misuse of language. Melis, Dyer and Blunsom ("On the State of the Art of Evaluation in Neural Language Models", 2017) re-tuned older baselines and found that a standard LSTM beat several newer architectures reported as improvements. Henderson et al. ("Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters", AAAI 2018) showed that reinforcement-learning results could change substantially with random seeds, codebase and hyperparameters. The pattern is the same: the headline gap was smaller than the noise or the tuning effort around it.

Turn that history into a checklist you run on any paper whose number you intend to rely on.

QuestionWhat to look forRed flag
What exactly is claimed?One sentence with a number, a baseline and a condition."State of the art" with no table that says on what.
Were baselines given equal effort?Same tuning budget, same data, same compute, same model size.Baseline numbers copied from an older paper with different settings.
How noisy is the result?Several seeds, error bars, or a stated test-set size.One run, a gap under a point, no interval.
Could it be leakage?Test data kept apart from training and tuning; contamination checks for web-scale data.Hyperparameters chosen on the test set; benchmark text likely in the training corpus.
Is the gain explained by more resources?Parameters, tokens and compute reported for every row.The new model is bigger, trained longer or given more samples than the baseline.
Does the ablation support the story?Removing the proposed part hurts; removing others does not hurt more.No ablations, or ablations on a different dataset.
Does the metric match the claim?The measured quantity is what the abstract talks about."Reasoning improves" backed only by multiple-choice accuracy on one benchmark.
Who ran it?Independent replication, or code and data you can run.Only the authors' own numbers, no code, no model.

Variance: the seed test

You met seeds in Reproducible Experiments. Here is why they matter when you read. The script below builds a toy comparison: a baseline logistic-regression classifier and a "variant" that differs only in the scale of the random initial weights. Each seed draws its own small training set (40 points) and its own small test set (100 points). We run both for 30 seeds and look at the same comparisons a paper might report.

import numpy as np

def make_data(n, rng):
    """Two overlapping Gaussian blobs in 2-D."""
    y = rng.integers(0, 2, n)
    x = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 2)) + np.where(y[:, None] == 1, 0.9, -0.9)
    return x, y

def train_logreg(x, y, rng, steps=30, lr=0.5, init_scale=1.0):
    w = rng.normal(0, init_scale, 2)
    b = 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(x @ w + b)))
        w -= lr * x.T @ (p - y) / len(y)
        b -= lr * np.mean(p - y)
    return w, b

def accuracy(w, b, x, y):
    return np.mean(((x @ w + b) > 0) == y)

def run(seed, init_scale):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    xtr, ytr = make_data(40, rng)
    xte, yte = make_data(100, rng)     # a fresh small test set per seed
    w, b = train_logreg(xtr, ytr, rng, init_scale=init_scale)
    return accuracy(w, b, xte, yte)

baseline = np.array([run(s, 1.0) for s in range(30)])
variant = np.array([run(s, 0.1) for s in range(30)])   # the "new trick": smaller init

print(f"baseline: best seed {baseline.max():.3f}, mean {baseline.mean():.3f}, sd {baseline.std(ddof=1):.3f}")
print(f"variant : best seed {variant.max():.3f}, mean {variant.mean():.3f}, sd {variant.std(ddof=1):.3f}")
print(f"'paper' claim (seed 0 vs 0):    {100*(variant[0]-baseline[0]):+.1f} points")
d = variant - baseline                                  # paired by seed
print(f"largest single-seed gap:       {100*d.max():+.1f} points")
print(f"seeds where variant is worse:  {(d<0).sum()} of {len(d)}")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
boots = [rng.choice(d, len(d)).mean() for _ in range(10000)]
lo, hi = np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"paired mean difference: {100*d.mean():+.2f} points, 95% bootstrap CI [{100*lo:+.2f}, {100*hi:+.2f}]")
baseline: best seed 0.960, mean 0.888, sd 0.031
variant : best seed 0.960, mean 0.893, sd 0.029
'paper' claim (seed 0 vs 0):    +4.0 points
largest single-seed gap:       +4.0 points
seeds where variant is worse:  3 of 30
paired mean difference: +0.47 points, 95% bootstrap CI [+0.10, +0.87]

A paper that reported only seed 0 would claim a 4-point win, as flattering as any seed in this experiment. Across all 30 seeds the paired mean difference is +0.47 points with a bootstrap interval that barely excludes zero (the bootstrap is the resampling method from Statistics for Experiments; it returns in Statistics for Evals). The variant probably helps a little. It does not help by four points. The picture makes the same point.

The noise floor from the test-set size

Even when a paper runs one seed, you can bound how much of a gap is noise from the size of the test set alone. An accuracy measured on n independent items has standard error √(p(1−p)/n). The difference of two such scores has a standard error about √2 times larger, and a gap smaller than two of those is not distinguishable from noise. Treating the two scores as independent overstates the noise when both models are scored on the same items (a paired test does better), so read the result as a generous upper bound on the floor, not a verdict. It is still a fast sanity check you can do on a first pass:

import math

def accuracy_se(acc, n):
    """Standard error of an accuracy measured on n independent test items."""
    return math.sqrt(acc * (1 - acc) / n)

def smallest_detectable_gap(acc, n):
    """Rough gap (in accuracy points) between two models, each scored on the same n items,
    below which the difference is within about two standard errors of noise.
    Treats the two scores as independent: a conservative upper bound (paired tests do better)."""
    return 2 * math.sqrt(2) * accuracy_se(acc, n)

claims = [("Paper A", 0.812, 0.805, 1000),
          ("Paper B", 0.874, 0.851, 5000),
          ("Paper C", 0.652, 0.640, 500)]
for name, new, old, n in claims:
    gap = new - old
    floor = smallest_detectable_gap(old, n)
    verdict = "above noise floor" if gap > floor else "within noise"
    print(f"{name}: claimed +{100*gap:.1f} pts on n={n}; noise floor about {100*floor:.1f} pts -> {verdict}")
Paper A: claimed +0.7 pts on n=1000; noise floor about 3.5 pts -> within noise
Paper B: claimed +2.3 pts on n=5000; noise floor about 1.4 pts -> above noise floor
Paper C: claimed +1.2 pts on n=500; noise floor about 6.1 pts -> within noise

Paper A and Paper C claim gains that a 500- or 1,000-item test set cannot resolve, however well the experiment was run. Paper B's gain is bigger than the floor and deserves a closer look at baselines instead. This check is crude by design. It does not prove a claim; it tells you where to spend your scepticism.

Common misconception

"If it is on arXiv with a famous author, or was accepted at a top conference, the result holds." Neither is a verification. arXiv has no review at all, and conference reviewers read a PDF in a few hours; they usually do not run the code or retune the baselines. Reputation tells you where to look first, not what you will find. Equally wrong is the opposite reflex of dismissing everything: most papers report something true about the setting they tested. The question to ask is "true under which conditions, by how much, and would I see it in my setting?"

Reproducing a result

A reproduction reruns the authors' own code and data to see whether you obtain their numbers. A replication asks the same question with new data or a fresh implementation. The terms are used inconsistently across fields, so say which you did. Reproduction tests the paper's reporting; replication tests the finding. You rarely need either for every paper. Do one when a decision rests on the number, when a result looks too good for the effort behind it, or when you plan to build on the method for months.

A ladder of effort

  1. Check the artefacts. Is there code, a licence you can live with, a pinned environment, released weights, the evaluation set? A missing item here tells you the cost of the next rung. Papers that pass venue reproducibility checklists (Pineau et al., "Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research", JMLR 2021, describes the NeurIPS programme) are more likely to ship these.
  2. Run their evaluation on their released model. This is cheap and separates "the numbers in the table are what the checkpoint produces" from everything else. If the released model cannot reach its table, stop and note it.
  3. Rerun their training at a small scale. Use the smallest configuration that still shows the effect: fewer steps, a smaller model, a subset. Compare the direction and rough size of the effect, not the third decimal. Estimate the cost first (next chapter, GPUs, Clouds & What Compute Costs).
  4. Add the controls the paper lacked. More seeds, a better-tuned baseline, a second dataset, a held-out check for leakage. This is where most of the information is.
  5. Re-implement the core. Write the key component from the paper alone, in the style of the third pass. Differences between your version and theirs show which details the paper under-specified.

Keep a run log as in Reproducible Experiments: commit, data hash, environment, seed, command, result. Put a time box on the work before you begin ("two days, then I report what I have"), because reproductions are a classic way to lose a week.

When the numbers do not match

Failing to reproduce a result is not the same as the paper being wrong. Work through causes in rough order of likelihood: an environment or library version difference; a different data split or preprocessing step; an unreported detail (learning-rate schedule, warm-up, evaluation prompt, number of samples); randomness larger than you assumed; a bug in your code; and, last, an error or inflated claim in the paper. Check the repository's issues for others who hit the same gap. Write to the authors with a short, specific message: what you ran, what you got, what you suspect. Report your outcome using plain categories: reproduced, reproduced with caveats (say which), or not reproduced (say what you tried).

Reading in volume: surveys and note-taking

When you are learning a field rather than one paper, Keshav's literature-survey recipe works well. Use a scholarly search tool (Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, arXiv listing pages) with a few keywords to find three to five recent papers on the topic, ideally including a survey. Read each with pass 1 and follow their reference lists backwards. Note which authors and venues recur, then scan recent proceedings for those venues. Make two passes through the shortlist. If many papers cite one you have not found, get that one and iterate. Following citations forward (who cites this paper?) finds the critiques and the follow-ups, which is often where the caveats live.

Keep one page of notes per paper you get to pass 2, in the same shape every time so you can search them later:

Title, authors, year, link
Kind of paper: new method / analysis / benchmark / report / survey
Claim (one sentence, with the headline number and its baseline):
Evidence I believe:
Evidence I do not believe yet, and why:
Setup details I would need to reproduce it (data, compute, tuning):
One idea to reuse:
Follow-ups (papers to read, code to run):

Reading groups multiply the effect. Pass 1 in advance, one person presents the claim and the evidence, everyone else attacks the evidence. Ten minutes of disagreement teaches more than another solo hour.

State of play, October 2026

Where papers are found changes. arXiv remains the main preprint server for ML and OpenReview hosts the reviews and discussion for several major venues. The long-running site Papers with Code, which linked papers to code and leaderboards, was shut down in late July 2025; its address now leads to a Hugging Face "Trending Papers" page, which lists popular papers with their code but does not replace the benchmark leaderboards. I could confirm this from secondary reports, not from an official statement, so check the current site. Because leaderboards are self-reported and often stale, treat any leaderboard number as a lead for the checklist above, not as a measurement (see Reading Leaderboards Critically). Venue practices such as reproducibility checklists and code-submission policies also change between years.

Systems connection

You already read dense technical documents sceptically: RFCs, vendor datasheets, a postmortem after an outage. The habits transfer. A vendor's throughput figure is a claim with a benchmark and a configuration, like a paper's accuracy number; see Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation for sanity-checking it, and Postmortems & Learning from Incidents for reading an account of what happened without being captured by its narrative. Reproducing a paper is also a form of hypothesis-driven testing: state what you expect before you run it.

Key takeaways

  • A paper is a claim plus evidence. Write the claim in one sentence, with its number and baseline, before judging it.
  • Read in up to three passes (Keshav, 2007): scan in five to ten minutes, grasp in up to an hour, re-create only the few papers that matter. Stop as soon as your question is answered.
  • Spend your attention on the experiments, baselines and ablations. Defer proofs, related work and the appendix.
  • Check claims against variance, baseline fairness, leakage, resources and metric fit. Small gaps measured once are usually noise.
  • A quick noise floor from the test-set size, √(2p(1−p)/n) per standard error, tells you which gaps are even resolvable.
  • Reproduce when a decision rests on the number. Start with their evaluation of their checkpoint, time-box the work, and report one of three outcomes.
  • The same habits apply to specs, datasheets and benchmarks. The statistics return in the evals course; the writing-up of what you found is the subject of Writing for Engineers.

Check your understanding

You have ten minutes and a new paper that might affect your team's choice of embedding model. Following the three-pass method, what do you do?

  1. Read it from the abstract to the conclusion at a steady pace, skipping only the appendix.
  2. Read the title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusions and figures, then write the claim, the headline number and its baseline.
  3. Read the related-work section to see who else is working on the problem, then decide.
  4. Reimplement the main equation to check it, since only that proves the paper is correct.
The first pass is a bird's-eye view that lets you decide whether to continue. Reading straight through is exactly what the method replaces. Related work is a skim item, and reimplementation belongs to the third pass, which is for papers you will build on or bet on, not for triage.

A paper reports 80.5% against a previous 79.8% on a 1,000-item test set, one run. Which statement is best supported?

  1. The method is better, because the gap is positive and it is the paper's own run.
  2. The method is worse, because a gap under one point is always negative in practice.
  3. The result is a clear win if the training set was large enough.
  4. The gap is well inside the noise from the test-set size alone, so the paper has not shown an improvement.
Two standard errors for the difference at this accuracy and size are around 3 to 4 points, far more than 0.7. That says the evidence is too weak, not that the method is worse. A bigger training set does not shrink the noise from a small test set.

In the seed experiment, seed 0 shows a 4-point gain but the mean over 30 seeds is +0.47 points. Which lesson is most accurate?

  1. A single run can badly overstate a typical effect, so claims should come with several seeds and an interval.
  2. Seeds do not matter in practice, because the mean is still positive.
  3. The authors must have cheated by choosing seed 0.
  4. Averages are always more trustworthy than individual runs, so single runs should be ignored entirely.
The point is the spread: one run can sit anywhere in a distribution. Nothing suggests cheating; the same thing happens honestly when only one run is affordable. A single run is still data, but it needs the caveat that its noise is unknown.

Your reproduction of a published training run lands 2 points below the paper's number. What should you check first?

  1. Write to the editor asking for a retraction.
  2. Assume the paper's number was fabricated and drop the method.
  3. Environment and library versions, data split and preprocessing, and any unreported detail such as the schedule or evaluation prompt.
  4. Nothing: a 2-point difference is always within noise.
Mismatches are far more often caused by version differences, preprocessing or unreported details than by errors in the paper. Whether 2 points is noise depends on seeds and test-set size, which you should measure, not assume. Accusations come last, after you have exhausted the ordinary causes.

A new model beats the baseline in every row of the paper's table. The new model has twice the parameters and was trained on twice the tokens. What is the most important question?

  1. Whether the table has enough decimal places.
  2. Whether the gain comes from the method or from the extra resources, which needs a comparison at equal parameters and compute.
  3. Whether the paper has a section called "Related Work".
  4. Whether the authors used British or American spelling in the figures.
An improvement confounded with resources does not isolate the method. The remaining options concern presentation, not the evidence. A resource-matched baseline or a scaling comparison is what turns "bigger is better" into a claim about the method.

Which reason best justifies spending two days reproducing a paper?

  1. The paper is popular on social media.
  2. You want to be certain every number in the paper is correct to the last digit.
  3. It is a requirement to reproduce every paper you read.
  4. Your team is about to base a months-long project on its headline result, and a cheap check of the released model is possible.
Reproduction is an investment sized by the decision it informs. Popularity is not a reason, exact digits are neither achievable nor necessary, and reproducing everything would stop you reading. Starting with the released model's evaluation also keeps the cost low.
