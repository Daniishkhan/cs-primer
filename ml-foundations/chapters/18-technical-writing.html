Writing for Engineers

Most technical decisions are made by people who did not do the work, reading a document in the few minutes they can spare. This chapter shows how to write for that reader: put the answer first, make claims checkable, and use three working documents (the design doc, the experiment report and the error-analysis memo) that turn your work into decisions.

Why this matters

An experiment nobody understood changes nothing. A design that lives in one head cannot be reviewed, and a failure analysis that stays in a spreadsheet does not get fixed. At work, your results reach decision-makers through writing, and the quality of that writing decides whether the right thing happens next. In AI work the stakes are higher than usual: the results are statistical, the numbers carry uncertainty, and the temptation to write a persuasive story from a noisy chart is constant. After this chapter you will be able to write a one-page design proposal, an experiment report whose conclusion matches its evidence, and an error-analysis memo that ends in a ranked list of fixes. The same documents are the deliverables of the later courses: Error Analysis is the method behind the memo, and the take-home studios in the delivery course grade your writing as much as your code.

Write for a reader with five minutes

Start from the reader, not from the work. Your reader is a busy person who knows less about your project than you do and who must decide something: approve the design, ship the model, fund the next month. Google's technical writing course names the trap in one phrase, the curse of knowledge: once you know something it is very hard to remember what it was like not to know it, so you skip steps, use your own shorthand and bury the point under the history of how you found it. The course's advice is to identify your target audience, what they already know and what they need to learn, and to state the key points at the start of the document along with its scope and audience.

That advice gives the structure of nearly every working document, the inverted pyramid borrowed from journalism. The first paragraph holds the answer and the decision you want. The next layer holds the evidence that makes the answer believable. The bottom layer holds the detail needed to check or repeat the work. Readers stop at the level they need, and nothing important is below the level where most of them stop.

Sentences, numbers and tables

A few habits do most of the work at sentence level. They are the core of the Google course and are worth practising until they are automatic.

  • Short sentences, active voice. "We trained the model for 3 epochs" beats "The model was trained for 3 epochs", which hides who made which choice. Passive voice is not banned, but it often conceals the missing actor.
  • One term, one meaning. If you call it "the validation set" in one paragraph, do not call it "the dev data" in the next. Define every abbreviation at first use.
  • Lead sentences. Open each paragraph with its point. A reader who reads only the first sentence of each paragraph should still follow the argument.
  • Numbers with context. A bare number is nearly useless. Give the baseline, the unit, the sample size and the uncertainty: "accuracy rose from 71.0% to 73.8% (+2.8 points, 95% interval 0.9 to 4.7, 500 questions, 5 seeds)".
  • Lists and tables for structure. Use a numbered list for steps in order, bullets for unordered items, and a table whenever you compare several things on several measures.

Here is the difference in practice, on the same underlying result.

Before

We looked into whether the new reranker helps. After a lot of experimentation with different settings, and some issues with the data loader that we eventually fixed, we found that it seems to do better on the evaluation set, which suggests it might be worth considering for rollout.

After

Recommendation: ship the new reranker behind a flag at 10% of traffic. On 500 labelled questions it raises answer accuracy from 71.0% to 73.8% (95% interval for the gain: +0.9 to +4.7 points, 5 seeds). It adds 40 ms at the median. Risks and open questions are in section 4.

The second version gives a decision, a number, a baseline, an interval, a cost and a pointer. A reader can disagree with it, which is a feature: a document is useful when it can be argued with.

Figures and captions

Plots from Data Wrangling and Statistics for Experiments are for the reader, so help them. Label both axes with units. Show uncertainty (error bars, several seeds, intervals). Use the same scale when comparing panels. Write the caption as the conclusion ("Gains shrink above 10,000 training examples"), not the topic ("Accuracy versus training set size"). Never make the reader decode a colour legend with eight nearly identical shades.

Analogy: a document is an API

A document has callers (readers), an interface (the title, summary and headings), and an implementation (the details). Callers should be able to use the interface without reading the implementation, as with a good function signature and docstring; and the interface should not change in surprising ways. Section names are method names: "Results" and "Limitations" are conventions callers rely on to find things quickly.

Where it breaks down: an API caller is a program that executes exactly what the contract says. A human reader is tired, interrupted, sceptical, and will fill gaps with whatever they already believe. You cannot rely on them to ask clarifying questions, so the document has to answer them in advance.

Three documents that do real work

Write less than you think, but write these three. They map to the three moments in a typical piece of AI work: before you build, after you run, and after you look at what went wrong.

1. The design doc: decide before you build

A design doc proposes a solution to a problem, explains the trade-offs, and invites criticism while changing course is still cheap. Malte Ubl's "Design Docs at Google" describes a loose structure: context and scope (kept succinct), goals and non-goals, the actual design (an overview, then detail), alternatives considered, and cross-cutting concerns such as security, privacy and observability. He suggests roughly ten to twenty pages for a large project and a one-to-three-page "mini design doc" for incremental work, and he says a doc is worth writing when the design is unclear or contested, not when it would only be an implementation manual.

The sections that do the most work are the two people skip. Non-goals are things that could reasonably be goals but you have chosen not to pursue ("we will not support languages other than English in this version"). They stop a review from drifting into scope you never meant to cover. Alternatives considered show that you looked, and give reviewers somewhere to disagree: if you only describe the winner, a reviewer cannot tell whether you rejected their favourite option or never thought of it. For AI features add what ordinary designs omit: how you will evaluate it (Assertions & Eval Harnesses), what it will cost (GPUs, Clouds & What Compute Costs), what happens when the model is wrong, and what data it touches. The engineering side of design documents is in Trade-offs, Communication & Design Docs, and the security section of a review is in Secure Design Reviews.

2. The experiment report: say what changed, and how sure you are

An experiment report answers whether a change helped. It repeats the ideas of Reproducible Experiments and Statistics for Experiments in a form a colleague can read in a few minutes. Write down the question and what would change your mind before you see results; this single habit prevents most storytelling after the fact. A reliable shape is:

  1. Summary. The answer, the number with its interval, the baseline, and the decision. Fewer than 90 words.
  2. Question and hypothesis. What you wanted to know, and what result would count as "no".
  3. Setup. Data (version or hash), code (commit), configuration, seeds, hardware and cost, enough to repeat it.
  4. Results. A table or figure comparing the change with the baseline, with uncertainty. Report everything you ran, including what did not work.
  5. Interpretation. What the results do and do not show. Separate observation ("accuracy rose") from explanation ("because the reranker fixes ordering"); only the first is measured.
  6. Limitations. Where this might not hold: other data, other model sizes, small test set, one run.
  7. Decision and next steps. What happens now, and who owns it.

The uncomfortable parts of this list, the interval, the baseline and the limitations, are the parts that make a report credible. A checklist can be mechanical. This script lints a draft report against a few of the rules above. It cannot judge whether the writing is good, but it catches the commonest omissions, and it is the kind of check you can run in review automation.

import re

REQUIRED = ["Summary", "Question", "Setup", "Results", "Limitations", "Decision"]

def lint(report: str) -> list:
    problems = []
    headings = re.findall(r"^#+\s*(.+)$", report, flags=re.M)
    for need in REQUIRED:
        if not any(h.lower().startswith(need.lower()) for h in headings):
            problems.append(f"missing section: {need}")
    summary = report.split("## Setup")[0]
    if not re.search(r"\d", summary):
        problems.append("summary contains no number")
    if "baseline" not in report.lower():
        problems.append("no baseline mentioned")
    if not re.search(r"(CI|interval|±|seeds?)", report):
        problems.append("no uncertainty: no interval, no seed count")
    if len(summary.split()) > 90:
        problems.append("summary is over 90 words; nobody will read it")
    return problems

draft = """# Reranker experiment
## Summary
The new reranker is better and we should ship it.
## Setup
Same index, same 500 questions.
## Results
Accuracy went from 71 to 74.
"""
for p in lint(draft):
    print("-", p)
- missing section: Question
- missing section: Limitations
- missing section: Decision
- summary contains no number
- no baseline mentioned
- no uncertainty: no interval, no seed count

The draft "Accuracy went from 71 to 74" fails six checks. It is the kind of report that gets a feature shipped on noise.

3. The error-analysis memo: turn failures into a ranked list

After a model or pipeline is built, the highest-value writing is often an error-analysis memo: what fails, how often, why, and what to fix first. Hamel Husain's widely read "Your AI Product Needs Evals" (2024) makes the point that you must remove friction from looking at data and keep looking at it; the method is taught in detail in Error Analysis. The memo is where the looking becomes a decision. Its steps:

  1. Sample the failures. Draw a random sample (say 100 to 200) from the failing cases, and record the seed, so someone else can draw the same ones.
  2. Label the first failure in each. Read the case and write down the first thing that went wrong. Later errors often follow from the first.
  3. Count, with uncertainty. A category's share in a sample of 120 has a wide interval. Report it.
  4. Show examples. Two or three representative cases per category, quoted, so the reader can see what you mean.
  5. Rank fixes. Estimate for each category how much of the total error it would remove (its share) and how hard it is to fix, and order the work.

The script below produces the table for such a memo from a labelled sample, with Wilson intervals (95%) on each share. The sample is a stand-in for a spreadsheet of 120 hand-labelled failures from a retrieval-augmented question-answering system (the counts are invented for illustration, not measured on a real system).

import math, random
from collections import Counter

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    """95% Wilson interval for a proportion k/n."""
    p = k / n
    centre = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / (1 + z*z/n)
    half = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / (1 + z*z/n)
    return centre - half, centre + half

# Stand-in for a spreadsheet of 120 hand-labelled failures: one first-failure category each.
random.seed(7)
cats = ["retrieval missed the document"] * 49 + ["answer ignored the retrieved text"] * 31 \
     + ["tool call had wrong arguments"] * 22 + ["gold label is wrong"] * 12 + ["other"] * 6
random.shuffle(cats)

n = len(cats)
print(f"Sampled {n} failures from 1,840 failing cases (random, seed 7).\n")
print(f"{'first failure':<36}{'count':>6}{'share':>8}   95% interval")
for cat, k in Counter(cats).most_common():
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    print(f"{cat:<36}{k:>6}{k/n:>8.0%}   {lo:.0%} to {hi:.0%}")

first failure                        count   share   95% interval
retrieval missed the document           49     41%   32% to 50%
answer ignored the retrieved text       31     26%   19% to 34%
tool call had wrong arguments           22     18%   12% to 26%
gold label is wrong                     12     10%   6% to 17%

Two lines from the memo's text show how to write from this table: "Retrieval failures account for about 41% of failures (95% interval 32 to 50%); fixing them could remove up to about 750 of the 1,840 failing cases, if every one were fixed" and "10% of failures are wrong gold labels, so about a tenth of what the dashboard calls errors are our test set's mistakes". The second finding is common and valuable: the fix is to the evaluation, not the model.

Common misconception

"A longer document shows more effort, and writing is something you do after the real work is finished." Length is a cost you impose on the reader; the best reports are the shortest ones that carry the decision, with the detail one click away. And writing is part of the work: a design doc is where you find the holes in your plan, and an experiment report you cannot write clearly often means the experiment did not answer a clear question. Draft the summary first, even if it changes later.

Review, record and maintain

Writing is only half of the loop; review is the other. Send the draft to two or three named reviewers with a deadline and a specific question ("is the evaluation plan convincing?"). Read comments as information about where the document failed to answer something, not as attacks. When reviewing, say what you understood in your own words, quote the passage that confused you, and separate blocking concerns from preferences.

Keep the decision with the document. A short decision record (what was decided, the date, who decided, and the alternatives rejected) saves the next engineer from reopening the same debate. Mark documents with a status ("accepted", "superseded by...") rather than silently editing them, and link reports to the run records of Reproducible Experiments. A document that no one updates becomes misleading; a dated, versioned one is still useful as history.

Try it yourself

Take the last result you produced (a course exercise will do). Write its summary in 90 words or fewer, covering the answer, the number, the baseline, the interval and the decision. Then paste your whole report into a file with ## headings as in lint_report.py and run the lint. Finally give the summary to a friend who has not seen the work and ask them to repeat the conclusion back. Where they got it wrong is the sentence to rewrite.

Systems connection

You already write for readers in operations: a runbook is read at 3 a.m. by someone who did not write it, and a postmortem is a document that turns an incident into learning, written without blame and with contributing factors rather than a single root cause. The error-analysis memo is the postmortem of a model's failures. The design-doc review practices are in Trade-offs, Communication & Design Docs, and documenting decisions as you go is part of software architecture.

Key takeaways

  • Write for a busy reader who must decide something. Put the answer and the decision in the first paragraph, the evidence next, the detail last.
  • Prefer short sentences, active voice, one term per meaning, and numbers with a baseline, a sample size and an uncertainty.
  • Use three documents: a design doc before building (with non-goals and alternatives), an experiment report after a run (with an interval and limitations), and an error-analysis memo after reading failures (with counts and ranked fixes).
  • State what would change your mind before you see results, and separate observations from explanations.
  • A table or figure caption should state the conclusion, with uncertainty drawn.
  • Invite review, record decisions, and mark outdated documents rather than leaving them to mislead.
  • Drafting with a language model is fine if you check every claim; the next chapter, Learning with AI Assistants, covers how to use one without losing the understanding.

Check your understanding

A colleague's report starts with two pages of background, a history of failed attempts and then the result on page 3. What is the most useful fix?

  1. Add a table of contents so readers can find page 3.
  2. Move the answer, the number with its baseline and the decision wanted to the first paragraph, and move the history to an appendix.
  3. Remove the history entirely, because failed attempts are never useful.
  4. Rewrite it in the passive voice so it sounds more objective.
The inverted pyramid puts what the busiest reader needs first. A contents list helps navigation but still makes the reader dig. The history can be valuable (it shows what has been tried), so it belongs lower down, not deleted. Passive voice usually hides the actor and does not make a document more objective.

Which statement is the best summary sentence for an experiment report?

  1. "We ran several experiments with the reranker and the results were promising."
  2. "Accuracy went from 71 to 74."
  3. "The reranker works because it fixes the ordering of documents."
  4. "Ship the reranker at 10% of traffic: accuracy rises from 71.0% to 73.8% (95% interval +0.9 to +4.7, 500 questions); it adds 40 ms at the median."
Only the last gives a decision, a baseline, an interval, a sample size and a cost. The second has a number with no context. The first is vague, and the third states an explanation that the experiment did not measure.

Why does a design doc include a "non-goals" section?

  1. To list reasonable things you have chosen not to do, so a review stays within the scope you intended.
  2. To record everything that went wrong in earlier projects.
  3. To make the document longer and more thorough-looking.
  4. To list requirements the system will fail to meet by accident.
Non-goals are explicit decisions about scope: things that could plausibly be goals but are not. They prevent review debates about work nobody planned. They are not a log of past failures or an admission of defects.

In the error-analysis memo example, "retrieval missed the document" is 41% of 120 sampled failures (interval 32 to 50%) and "answer ignored the retrieved text" is 26% (interval 19 to 34%). What should the memo conclude?

  1. Retrieval is exactly 15 points worse, so fix it first and ignore the other category.
  2. The two are equally important because the intervals are wide.
  3. Retrieval failures are probably the larger share, but the intervals touch, so both deserve work and the claim should be hedged.
  4. Nothing can be concluded from a sample of 120.
The point estimates favour retrieval but the intervals overlap between 32 and 34%, so the ranking is likely rather than certain. Wide intervals do not make the categories equal, and 120 cases still reliably show that those two dominate the last two. The memo should say what the data supports and no more.

You plan to write a one-line figure caption for a plot of accuracy against training-set size, where gains flatten after 10,000 examples. Which is best?

  1. "Figure 3: Accuracy versus training set size."
  2. "Figure 3: Accuracy gains flatten beyond about 10,000 training examples (mean of 5 seeds, bars show 95% intervals)."
  3. "Figure 3: Our model clearly outperforms the baseline."
  4. "Figure 3: See text."
A good caption states the conclusion and how the figure was made, so a reader skimming figures gets the point. The first only names the axes. The third claims something the plot description does not show, and the fourth makes the reader search.

Why should you state "what result would change my mind" before running an experiment?

  1. It makes the report longer and so more professional.
  2. It guarantees the experiment will succeed.
  3. It is required by the statistics of confidence intervals.
  4. It stops you from reading whatever result you get as support for your idea after the fact.
Committing in advance limits the flexibility to reinterpret results, a close relative of the peeking and forking-paths problems in the statistics chapter. It does not affect whether the experiment succeeds, and no statistical formula requires it; it is a discipline for honest reasoning.
