Entropy, Cross-Entropy & KL Divergence

How surprised should a model be by what actually happens, and how far apart are two models of the same thing? Information theory turns those questions into numbers: entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence and perplexity. They are the language in which language models are scored and in which post-training keeps a model tethered to where it started.

Why this matters

A language model's loss, its perplexity and its bits per byte are one quantity in three units. The penalty that stops a reinforcement-learning-tuned model from drifting away from its starting point is a KL divergence, in PPO and RLHF (A3, chapter 8), where it reappears in the closed form behind DPO (A3, chapter 12) and in GRPO (A3, chapter 14). Distillation trains a small model to match a large one's output distribution by minimising a KL (A3, chapter 15). Sampling temperature changes the entropy of every token (A2, chapter 15). If these are only formulas you have seen, a loss curve is only a line going down. After this chapter you will be able to say what a loss of 2.1 means in bits and in choices per token, why KL is not symmetric and which direction a given method uses, and how to estimate KL from the samples a training run has in hand. The chapter builds directly on chapter 12 (distributions, expectation, sampling) and chapter 13 (negative log-likelihood). Lab 4 (Sampling, Likelihood & Entropy) has you implement entropy, KL and temperature from scratch.

Surprise, measured in bits

Suppose a friend tells you that the sun rose this morning. You learn nothing. Suppose instead they tell you a lottery ticket you hold with a one-in-a-million chance has won. You learn a great deal. Information theory, founded by Claude Shannon in "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948), makes this precise: the surprise (or information content) of an event of probability p is

I(p) = −log2 p   bits.

Why that formula? Ask for three properties. A certain event (p = 1) should carry no surprise. A rarer event should carry more. And surprises should add for independent events: learning that two unrelated things both happened tells you the sum of what each told you, while their probabilities multiply. The only functions that turn a product into a sum are logarithms, and the sign makes rare events large and positive. The base fixes the unit: base 2 gives bits, and one bit is the surprise of a fair coin landing heads, the answer to one yes/no question that splits the possibilities in half. Base e gives nats, which is what deep-learning frameworks use because natural logarithms differentiate cleanly: 1 nat = 1.4427 bits.

The first script computes some surprises and the entropy of several distributions, which we define next.

"""Surprise in bits, and the entropy of a few distributions."""

Entropy: the average surprise

A source of data (a coin, a die, a language model's next token) produces outcomes with some distribution p. The entropy of p is the expected surprise:

Plug in numbers. The script follows those four steps for the four-outcome source, then reuses the recipe on coins of different bias to show how entropy peaks at a toss-up.

The idea

  1. p ← (0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125)
  2. surprise[x] ← −log2 p[x]
  3. terms[x] ← p[x] × surprise[x]
  4. H ← sum of terms
  5. same recipe for coins with P(heads) = 0.5, 0.9, 0.999, 1
"""Entropy by hand: H(p) = sum over x of p(x) * (-log2 p(x)), in bits.
A four-outcome source, then a coin, to see what the number means."""
import math

# ── 1. A distribution over four outcomes ─────────────────────────────────
P = [0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125]            # p(x) for x = a, b, c, d

# ── 2. Surprise of each outcome: -log2 p(x) ──────────────────────────────
surprise = [-math.log2(p) for p in P]
print("surprise  =", surprise, "bits")

# ── 3. Weight each surprise by how often it happens ──────────────────────
terms = [p * s for p, s in zip(P, surprise)]
print("p * surprise =", terms)

# ── 4. Add up: the average surprise, i.e. the entropy ────────────────────
H = sum(terms)
print(f"H(p) = {H} bits")


# ── 5. Same recipe for a coin: entropy is biggest when it is a toss-up ───
def entropy(dist):
    return -sum(p * math.log2(p) for p in dist if p > 0) + 0.0


for heads in (0.5, 0.9, 0.999, 1.0):

Read it as a measure of uncertainty: how many yes/no questions, on average, you must ask to find out the outcome if you ask the best possible questions. The table in the script's output shows the pattern. A fair coin has 1 bit, the maximum for two outcomes. A coin that lands heads 90% of the time has only 0.469 bits: usually you can guess it. A 99.9%-heads coin has 0.0114 bits, nearly nothing to learn. A fair die has log2 6 = 2.585 bits, but loading it so that half the rolls are sixes drops this to 2.161. For a fixed number of outcomes, entropy is largest when all are equally likely and zero when one is certain.

Entropy is a compression limit

The "number of questions" reading has a sharp consequence, and it is the reason the units are called bits. Shannon's source coding theorem says that no lossless code can write a source's output in fewer than H bits per symbol on average, and that codes exist that come within one bit of that. The idea is to give short codewords to likely symbols and long ones to rare symbols, so that the average length tracks the average surprise. Huffman coding (1952) builds such a code optimally: repeatedly merge the two least likely symbols into one, and read each symbol's code off its path in the resulting tree. The next script does this for the characters of a short text and compares the average code length with the entropy.

"""Entropy as a limit on compression: build a Huffman code for the characters of a text and compare its length with the entropy."""
import heapq, math
from collections import Counter

text = ("the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog while the cat watches from the window. "
        "a language model assigns a probability to every possible next character, and a code that gives "
        "short words to likely characters can write the text in fewer bits than a fixed eight bits each. "
        "the shorter the code, the better the model of the text, and that is the whole idea of compression. ") * 2

freq = Counter(text)
n = len(text)
p = {c: k / n for c, k in freq.items()}
H = -sum(q * math.log2(q) for q in p.values())

# Huffman's algorithm: repeatedly merge the two least likely nodes.
heap = [(k, i, c) for i, (c, k) in enumerate(freq.items())]
heapq.heapify(heap)
counter = len(heap)
while len(heap) > 1:
    a = heapq.heappop(heap); b = heapq.heappop(heap)
    heapq.heappush(heap, (a[0] + b[0], counter, (a[2], b[2]))); counter += 1
codes = {}
def walk(node, prefix=""):
    if isinstance(node, str):
        codes[node] = prefix or "0"
    else:
        walk(node[0], prefix + "0"); walk(node[1], prefix + "1")
walk(heap[0][2])

avg = sum(p[c] * len(codes[c]) for c in p)
print(f"{n} characters, {len(freq)} distinct")
print(f"entropy of the character distribution : {H:.3f} bits per character")
print(f"Huffman code average length           : {avg:.3f} bits per character")
print(f"fixed-width ASCII                     : 8.000 bits per character")
print(f"compressed size: {avg * n / 8:.0f} bytes versus {n} bytes")
for c in (" ", "e", "t", "q", "z", "."):
    print(f"  {c!r}: probability {p[c]:.4f}, ideal {-math.log2(p[c]):.2f} bits, Huffman code {codes[c]} ({len(codes[c])} bits)")

entropy of the character distribution : 4.148 bits per character
Huffman code average length           : 4.190 bits per character
fixed-width ASCII                     : 8.000 bits per character
compressed size: 391 bytes versus 746 bytes

  'e': probability 0.1099, ideal 3.19 bits, Huffman code 010 (3 bits)

The character distribution of this text has an entropy of 4.148 bits, and the Huffman code averages 4.190, only 0.042 bits above the limit and nearly half of the 8 bits a fixed-width encoding spends. The codes show the principle: the space (probability 0.185) gets 3 bits, the letters q and z (0.0027 each) get 8. This is a unigram model, which ignores context. A better model of the text, one that predicts a letter from the ones before it, assigns higher probabilities to what actually comes next, and so a lower entropy and a shorter code. In this sense a language model is a compressor: the better it predicts, the fewer bits the text needs, and that is the point of the next section. (Shannon estimated in 1951, using human guessers, that English text carries roughly 0.6 to 1.3 bits per letter once context is exploited, far below the unigram figure above.)

Cross-entropy: the price of the wrong model

Entropy assumes that you know the true distribution p and design your code for it. What if you design the code for a different distribution q, a model of the world that is not quite right? You assign each symbol a code of length −log2 q(x), but the symbols still arrive with frequencies p(x). The average length you pay is the cross-entropy:

H(p, q) = −∑x p(x) log2 q(x) = Ex∼p[ −log2 q(x) ].

Look at the last form: the cross-entropy is the average surprise of the model q over data that come from p. That is exactly the training loss of chapter 13. With data x1, …, xn drawn from p, the average negative log-likelihood (1/n) ∑i −log q(xi) is an unbiased estimate of H(p, q) (the law of large numbers, chapter 12). A cross-entropy loss is a cross-entropy, estimated from a sample. The word was not an arbitrary choice.

Analogy: a codebook tuned to the wrong language

Morse code gives short signals to common English letters (E is a single dot). Use it to send Finnish and you still get through, but you spend more signal than a code tuned to Finnish would, because the short symbols are on the wrong letters. Entropy is the cost with the code tuned perfectly to the language; cross-entropy is the cost with the code tuned to a different one; the extra is the cost of the mismatch.

Where it breaks down: a real code must use a whole number of bits per symbol, whereas −log2 q(x) is usually fractional. Arithmetic coding closes most of that gap, and the mathematics of cross-entropy does not care: it is defined for fractional lengths.

The next script takes one true distribution p = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and four models, and computes entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence (defined next) for each.

"""Cross-entropy and KL divergence: three candidate models q for one true distribution p."""
import math
import numpy as np

def H(p):        return -sum(x * math.log2(x) for x in p if x > 0)
def CE(p, q):    return -sum(x * math.log2(y) for x, y in zip(p, q) if x > 0)
def KL(p, q):
    if any(x > 0 and y == 0 for x, y in zip(p, q)):
        return math.inf                            # q rules out something that p allows
    return sum(x * math.log2(x / y) for x, y in zip(p, q) if x > 0)

p = [0.70, 0.20, 0.10]
models = {"q = p (perfect)": [0.70, 0.20, 0.10], "q slightly off": [0.60, 0.25, 0.15],
          "q uniform": [1 / 3, 1 / 3, 1 / 3], "q confidently wrong": [0.05, 0.05, 0.90]}
print(f"true distribution p = {p}, entropy H(p) = {H(p):.4f} bits\n")
print(f"{'model':<22} {'H(p,q)':>8} {'H(p)':>8} {'KL(p||q)':>9} {'KL(q||p)':>9}")
for name, q in models.items():
    print(f"{name:<22} {CE(p, q):>8.4f} {H(p):>8.4f} {KL(p, q):>9.4f} {KL(q, p):>9.4f}")
print("\nH(p,q) = H(p) + KL(p||q) holds in every row, and KL is not symmetric.")

# Cross-entropy is the average surprise of the data under the model: sample from p, score with q.

KL divergence: the extra cost

That excess has a name. The Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL divergence, or relative entropy) from p to q is

Plug in numbers. A true distribution over three outcomes and two models of it. The script computes each term, checks the cross-entropy split, and confirms that KL is 0 for a perfect model and is not symmetric.

The idea

  1. p ← (0.7, 0.2, 0.1); q ← uniform, then (0.6, 0.3, 0.1)
  2. term[x] ← p[x] × log2( p[x] ÷ q[x] )
  3. KL ← sum of terms
  4. check: cross-entropy H(p, q) − entropy H(p) gives the same
  5. compare KL(p||p), KL(p||q) and KL(q||p)
"""KL divergence by hand: KL(p || q) = sum_x p(x) log2( p(x) / q(x) ).
The truth p has three outcomes; two models q try to describe it."""
import math

# ── 1. The truth, and two models of it ───────────────────────────────────
P = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1]                      # true distribution
Q_UNIFORM = [1 / 3, 1 / 3, 1 / 3]        # knows nothing
Q_CLOSE = [0.6, 0.3, 0.1]                # a decent guess


def kl(p, q):
    # ── 2. Per outcome: p(x) * log2( p(x) / q(x) ) ───────────────────────
    # ── 3. Add them up: the expected log-ratio under p ───────────────────
    return sum(a * math.log2(a / b) for a, b in zip(p, q) if a > 0)


for name, q in (("uniform", Q_UNIFORM), ("close", Q_CLOSE)):
    terms = [a * math.log2(a / b) for a, b in zip(P, q)]
    print(f"{name:8} terms = {[round(t, 4) for t in terms]}  "
          f"KL = {kl(P, q):.4f} bits")

# ── 4. The same number from cross-entropy minus entropy ──────────────────
H = -sum(a * math.log2(a) for a in P)
cross = -sum(a * math.log2(b) for a, b in zip(P, Q_UNIFORM))
print(f"H(p) = {H:.4f}  H(p,q) = {cross:.4f}  difference = {cross - H:.4f}")

# ── 5. Properties: zero for a perfect model, and not symmetric ───────────
print(f"KL(p || p)       = {kl(P, P):.4f}")
print(f"KL(p || close)   = {kl(P, Q_CLOSE):.4f}")
print(f"KL(close || p)   = {kl(Q_CLOSE, P):.4f}")
uniform  terms = [0.7493, -0.1474, -0.1737]  KL = 0.4282 bits
close    terms = [0.1557, -0.117, 0.0]  KL = 0.0387 bits
H(p) = 1.1568  H(p,q) = 1.5850  difference = 0.4282
KL(p || p)       = 0.0000
KL(p || close)   = 0.0387
KL(close || p)   = 0.0421

It is the expected log-ratio between the two probabilities, with the expectation taken under p, and it is the extra bits (or nats) you pay per symbol for using q when the truth is p. Three properties carry nearly all the weight.

  • It is never negative, and is zero only when q = p. No model beats the truth on average. (This is Gibbs' inequality; the short proof is below.)
  • It is not symmetric. KL(p || q) and KL(q || p) differ, as the table shows (0.428 against 0.468 for the uniform model, 2.748 against 2.563 for the confident-wrong one). It is not a distance: it also fails the triangle inequality. Always say which direction you mean, and always check which distribution a library's argument order assumes.
  • It is infinite if q rules out something p allows. If q(x) = 0 while p(x) > 0, the log-ratio blows up. The last line of the output shows KL(p || q) = infinity with q = (0.8, 0.2, 0) but a finite KL(q || p) = 0.154 the other way. This is the same disaster as the bigram model in chapter 13 that gave a held-out character probability 0 and an infinite loss, and for the same reason: a model must never declare possible things impossible.
Go deeper optional: why KL is never negative

The inequality ln u ≤ u − 1 holds for every u > 0 (the curve ln u lies below its tangent line at u = 1). Apply it to u = q(x)/p(x) at every x where p(x) > 0:

−KL(p || q) = ∑ p(x) ln( q(x)/p(x) ) ≤ ∑ p(x) ( q(x)/p(x) − 1 ) = ∑ q(x) − ∑ p(x) ≤ 1 − 1 = 0.

Equality needs u = 1 everywhere p has mass, that is, q = p there. The same line of reasoning gives the unbiased low-variance KL estimator later in this chapter: the quantity (r − 1) − ln r, with r a ratio of probabilities, is the vertical gap between ln r and its tangent, so it is always at least 0.

Forward versus reverse: which mistake do you want to avoid?

Because the two directions differ, they penalise different errors. KL(p || q), averaged under the truth p, is large wherever p has mass that q lacks: forward KL punishes a model for missing something that happens. KL(q || p), averaged under the model, is large wherever q puts mass that p considers impossible: reverse KL punishes a model for producing something that should not happen. When the model is too simple to match the truth exactly, the two lead to visibly different compromises. The script fits a single Gaussian q to a two-humped p by brute-force search over its mean and spread, once minimising each direction.

"""Forward versus reverse KL: fit one Gaussian q to a two-humped p by minimising each divergence on a grid."""
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(-8, 8, 3201)
dx = x[1] - x[0]
def gauss(x, mu, s): return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - mu) / s) ** 2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
p = 0.5 * gauss(x, -2.5, 0.7) + 0.5 * gauss(x, 2.5, 0.7)          # two equal humps

def kl(a, b):                                                     # KL(a || b) in nats, integrated on the grid
    mask = a > 1e-12
    return np.sum(a[mask] * np.log(a[mask] / np.maximum(b[mask], 1e-300))) * dx

best_f = best_r = (np.inf, None)
for mu in np.arange(-4, 4.01, 0.05):
    for s in np.arange(0.3, 4.01, 0.05):
        q = gauss(x, mu, s)
        f, r = kl(p, q), kl(q, p)
        if f < best_f[0]: best_f = (f, (mu, s))
        if r < best_r[0]: best_r = (r, (mu, s))
mean = np.sum(x * p) * dx; sd = np.sqrt(np.sum((x - mean) ** 2 * p) * dx)
print(f"p: two humps at -2.5 and +2.5; mean {mean:.2f}, standard deviation {sd:.3f}")
print(f"minimise KL(p||q) (forward): q = N({best_f[1][0]:+.2f}, {best_f[1][1]:.2f}),  KL = {best_f[0]:.3f} nats   <- matches p's mean and spread")
print(f"minimise KL(q||p) (reverse): q = N({best_r[1][0]:+.2f}, {best_r[1][1]:.2f}),  KL = {best_r[0]:.3f} nats   <- sits on one hump (the mirror image is equally good)")
print(f"density at x = 0, between the humps: p {p[1600]:.5f}, forward fit {gauss(np.array([0.0]), *best_f[1])[0]:.5f}, reverse fit {gauss(np.array([0.0]), *best_r[1])[0]:.5f}")
print(f"ln 2 = {np.log(2):.3f}: covering one of two equal humps exactly costs ln 2 nats under the reverse KL")

minimise KL(q||p) (reverse): q = N(-2.50, 0.70),  KL = 0.693 nats   <- sits on one hump (the mirror image is equally good)

Forward KL, which is what training on data by maximum likelihood minimises, returns the Gaussian that matches p's overall mean and spread: centred at 0 with standard deviation 2.60, wide enough to cover both humps and therefore placing density 0.153 in the middle where p has almost none (0.001). Reverse KL returns a narrow Gaussian on one hump, paying exactly ln 2 = 0.693 nats for ignoring the other half. Which is better depends on the use. A language model trained on data should cover every way people write, so it uses forward KL (cross-entropy). A policy being tuned with a penalty "do not stray from the reference model" is penalised in the reverse direction, KL(π || πref), which allows it to concentrate on a subset of what the reference could do but forbids it from putting mass where the reference does not. That is the direction used in RLHF-style training, as we will see below.

Cross-entropy training is minimising KL

The decomposition H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p || q) explains something that chapter 13 asserted: why maximum likelihood finds the closest model in a family. When we train a model qw on data from the true distribution p, the loss is the cross-entropy H(p, qw). The entropy H(p) is a property of the data, not of the weights, so it is a constant. Minimising H(p, qw) over w is therefore the same as minimising KL(p || qw): fitting a model by maximum likelihood is choosing the member of the family nearest to the truth in forward KL. And it explains what a loss value means. A language model's loss cannot fall below the entropy of the text itself; what you can reduce is the gap, the KL, between the model and the true next-token distribution. A loss of 1.9 nats on text that really carries 1.7 nats per token of irreducible uncertainty means 0.2 nats of room, not 1.9.

Common misconception

"KL divergence is the distance between two distributions." It measures how costly it is to use one distribution in place of another, which is a different thing: it is asymmetric, it can be infinite, and it does not obey the triangle inequality. Two models can be equally "far" from p under one direction and very different under the other. When a paper says "close in KL", the next question is: in which direction, and under which distribution are the expectations taken?

Perplexity: entropy as a number of choices

Entropies in bits are awkward to feel. Perplexity converts them back into a count of choices:

perplexity = 2H(p, q) = e(cross-entropy in nats).

If a model has a perplexity of 8 on some text, it is on average as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 8 equally likely options at every token: the effective branching factor. A model with perplexity 1 predicts everything perfectly. A model that knows nothing about a vocabulary of V tokens has perplexity V. A loss of 2.3 nats per token (chapter 13) is a perplexity of e2.3 ≈ 10; a loss of 3 bits is a perplexity of 8.

The number has a trap that matters whenever someone reports one. Perplexity is per token, and what a token is depends on the tokeniser (A2, chapter 13). A model that reads characters has a small vocabulary and a low per-token perplexity, because most characters are easy to predict from the ones before; a model that reads whole words has a huge vocabulary and a much higher per-token perplexity for the same quality of understanding. The script below makes this concrete with one model scored two ways: a character bigram model, evaluated on a short held-out sentence, counted per character and then per word. A second model reads whole words.

"""Perplexity and bits per character: why a per-token number cannot be compared across tokenisers."""
import math
from collections import Counter

train = ("the cat sat on the mat. the dog sat on the log. a cat and a dog sat in the sun. "
         "the sun set and the cat ran to the dog. the dog ate the bone on the mat. ")
test = "the dog sat on the mat. the cat ran to the sun."

def char_bigram_bits(train, test, k=0.1):            # total bits for every character after the first
    V = sorted(set(train + test))
    pair, single = Counter(zip(train, train[1:])), Counter(train[:-1])
    return sum(-math.log2((pair[(a, b)] + k) / (single[a] + k * len(V))) for a, b in zip(test, test[1:]))

def word_unigram_bits(train, test, k=0.1):           # total bits for each word (spaces are free: a simplification)
    words_tr, words_te = train.split(), test.split()
    V = sorted(set(words_tr + words_te)); c = Counter(words_tr)
    return sum(-math.log2((c[w] + k) / (len(words_tr) + k * len(V))) for w in words_te)

n_chars, n_words = len(test) - 1, len(test.split())
bits_char = char_bigram_bits(train, test)
bits_word = word_unigram_bits(train, test)
print(f"test text: {test!r}: {n_chars} scored characters, {n_words} words\n")
print(f"{'model':<18} {'counted per':<12} {'total bits':>11} {'bits/token':>11} {'perplexity':>11}")
for name, unit, bits, n in (("char bigram", "character", bits_char, n_chars), ("char bigram", "word", bits_char, n_words), ("word unigram", "word", bits_word, n_words)):
    print(f"{name:<18} {unit:<12} {bits:>11.1f} {bits / n:>11.3f} {2 ** (bits / n):>11.2f}")
print(f"\nthe first two rows are the SAME model on the SAME text: bits per character {bits_char / n_chars:.3f}, but perplexity {2 ** (bits_char / n_chars):.2f} or {2 ** (bits_char / n_words):.2f} depending on what a 'token' is.")
print(f"a model that knew nothing over {len(set(train + test))} characters would have perplexity {len(set(train + test))} per character.")

The first two rows are one model on one text: 76.2 bits in total. Counted per character it has perplexity 3.15; counted per word, 81.54. Neither is wrong, and neither is comparable with a model that uses a different split. The word model's 11.51 per word looks "better" than 81.54 but it scores only the words (the spaces are free), so the comparison is meaningless as it stands. What is comparable is the total number of bits to encode the same text, or equivalently bits per character or bits per byte of the original text, which is how language models trained with different tokenisers are compared fairly. (This toy example is not a result about word versus character models: the scale is tiny and the models are crude.) The rule is simple: compare perplexities only between models with the same tokeniser and the same test set, and convert to bits per byte otherwise.

Temperature and entropy

Chapter 12 introduced temperature as a divisor on the logits before the softmax. Entropy tells us what it does to the information content of a distribution. The script holds the logits fixed and sweeps T.

"""Temperature and entropy: the same logits, flattened or sharpened."""
import numpy as np

logits = np.array([3.0, 2.2, 1.5, 1.4, 0.2, -1.0])
print(f"{'T':>5} {'max prob':>9} {'entropy (bits)':>15} {'perplexity':>11}   (a uniform draw over 6 tokens has entropy {np.log2(6):.3f} bits)")
for T in (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 100.0):
    z = logits / T
    p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum()
    h = -np.sum(p[p > 0] * np.log2(p[p > 0]))
    print(f"{T:>5} {p.max():>9.3f} {h:>15.3f} {2 ** h:>11.2f}")

At T = 1 the distribution has an entropy of 1.897 bits, an effective 3.7 choices. Sharpening to T = 0.25 reduces it to 0.28 bits (essentially one choice); flattening to T = 5 raises it to 2.54 bits, near the 2.585-bit ceiling of a uniform distribution, and at T = 100 the logits stop mattering. Temperature is therefore a dial on entropy: it controls how many tokens are effectively on the table at each step. This is why a sampler with a high temperature produces varied but less coherent text, and why "greedy" decoding is the T → 0 limit. In distillation the same dial is used on the teacher: a temperature above 1 reveals the relative probabilities of the wrong answers (which tokens the teacher considers nearly right), the "dark knowledge" of Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015), and the student is trained to match the softened distribution by minimising a KL.

KL in post-training: the leash

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) takes a pretrained or instruction-tuned model, the reference policy πref, and tunes a copy π to maximise a reward r(y) that stands in for human preference, over replies y. Optimising a reward alone is dangerous: the policy can find replies that score highly for reasons unrelated to quality, and it can forget how to write fluent text (A3, chapter 13). The standard remedy, used since the early work on fine-tuning language models from human preferences (Ziegler et al., 2019) and in InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), is to penalise the policy for moving away from the reference. The objective is

maximise   Ey∼π[ r(y) ] − β · KL( π || πref ),

where β > 0 sets how long the leash is. Notice the direction: expectation under the new policy, which is the reverse KL of the previous section, and which we can estimate from samples the policy itself produces. This objective has a closed-form solution, which is worth deriving because it recurs. Write the objective as a sum over replies, ∑y π(y) [ r(y) − β ln(π(y)/πref(y)) ], add a Lagrange multiplier λ for the constraint ∑ π(y) = 1, and set the derivative with respect to each π(y) to zero: r(y) − β ln(π(y)/πref(y)) − β − λ = 0. Solving for π(y):

π*(y) = πref(y) · exp( r(y)/β ) / Z,

where Z normalises the result. The optimal policy is the reference reweighted by the exponentiated reward. The script checks this against a numerical optimiser on four candidate replies, and shows how the answer moves with β.

"""The KL leash: maximise expected reward minus beta * KL(pi || ref) over a policy on four candidate replies."""
import numpy as np

ref = np.array([0.55, 0.25, 0.15, 0.05])            # reference model: how likely each reply is before tuning
reward = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0])             # a reward model's score for each reply (the last one is 'exploited')

def objective(pi, beta):
    return pi @ reward - beta * np.sum(pi * np.log(pi / ref))

def closed_form(beta):                               # pi*(y) proportional to ref(y) * exp(reward(y) / beta)
    w = ref * np.exp((reward - reward.max()) / beta)
    return w / w.sum()

def gradient_ascent(beta, steps=20_000, lr=0.05):    # check numerically, over softmax logits
    z = np.log(ref).copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        pi = np.exp(z - z.max()); pi /= pi.sum()
        g = reward - beta * (np.log(pi / ref) + 1)   # d objective / d pi
        z += lr * pi * (g - pi @ g)                  # chain rule through the softmax
    pi = np.exp(z - z.max()); return pi / pi.sum()

print(f"reference policy: {ref}   rewards: {reward}")
print(f"{'beta':>6} {'optimal policy (closed form)':>34} {'ascent agrees?':>15} {'E[reward]':>10} {'KL(pi||ref)':>12}")
for beta in (100.0, 10.0, 3.0, 1.0, 0.3):
    pi = closed_form(beta)
    ok = np.allclose(pi, gradient_ascent(beta), atol=1e-3)
    print(f"{beta:>6} {str(np.round(pi, 3)):>34} {str(ok):>15} {pi @ reward:>10.3f} {np.sum(pi * np.log(pi / ref)):>12.3f}")

Every row's numerical optimum matches the closed form. If reply D is the one the reward model over-values (a reward-hacking exploit), the leash is what limits the damage. This closed form is also the starting point of direct preference optimisation: rearranging it expresses the reward in terms of the log-ratio β ln(π*/πref), which lets DPO train on preference pairs without an explicit reward model (Rafailov et al., 2023; derived in A3, chapter 12).

Estimating KL from samples

For a real language model, the exact KL(π || πref) at a token is a sum over a vocabulary of 100,000 entries, and over a whole reply it is an expectation over an astronomical number of sequences. Training code does not compute it exactly. It samples tokens from π (the policy was generating them anyway), looks up both models' log-probabilities for the sampled token, and averages. John Schulman's 2020 note "Approximating KL Divergence" compares three per-sample estimators. Write r = πref(x)/π(x) for the probability ratio at a sampled token x:

  • k1 = −ln r = ln(π/πref). Unbiased, since its expectation under π is the KL, but it is negative for about half the samples even though the true KL is positive, so it has high variance.
  • k2 = (ln r)2 / 2. Always non-negative and low variance but slightly biased.
  • k3 = (r − 1) − ln r. Unbiased and never negative. It is k1 plus a control variate, the term r − 1, which has expectation 0 under π (because ∑ π (πref/π) = 1) and is negatively correlated with k1.

The script compares them on two five-token distributions whose exact KL is 0.04882 nats, from 200,000 single-sample estimates each.

"""Estimating KL(pi || ref) from samples of pi, as RLHF-style training does token by token: the k1, k2 and k3 estimators."""
import numpy as np

pi  = np.array([0.50, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.05])     # current policy's next-token distribution
ref = np.array([0.35, 0.25, 0.20, 0.12, 0.08])     # frozen reference model
exact = float(np.sum(pi * np.log(pi / ref)))
print(f"exact KL(pi || ref) = {exact:.5f} nats")

rng = np.random.default_rng(20)
n_draws, n_trials = 1, 200_000
x = rng.choice(5, size=(n_trials, n_draws), p=pi)       # one sampled token per trial
r = ref[x] / pi[x]                                      # density ratio ref / pi at the sampled token
k1 = -np.log(r)                                         # log(pi/ref): unbiased, can be negative
k2 = 0.5 * np.log(r) ** 2                               # biased but always positive
k3 = (r - 1) - np.log(r)                                # unbiased and always >= 0
print(f"\nper-token estimates from a single sample, {n_trials:,} repeats")
print(f"{'estimator':<22} {'mean':>9} {'std dev':>9} {'std/mean':>9} {'share < 0':>10}")
for name, k in (("k1 = log(pi/ref)", k1), ("k2 = (log r)^2 / 2", k2), ("k3 = (r-1) - log r", k3)):
    print(f"{name:<22} {k.mean():>9.5f} {k.std():>9.5f} {k.std() / k.mean():>9.2f} {np.mean(k < 0):>10.3f}")
print("\nk1 and k3 are both unbiased (means match the exact value); k3 has far lower variance and is never negative.")

Both k1 and k3 centre on the true value (0.04873 and 0.04886), but k1 has a standard deviation of 0.313 (more than six times its own mean) and is negative on exactly half the samples, while k3 has a standard deviation of 0.0237, a thirteen-fold reduction, and is never negative. The biased k2 is slightly off (0.0502) but also low-variance. The result is why k3 is popular in practice. DeepSeekMath's GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) uses this unbiased, non-negative estimator, in the form πref/π − ln(πref/π) − 1, added directly to the loss, whereas the original RLHF recipes subtract a per-token log-ratio from the reward. Both are KL penalties; they differ in estimator and in where it enters.

State of play, October 2026: conventions vary by library

The mathematics above (the leash objective, its closed form, the three estimators) is stable. Conventions are not. Whether the KL term is added to the reward or to the loss, which estimator is used, whether the per-token estimate is summed or averaged over a reply, and whether it is dropped altogether in some recipes with verifiable rewards, differ between the papers and open-source trainers. The sources I checked for this chapter were Schulman's 2020 note, the DeepSeekMath paper (2024) and the PPO and DPO chapters of this path; I did not survey the current code of every trainer. Mutual information

One more quantity completes the picture. The mutual information between two variables is the KL divergence from the joint distribution to the product of the marginals: I(X; Y) = KL( p(x, y) || p(x) p(y) ) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X, Y). It is how many bits of uncertainty about one variable disappear when you learn the other: zero for independent variables (the joint equals the product), and up to H(X) when Y determines X. It appears in feature selection, in contrastive learning objectives that maximise a bound on the mutual information between two views of the same example, and in analyses of what a representation retains. For the spam-and-"free" table of chapter 12:

"""Mutual information: how much knowing one variable reduces the surprise of another, using the spam and 'free' table from chapter 12."""
import math
counts = {("spam", "free"): 180, ("spam", "no-free"): 120, ("ham", "free"): 40, ("ham", "no-free"): 660}
n = sum(counts.values())
joint = {k: v / n for k, v in counts.items()}
pc = {c: sum(p for (a, _), p in joint.items() if a == c) for c in ("spam", "ham")}
pw = {w: sum(p for (_, b), p in joint.items() if b == w) for w in ("free", "no-free")}

H = lambda d: -sum(p * math.log2(p) for p in d.values() if p > 0)
mi = sum(p * math.log2(p / (pc[c] * pw[w])) for (c, w), p in joint.items())
print(f"H(spam) = {H(pc):.4f} bits,  H(free) = {H(pw):.4f} bits,  H(spam, free) = {H(joint):.4f} bits")
print(f"I(spam; free) = KL(joint || product of marginals) = {mi:.4f} bits")
print(f"check: H(spam) + H(free) - H(spam, free) = {H(pc) + H(pw) - H(joint):.4f} bits")
print(f"so the word 'free' removes {mi / H(pc):.1%} of the uncertainty about whether an email is spam")

Systems connection

Entropy is the theory behind every compressor you use. Gzip and zstd approximate the statistics of the data with a dictionary and entropy coding; columnar stores compress a column of similar values far better than a row store compresses mixed ones, because a column's distribution is narrower (column stores). A language model used as a predictor inside an arithmetic coder is a compressor whose size is its cross-entropy: a lower loss literally means a smaller file. Surprise is also a monitoring signal. A model of "normal" log lines or request shapes assigns each new event a probability, and an event with a high surprise is a candidate anomaly (A1, chapter 12), which is the idea behind many log-anomaly and drift detectors. Finally, the cost of a token is not only compute: a prompt with high-entropy content such as random identifiers or encoded blobs costs tokens that predict nothing, a reason to keep identifiers short and structured in prompts and logs (A4, chapter 10).

Key takeaways

  • The surprise of an event of probability p is −log p. Entropy H(p) is the average surprise, and is the least number of bits per symbol any lossless code can achieve. It is largest for a uniform distribution and zero for a certain one.
  • Cross-entropy H(p, q) is the average surprise of a model q over data from p. A training loss is a sample estimate of it. H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p || q), so the loss cannot fall below the entropy of the data; what you can reduce is the KL.
  • KL divergence is non-negative, zero only for equal distributions, asymmetric, and infinite if q gives zero probability to something p allows. It is not a distance.
  • Forward KL(p || q) punishes missing probability mass, so a simple q covers all modes; reverse KL(q || p) punishes mass where p has none, so q locks onto one mode. Maximum likelihood minimises the forward direction; the RLHF-style leash uses the reverse.
  • Perplexity is 2bits, the effective number of equally likely choices per token. It depends on what a token is, so compare models only with the same tokeniser, or use bits per byte.
  • Temperature is a dial on entropy. The KL-regularised reward objective has the closed-form optimum π* ∝ πref er/β, which underlies PPO-style RLHF and DPO.
  • KL for a language model is estimated from sampled tokens. The estimator k3 = (r − 1) − ln r, with r = πref/π, is unbiased, non-negative and much lower in variance than the naive log-ratio.

Check your understanding

A source emits four symbols with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/8. What is its entropy?

  1. 2 bits, the maximum for four symbols
  2. 1.5 bits
  3. 1.75 bits
  4. 1 bit
H = (1/2)(1) + (1/4)(2) + (1/8)(3) + (1/8)(3) = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75 bits. Two bits would require all four symbols to be equally likely (a uniform source), which this one is not. A Huffman code for it assigns 1, 2, 3 and 3 bits and achieves exactly 1.75 on average, because every probability is a power of 1/2.

You train a classifier on a fixed labelled dataset by minimising cross-entropy. Which statement correctly relates this to KL divergence?

  1. It minimises KL(data || model), because the entropy of the data is a constant that does not depend on the weights
  2. It minimises KL(model || data), because the model produces the predictions
  3. It minimises the entropy of the model, making the predictions as confident as possible
  4. It is unrelated to KL; cross-entropy and KL are two different measures
H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p || q), and H(p) does not change when the weights change, so minimising cross-entropy over the weights minimises the forward KL from the data distribution to the model. The reverse direction is what the policy-regularisation penalty uses, not ordinary training. Cross-entropy does not reward confidence for its own sake: confident wrong answers are penalised heavily.

A model gives probability 0 to a token that does occur in the test data with probability 0.01 under the true distribution. What is KL(p || q) from the truth p to the model q, and what does it tell you?

  1. 0.01 bits, the probability of the missed token
  2. Zero, since a single token is a negligible part of the distribution
  3. A large but finite number that depends on the vocabulary size
  4. Infinity: the model declares an event impossible that actually happens, so one such token makes the divergence (and the held-out cross-entropy) unbounded
The term p(x) log(p(x)/q(x)) with q(x) = 0 and p(x) > 0 is infinite, so KL(p || q) = infinity however rare the token is. The same holds for the test cross-entropy, as in the chapter 13 bigram model. This is why language models smooth or are built with a softmax, which never outputs exactly zero, and why a rare-event miss in a held-out set is so costly.

A language model has a cross-entropy of 3 bits per token on a test set. What is its perplexity, and what does that mean?

  1. 3: it is as uncertain as a choice between 3 tokens at each step
  2. 8: it is as uncertain as a uniform choice between 8 tokens at each step
  3. 9: perplexity is the square of the cross-entropy
  4. 0.125: perplexity is the probability of the correct token
Perplexity is 2 raised to the cross-entropy in bits: 23 = 8, the effective number of equally likely choices per token. Reading 3 as the number of choices confuses bits with options. It is not a probability, and it is not the square of the entropy.

Model A uses a tokeniser with a 32,000-token vocabulary and reports a test perplexity of 9. Model B uses a character-level tokeniser and reports a perplexity of 3.2 on the same text. Which comparison of their quality is justified?

  1. None yet: the perplexities are per token and the tokens differ; convert both to total bits (or bits per byte) of the same text first
  2. Model B is better because 3.2 is lower than 9
  3. Model A is better because it has more tokens in its vocabulary
  4. They are equal, since perplexity always measures the same thing
A character-level model makes many more predictions per sentence, each easier than a subword model's, so its per-token perplexity is naturally lower. The fair comparison is total bits on identical text: multiply each model's bits per token by its tokens per byte (or character) of the text, giving bits per byte. Vocabulary size on its own says nothing about quality.

In the KL-regularised objective, expected reward minus β times KL(π || πref), what happens to the optimal policy as β is increased from 1 to 100?

  1. It concentrates more on the highest-reward reply
  2. It becomes uniform over all possible replies
  3. It moves closer to the reference policy, since the reweighting exp(r/β) tends to 1 for every reply
  4. Nothing: β only rescales the loss and does not change the optimum
The optimum is πref times exp(r/β), normalised. As β grows, r/β shrinks toward 0 for every reply, the exponential tends to 1, and the optimum tends to πref. It does not become uniform (the reference need not be). A smaller β, not a larger one, concentrates the policy on high-reward replies. And β clearly changes the optimum, as the numbers in the table show.
