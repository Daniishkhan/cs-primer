Eigenvectors, SVD & Low Rank

Some matrices have special directions that they only stretch, and every matrix can be broken into a rotation, a stretch along axes and another rotation. This chapter introduces eigenvectors and the singular value decomposition (SVD), shows the one theorem that makes them useful (the best low-rank approximation of a matrix is read straight off its SVD), and explains why so many matrices in machine learning turn out to be well approximated by a few directions.

Why this matters

Chapters 5 and 6 gave you vectors and matrices as functions. This chapter asks what a matrix really does, and the answer pays off throughout the path. PCA (A1, chapter 11) is an eigen-problem. LoRA (A3, chapter 11) fine-tunes a model by learning a low-rank update, which only makes sense once you know what rank is and what it costs to lose it. Recommenders, latent semantic search, embedding compression and some attention variants that shrink the KV cache all rely on the same fact: a large table of numbers is often a few patterns in disguise. After this chapter you will be able to compute and interpret an eigen-decomposition and an SVD, state what the truncated SVD guarantees, and judge whether a given matrix is likely to compress well.

Eigenvectors: directions a matrix only stretches

When a matrix acts on a vector it usually changes both the length and the direction. For most matrices, though, there are a few special directions that come out pointing the same way, only longer, shorter or flipped. An eigenvector of a square matrix A is a non-zero vector v with

A v = λ v

and the number λ (lambda) is its eigenvalue: the factor by which A stretches that direction. (The words come from the German eigen, "own": the matrix's own directions.) A negative eigenvalue flips the vector; an eigenvalue of 1 leaves it unchanged; 0 means the matrix squashes that direction to nothing.

Finding them by hand

Take A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]]. Rearrange A v = λ v into (A − λI) v = 0. A non-zero v can satisfy that only if A − λI is singular, i.e. has determinant zero (chapter 6). For a 2×2 matrix:

det(A − λI) = (4 − λ)(3 − λ) − 1×2 = λ² − 7λ + 10 = (λ − 5)(λ − 2)

so λ = 5 or λ = 2. Note the pattern: 7 is the trace of A (the sum of its diagonal) and 10 is its determinant. In general the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant. Now find the direction for each. For λ = 5, (A − 5I) = [[−1, 1], [2, −2]], and v = (1, 1) solves it. For λ = 2, (A − 2I) = [[2, 1], [2, 1]], solved by v = (1, −2). The code agrees, up to scaling the vectors to length 1.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[4.0, 1.0],
              [2.0, 3.0]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
vals, vecs = vals.real, vecs.real            # imaginary parts are all zero here
print("trace, det        :", np.trace(A), round(np.linalg.det(A), 6))
print("eigenvalues       :", vals)
for lam, v in zip(vals, vecs.T):
    print(f"lambda={lam:.0f}  v={np.round(v, 4)}  A@v={np.round(A @ v, 4)}  lambda*v={np.round(lam * v, 4)}")

# a rotation keeps no direction fixed: its eigenvalues are complex
R = np.array([[0.0, -1.0], [1.0, 0.0]])
print("rotation 90 deg   :", np.linalg.eig(R)[0])
trace, det        : 7.0 10.0
eigenvalues       : [5. 2.]

lambda=2  v=[-0.4472  0.8944]  A@v=[-0.8944  1.7889]  lambda*v=[-0.8944  1.7889]
rotation 90 deg   : [0.+1.j 0.-1.j]

The last line of the output is a reminder that not every matrix has real eigenvectors. A 90-degree rotation keeps no direction fixed, and its eigenvalues are the complex numbers i and −i. This is why general-purpose eigen routines may return complex results, and why the matrices where everything is well behaved, the symmetric ones in the next section, deserve special attention.

Repeating a matrix: the power of the biggest eigenvalue

Eigenvectors become powerful when you apply a matrix again and again. Write any starting vector as a combination of eigenvectors, x = c1v1 + c2v2. Each application multiplies each piece by its own eigenvalue, so after k steps Akx = c1λ1kv1 + c2λ2kv2. With λ1 = 5 and λ2 = 2, the first term grows like 5k and the second like 2k, so the direction of Akx tends to that of v1 whatever you start with. The ratio λ2/λ1 = 0.4 sets how fast. This is the power iteration, and its three lines of code give the largest eigenvalue without any determinant. Normalising each step keeps the numbers from overflowing (a precaution that chapter 11 explains).

import math

A = [[4.0, 1.0], [2.0, 3.0]]
def matvec(M, v): return [sum(m * x for m, x in zip(row, v)) for row in M]
def norm(v): return math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v))

v = [1.0, -0.5]                      # an arbitrary starting vector
for step in range(1, 9):
    w = matvec(A, v)
    lam = norm(w) / norm(v)          # growth factor of this step
    v = [x / norm(w) for x in w]     # renormalise so numbers stay small
    print(f"step {step}: v = ({v[0]:.4f}, {v[1]:.4f})   growth = {lam:.4f}")
print("exact: v = (0.7071, 0.7071), eigenvalue 5; error shrinks by 2/5 = 0.4 per step")

step 2: v = (0.8627, 0.5057)   growth = 4.7539

step 4: v = (0.7341, 0.6791)   growth = 5.0781

The direction homes in on (0.7071, 0.7071) and the growth factor on 5, with the error shrinking by about 0.4 per step, as predicted. You will implement this in Lab 1. The same argument explains several things you will see later:

  • PageRank is the power iteration on the web's link matrix.
  • Exploding and vanishing gradients in a recurrent network come from multiplying by the same matrix at every time step: eigenvalues above 1 in size blow up, those below 1 die away (A2, chapter 8).
  • Curvature of a loss surface is described by eigenvalues of its matrix of second derivatives, and the largest one limits the learning rate you can use (chapter 10).

Symmetric matrices, covariance and PCA

A matrix is symmetric if it equals its transpose, A = AT: the entry in row i, column j equals the entry in row j, column i. These matrices are everywhere in machine learning (covariance matrices, Gram matrices of dot products, Hessians) and they are the nicest case. The spectral theorem says that a real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues, and its eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular to each other and of unit length. Stacking those eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix Q (with QTQ = I, an orthogonal matrix, which preserves lengths and angles, so it is a pure rotation or reflection), the matrix factorises as

A = Q Λ QT

where Λ is diagonal with the eigenvalues. Read right to left, that is "rotate so that the eigenvectors become the axes, stretch each axis by its eigenvalue, rotate back". A symmetric matrix is a pure stretch along perpendicular axes, seen from an angle.

Where do symmetric matrices come from in data? Take a data matrix X with one example per row and one feature per column, with each column's mean subtracted (centred). The covariance matrix C = XTX/(n − 1) has shape (features, features); entry (i, j) is the covariance of features i and j, and the diagonal holds variances. It is symmetric by construction. For a unit direction u, the variance of the data projected onto u (chapter 5) is exactly uTC u. Which u makes it largest? The answer, which the details box proves, is the top eigenvector of C, and the maximum variance is the top eigenvalue. This is principal component analysis (PCA): the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered by eigenvalue, are the principal components, the directions along which the data varies most, and each eigenvalue is the variance along its direction.

Go deeper why the top eigenvector maximises variance

Write u in the eigenbasis of C: u = a1q1 + … + anqn with ∑ ai² = 1 because u has length 1 and the qi are orthonormal. Then C u = ∑ aiλiqi and uTC u = ∑ λiai². This is a weighted average of the eigenvalues with weights ai² that sum to 1, so it cannot exceed the largest eigenvalue, and it reaches it exactly when all the weight is on q1. Restricting to directions perpendicular to q1 and repeating gives q2, and so on.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

n = 500
t = rng.standard_normal(n)                       # one hidden factor
X = np.column_stack([2.0 * t + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal(n),
                     1.0 * t + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal(n)])
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)                          # centre the data

C = Xc.T @ Xc / (n - 1)                          # covariance matrix, shape (2, 2)
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)                   # eigh: for symmetric matrices
order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]
vals, vecs = vals[order], vecs[:, order]
print("covariance:\n", np.round(C, 3))
print("eigenvalues (variance along each direction):", np.round(vals, 3))
print("first principal direction:", np.round(vecs[:, 0], 3))
print("share of variance in first direction:", round(vals[0] / vals.sum(), 3))
print("directions orthogonal:", round(float(vecs[:, 0] @ vecs[:, 1]), 12))

U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)  # the same answer from the SVD
print("S**2/(n-1):", np.round(S ** 2 / (n - 1), 3))
print("same direction (up to sign):", np.allclose(abs(Vt[0]), abs(vecs[:, 0])))

The last two output lines show something worth remembering: the same answer comes from the SVD of the centred data matrix, which is what libraries actually use, as the next section explains.

The singular value decomposition

Eigenvectors need a square matrix, and even then real ones are not guaranteed. The singular value decomposition (SVD) works for every matrix, of any shape, and always with real numbers. Any (m, n) matrix A can be written as

A = U Σ VT

where VT is orthogonal (a rotation or reflection of the input space), Σ is a diagonal matrix of non-negative numbers σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ … called the singular values (a stretch along the axes), and U is orthogonal (a rotation or reflection of the output space). The columns of V are the right singular vectors and the columns of U the left singular vectors. In words: every matrix is a rotation, then an axis-aligned stretch, then another rotation. Step through it for a 2×2 matrix.

Step through

Any matrix: rotate, stretch, rotate

  1. The input. A unit circle, with two perpendicular directions marked (yellow and green): these are the right singular vectors v1 and v2, and the red dot tracks one ordinary point. The matrix is M = [[2, 1], [0, 1.5]].
  2. Apply VT. A rotation. The circle is still a circle, but v1 and v2 now lie exactly along the horizontal and vertical axes. This is the choice of input directions that makes the rest easy.
  3. Apply Σ. Stretch the horizontal axis by σ1 = 2.38 and the vertical axis by σ2 = 1.26. The circle becomes an axis-aligned ellipse whose semi-axes are the singular values.
  4. Apply U. Rotate again. The result is the same ellipse the matrix M produces from the unit circle, and the yellow and green arrows are M v1 = σ1u1 and M v2 = σ2u2: the longest and shortest stretches M can produce, at right angles.

The singular values therefore answer "how much can this matrix stretch a vector?": the largest, σ1, is the most; the smallest is the least. Their ratio σ1/σmin is exactly the condition number promised in chapter 6, and a zero singular value is a direction that is flattened away (a singular matrix).

The connection to eigenvalues

Compute ATA = V ΣTUTU Σ VT = V Σ² VT, using UTU = I. That is a symmetric matrix factorised as in the previous section, so the right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of ATA and the singular values are the square roots of its eigenvalues. This explains the last two lines of the PCA output: the squared singular values of the centred data matrix, divided by (n − 1), are the covariance eigenvalues (4.607 and 0.276).

Shapes in practice

A matrix with m rows and n columns has at most k = min(m, n) non-zero singular values. In the "thin" form that libraries return by default (full_matrices=False), U has shape (m, k), Σ is stored as just a vector of k numbers, and VT has shape (k, n). Here is a (5, 3) matrix.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

A = rng.integers(-3, 4, size=(5, 3)).astype(float)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
print("A", A.shape, "-> U", U.shape, " S", S.shape, " Vt", Vt.shape)
print("singular values:", np.round(S, 4))
print("A == U diag(S) Vt :", np.allclose(A, U @ np.diag(S) @ Vt))
print("U^T U = I         :", np.allclose(U.T @ U, np.eye(3)))
print("Vt Vt^T = I       :", np.allclose(Vt @ Vt.T, np.eye(3)))
print("sqrt(eig(A^T A))  :", np.round(np.sqrt(np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(A.T @ A))[::-1]), 4))

# A as a sum of rank-1 pieces: sigma_i * u_i v_i^T
pieces = [S[i] * np.outer(U[:, i], Vt[i]) for i in range(3)]
print("sum of 3 rank-1 pieces == A:", np.allclose(sum(pieces), A))
print("rank of each piece:", [int(np.linalg.matrix_rank(p)) for p in pieces], " rank of A:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(A))

Note the final two lines. The matrix is the sum of three pieces σiuiviT, each the product of a column and a row (an outer product). Each piece has rank 1, and their number is the rank of A.

Rank: how many independent patterns

The rank of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns: columns that cannot be built from the others. Equivalently it is the number of non-zero singular values. A matrix of rank 1 is an outer product u vT: every row is a multiple of one row, so the whole table is one pattern scaled up and down. A (1000, 1000) matrix has a million numbers, but if its rank is 3 it is determined by three columns and three rows, about 6,000 numbers.

Exact low rank is rare in real data, because noise and small effects fill in the remaining singular values. The interesting case is approximately low rank: a few large singular values and a long tail of small ones. In floating point, "zero" means "below a tolerance" (np.linalg.matrix_rank uses the largest singular value times max(m, n) times machine epsilon by default), and what counts as small is a judgement about your task.

The best low-rank approximation

Suppose you keep only the k largest terms of the sum, Ak = σ1u1v1T + … + σkukvkT. This is the truncated SVD. The theorem that justifies its use is due to Eckart and Young (1936): among all matrices of rank at most k, Ak is the closest to A, measured by the Frobenius norm (the square root of the sum of all squared entries, i.e. the vector norm of chapter 5 applied to the flattened matrix), and the error is determined entirely by the discarded singular values:

‖A − Ak‖F = √(σk+1² + σk+2² + …)

The theorem holds for the Frobenius norm and also for the spectral norm (the largest stretch, with error σk+1), a generalisation due to Mirsky (1960). There is no cleverer rank-k matrix to find; no search is needed. The next run uses a 24×24 greyscale picture, a small house with a little noise added, and compares the actual error with the formula.

import numpy as np
from mkimage import make

A = make()                                         # a 24 x 24 greyscale picture
m, n = A.shape
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
print("singular values (first 8):", np.round(S[:8], 2))
print(f"{'k':>3s} {'stored':>7s} {'of':>5s} {'rel. error':>11s} {'eckart-young':>13s}")
for k in (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12):
    Ak = (U[:, :k] * S[:k]) @ Vt[:k]
    err = np.linalg.norm(A - Ak) / np.linalg.norm(A)
    ey = np.sqrt(np.sum(S[k:] ** 2)) / np.linalg.norm(A)    # from discarded singular values
    print(f"{k:3d} {k * (m + n + 1):7d} {m * n:5d} {err:11.4f} {ey:13.4f}")

Telling structure from noise

The shape of the singular value list, the spectrum, is the diagnostic. A matrix with real low-rank structure shows a cliff: a few large values, then a sudden drop to a floor set by the noise. A matrix with no structure shows a gentle slope. The figure compares a 100×100 matrix built as five patterns plus noise with pure noise at a comparable overall scale.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n, r = 100, 5
low = rng.standard_normal((n, r)) @ rng.standard_normal((r, n))      # exactly rank 5
low_noisy = low + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((n, n))                  # plus noise
noise_only = rng.standard_normal((n, n)) * np.sqrt(r * 1.0)          # similar overall scale

for name, M in (("rank 5 + noise", low_noisy), ("pure noise", noise_only)):
    s = np.linalg.svd(M, compute_uv=False)
    share5 = (s[:5] ** 2).sum() / (s ** 2).sum()
    print(f"{name:15s} s1..s7 =", np.round(s[:7], 1), f" top-5 energy share = {share5:.2f}")
rank 5 + noise  s1..s7 = [120.7 115.9  92.1  87.   74.5   9.5   9.3]  top-5 energy share = 0.96
pure noise      s1..s7 = [43.7 43.5 41.9 41.1 40.4 38.6 38.4]  top-5 energy share = 0.18

Analogy: a caricature

A good caricaturist captures a face in a handful of strokes: the big nose, the wide jaw, the tall forehead. Those strokes are the large singular values. Each added stroke is a smaller correction, and after a dozen you could not tell the sketch from a photograph. The truncated SVD is the best possible sketch with k strokes, in the sense of least squared error.

Where it breaks down: "best" means least squared error over the whole matrix, not "most useful for your task". A small singular value can carry the one rare feature you care about, such as the signal that separates fraud from normal traffic, and a caricature only exaggerates what is big. Judge a truncation by what it does to the downstream result, not only by the energy kept.

Common misconception

"Eigenvectors and singular vectors are the same thing, and small singular values are just noise." Neither holds in general. Eigenvectors exist only for square matrices and are not orthogonal unless the matrix is symmetric; singular vectors always exist and are always orthogonal. For symmetric positive semi-definite matrices such as covariance matrices the two coincide, which is why PCA can be done either way. And a long tail of small singular values is noise only if the spectrum shows a cliff, as in the left bars above; a smooth slope means information is spread across many directions, and truncating loses it.

Why low rank is everywhere

Real matrices are often close to low rank for a simple reason: they are generated by a small number of underlying factors. Four settings matter in this path.

  • Data driven by a few factors. Customers' ratings of films depend on a handful of tastes, so a (users, films) table is nearly low rank; the winning approaches to the Netflix Prize and most recommenders since factorise it into user and item vectors (Koren, Bell and Volinsky, 2009). The same idea applied to word-document counts is latent semantic analysis (Deerwester et al., 1990), an ancestor of embeddings (A2, chapter 6).
  • Updates to a pretrained model. LoRA freezes a weight matrix W and learns an update as a product B A with small rank r (Hu et al., 2021). The motivation is the observation that fine-tuning moves a model in a low-dimensional subspace: Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer and Gupta (2020) found that language models can reach most of their fine-tuned quality while training in a random subspace of far fewer dimensions than the parameter count. Chapter 6 showed the arithmetic of the saving.
  • Compressing the KV cache. The cache that makes decoding fast (A3, chapter 18) can be large; DeepSeek-V2 (2024) introduced Multi-head Latent Attention, which, according to the paper's abstract, compresses keys and values into a smaller latent vector. A compress-then-expand step of that kind is a low-rank factorisation in spirit, applied to activations rather than weights.
  • Smaller indexes. Embeddings often have many correlated dimensions. Projecting 768-dimensional vectors onto their top 128 principal components cuts 1,000,000 vectors from 3.07 GB to 512 MB in float32 (1,000,000 × 128 × 4 bytes), at a cost in recall that must be measured, not assumed (A5, chapter 11).

State of play, October 2026

Low-rank adapters are a standard option for fine-tuning open-weight models in widely used libraries, and several variants (different initialisations, rank allocation per layer, quantised bases) exist; check the current documentation of the library you use rather than relying on this chapter for defaults. What is not new and not likely to change is the mathematics above. What is empirical, and does change from model to model, is how much rank a given task needs: harder shifts of behaviour tend to need larger ranks, so treat r as a hyperparameter to tune on a validation set.

Doing it on a computer

Four practical rules, most of which come from the mathematics above.

  1. Use the right routine. np.linalg.eigh for symmetric matrices (real, sorted, orthonormal output); np.linalg.eig only for general square ones, expecting possibly complex results; np.linalg.svd for anything rectangular or when you want singular values.
  2. Do not get singular values from eigenvalues of ATA. Forming ATA squares the condition number, and small singular values vanish in rounding error. The next run shows it.
  3. Mind the cost. A full SVD of an (m, n) matrix takes work of order mn·min(m, n). For a large matrix where you need only the top k, use a truncated or randomised method (Halko, Martinsson and Tropp, 2011; in SciPy, scipy.sparse.linalg.svds), which costs about mnk.
  4. Signs are arbitrary. If (u, v) is a singular pair, so is (−u, −v), and repeated singular values leave the vectors non-unique. Compare results up to sign (as the PCA output did with abs), and never assert on the sign of a component.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
Q1, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.standard_normal((6, 6)))
Q2, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.standard_normal((6, 6)))
true_s = np.array([1.0, 0.1, 1e-2, 1e-4, 1e-6, 1e-9])
A = Q1 @ np.diag(true_s) @ Q2.T

s_svd = np.linalg.svd(A, compute_uv=False)
w = np.linalg.eigvalsh(A.T @ A)[::-1]                 # squares the singular values
s_eig = np.sqrt(np.clip(w, 0, None))
fmt = lambda v: "  ".join(f"{x:9.2e}" for x in v)
print("true singular values:", fmt(true_s))
print("np.linalg.svd       :", fmt(s_svd))
print("sqrt(eig(A^T A))    :", fmt(s_eig))
true singular values:  1.00e+00   1.00e-01   1.00e-02   1.00e-04   1.00e-06   1.00e-09
np.linalg.svd       :  1.00e+00   1.00e-01   1.00e-02   1.00e-04   1.00e-06   1.00e-09
sqrt(eig(A^T A))    :  1.00e+00   1.00e-01   1.00e-02   1.00e-04   1.00e-06   0.00e+00

The matrix has a smallest singular value of 10−9. Squaring it gives 10−18, below the resolution of 64-bit floats relative to the largest value (about 10−16), so the eigenvalue route reports 0 and would call a full-rank matrix singular. The SVD recovers all six.

Systems connection

Low rank is a systems technique as much as a mathematical one. Single-token decoding is limited by how many bytes of weights and cache must be read per token (A3, chapter 18; Performance Engineering), so factorising a matrix into thin factors, or compressing a cache, reduces the memory traffic as well as the arithmetic. The same logic governs vector search: dimension reduction shrinks index memory, which decides how many vectors fit on one machine and therefore how many shards a service needs (Designing with ML & LLMs). The cost of the SVD itself, cubic in the size for a dense matrix, is why it is run offline or on a sample, not on the request path.

Try it yourself

Build your own check of the cliff test. Take any embedding matrix you can get hold of (for example, a few thousand sentence embeddings from a model you use, or the rows of a pretrained weight matrix if you have a framework installed), centre it, and compute its singular values with np.linalg.svd(X, compute_uv=False). Print the cumulative share of squared singular values. How many components capture 90% of the energy? Is there a cliff, or a slope? Then repeat with a random Gaussian matrix of the same shape as a control. The gap between the two answers is how much low-rank structure your matrix really has, and it predicts how much a rank-r adapter or a PCA projection can save.

Key takeaways

  • An eigenvector of a square matrix keeps its direction; its eigenvalue is the stretch. Eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant. Repeated application drives any vector towards the top eigenvector (power iteration).
  • Symmetric matrices, such as covariance matrices, have real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors: A = Q Λ QT. The top eigenvector of the covariance is the direction of greatest variance, which is PCA.
  • Every matrix has an SVD, A = U Σ VT: rotate, stretch along axes by the singular values, rotate. Singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATA, and their ratio is the condition number.
  • Rank is the number of non-zero singular values. The sum of the top k terms, the truncated SVD, is the best rank-k approximation (Eckart–Young), with error given by the discarded singular values.
  • Low rank saves storage only when k is small against the dimensions: k(m + n + 1) against mn. A cliff in the spectrum signals real structure; a slope does not.
  • Low-rank structure underlies PCA, recommenders, LoRA, KV-cache compression and embedding compression. Whether it holds is empirical, and "small" singular values may still matter for your task.
  • Compute SVDs with an SVD routine, not by eigen-decomposing ATA, and expect arbitrary signs.

A matrix has eigenvalues 3 and 0.5. You apply it ten times to a starting vector that has some component along each eigenvector. What happens to the direction and the size?

  1. It approaches the direction of the eigenvalue-0.5 eigenvector and shrinks
  2. It approaches the direction of the eigenvalue-3 eigenvector and grows by roughly 310, about 59,000 times
  3. It stays exactly where it started
  4. It rotates through all directions in turn
Each eigen-component is multiplied by its eigenvalue every step: the 3-component by 310 = 59,049, the 0.5-component by 0.510, about 0.001. The first swamps the second, so the direction converges to the top eigenvector. This is the power iteration. Ten steps are enough for the dominant term to be thousands of times larger than the other.

The covariance matrix of a dataset has eigenvalues 9, 1 and 0.5. You want to keep at least 95% of the variance. How many principal components do you need?

  1. 1
  2. 3
  3. 2
  4. It cannot be told from the eigenvalues
The total variance is 10.5. One component keeps 9/10.5 = 85.7%, two keep 10/10.5 = 95.2%, which meets the target. Eigenvalues of the covariance are exactly the variances along the principal directions, so the answer can be read off them without seeing the data.

You call np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False) on A of shape (1000, 50). What are the shapes of U, S and Vt?

  2. (1000, 1000), (1000,), (50, 50)

With k = min(1000, 50) = 50 there are at most 50 non-zero singular values: U is (m, k), the singular values come back as a vector of length k, and Vt is (k, n) = (50, 50). Option 2 is the shape of the full decomposition, which wastes memory on columns that multiply zeros.

A matrix has singular values 10, 5, 2 and 1. What is the Frobenius-norm error of its best rank-2 approximation?

  1. 1
  2. 3
  3. 11.2
  4. About 2.24
By Eckart–Young the error is the square root of the sum of the squared discarded singular values: √(2² + 1²) = √5 ≈ 2.24. Option 1 is the largest discarded value, which is the error in the spectral norm; option 2 adds the discarded values without squaring and rooting. 11.2 is the root of the sum of the squares of the kept values.

A full weight update has shape (1024, 1024). A rank-4 LoRA-style factorisation B A is used instead. About how many fewer numbers are trained?

  1. 4 times fewer
  2. 16 times fewer
  3. 128 times fewer
  4. 1,024 times fewer
The full update has 1024 × 1024 = 1,048,576 numbers. B is (1024, 4) and A is (4, 1024): 2 × 1024 × 4 = 8,192 numbers in all. The ratio is 128. The rank r appears in the denominator only once per factor, so the saving is d/(2r), not d/r.

A colleague computes the singular values of a tall data matrix as the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATA, because it is a small symmetric matrix. What is the main risk?

  1. The singular values come out in the wrong order
  2. Forming ATA squares the condition number, so small singular values can be lost to rounding
  3. ATA has complex eigenvalues
  4. It only works for matrices with equal numbers of rows and columns
The mathematics is right, since the singular values really are those square roots, but the numerics are not: a singular value of 10−9 becomes an eigenvalue of 10−18 and disappears below floating-point resolution, as the run showed. ATA is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real; the order is easily fixed; and no squareness is needed.
