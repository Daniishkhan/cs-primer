Probability, Distributions & Sampling

Almost everything a machine-learning system outputs is a probability, and almost everything it does with data is a form of sampling or averaging. This chapter builds the working vocabulary: random variables, the handful of distributions that cover most cases, expectation, Bayes' rule, and the practical business of drawing random numbers from any distribution you can write down.

Why this matters

A language model does not output a word; it outputs a probability for every word in its vocabulary, and a separate step samples one (A2, chapter 15). A classifier's score is only useful if it can be read as a probability (A1, chapter 15). Minibatch training replaces an average over the whole dataset with an average over a random handful (chapter 10), and an evaluation score on 500 test questions is one random draw of what the score would be on all the questions you might have asked (A6, chapter 4). If you cannot say what a random variable, an expectation or a conditional probability is, each of those statements is a slogan. After this chapter you will be able to compute with them, simulate them, and check your answers numerically. The next three chapters build on it: chapter 13 turns probabilities into loss functions, chapter 14 measures how different two distributions are, and chapter 15 asks what a measured number can support. Lab 4 (Sampling, Likelihood & Entropy) puts the samplers of this chapter into code.

Probability as a model of repeated chances

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 attached to something that might happen, such that 0 means it never does, 1 means it always does, and a value in between measures how often it happens in the long run. "A fair die shows a six with probability 1/6" is a claim about what you would see if you could roll it a very large number of times: about one roll in six. That reading, probability as long-run frequency, is not the only one (a forecaster who says "70% chance of rain tomorrow" is expressing a degree of belief about a single day), but both readings obey the same arithmetic, and the arithmetic is what we need.

The vocabulary is short. An outcome is one thing that can happen (the die shows 4). The sample space is the set of all outcomes ({1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}). An event is a set of outcomes you care about ("the roll is even" is {2, 4, 6}). The rules are three:

  1. Every probability is at least 0.
  2. The probabilities of all the outcomes add to 1: something must happen.
  3. If two events cannot both happen, the probability that one or the other happens is the sum of their probabilities. Hence P(even) = 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/2, and P(not A) = 1 − P(A).

When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes in it divided by the total number of outcomes. That is enough to compute a surprising amount by counting. Take two fair dice and ask about their sum. There are 36 equally likely pairs, and 6 of them sum to 7 (1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1), so P(sum is 7) = 6/36 = 1/6, the most likely total. The script below enumerates the 36 pairs for the exact answer and then rolls 100,000 simulated pairs from a seeded generator to see whether the long-run frequency agrees.

"""Two dice: the exact distribution of the sum, its mean and variance, and a simulation."""
import random
from collections import Counter

# Exact: enumerate all 36 equally likely outcomes.
pmf = Counter()
for a in range(1, 7):
    for b in range(1, 7):
        pmf[a + b] += 1 / 36

mean = sum(s * p for s, p in pmf.items())
var = sum((s - mean) ** 2 * p for s, p in pmf.items())
print(f"sum of probabilities = {sum(pmf.values()):.6f}")
print(f"E[S] = {mean:.4f}   Var[S] = {var:.4f}   std = {var ** 0.5:.4f}")
print(f"P(S = 7) = {pmf[7]:.4f}   P(S >= 10) = {sum(p for s, p in pmf.items() if s >= 10):.4f}")

# Simulation: 100,000 rolls with a seeded generator.
rng = random.Random(12)
rolls = [rng.randint(1, 6) + rng.randint(1, 6) for _ in range(100_000)]
m = sum(rolls) / len(rolls)
v = sum((r - m) ** 2 for r in rolls) / len(rolls)
print(f"simulated mean = {m:.4f}   variance = {v:.4f}")
print(f"simulated P(S = 7) = {rolls.count(7) / len(rolls):.4f}")
sum of probabilities = 1.000000

P(S = 7) = 0.1667   P(S >= 10) = 0.1667
simulated mean = 7.0034   variance = 5.8502
simulated P(S = 7) = 0.1647

The simulated frequency of a 7 is 0.1647 against an exact 0.1667: close, not equal, and that gap is the subject of a later section. The same two-step habit (compute exactly when you can; simulate to check, or when you cannot) is how the rest of the course treats probability.

Random variables, mass functions and densities

It is awkward to talk about "the event that the sum is 7" and "the event that the sum is at least 10" separately every time. A random variable is a number whose value depends on the outcome of a chance process: S, the sum of two dice, is a random variable, and so is the number of requests that reach a server in the next second, the height of a randomly chosen adult, or the next token id a language model emits. By convention a capital letter (S, X) names the random variable and a lowercase letter (s, x) names a particular value it might take. Writing P(X = x) says "the probability that the random variable X takes the value x".

For a random variable that takes separate values (a discrete variable: dice, counts, token ids), the probability mass function (pmf) lists P(X = x) for each value. The values are non-negative and sum to 1. For a continuous variable, which can take any value in a range (a height, a latency, a weight in a neural network), the probability of any single exact value is 0, and what describes the variable is a probability density function (pdf) f(x), a curve whose area over an interval is the probability of landing in that interval:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx,    ∫ f(x) dx over all x = 1.

Common misconception

"A density is a probability, so it can never exceed 1." A density is probability per unit of x. A variable that is uniformly spread over the interval from 0 to 0.5 has density 2 everywhere on it (the area is 2 × 0.5 = 1), and a very narrow Gaussian has an enormous peak. Only areas are probabilities. This matters later: the log-likelihood of continuous data (chapter 13) can be positive for exactly this reason, and a "loss" that is negative is not a bug.

Both kinds are summarised by the cumulative distribution function (cdf), F(x) = P(X ≤ x), which rises from 0 to 1 and exists for every random variable. We will use it for sampling: its inverse is the key to turning uniform random numbers into any distribution.

Expectation and variance

The expectation (or expected value, or mean) of a random variable is the average of its values, each weighted by its probability:

E[X] = ∑x x · P(X = x)    (for a continuous variable: ∫ x f(x) dx).

For one fair die, E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 3.5, a value the die can never show; the expectation is the centre of mass of the distribution, not a likely outcome. More generally, the expectation of any function g of the variable is E[g(X)] = ∑ g(x) P(X = x): weight the function's values, not the variable's. This one formula is how the whole of machine learning is written. The loss you minimise is, in principle, an expectation: L(w) = E[ℓ(w; x, y)], the average per-example loss over the data distribution. A minibatch loss is a sample average that estimates it.

The most useful property of expectation is linearity: for any random variables X and Y and constants a and b,

E[aX + bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y],

and this holds even when X and Y depend on each other. That is why the expected sum of two dice is 3.5 + 3.5 = 7 without any need to enumerate 36 pairs, and why the expected number of heads in n flips of a coin with heads-probability p is np: write the count as a sum of n indicators, each with expectation p.

The expectation says where the distribution sits; the variance says how widely it spreads around that point:

Var[X] = E[(X − E[X])2] = E[X2] − (E[X])2.

The standard deviation is its square root, in the same units as X. For the die, E[X2] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6, so Var[X] = 91/6 − 3.52 = 35/12 ≈ 2.917 and the standard deviation is about 1.708. Unlike the mean, variance adds only for independent variables (those whose values carry no information about each other): Var[X + Y] = Var[X] + Var[Y]. Two dice therefore have variance 2 × 35/12 = 5.833, which is the 5.8333 the script printed, and the standard deviation of a sum of n independent copies grows as √n, not n. Keep that square root in mind; it governs the next section.

Go deeper optional: why variance adds for independent variables

Write X' = X − E[X] and Y' = Y − E[Y], both with mean 0. Then Var[X + Y] = E[(X' + Y')2] = E[X'2] + 2 E[X'Y'] + E[Y'2] = Var[X] + Var[Y] + 2 Cov[X, Y], where the covariance Cov[X, Y] = E[X'Y'] measures whether the two move together. For independent variables E[X'Y'] = E[X']E[Y'] = 0, so the cross term vanishes. When variables are positively correlated, the cross term is positive and the sum is more variable than independence would suggest. This is why averaging 100 gradients from the same minibatch does not reduce noise the way 100 independent minibatches do, and why a model ensemble whose members make the same mistakes gains little.

The law of large numbers, and the 1/√n rule

Why does the long-run frequency of a 7 approach 1/6? The law of large numbers says that the average of n independent draws of a random variable gets close to its expectation as n grows. It is the reason an estimate from data is worth anything. But "close" needs a size. Apply the variance rule to the average of n independent draws, each with variance σ2: the sum has variance nσ2, and dividing by n divides the variance by n2, leaving

Var[average] = σ2 / n,    so the typical error of the average is σ / √n.

The quantity σ/√n is called the standard error. The script averages n die rolls in 300 independent experiments for several n and compares the typical error against the prediction (for a die σ = √(35/12)).

"""The law of large numbers, and how fast it works: the error of a sample mean shrinks like 1/sqrt(n)."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sigma = np.sqrt(35 / 12)             # standard deviation of one fair die (we derive this in the text)
print(f"{'n':>7} {'rms error':>13} {'sigma/sqrt(n)':>14} {'ratio':>6}")
for n in (10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000):
    # 300 independent experiments, each averaging n die rolls
    means = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(300, n)).mean(axis=1)
    rms_error = np.sqrt(np.mean((means - 3.5) ** 2))
    predicted = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    print(f"{n:>7} {rms_error:>13.5f} {predicted:>14.5f} {rms_error / predicted:>6.2f}")
      n     rms error  sigma/sqrt(n)  ratio
     10       0.49719        0.54006   0.92
    100       0.16654        0.17078   0.98
   1000       0.05351        0.05401   0.99
  10000       0.01678        0.01708   0.98
 100000       0.00535        0.00540   0.99

The ratio sits at 1 within a few per cent at every n, so σ/√n is the right law. Its consequences are everywhere in this course. To halve the error of an estimate you need four times the data. A minibatch of 256 examples gives a gradient estimate whose noise is a quarter of that from a batch of 16, at 16 times the cost, so the returns diminish (chapter 10). A test set of 100 questions leaves an accuracy uncertain by roughly ±10 percentage points (a 95% interval, at worst); getting to ±1 takes nearly 10,000 (chapter 15).

A small zoo of distributions

You can describe any random variable by listing its probabilities, but a few named families keep reappearing because they arise from simple mechanisms. Learn the mechanism and you can guess which one fits a new situation.

Bernoulli(p)
One yes/no trial: 1 with probability p, 0 otherwise. Mean p, variance p(1 − p). A click, a spam label, one correct answer.
Categorical(p1, …, pK)
One draw from K options with given probabilities that sum to 1. The output of a softmax layer is a categorical distribution: a language model's next token is one draw from it.
Binomial(n, p)
The number of successes in n independent Bernoulli(p) trials. Mean np, variance np(1 − p). The number of correct answers on an n-question test, if every question were answered correctly with the same probability.
Poisson(λ)
The count of events in a fixed interval when events arrive independently at an average rate λ. Mean and variance are both λ. Requests per second, typos per page, defects per metre. It is the limit of a binomial with many trials, each rarely successful.
Uniform(a, b)
Every value in the interval equally likely, density 1/(b − a). The raw material of every random number generator.
Gaussian (normal) N(μ, σ2)
The bell curve with density (1/(σ√(2π))) exp(−(x − μ)2/(2σ2)). 68% of the mass lies within one standard deviation of the mean and 95% within two. Measurement noise, initial weights, and (next section) the shape of averages.
Exponential(λ)
The waiting time until the next Poisson event; mean 1/λ. It is memoryless: having waited five seconds tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait.

Three relationships tie these together and make them easy to remember. A Binomial is a sum of Bernoullis. A Poisson is a Binomial with a huge n and a tiny p, holding np = λ fixed. An Exponential describes the gaps between the events of a Poisson process. If requests arrive as a Poisson process with rate 200 per second, the number arriving in any given second is Poisson(200) and the gap between consecutive requests is Exponential with mean 5 ms, which is the model behind the queueing results in Reliability, chapter 8.

Real data often departs from these tidy shapes. Latencies, file sizes, word frequencies and incomes are heavy-tailed: extreme values are far more common than a Gaussian with the same mean would allow. One extreme case is the Cauchy distribution, whose tails are so heavy that its mean does not exist. We will meet it in a moment, because it breaks the most-quoted theorem in statistics.

Why the Gaussian is everywhere: the central limit theorem

The law of large numbers says the average of many draws approaches the mean. The central limit theorem (CLT) describes the shape of the leftover error: for independent draws with finite variance, the distribution of the average, after subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard error, approaches a standard Gaussian as n grows, whatever the shape of the original distribution. A skewed exponential, a flat uniform, a lopsided die: average enough of them and the histogram of averages becomes a bell. This is why the Gaussian is the default model for noise (noise is usually the sum of many small independent effects) and why a formula like "mean ± 1.96 standard errors" works for statistics that were never Gaussian themselves.

The script checks this on uniform draws, which are as un-bell-shaped as a distribution gets (flat, with hard edges). It sums k of them, standardises, and measures how often the result lands within one and two standard deviations of its mean, against the Gaussian's 0.6827 and 0.9545. A second part contrasts the running mean of a Gaussian with that of a Cauchy.

"""The central limit theorem, and its failure: averages of well-behaved variables turn Gaussian; averages of Cauchy variables never settle."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
# Part 1: standardised sums of k Uniform(0,1) variables. Mean k/2, variance k/12.
print("fraction of standardised sums within 1 and 2 standard deviations (Gaussian: 0.6827, 0.9545)")
for k in (1, 2, 5, 30):
    s = rng.random((200_000, k)).sum(axis=1)
    z = (s - k / 2) / np.sqrt(k / 12)
    print(f"  k = {k:>2}: within 1 sd {np.mean(np.abs(z) < 1):.4f}   within 2 sd {np.mean(np.abs(z) < 2):.4f}")

# Part 2: running mean of a Gaussian versus a Cauchy (a heavy-tailed distribution with no mean).
g = rng.standard_normal(1_000_000)
c = rng.standard_cauchy(1_000_000)
print("\nrunning mean after n draws")
print(f"{'n':>9} {'Gaussian':>10} {'Cauchy':>10}")
for n in (100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000):
    print(f"{n:>9} {g[:n].mean():>10.4f} {c[:n].mean():>10.4f}")
print("largest single Cauchy draw in 1,000,000:", round(float(np.abs(c).max())))

The theorem needs a finite variance, and when it fails there is nothing to converge to.

Analogy: a distribution is the spec sheet of a random machine

Think of a random variable as a machine that emits a number each time you press its button. The distribution is the machine's spec sheet: it tells you how often each value comes out and which values are possible, but it does not say what the next press will produce. The mean is the centre of mass of the spec sheet, the variance how wide it is, and sampling means pressing the button.

Where it breaks down: in real work you never receive the spec sheet. You see only the outputs of the machine, and have to infer the sheet from them, which is the statistics of chapter 13 and chapter 15. The real machine can also be rebuilt behind your back when the world changes, which is distribution shift.

Joint, marginal and conditional probability

Real problems involve several random quantities at once: does an email contain the word "free", and is it spam? The joint distribution P(A, B) gives the probability of each combination. From it you get the marginal distribution of one variable by adding over the other (P(A) = ∑B P(A, B): "marginal" because these sums were once written in the margins of the table), and the conditional distribution by restricting attention to the cases where one thing is known and renormalising:

P(A | B) = P(A, B) / P(B).

Read P(A | B) as "the probability of A, given that B happened". Rearranged, the definition is the product rule, P(A, B) = P(A | B) P(B), and applying it repeatedly gives the chain rule of probability:

P(x1, x2, …, xn) = P(x1) P(x2 | x1) P(x3 | x1, x2) … P(xn | x1, …, xn−1).

This is the equation behind every autoregressive language model. The probability of a whole sentence is the product of the probability of each token given everything before it, and a model that outputs P(next token | previous tokens) defines a distribution over all possible texts. Two variables are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other, P(A, B) = P(A) P(B), or equivalently P(A | B) = P(A). The script builds the email table from counts and checks the chain rule on a toy language in which each word depends only on its predecessor.

"""Joint, marginal and conditional probabilities from a table of counts, and the chain rule on a toy language."""
from collections import defaultdict

# 1000 emails, classified by (is it spam?, does it contain the word 'free'?)
counts = {("spam", "free"): 180, ("spam", "no-free"): 120, ("ham", "free"): 40, ("ham", "no-free"): 660}
total = sum(counts.values())
joint = {k: v / total for k, v in counts.items()}

marg_class, marg_word = defaultdict(float), defaultdict(float)
for (c, w), p in joint.items():
    marg_class[c] += p
    marg_word[w] += p
print("joint:   ", {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in joint.items()})
print("marginal:", {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in marg_class.items()}, {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in marg_word.items()})
print(f"P(spam | free)    = {joint[('spam', 'free')] / marg_word['free']:.3f}")
print(f"P(free | spam)    = {joint[('spam', 'free')] / marg_class['spam']:.3f}")
print(f"P(spam) * P(free) = {marg_class['spam'] * marg_word['free']:.3f}  vs joint {joint[('spam', 'free')]:.3f}  -> not independent")

# Chain rule: P(w1, w2, w3) = P(w1) P(w2 | w1) P(w3 | w1, w2). A toy model where each word depends only on the previous one.
p_first = {"the": 0.6, "a": 0.4}
p_next = {"the": {"cat": 0.5, "dog": 0.5}, "a": {"cat": 0.25, "dog": 0.75},
          "cat": {"sat": 0.8, "ran": 0.2}, "dog": {"sat": 0.3, "ran": 0.7}}
def prob(sentence):
    p = p_first[sentence[0]]
    for prev, word in zip(sentence, sentence[1:]):
        p *= p_next[prev][word]
    return p
for s in (["the", "cat", "sat"], ["a", "dog", "ran"]):
    print(f"P({' '.join(s)}) = {prob(s):.4f}")
total_prob = sum(prob([a, b, c]) for a in p_first for b in p_next[a] for c in p_next[b])
print(f"sum over all 8 three-word sentences = {total_prob:.4f}")

Two lessons sit in that output. First, P(spam | free) = 0.818 and P(free | spam) = 0.600 are different numbers answering different questions; confusing them is the most common error in probabilistic reasoning, and the next section fixes it. Second, the product P(spam) × P(free) = 0.066 is far from the joint 0.180, so the two are not independent, which is exactly why the word is useful as a feature. Bayes' rule

We often know P(evidence | cause) and want P(cause | evidence). A medical test's maker knows how often the test is positive for sick and for healthy people; the patient who tested positive wants to know how likely they are to be sick. The product rule gives the link in one line. Since P(A, B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A), dividing by P(B) gives Bayes' rule:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) P(H) / P(E),    with P(E) = ∑H P(E | H) P(H).

The pieces have names. H is a hypothesis and E the evidence. P(H) is the prior: your belief before seeing the evidence. P(E | H) is the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true. P(H | E) is the posterior: your updated belief. The denominator P(E) is only the normaliser that makes the posteriors add to 1.

Here is the classic trap. A condition affects 1% of a population. A screening test catches 90% of cases (sensitivity 0.90) and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people (false-positive rate 0.05). You test positive. How likely are you to have the condition? Most people answer something near 90%, and versions of this question given to physicians in several well-known studies drew similar over-estimates. The calculation says otherwise, and the easiest way to see why is to count people instead of multiplying probabilities (an idea Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage showed in 1995 makes Bayesian reasoning far easier). Step through the figure.

Step through

Bayes' rule by counting people

  1. A population of 10,000. Pick any large number; the answer will not depend on it. We will follow everyone through the test.
  2. Apply the prior. With a prevalence of 1%, 100 people have the condition and 9,900 do not.
  3. Apply the likelihoods. Of the 100 sick, 90% (90 people) test positive. Of the 9,900 healthy, 5% (495 people) also test positive: the false alarms.
  4. Condition on the evidence. You tested positive, so you are one of the 90 + 495 = 585 people in the dashed box. Everyone outside it is irrelevant now.
  5. Read off the posterior. 90 of those 585 are truly sick: 15.4%. The false alarms from the large healthy group outnumber the true detections more than five to one, because the condition is rare.

The same calculation with the formula, plus two further views, is in the script. The second view is the odds form of Bayes' rule, which makes updating a single multiplication: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio, where the likelihood ratio is P(E | H) / P(E | not H) = 0.90 / 0.05 = 18. A positive test multiplies the odds of disease by 18, but the prior odds were only 1 to 99.

"""Bayes' rule on a screening test, three ways: formula, natural frequencies and repeated updates."""
prevalence, sensitivity, false_pos = 0.01, 0.90, 0.05

# 1. The formula.
p_pos = sensitivity * prevalence + false_pos * (1 - prevalence)
posterior = sensitivity * prevalence / p_pos
print(f"P(positive) = {p_pos:.4f}   P(disease | positive) = {posterior:.4f}")

# 2. Natural frequencies: imagine 10,000 people.
n = 10_000
sick = n * prevalence
true_pos = sick * sensitivity
false_positives = (n - sick) * false_pos
print(f"of {n:,} people: {sick:.0f} sick, {true_pos:.0f} of them test positive; "
      f"{n - sick:.0f} healthy, {false_positives:.0f} of them test positive")
print(f"so {true_pos:.0f} of {true_pos + false_positives:.0f} positives are really sick = {true_pos / (true_pos + false_positives):.4f}")

# 3. Repeated updates: yesterday's posterior is today's prior (assumes independent tests).
belief = prevalence
for i in range(1, 4):
    belief = sensitivity * belief / (sensitivity * belief + false_pos * (1 - belief))
    print(f"after positive test {i}: P(disease) = {belief:.4f}")

# 4. Odds form: posterior odds = prior odds x likelihood ratio.
lr = sensitivity / false_pos
odds = prevalence / (1 - prevalence)
print(f"likelihood ratio = {lr:.0f}; prior odds {odds:.4f} -> posterior odds {odds * lr:.4f} -> P = {odds * lr / (1 + odds * lr):.4f}")
P(positive) = 0.0585   P(disease | positive) = 0.1538

The third part shows sequential updating: yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior. Two positive tests lift the probability to 76.6%, three to 98.3%. One caveat belongs next to that arithmetic: it assumes the tests err independently. Re-running the same test on the same sample, or two tests that fail for the same reason, are correlated, and the true posterior rises more slowly. Treating correlated evidence as independent is the central approximation of naive Bayes classifiers (A1, chapter 7), and it is why they are overconfident.

The base-rate lesson is not about medicine. A fraud model with 99% sensitivity and a 1% false-alarm rate, applied to a stream in which 1 in 1,000 transactions is fraudulent, flags roughly ten false alarms for every real fraud (the same arithmetic: 0.001 × 0.99 against 0.999 × 0.01), so most alerts are wrong. A guardrail classifier on a mostly-benign stream behaves the same way (A6, chapter 15). Whenever you hear "the detector is 99% accurate", the first question is how rare the thing is.

Sampling: from uniform numbers to any distribution

Everything so far described distributions. A sampler is a procedure that produces draws from one. Computers have exactly one primitive source of randomness: a pseudo-random number generator (PRNG), a deterministic algorithm that, given a starting seed, produces a long sequence of numbers that behave like independent draws from the uniform distribution on [0, 1). Same seed, same sequence, which is what makes experiments reproducible (chapter 4). Everything else is built by transforming those uniform numbers.

The inverse-CDF method

The cdf F(x) = P(X ≤ x) maps a value to a probability in [0, 1]. Run it backwards: draw u uniformly in [0, 1], and return x = F−1(u), the value at which the cdf reaches u. Then P(X ≤ x) = P(F−1(U) ≤ x) = P(U ≤ F(x)) = F(x), exactly the right distribution. For an Exponential with rate λ, F(x) = 1 − e−λx, which inverts to x = −ln(1 − u)/λ.

For a categorical distribution the cdf is a staircase of cumulative probabilities, and inversion becomes: walk along the cumulative sums until you pass u. That is how a token is drawn from a softmax output, and it is a few lines of code. The Gaussian's cdf has no closed-form inverse, so a different transformation is used: Box–Muller turns two independent uniforms into two independent standard Gaussians using polar coordinates, z1 = √(−2 ln u1) cos(2πu2), z2 = √(−2 ln u1) sin(2πu2). When no inverse is available at all, rejection sampling works: propose a point uniformly under a box that contains the density, keep it if it lies under the curve, and discard it otherwise. All four are in the next script, in pure Python.

"""Turning uniform random numbers into other distributions: inverse CDF, categorical, Box-Muller, rejection."""
import math, random

rng = random.Random(7)
N = 200_000

# 1. Inverse CDF. Exponential with rate 2: F(x) = 1 - exp(-2x), so F^-1(u) = -ln(1 - u) / 2.
xs = [-math.log(1 - rng.random()) / 2 for _ in range(N)]
print(f"exponential(rate 2): mean {sum(xs)/N:.4f} (exact 0.5), P(X > 1) {sum(x > 1 for x in xs)/N:.4f} (exact {math.exp(-2):.4f})")

# 2. Categorical: walk along the cumulative sums until the uniform draw is covered.
probs = [0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05]
def sample_categorical(p):
    u, acc = rng.random(), 0.0
    for i, pi in enumerate(p):
        acc += pi
        if u < acc:
            return i
    return len(p) - 1                       # guard against rounding at the very top
draws = [sample_categorical(probs) for _ in range(N)]
print("categorical frequencies:", [round(draws.count(i) / N, 4) for i in range(4)], "target", probs)

# 3. Box-Muller: two uniforms become two independent standard Gaussians.
def box_muller():
    u1, u2 = 1 - rng.random(), rng.random()
    r = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u1))
    return r * math.cos(2 * math.pi * u2), r * math.sin(2 * math.pi * u2)
zs = [z for _ in range(N // 2) for z in box_muller()]
m = sum(zs) / len(zs); v = sum((z - m) ** 2 for z in zs) / len(zs)
print(f"Box-Muller: mean {m:.4f}, variance {v:.4f}, P(|Z| < 1.96) {sum(abs(z) < 1.96 for z in zs)/len(zs):.4f} (exact 0.9500)")

# 4. Rejection sampling from a density f(x) = 6x(1-x) on [0,1] using a uniform proposal. Its maximum is 1.5, so accept with prob f(x)/1.5.
accepted, tries = [], 0
while len(accepted) < 50_000:
    x, u = rng.random(), rng.random()
    tries += 1
    if u < 6 * x * (1 - x) / 1.5:
        accepted.append(x)
print(f"rejection: mean {sum(accepted)/len(accepted):.4f} (exact 0.5), acceptance rate {len(accepted)/tries:.3f} (exact {1/1.5:.3f})")
exponential(rate 2): mean 0.4996 (exact 0.5), P(X > 1) 0.1355 (exact 0.1353)

State of play, October 2026: seeds are not forever

A seed reproduces a stream only for the same generator, library version and call pattern. Python's random module uses the Mersenne Twister; NumPy's recommended default_rng uses a different algorithm (PCG64 by default). NumPy's documentation states that Generator streams are reproducible for the same seed, call sequence, NumPy build and machine, but that a feature release (an X.Y version, never a bug-fix X.Y.Z) may change the stream, and that calling rng.random() five times is not guaranteed to give the numbers that rng.random(5) gives. The older RandomState is frozen and will not change. For long-lived experiments, record the library versions with the seed (chapter 4), and for parallel workers spawn child generators from a SeedSequence as the last lines of the script do, rather than seeding each worker with 0, 1, 2. Deep-learning frameworks keep their own generators, and GPU kernels add further non-determinism. Source: NumPy random-number compatibility policy, checked October 2026.

Sampling the next token

A language model's last layer produces one real number per vocabulary entry, the logits z. The softmax turns them into a categorical distribution, pi = ezi/T / ∑j ezj/T, with a temperature T. Dividing by T below 1 sharpens the distribution toward the biggest logit; above 1 it flattens it toward uniform; as T approaches 0 the draw becomes the arg-max. (The subtract-the-maximum trick in the code keeps the exponentials from overflowing, chapter 11.) Top-k sampling zeroes everything except the k most probable tokens and renormalises, and top-p (nucleus) sampling keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, a set whose size adapts to how confident the model is (Holtzman et al., 2019, "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration"). A3 and A4 treat what these settings do to quality; here the point is that every one is a small, checkable piece of probability. Below, six candidate tokens, three temperatures, a top-k and a top-p, with the exact probabilities beside the frequencies in 100,000 draws:

"""Sampling the next token: temperature, top-k and top-p applied to the same logits, with exact probabilities beside the sampled frequencies."""
import numpy as np

tokens = ["the", "a", "cat", "dog", "ran", "sat"]
logits = np.array([3.0, 2.2, 1.5, 1.4, 0.2, -1.0])

def softmax(z, T=1.0):
    z = z / T
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())                  # subtract the max for stability (chapter 11)
    return e / e.sum()

def top_k(p, k):
    out = np.zeros_like(p); idx = np.argsort(p)[::-1][:k]; out[idx] = p[idx]
    return out / out.sum()

def top_p(p, nucleus):
    order = np.argsort(p)[::-1]
    keep = np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p[order]), nucleus) + 1     # smallest set whose mass reaches `nucleus`
    out = np.zeros_like(p); out[order[:keep]] = p[order[:keep]]
    return out / out.sum()

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
def show(name, p):
    draws = rng.choice(len(p), size=100_000, p=p)
    freq = np.bincount(draws, minlength=len(p)) / len(draws)
    print(f"{name:<12} exact " + " ".join(f"{x:.3f}" for x in p))
    print(f"{'':<12} drawn " + " ".join(f"{x:.3f}" for x in freq))

print(f"{'':<19}" + " ".join(f"{t:>5}" for t in tokens))
show("T = 1", softmax(logits))
show("T = 0.5", softmax(logits, 0.5))
show("T = 2", softmax(logits, 2.0))
show("top-k, k=3", top_k(softmax(logits), 3))
show("top-p, 0.9", top_p(softmax(logits), 0.9))

Monte Carlo: estimating expectations by sampling

Many quantities of interest are expectations that cannot be computed in closed form: the average reward of a policy, the expected loss over a data distribution, a probability that depends on a complicated simulation. Monte Carlo estimation replaces the expectation E[g(X)] with the sample average of g over draws x1, …, xn, which by the law of large numbers converges to the truth at the 1/√n rate whatever the dimension of X. That dimension-free rate is why Monte Carlo is the workhorse of reinforcement learning, of Bayesian computation and of the "estimate KL from sampled tokens" trick in chapter 14.

It has a weak spot: rare events. To estimate a probability near 3 × 10−5 with 100,000 draws you expect to see only about three hits, so the estimate is mostly noise. Importance sampling fixes this by drawing from a different distribution q, one that makes the interesting region common, and correcting each draw by the ratio of densities p(x)/q(x), because Ep[g(X)] = Eq[g(X) p(X)/q(X)]. The next script estimates P(Z > 4) for a standard Gaussian both ways.

"""Monte Carlo estimation, and importance sampling for a rare event: P(Z > 4) for a standard Gaussian."""
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
exact = 0.5 * math.erfc(4 / math.sqrt(2))
print(f"exact P(Z > 4) = {exact:.3e}")

# Naive Monte Carlo: count how many samples exceed 4.
n = 100_000
reps = [np.mean(rng.standard_normal(n) > 4) for _ in range(5)]
print("naive, 100,000 samples, 5 repeats:", ["%.1e" % r for r in reps])

# Importance sampling: draw from q = N(4, 1), where the event is common, and correct with the weight p(x)/q(x).
def log_pdf(x, mu): return -0.5 * (x - mu) ** 2 - 0.5 * math.log(2 * math.pi)
ests = []
for _ in range(5):
    x = rng.normal(4, 1, n)
    w = np.exp(log_pdf(x, 0) - log_pdf(x, 4))
    ests.append(np.mean((x > 4) * w))
print("importance, same budget, 5 repeats:", ["%.3e" % e for e in ests])
exact P(Z > 4) = 3.167e-05
naive, 100,000 samples, 5 repeats: ['4.0e-05', '5.0e-05', '2.0e-05', '4.0e-05', '4.0e-05']
importance, same budget, 5 repeats: ['3.165e-05', '3.162e-05', '3.181e-05', '3.147e-05', '3.168e-05']

The exact value is 3.167 × 10−5. Naive sampling returns 2, 4 or 5 × 10−5, off by up to 60% and quantised to multiples of 10−5 because it counted a handful of hits. Importance sampling with the same 100,000 draws lands between 3.147 and 3.181 × 10−5, within about 1% of the truth. The correction weight p/q is the same importance ratio that appears in the clipped objective of PPO (A3, chapter 8), where samples were produced by an older policy but are used to improve a newer one. The cost is that if q puts too little mass where p does, the weights become huge and erratic, so a poor proposal can be worse than none.

Systems connection

Probability turns up in the infrastructure around models as much as in the models. Request arrivals are well modelled as a Poisson process, and the exponential gaps between them are the input to the latency-versus-utilisation curve in queueing theory. Latency itself is heavy-tailed, which is why the average hides the user experience and why tail latency needs percentiles, not means: the Cauchy warning above is the extreme case of the same effect. Trace sampling (Reliability, chapter 6) is Bernoulli sampling with a chosen p, and the sampled counts must be scaled back up by 1/p, which is importance weighting in disguise. And an A/B test that assigns users by hashing their id is a deterministic stand-in for a uniform draw: the same user always gets the same arm, and the split is as good as the hash is uniform.

Key takeaways

  • A random variable is a number produced by a chance process. Discrete ones have a mass function; continuous ones have a density whose area is probability, so a density can exceed 1.
  • Expectation is the probability-weighted average and is linear, even for dependent variables. Variance measures spread and adds for independent variables; the typical error of an average of n draws is σ/√n, so four times the data halves the error.
  • A handful of distributions cover most uses: Bernoulli and categorical (labels, softmax outputs), binomial and Poisson (counts), exponential (waiting times), Gaussian (noise and averages). Heavy tails and the Cauchy show where the textbook shapes fail.
  • The central limit theorem makes averages Gaussian when the variance is finite; it says nothing when the variance is infinite.
  • P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different. Bayes' rule, posterior ∝ likelihood × prior, converts one into the other, and counting people in a population of 10,000 makes the arithmetic honest. Base rates dominate when the event is rare.
  • The chain rule P(x1, …, xn) = ∏ P(xi | x<i) is how a language model assigns probability to text.
  • Samplers are built from uniform numbers: inverse cdf, Box–Muller, rejection. Sampling a token from a softmax with temperature, top-k or top-p is categorical sampling on modified probabilities. Monte Carlo averages converge at 1/√n, and importance sampling rescues rare events at the price of weight variance.

Check your understanding

A continuous random variable is uniform on the interval from 0 to 0.2. What is its density at a point inside the interval?

  1. 0.2, the width of the interval
  2. 1, because probabilities cannot exceed 1
  3. 5, because the area under the density must equal 1
  4. 0, because the probability of any single value is zero
A uniform density is constant at 1/(width) = 1/0.2 = 5, so that the rectangle of width 0.2 has area 1. The density is probability per unit of x, not a probability, so exceeding 1 is fine. The tempting answer 0 confuses the density at a point with the probability of that exact value, which is indeed 0 for every continuous variable; the density is the height of the curve, whose area gives probabilities.

You average n independent measurements, each with standard deviation 4. Roughly how many measurements make the standard error of the average equal to 0.5?

  1. 64
  2. 8
  3. 32
  4. 16
The standard error is σ/√n, so 4/√n = 0.5 gives √n = 8 and n = 64. The tempting answer 8 forgets the square root: it would be right if error fell as 1/n. Going from the error of one measurement (4) down to 0.5 is a factor of 8, which costs 82 = 64 times the data.

A fraud detector flags 99% of fraudulent transactions and wrongly flags 1% of legitimate ones. Fraud affects 1 in 1,000 transactions. Of the transactions it flags, about what fraction are really fraud?

  1. 99%
  2. 50%
  3. About 25%
  4. About 9%
Per 100,000 transactions: 100 are fraud and 99 of them are flagged; 99,900 are legitimate and 999 of them are flagged. So 99 of 1,098 flags are real, about 9%. The tempting answer 99% reads the detector's sensitivity, P(flag | fraud), as if it were P(fraud | flag). When the event is rare, false alarms from the huge legitimate group swamp the true detections.

You average 1,000,000 draws from a Cauchy distribution and get 0.54. If you average 1,000,000 more, what should you expect?

  1. A value very close to 0.54, since the law of large numbers has settled it
  2. A value that may differ from 0.54 by an amount as large as for a single average of a few draws, because averages of Cauchy draws are Cauchy
  3. A value close to 0, the centre of the distribution, within about 0.001
  4. A value near 0.54 × 2, because the sum doubles
The Cauchy has no finite mean or variance, so neither the law of large numbers nor the central limit theorem applies: the average of n Cauchy draws has the same Cauchy distribution as one draw, however large n is. A new million-draw average is as unpredictable as a single draw. The distribution is symmetric about 0, which tempts the answer "close to 0", but that is its centre, not a limit that averages converge to.

Why does top-p (nucleus) sampling with p = 0.9 sometimes keep 2 tokens and sometimes 200?

  1. Because it keeps a fixed fraction of the vocabulary, which varies with prompt length
  2. Because the temperature is changed at each step to reach 0.9
  3. Because it keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities sum to at least 0.9, and a confident model reaches that with few tokens while an uncertain one needs many
  4. Because it samples 90% of tokens at random and discards the rest
Top-p sorts tokens by probability and keeps just enough of them to cover 90% of the probability mass, then renormalises. A peaked distribution reaches 0.9 almost immediately; a flat one needs many tokens. Top-k, by contrast, keeps a fixed number of tokens regardless of confidence. Temperature is a separate knob applied to the logits beforehand.

To estimate a probability of about 10−6 by Monte Carlo you draw 100,000 samples from the original distribution and see zero hits. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The estimate of 0 is uninformative: with about 0.1 hits expected, you need a different sampler such as importance sampling, or many more draws
  2. The probability is exactly 0, since none occurred
  3. The probability is below 10−6 with certainty
  4. The random seed was unlucky; try seed 1 and average the two runs
At probability 10−6 and 105 draws you expect 0.1 hits, so seeing none is the most likely outcome and says almost nothing about the true value. Concluding that the probability is zero, or certainly below 10−6, over-reads an empty sample. Reseeding does not change the expected count. Drawing from a distribution that makes the event common and re-weighting, as in mc.py, or using far more samples, is what helps.
