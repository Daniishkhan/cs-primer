Statistics for Experiments

You ran an experiment and got a number. How much should you trust it? This chapter gives you the tools to answer: standard errors and confidence intervals, the bootstrap for statistics with no formula, hypothesis tests and p-values, power and sample size, and the two traps that ruin more experiments than any bug: peeking at results as they arrive and testing many things at once.

Why this matters

Nearly every decision in applied AI is made on a measured difference: the new prompt scored 82.0% and the old one 80.4%, the fine-tuned model won 54% of head-to-head comparisons, the ranker change lifted click-through by 0.9%. Each is a sample from a noisy process, and the honest question is whether the difference would survive a re-run. Teams that skip this step ship regressions, tune to noise, and cite leaderboard gaps that are smaller than the evaluation's own error bars. This chapter is the foundation for the statistics of evaluation (A6, chapter 4, which returns to the bootstrap, paired comparisons and clustered questions), for turning models into value with online experiments (A1, chapter 21), for held-out evaluation of systems that search over many variants (A7, chapter 16), and for the take-home work of A8. It uses the standard error of chapter 12 and the likelihood ideas of chapter 13. Studio 1 (Design an Experiment That Can Fail), Lab 5 (A Statistics Toolkit for Experiments) and Case 5 (Peeking at A/B Tests) apply it.

Samples, populations and how much a number wobbles

Statistics begins with a distinction. The population is everything you want to know about: every user who might ever see the new page, every question the model might ever be asked. The sample is the part you actually measured. A number computed from the sample, such as the mean latency or the accuracy, is an estimate of the corresponding population quantity, and because the sample is random, the estimate is itself a random variable: run the experiment again and you get a slightly different number. How different is the central question.

Chapter 12 gave the answer for a mean: the typical wobble is the standard error, σ/√n, where σ is the spread of individual measurements and n the sample size. For a proportion p measured on n trials it is √(p(1 − p)/n). The script makes the idea tangible with a deliberately awkward population: one million simulated latencies from a skewed exponential distribution, mean 100 ms. It draws 5,000 samples of each size n, takes each sample's mean, and measures how those means are spread.

"""The sampling distribution of a mean: take many samples from a skewed population and watch the spread of their means."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
population = rng.exponential(scale=100.0, size=1_000_000)       # a skewed 'latency in ms' population
mu, sigma = population.mean(), population.std()
print(f"population: mean {mu:.1f} ms, std dev {sigma:.1f} ms, median {np.median(population):.1f} ms")
print(f"{'n':>6} {'std dev of 5,000 sample means':>30} {'sigma/sqrt(n)':>14} {'skewness of the means':>22}")
for n in (5, 20, 80, 320, 1280):
    means = rng.choice(population, size=(5_000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    skew = np.mean(((means - means.mean()) / means.std()) ** 3)
    print(f"{n:>6} {means.std():>30.2f} {sigma / np.sqrt(n):>14.2f} {skew:>22.2f}")
print("(the population's own skewness is 2.0; the means become symmetric as n grows)")

The spread of the sample means tracks σ/√n at every size (45.11 against 44.59 at n = 5, 2.77 against 2.79 at n = 1280), even though the individual latencies are heavily skewed. The right-hand column adds the second half of the story, the central limit theorem of chapter 12 in action: the skewness of the means falls from 0.84 at n = 5 to 0 at n = 1280, so the means become symmetric and nearly Gaussian even though one latency is far from Gaussian. This is the licence for the formulas that follow, and the reason their accuracy depends on n: with five observations they are shaky; with a thousand they are very good.

Confidence intervals

A single estimate hides its uncertainty. A confidence interval reports both. For a mean, the usual recipe is

estimate ± 1.96 × standard error,

and it is called a 95% interval because of how the Gaussian curve works: 95% of the time the sample mean falls within 1.96 standard errors of the true mean (chapter 12), so 95% of the intervals built this way contain it. Now the part that people get wrong.

Common misconception

"There is a 95% probability that the true value lies in this interval." Once the interval is computed, the true value is either inside it or not; nothing random is left. The 95% describes the procedure: if you repeated the whole experiment many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the truth. The practical reading that is safe: the interval is the range of values that the data do not rule out, given the assumptions of the method. A narrow interval means the data pin the quantity down; a wide one means they do not.

The figure shows the repeated-experiment reading directly. Of 40 intervals, 2 missed the truth, consistent with 5%. The next script counts the misses over 20,000 repeats, and tests the recipe where it is weakest.

"""What '95%' means: repeat an experiment thousands of times and count how often the interval catches the truth."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
E = 20_000

# (1) Mean of a skewed population (exponential, true mean 100), interval = mean +/- 1.96 standard errors.
for n in (10, 30, 200):
    x = rng.exponential(100.0, size=(E, n))
    m, se = x.mean(axis=1), x.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    covered = np.mean((m - 1.96 * se <= 100) & (100 <= m + 1.96 * se))
    print(f"mean of an exponential, n = {n:>3}: the interval contains the truth in {covered:.1%} of {E:,} experiments")

# (2) A proportion: the simple (Wald) interval versus the Wilson interval, true p = 0.02, n = 100 trials.
p_true, n = 0.02, 100
k = rng.binomial(n, p_true, size=E)
p_hat = k / n
wald_half = 1.96 * np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
cover_wald = np.mean((p_hat - wald_half <= p_true) & (p_true <= p_hat + wald_half))
z = 1.96
centre = (p_hat + z * z / (2 * n)) / (1 + z * z / n)
half = z * np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / (1 + z * z / n)
cover_wilson = np.mean((centre - half <= p_true) & (p_true <= centre + half))
print(f"\nproportion, true p = {p_true}, n = {n}: Wald covers {cover_wald:.1%}, Wilson covers {cover_wilson:.1%}")
print(f"share of experiments that saw zero successes: {np.mean(k == 0):.1%} (the Wald interval is then the single point 0)")

Two cautions come out of that output. First, the recipe is an approximation that needs enough data: for the skewed exponential, the nominal 95% interval catches the truth only 86.7% of the time at n = 10, 91.7% at n = 30, and 94.3% at n = 200. Second, a proportion near 0 or 1 needs a better interval than “p̂ ± 1.96 √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n)” (the Wald interval). With a true rate of 2% and 100 trials, the Wald interval covers only 86.8% of the time, and in 13.1% of experiments it sees no successes at all and reports the single point 0 with no uncertainty. The Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927) fixes this by building the interval from the test statistic rather than plugging in p̂:

Plug in numbers. The script applies the formula to 20 correct out of 20, and to 0 out of 100, printing the pieces named above.

The idea

  1. p̂ ← successes ÷ n; shrink ← 1 + z²/n
  2. centre ← (p̂ + z²/2n) ÷ shrink
  3. half ← z √(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n²) ÷ shrink
  4. interval ← centre ± half, clipped to [0, 1]
  5. run on 20 of 20 and 0 of 100
"""Wilson interval by hand for a proportion:
centre = (p + z^2/2n) / (1 + z^2/n)
half   = z * sqrt(p(1-p)/n + z^2/4n^2) / (1 + z^2/n)"""
import math

Z = 1.96                                 # 95% two-sided


def wilson(successes, n):
    p = successes / n                    # p-hat: the observed proportion
    # ── 1. The shared denominator: 1 + z^2/n ─────────────────────────────
    shrink = 1 + Z ** 2 / n
    # ── 2. Centre: p-hat nudged towards 1/2 by z^2/2n ────────────────────
    centre = (p + Z ** 2 / (2 * n)) / shrink
    # ── 3. Half-width: sampling noise plus a z^2/4n^2 safety term ────────
    noise = p * (1 - p) / n
    extra = Z ** 2 / (4 * n ** 2)
    half = Z * math.sqrt(noise + extra) / shrink
    print(f"{successes}/{n}: p-hat={p:.3f}  1+z^2/n={shrink:.4f}")
    print(f"  centre = {centre:.4f}   noise={noise:.5f}  extra={extra:.5f}")
    print(f"  half-width = {half:.4f}")
    # ── 4. The interval, kept inside [0, 1] ──────────────────────────────
    lo, hi = max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half)
    print(f"  interval = [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")
    return p


# ── 5. 20 out of 20, and 0 out of 100 ────────────────────────────────────
wilson(20, 20)
wilson(0, 100)

# For comparison, the simple Wald interval for 20 out of 20.
wald = Z * math.sqrt(1.0 * 0.0 / 20)
print(f"Wald for 20/20: 1.000 +- {wald:.3f}  (a single point, no doubt)")

  centre = 0.9194   noise=0.00000  extra=0.00240
  half-width = 0.0806
  interval = [0.839, 1.000]
0/100: p-hat=0.000  1+z^2/n=1.0384
  centre = 0.0185   noise=0.00000  extra=0.00010
  half-width = 0.0185
  interval = [0.000, 0.037]

with z = 1.96, and it covers 94.7% here. It is also never outside [0, 1], and it is the right default for accuracy-like numbers (Brown, Cai and DasGupta, 2001, survey the alternatives). When an evaluation reports "100% on 20 examples", a good interval gives the honest view: the Wilson interval for 20 out of 20 runs from about 84% to 100%.

The bootstrap: intervals without formulas

The standard-error formulas exist for means and proportions. What about the median latency, the 90th-percentile latency, an F1 score, or the ratio of two means? Each has a standard error, but deriving it is hard or impossible in closed form. The bootstrap (Efron, 1979, "Bootstrap Methods: Another Look at the Jackknife") replaces the mathematics with computation, using one idea: treat your sample as a stand-in for the population, and re-draw samples from it. The procedure is mechanical:

  1. Take the n observations you have.
  2. Build a resample of the same size n by drawing from them with replacement: some observations appear twice or more, others not at all.
  3. Compute your statistic on the resample.
  4. Repeat thousands of times. The resulting values show how the statistic varies from sample to sample.
  5. Read an interval off them: the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a 95% percentile interval.
Step through

The bootstrap, on eight latencies

  1. One observed sample. Eight latencies in milliseconds, with a median of 112.5. We want a 95% interval for the true median but have no formula.
  2. Resample with replacement. Draw eight values from the eight, each draw picking any of them at random. Values drawn more than once are shown in orange; some originals are missing. This is a plausible “other sample” that the same process could have produced.
  3. Compute the statistic on each resample. Each resample has its own median. They differ from 112.5 and from each other, and that variation is the wobble we want to measure.
  4. Repeat many times. Twenty thousand resamples give a distribution of medians: the bootstrap distribution. It is lumpy here because with only eight values the median can take few distinct values.
  5. Read the interval. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution bound the 95% interval. With n = 8 it is wide (95 to 210 ms), which is the honest answer: eight observations say little about a median.

The next script applies it to 60 simulated latencies, giving intervals for the mean, median and 90th percentile with the same code, 10,000 resamples at a time. It also checks, by repeating the whole experiment, that the interval for the median covers the true value about as often as promised.

"""The bootstrap: a confidence interval for a statistic with no formula (the median and the 90th percentile of latencies)."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sample = rng.lognormal(mean=4.6, sigma=0.7, size=60)            # 60 observed latencies in ms (a heavy-ish right tail)
print(f"observed: n = {len(sample)}, mean {sample.mean():.1f}, median {np.median(sample):.1f}, p90 {np.percentile(sample, 90):.1f} ms")

B = 10_000
idx = rng.integers(0, len(sample), size=(B, len(sample)))       # B resamples, each of size n, with replacement
resamples = sample[idx]
for name, stat in (("mean", lambda a: a.mean(axis=1)), ("median", lambda a: np.median(a, axis=1)), ("p90", lambda a: np.percentile(a, 90, axis=1))):
    boots = stat(resamples)
    lo, hi = np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])
    print(f"  {name:<7} 95% bootstrap interval [{lo:6.1f}, {hi:6.1f}]   (width {hi - lo:5.1f}, bootstrap std error {boots.std():.1f})")
se = sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(sample))
print(f"  formula for the mean: {sample.mean():.1f} +/- 1.96 * {se:.1f} = [{sample.mean() - 1.96 * se:.1f}, {sample.mean() + 1.96 * se:.1f}]")

# Does the percentile interval for the median cover the true median? The truth is exp(4.6).
true_median = np.exp(4.6)
hits, trials = 0, 400
for _ in range(trials):
    s = rng.lognormal(4.6, 0.7, 60)
    b = np.median(s[rng.integers(0, 60, size=(1000, 60))], axis=1)
    lo, hi = np.percentile(b, [2.5, 97.5])
    hits += lo <= true_median <= hi
print(f"coverage of the bootstrap interval for the median over {trials} repeated experiments: {hits / trials:.1%}")
observed: n = 60, mean 103.7, median 93.5, p90 181.9 ms
  mean    95% bootstrap interval [  87.5,  122.6]   (width  35.0, bootstrap std error 8.9)
  median  95% bootstrap interval [  71.2,  104.8]   (width  33.6, bootstrap std error 8.7)
  p90     95% bootstrap interval [ 142.0,  257.5]   (width 115.5, bootstrap std error 30.0)
  formula for the mean: 103.7 +/- 1.96 * 9.0 = [86.1, 121.3]

Look at what each interval says. The mean's bootstrap interval, [87.5, 122.6], agrees with the formula's [86.1, 121.3]: where a formula exists, the bootstrap reproduces it. The median's interval needed no formula at all. And the 90th percentile has an interval about three times as wide as the median's (115.5 against 33.6 ms): tail statistics are estimated from the few extreme observations, so they are inherently noisier, which is why a p99 latency measured over a short test is not worth quoting to the millisecond. The coverage check gave 96.5% over 400 repeats (itself uncertain by about ±2 points), comfortably consistent with the 95% target.

Analogy: photocopying the crowd

You want to know how much a crowd's average height would vary between random groups of 60 people, but you can only measure one group. So you photocopy that group, treat the copies as the whole crowd, and draw new groups of 60 from the copies, again and again. The variation between those redrawn groups stands in for the variation between real groups.

Where it breaks down: the copies contain only the people you already measured. If your group missed the tall tail of the population (a rare slow request, a hard category of questions), no amount of redrawing will find it. The bootstrap cannot supply information the sample never had, and it performs badly for statistics like the maximum and for tiny samples.

The paired bootstrap: comparing two systems on the same questions

The most common use of the bootstrap in AI work is comparing two systems on a shared test set. The key rule: resample the questions, not the answers, so that each question stays attached to both systems' results. Two models tend to find the same questions easy and the same ones hard, so their errors are strongly correlated, and the difference between them is far less noisy than either accuracy alone. Treating the two accuracies as independent throws that away. The script builds an example where model B is a lightly fine-tuned copy of A (it changes the answer on about 7% of questions) and analyses it both ways.

"""Comparing two models on the same 500 questions: why a paired analysis is much tighter than comparing two separate intervals."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 500
difficulty = rng.normal(0, 1.2, n)
a = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(1.6 - difficulty)))).astype(float)
# Model B is a lightly fine-tuned copy of A: it changes the answer on only a few questions.
fix = (a == 0) & (rng.random(n) < 0.20)                            # wrong -> right on 20% of A's mistakes
slip = (a == 1) & (rng.random(n) < 0.02)                           # right -> wrong on 2% of A's successes
b = np.where(fix, 1.0, np.where(slip, 0.0, a))
print(f"model A accuracy {a.mean():.3f}, model B accuracy {b.mean():.3f}, difference {b.mean() - a.mean():+.3f}")
print(f"questions where only A is right: {int(np.sum((a == 1) & (b == 0)))}, only B: {int(np.sum((a == 0) & (b == 1)))}, both right: {int(np.sum((a == 1) & (b == 1)))}, both wrong: {int(np.sum((a == 0) & (b == 0)))}")

# Unpaired: each accuracy has its own binomial standard error; combine as if the models were independent.
se_u = np.sqrt(a.mean() * (1 - a.mean()) / n + b.mean() * (1 - b.mean()) / n)
d = b.mean() - a.mean()
print(f"\nunpaired 95% interval for the difference: [{d - 1.96 * se_u:+.3f}, {d + 1.96 * se_u:+.3f}]   (std error {se_u:.4f})")

# Paired: resample QUESTIONS, keeping each question's (A, B) pair together.
B = 20_000
idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))
boot = b[idx].mean(axis=1) - a[idx].mean(axis=1)
lo, hi = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"paired bootstrap 95% interval            : [{lo:+.3f}, {hi:+.3f}]   (std error {boot.std():.4f})")

# A sign-flip permutation test on the per-question differences.
diff = b - a
obs = diff.mean()
flips = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(B, n)) * diff
p = np.mean(np.abs(flips.mean(axis=1)) >= abs(obs))
print(f"paired permutation test, two-sided p-value: {p:.4f}")
model A accuracy 0.772, model B accuracy 0.806, difference +0.034
questions where only A is right: 9, only B: 26, both right: 377, both wrong: 88

unpaired 95% interval for the difference: [-0.017, +0.085]   (std error 0.0258)
paired bootstrap 95% interval            : [+0.012, +0.058]   (std error 0.0117)
paired permutation test, two-sided p-value: 0.0069

B's 80.6% against A's 77.2% looks like a modest lead on 500 questions. Analysed as two independent accuracies, the interval for the difference is [−1.7, +8.5] points: it includes zero, so the experiment looks inconclusive. Analysed as paired data, the interval is [+1.2, +5.8], standard error 0.0117 instead of 0.0258, and the sign-flip permutation test gives p = 0.0069. The table of agreement shows why: the models agreed on 465 of 500 questions, and only the 35 that differ (26 where only B is right against 9 where only A is) carry any information about the difference. Ignoring the pairing more than doubled the standard error. A6 chapter 4 develops this, with clustered questions and repeated runs.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

A confidence interval answers "how big is the effect?". A hypothesis test answers a narrower, yes-or-no question: "could this result be a fluke?". The reasoning is a proof by contradiction made probabilistic.

  1. State a null hypothesis H0: there is no real difference (the new design converts exactly as well as the old).
  2. Choose a test statistic that measures the departure from H0, such as the difference in conversion rates.
  3. Ask: if H0 were true, how often would chance alone produce a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed? That probability is the p-value.
  4. If the p-value is smaller than a pre-chosen threshold α (conventionally 0.05), call the result statistically significant and reject H0.

Take a concrete test. Control converts at 10.0% and the new variant at 11.2% in truth (we know this because we simulate it); 8,000 users go to each arm. The script analyses the result two ways. The textbook two-proportion z-test computes the observed difference divided by its standard error under H0 (using the pooled rate, since under H0 the arms share one) and reads the p-value from the Gaussian tail. The permutation test needs no formula: if the variant makes no difference, the arm labels are arbitrary, so shuffle them at random many times, recompute the difference each time, and see how often a shuffle is as extreme as reality.

"""An A/B test analysed two ways: the textbook two-proportion z-test and a permutation test that needs no formula."""
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
n = 8_000
control = (rng.random(n) < 0.100).astype(int)               # true conversion 10.0%
treatment = (rng.random(n) < 0.112).astype(int)             # true conversion 11.2%
pa, pb = control.mean(), treatment.mean()
print(f"control {control.sum()}/{n} = {pa:.2%}   treatment {treatment.sum()}/{n} = {pb:.2%}   observed lift {pb - pa:+.2%}")

# Two-proportion z-test (pooled standard error under the null of no difference).
pooled = (control.sum() + treatment.sum()) / (2 * n)
se0 = math.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * 2 / n)
z = (pb - pa) / se0
p_z = math.erfc(abs(z) / math.sqrt(2))                       # two-sided p from the normal tail
print(f"z-test: z = {z:.3f}, two-sided p = {p_z:.4f}")

# Permutation test: if the arms did not matter, labels could be shuffled freely.
pooled_data = np.concatenate([control, treatment])
B = 20_000
diffs = np.empty(B)
for i in range(B):
    rng.shuffle(pooled_data)
    diffs[i] = pooled_data[n:].mean() - pooled_data[:n].mean()
p_perm = np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(pb - pa))
print(f"permutation test ({B:,} shuffles): two-sided p = {p_perm:.4f}")

# A confidence interval for the size of the effect (unpooled standard error).
se = math.sqrt(pa * (1 - pa) / n + pb * (1 - pb) / n)
print(f"95% interval for the lift: [{pb - pa - 1.96 * se:+.2%}, {pb - pa + 1.96 * se:+.2%}]")
control 790/8000 = 9.88%   treatment 864/8000 = 10.80%   observed lift +0.92%
z-test: z = 1.922, two-sided p = 0.0547
permutation test (20,000 shuffles): two-sided p = 0.0559
95% interval for the lift: [-0.02%, +1.87%]

The two tests agree (p = 0.0547 and 0.0559), which is no coincidence: the permutation distribution is the null distribution the z-test approximates with a Gaussian. The observed lift of 0.92 points just misses the conventional threshold, and the interval for the lift, [−0.02, +1.87] points, shows what that means: the data are compatible with no effect and also with an effect nearly twice the observed one. The true lift was 1.2 points; this experiment had a reasonable chance of missing it, as the power section will quantify.

What a p-value is not

The American Statistical Association felt it necessary to issue a statement on p-values (Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016) because they are so often misread. Four corrections:

  • It is not the probability that H0 is true. It is the probability of data this extreme assuming H0, a conditional probability turned the wrong way round, exactly the mistake of confusing P(positive | healthy) with P(healthy | positive) in chapter 12. To get the probability of a hypothesis you need a prior and Bayes' rule.
  • "Not significant" does not mean "no effect". The 0.055 above is weak evidence against H0, not evidence for it. An inconclusive test with a wide interval has not shown that the change is useless.
  • Significant does not mean important. With millions of users a 0.01-point lift can be significant; report the size of the effect and its interval, and compare it with what matters to the business.
  • 0.05 is a convention, not a law. A p-value of 0.049 and one of 0.051 are nearly identical evidence. Fix the threshold before seeing the data, and keep the exact p-value and interval in the report.

Two kinds of error frame the trade-off. A Type I error (false positive) is rejecting H0 when it is true; the threshold α is the long-run rate you accept. A Type II error (false negative) is failing to reject H0 when a real effect exists; its rate is β, and 1 − β is the power, the topic of the next section.

Power and sample size

Power is the probability that an experiment detects an effect of a given size when that effect is real. It depends on four things: the effect size you care about (the minimum detectable effect), the noise in the metric, the sample size, and the significance threshold. The convention is to design for 80% power. The sample size follows from a short argument. Under the alternative hypothesis the observed difference is roughly Gaussian, centred on the true difference δ with standard error se. We reject when the observed difference exceeds zα/2 standard errors under H0, so power is about the probability that a Gaussian centred on δ/se exceeds zα/2. Setting that to 80% needs δ/se = zα/2 + zβ = 1.96 + 0.84. For two proportions p1 and p2 with n users per arm, se2 = (p1(1 − p1) + p2(1 − p2))/n, and solving for n:

n per arm ≈ (zα/2 + zβ)2 · ( p1(1 − p1) + p2(1 − p2) ) / (p2 − p1)2.

The effect size is squared in the denominator, which gives the practical rule: halving the effect you want to detect needs four times the users. The script computes this for a 10% baseline, then checks the formula by brute-force simulation, and finally shows what happens when you run an underpowered test.

"""Power and sample size for a conversion test, checked by simulation, and the winner's curse of underpowered tests."""
import math
import numpy as np

def phi_inv(p):                                              # inverse normal cdf by bisection (standard library only)
    lo, hi = -10.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if 0.5 * math.erfc(-mid / math.sqrt(2)) < p: lo = mid
        else: hi = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

def n_per_arm(p1, p2, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    za, zb = phi_inv(1 - alpha / 2), phi_inv(power)
    var = p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2)
    return (za + zb) ** 2 * var / (p2 - p1) ** 2

p1 = 0.10
print("sample size per arm for 80% power, alpha = 0.05 (two-sided), baseline 10%:")
for lift in (0.03, 0.02, 0.01, 0.005):

print("  halving the effect multiplies n by about 4 (n goes as 1 / effect^2)")

The formula says a 1-point lift over a 10% baseline needs 14,748 users per arm for 80% power, and a simulation of 20,000 experiments at that size rejects 80.4% of the time, with a false-positive rate of 4.9% when there is no effect: the formula works. The cost of underpowered experiments: the winner's curse

The last block of output is the quiet danger. With a true lift of +0.5 points and only 2,000 users per arm, the power is 7.5%: the experiment almost always fails to detect a real effect. But the rare experiments that do reach significance are precisely those where noise happened to inflate the estimate, because only a large observed difference can clear the bar. Among the significant results the average observed lift was +2.31 points, 4.6 times the truth. Across all experiments the average was an accurate +0.49 points; it is the filter “report only the significant ones” that inflates. Gelman and Carlin (2014) call this a Type M (magnitude) error. It is why small studies that "worked" so often fail to replicate, and why a result that barely passes should be believed less than its point estimate suggests. The remedy is to decide the sample size from the effect you care about, before the experiment starts, and to report the interval rather than only the star.

Peeking and the multiple-comparisons trap

Every guarantee above assumes one analysis, planned in advance, of a sample of fixed size. Real experiments are run on dashboards that update every day, and a person watching a dashboard wants to stop as soon as it says something. That breaks the guarantee.

Peeking means checking the p-value repeatedly as data arrive and stopping when it first drops below 0.05. Each check is another chance for noise to cross the line. The z statistic of an experiment with no real effect behaves like a random walk that begins noisy and gradually settles, and a random walk that is allowed to be examined at many times will, sooner or later, wander out of any fixed band.

The script quantifies it. It simulates 100,000 A/A tests per row (both arms identical, 10% conversion, 10,000 users per arm at the planned end) and counts how often the test declares a difference. One look at the end is the honest design; the second column stops at the first of K equally spaced looks with p < 0.05. The second table shows two ways of repairing the design by using a stricter boundary at every look.

"""The peeking trap: simulate thousands of A/A tests (no real difference) and stop at the first look with p < 0.05."""
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
E = 100_000                                 # simulated experiments per row
N = 10_000                                  # users per arm at the planned end
p = 0.10

def phi_inv(q):
    lo, hi = -10.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if 0.5 * math.erfc(-mid / math.sqrt(2)) < q: lo = mid
        else: hi = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

def z_paths(looks):
    """z statistics at `looks` equally spaced looks, for E experiments with no true difference."""
    block = N // looks
    a = rng.binomial(block, p, size=(E, looks)).cumsum(axis=1)
    b = rng.binomial(block, p, size=(E, looks)).cumsum(axis=1)
    n = block * np.arange(1, looks + 1)
    pa, pb = a / n, b / n
    pooled = (pa + pb) / 2
    return (pb - pa) / np.sqrt(2 * pooled * (1 - pooled) / n)

print(f"{'looks':>5} {'one look at the end':>20} {'stop at first p<0.05':>21}   (nominal false-positive rate 5%)")
for looks in (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50):
    z = z_paths(looks)
    end_only = np.mean(np.abs(z[:, -1]) > 1.96)
    anytime = np.mean((np.abs(z) > 1.96).any(axis=1))
    print(f"{looks:>5} {end_only:>20.1%} {anytime:>21.1%}")

# Repairs: a stricter boundary used at every look. Bonferroni divides alpha by the number of looks;
# a Pocock-style constant boundary is the value c with 5% total false positives (found here by bisection).
print(f"\n{'looks':>5} {'Bonferroni z':>13} {'false pos.':>11} {'Pocock-style z':>15} {'false pos.':>11}")
for looks in (2, 5, 10, 20):
    z = np.abs(z_paths(looks))
    zb = phi_inv(1 - 0.05 / (2 * looks))
    lo, hi = 1.96, 4.0
    for _ in range(25):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if np.mean((z > mid).any(axis=1)) > 0.05: lo = mid
        else: hi = mid
    zp = (lo + hi) / 2
    print(f"{looks:>5} {zb:>13.3f} {np.mean((z > zb).any(axis=1)):>11.1%} {zp:>15.3f} {np.mean((z > zp).any(axis=1)):>11.1%}")

With a single look at the end the false-positive rate is the promised 5% at every K (the first column does not change). Peeking raises it to 8.3% with 2 looks, 14.0% with 5, 19.2% with 10, 24.9% with 20 and 31.9% with 50: an experiment with no effect has a one-in-five chance of producing a "win" if you check ten times. These numbers agree with the classic calculation of Armitage, McPherson and Rowe (1969), which first quantified the problem. The remedies in the second table belong to group sequential testing: use a stricter threshold at each look so that the total false-positive rate stays at 5%. The Bonferroni choice (divide α by the number of looks) is valid but wasteful, keeping the rate at only 2.5% for 10 looks; a constant boundary calibrated so that the total is exactly 5% (the idea of Pocock, 1977; the simulated values 2.17, 2.42 and 2.55 for 2, 5 and 10 looks agree with his published 2.178, 2.413 and 2.555 within simulation noise) is tighter. In practice, designs also spend their error budget unevenly: the O’Brien–Fleming boundaries (1979) are very strict at early looks and close to 1.96 at the end, and Lan and DeMets (1983) generalised this to flexible looks with an “alpha-spending function”.

Modern experimentation platforms often use always-valid inference, in which p-values or intervals remain correct no matter when you look (Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, 2017, "Peeking at A/B Tests: Why It Matters, and What to Do About It"). The price is somewhat lower power at any fixed sample size, in exchange for the freedom to stop early honestly. The widget lets you feel the problem and the cures. It simulates 800 A/B tests at a time with a 10% conversion rate in both arms (a lift of 0%), checks each at the number of looks you choose, and compares four stopping rules on identical data. Slide the true lift above zero to see how much power each rule keeps.

Many metrics at once

The same arithmetic bites when you test many things at one look: ten metrics, five user segments, twenty prompt variants. Each test at the 5% level has a 5% false-positive chance, and the chances accumulate. With 20 independent tests and no effect anywhere, the probability of at least one "significant" result is 1 − 0.9520 = 64%. This is the multiple-comparisons problem, and "we found that the change helped for mobile users in Germany" is its famous symptom: slice the data enough and something will light up. The script confirms the formula by simulation and applies two standard corrections.

"""Many metrics, one experiment: with 20 metrics and no true effect anywhere, how often does at least one look 'significant'?"""
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
E, M = 10_000, 20                                   # experiments, metrics per experiment
# Under the null each metric's difference is a standard normal z-score; convert to two-sided p-values.
z = rng.standard_normal((E, M))
p = np.array([[math.erfc(abs(v) / math.sqrt(2)) for v in row] for row in z])

print(f"P(at least one of {M} metrics has p < 0.05), formula 1 - 0.95^{M} = {1 - 0.95 ** M:.1%}")
print(f"simulated: {np.mean((p < 0.05).any(axis=1)):.1%}")
print(f"average number of 'significant' metrics per experiment: {np.mean((p < 0.05).sum(axis=1)):.2f} (expected {M * 0.05:.2f})")

# Bonferroni: test each metric at 0.05 / M.
print(f"Bonferroni (threshold {0.05 / M:.4f}): at least one significant in {np.mean((p < 0.05 / M).any(axis=1)):.1%} of experiments")

# Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate: among declared discoveries, the share that are false.
# Here: 18 null metrics and 2 with a real effect (z-shift of 3.5).
shift = np.zeros(M); shift[:2] = 3.5
zz = z + shift
pp = np.array([[math.erfc(abs(v) / math.sqrt(2)) for v in row] for row in zz])
fdp, found, true_found = [], 0, 0
for row in pp:
    order = np.argsort(row)
    k = np.nonzero(row[order] <= 0.05 * (np.arange(1, M + 1) / M))[0]
    if len(k):
        rejected = order[: k.max() + 1]
        fdp.append(np.mean(rejected >= 2)); found += len(rejected); true_found += np.sum(rejected < 2)
    else:
        fdp.append(0.0)
print(f"Benjamini-Hochberg at q = 0.05, 2 real effects among 20: found {true_found / E:.2f} of 2 real on average; false share of discoveries {np.mean(fdp):.1%}")

average number of 'significant' metrics per experiment: 0.98 (expected 1.00)

The 64.2% from the formula is reproduced (64.0% simulated), and an average of 0.98 metrics per experiment look significant by chance alone. Bonferroni (test each at α/M = 0.0025) brings the chance of any false alarm back to 4.8%, at the price of lower power per test. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure controls something different, the false discovery rate: among the metrics you declare significant, the expected share that are false. With two real effects hidden among 20 metrics it finds 1.49 of the 2 on average while only 4.4% of its discoveries are false, a better trade when you are searching for effects than when you must guard a single decision. The discipline that matters more than either correction is the cheapest one: name the primary metric and the decision rule before the experiment starts, treat everything else as exploratory, and write down how many things you looked at.

Experiment hygiene

Good statistics cannot rescue a broken experiment. A few checks catch most of the problems that lie upstream of the maths.

Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)The arms are not the size you intended, so assignment or logging is broken, and the comparison is biased in unknown waysCompare the observed split with the planned one by a chi-square test before looking at any metric; investigate if it fails
Randomisation unitIf users are randomised but you analyse sessions or requests, observations are not independent and the standard error is too smallAnalyse at the level you randomised, or use cluster-robust methods (resample users, not rows)

The SRM check is cheap enough to automate, and it deserves a strict threshold since it should almost never fire by chance. The script applies a chi-square test to four 50/50 splits.

"""Sample ratio mismatch: check that a 50/50 split really was 50/50 before looking at any metric."""
import math

def chi2_p(observed, expected):
    stat = sum((o - e) ** 2 / e for o, e in zip(observed, expected))
    return stat, math.erfc(math.sqrt(stat / 2))        # p-value for chi-square with 1 degree of freedom

for a, b in ((50_000, 50_000), (50_200, 49_800), (50_900, 49_100), (51_500, 48_500)):
    total = a + b
    stat, p = chi2_p((a, b), (total / 2, total / 2))
    verdict = "ok" if p > 0.001 else "INVESTIGATE: do not trust the results"
    print(f"control {a:>6,} vs treatment {b:>6,}: ratio {a / b:.4f}  chi-square {stat:6.2f}  p = {p:.2e}  {verdict}")
control 50,000 vs treatment 50,000: ratio 1.0000  chi-square   0.00  p = 1.00e+00  ok
control 50,200 vs treatment 49,800: ratio 1.0080  chi-square   1.60  p = 2.06e-01  ok
control 50,900 vs treatment 49,100: ratio 1.0367  chi-square  32.40  p = 1.25e-08  INVESTIGATE: do not trust the results
control 51,500 vs treatment 48,500: ratio 1.0619  chi-square  90.00  p = 2.38e-21  INVESTIGATE: do not trust the results

A split of 50,200 against 49,800 (ratio 1.008) is plausible chance: p = 0.21. A split of 50,900 against 49,100, a ratio of only 1.037, has p = 1.25 × 10−8: something systematic, such as a bot filter dropping events in one arm or a redirect failing, removed users unevenly, and nothing measured afterwards can be trusted. Practitioners commonly use a strict threshold such as 0.001 for this particular alarm, so that it rarely fires on a healthy experiment.

Statistics for evaluating models

An evaluation of a model on a fixed test set is an experiment like any other. The accuracy on n questions is a proportion, with standard error √(p(1 − p)/n), and everything above applies. The script tabulates the 95% half-width for common test-set sizes and measures how often two equally good models look different.

Several further traps live in A6 chapter 4: questions that come in clusters (many questions about the same document), LLM-judge noise stacked on top, run-to-run variation from sampling at temperature above 0, and the difference between seeds and test items as sources of variance. Systems connection

The same statistics run inside your infrastructure. A canary release compares an error rate or latency percentile in the canary against the baseline and must decide, on a short sample, whether a difference is real: that is a hypothesis test with a power problem, and automated canary analysis is a sequential test. Burn-rate alerts on an error budget are another: a multi-window rule exists to keep the false-alarm rate down while still catching a real burn quickly, the same trade-off between looking often and being wrong often. Load tests report percentiles with noise that the bootstrap quantifies. When a dashboard shows a metric "up 3%" after a deploy, the first question is what its error bar is.

Key takeaways

  • An estimate from a sample wobbles by its standard error, σ/√n for a mean and √(p(1 − p)/n) for a proportion. Four times the data halves the error.
  • A 95% confidence interval is a claim about the method: such intervals contain the truth in 95% of repeated experiments. Use Wilson, not Wald, for proportions near 0 or 1, and treat normal-theory intervals on small, skewed samples with suspicion.
  • The bootstrap resamples the data with replacement to get an interval for any statistic, with no formula. Resample the unit that is independent, and for comparing two systems, resample questions and keep them paired: the paired interval can be much tighter than two separate ones.
  • A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null is true, "not significant" is not "no effect", and significant is not important. Report the effect size and interval.
  • Power is the chance of detecting a real effect. The sample size scales as 1/effect2, so a half-size effect needs four times the users. Underpowered tests miss real effects and exaggerate the ones they catch (the winner's curse).
  • Peeking at a running test and stopping at the first p < 0.05 inflates false positives to about 19% with ten looks. Fix the sample size in advance or use a sequential method (stricter boundaries, alpha spending, always-valid inference).
  • Testing many metrics multiplies the false-positive chances (64% with 20 at the 5% level). Name the primary metric first, correct for the rest (Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg), check for sample ratio mismatch, and treat leaderboard gaps on a few hundred items as noise unless the comparison is paired.

Check your understanding

A model scores 78% on a test set and the 95% confidence interval is [74%, 82%]. Which statement is the most accurate reading?

  1. There is a 95% probability that the model's true accuracy lies between 74% and 82%
  2. If the evaluation were repeated many times on fresh samples, about 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true accuracy; values from 74% to 82% are compatible with the data
  3. 95% of future test questions will be answered with an accuracy between 74% and 82%
  4. The model's accuracy is 78% with a 5% chance of being wrong
The 95% belongs to the procedure, not to this particular interval: the true accuracy is a fixed number that this interval either contains or does not. The first option is the most common misreading. The third confuses an interval for a parameter with a range for individual outcomes, and the fourth invents a "5% chance of being wrong" that the interval does not say.

In a bootstrap confidence interval for a median, what exactly is resampled?

  1. New data collected from the population, so that the experiment is repeated for real
  2. The observed data without replacement, shuffled into a different order
  3. The test statistic, by adding random noise to the observed median
  4. The observed data, drawn with replacement to the same size, with the statistic recomputed on each resample
The bootstrap treats the observed sample as a stand-in for the population and draws new samples of the same size with replacement, recomputing the statistic each time. Shuffling without replacement reproduces the same set of numbers (and the same median) every time, so it measures nothing. Collecting new data would be a real replication, which is what the bootstrap approximates when you cannot afford one. Adding noise to the statistic is not the method.

An A/B test returns p = 0.03. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. If there were truly no difference, data this extreme or more would occur about 3% of the time; this is evidence against the null, not a 97% chance that the variant is better
  2. There is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true
  3. The variant is better with 97% probability
  4. The effect is large enough to matter commercially
A p-value is a probability about the data given the null, not a probability about the hypothesis. Options two and three turn the conditional round, the same error as confusing the false-alarm rate with the share of alarms that are false. And statistical significance says nothing about the size of the effect: check the interval and compare it with what matters.

  1. About 8,000
  2. About 2,000
  3. About 16,000
  4. About 4,000, since only the threshold changes
The required sample size scales with 1/(effect)2. Halving the effect from 2 points to 1 multiplies it by about 4, so roughly 16,000 per arm (the exact figure also shifts slightly because the variance depends on the rates). Doubling to 8,000 is the tempting linear guess; the sample size is not linear in the effect because the standard error only falls with the square root of n.

A team checks its A/B dashboard every day for 10 days and ships as soon as the p-value dips below 0.05. There is no real difference between the arms. About how often will they ship?

  1. 5% of the time, because that is the threshold
  2. Roughly 19% of the time, since each look is another chance for noise to cross the line
  3. About 50%, since the p-value will eventually cross 0.05 in half of experiments
  4. Never, because there is no real difference
The 5% guarantee holds only for a single, pre-planned analysis. The simulation in the chapter gives 19.2% for ten equally spaced looks (and 14% for five, 25% for twenty). The figure grows with the number of looks but does not reach 50% for ten. "Never" ignores that chance variation alone produces significant-looking results.

Model A scores 82.0% and model B 84.0% on the same 500 questions. The two intervals (±3.4 points each) overlap heavily. What is the best next step?

  1. Conclude that B is not better because the intervals overlap
  2. Conclude that B is better because 84 is greater than 82
  3. Collect 500 new questions for B only and compare its two scores
  4. Analyse the per-question differences on the shared questions (a paired bootstrap or test), because overlapping separate intervals do not settle whether the difference is real
Both models answered the same questions, so their errors are correlated and the difference between them is less noisy than either accuracy; the paired interval for the difference can exclude zero even when the two separate intervals overlap (as in the chapter's example), or include it when you hoped otherwise. Overlap is a conservative, unpaired shortcut, and "84 > 82" ignores noise altogether. Testing B on fresh questions alone would not give a comparison with A.
