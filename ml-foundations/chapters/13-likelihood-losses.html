Maximum Likelihood & Loss Functions

Squared error for regression, cross-entropy for classification and next-token prediction: they look like unrelated design choices, but they are one recipe applied to different probability distributions. This chapter derives that recipe, shows what each loss quietly assumes about noise, and explains where regularisation comes from.

Why this matters

Every model in this path is trained by minimising a loss, and the loss decides what "good" means. Pick squared error for data with occasional wild values and one bad record drags the whole fit. Train a classifier with the wrong loss and confident mistakes stop producing gradients. Misread the number a language model reports and you will not know whether a loss of 2.3 is excellent or hopeless. Once you see that each loss is the negative log of a probability, these choices stop being folklore: you pick the loss by asking what distribution the labels really follow. This chapter underpins linear and logistic regression (A1, chapter 5), regularisation (A1, chapter 6), the language-modelling objective (A2, chapter 14), supervised fine-tuning (A3, chapter 10) and the reward-model and preference losses of RLHF and DPO (A3, chapter 12). It uses the probability of chapter 12 and the stable numerics of chapter 11, and its gradients are the ones chapter 9 computes. Lab 4 (Sampling, Likelihood & Entropy) has you implement several of these losses and check them.

A model that predicts a distribution

The usual picture of a model is a function: put x in, get a prediction out. The picture that makes losses make sense is slightly different. A probabilistic model does not claim that y equals f(x); it claims that y is drawn from a distribution whose parameters depend on x. For a house price, the network outputs a mean μ(x), and the claim is "the price is around μ(x), give or take some noise". For a spam filter, the network outputs a number from which we build the probability that the email is spam. For a language model, the network outputs a score for every token in the vocabulary, and the softmax turns them into a distribution over the next token. In every case the model assigns a probability p(y | x; w) to each possible label, where w are the weights.

Given this, "how good are the weights?" has a natural answer: weights are good if they assign high probability to the labels we actually observed. The rest of the chapter is the consequences of taking that sentence seriously.

Likelihood

Start with the smallest example. A coin has an unknown probability θ of heads. We flip it 10 times and see 7 heads. For any proposed θ, we can compute the probability of exactly this data. If flips are independent, probabilities multiply: each head contributes θ, each tail 1 − θ, so

L(θ) = θ7 (1 − θ)3.

This function of θ is the likelihood. Notice the reversal of roles: in chapter 12 the parameter was fixed and the data varied (what will the coin do?); here the data are fixed and the parameter varies (which coin would have made this?). The maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) is the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable: θ̂ = arg max L(θ). Plot L and the winner is at 0.7; calculus confirms it, and the script does both.

"""Maximum likelihood for a coin: the likelihood curve, the log trick and why products underflow."""
import math

heads, n = 7, 10

def likelihood(theta, h, n):
    return theta ** h * (1 - theta) ** (n - h)

grid = [i / 100 for i in range(1, 100)]
best = max(grid, key=lambda t: likelihood(t, heads, n))
print(f"data: {heads} heads in {n} flips")
print(f"best theta on a 0.01 grid = {best}  (calculus: d/dtheta [h ln t + (n-h) ln(1-t)] = h/t - (n-h)/(1-t) = 0  ->  t = h/n = {heads / n})")
for t in (0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9):
    print(f"  L({t}) = {likelihood(t, heads, n):.6f}")
# More data makes the curve much narrower: how much less likely is theta = 0.6 than the best?
for h, m in ((7, 10), (70, 100), (700, 1000)):
    ratio = likelihood(0.6, h, m) / likelihood(h / m, h, m)
    print(f"  {h:>3} heads in {m:>4}: L(0.6) / L(best) = {ratio:.3e}")

# Why we work with logs: the likelihood of 1,500 flips with 1,050 heads underflows.
h, m = 1050, 1500
print("\nnaive product at theta = 0.7:", 0.7 ** h * 0.3 ** (m - h))
p = 1.0
for i in range(m):
    p *= 0.7 if i < h else 0.3
print("running product             :", p)
loglik = h * math.log(0.7) + (m - h) * math.log(0.3)
print(f"log-likelihood              : {loglik:.2f}  (finite, and its argmax is the same because log is increasing)")

Setting the derivative of ln L to zero gave θ̂ = h/n: the MLE of a probability is the observed frequency. That agrees with common sense, which is reassuring, but the final lines of the output show something that common sense does not tell you: how sharply the data speak. With 10 flips, θ = 0.6 is 81% as likely as the best value, so the data hardly distinguish them. With 100 flips it is 12% as likely; with 1,000 flips, one in 2.4 billion. Common misconception

"The likelihood L(θ) is the probability that the parameter equals θ." It is not. L(θ) is the probability of the data if the parameter were θ. Summed or integrated over θ, the likelihood does not add up to 1 and generally has no meaning as a probability of θ. To get a probability for the parameter you need a prior and Bayes' rule (chapter 12), which is where the section on MAP estimation below comes in.

Analogy: ranking suspects by how well each explains the evidence

A detective with a set of footprints asks of each suspect: if this person had walked here, how likely is it that we would find these prints? The suspect who makes the evidence least surprising ranks first. That is maximum likelihood: rank hypotheses by the probability they gave to what actually happened.

Where it breaks down: the detective also knows that one suspect was abroad and another is a frequent visitor. Likelihood ignores such background plausibility, so a wildly implausible hypothesis that happens to fit the data perfectly can win. Bringing the background knowledge in is the job of the prior.

Why we work with logs

With many examples the likelihood is a product of many probabilities, each below 1. The script ends on a worked failure: 1,500 flips at θ = 0.7 give a likelihood near 10−400, which is smaller than the smallest positive double-precision number (about 10−308), so the computer reports exactly 0.0 and every θ ties. The remedy is to take logarithms. Since the logarithm is strictly increasing, the θ that maximises L also maximises ln L, and ln turns the product into a sum:

ln L(w) = ∑i=1n ln p(yi | xi; w).

The sum is well-behaved numerically (here −916.30), its gradient is a sum of per-example gradients (so minibatching works, chapter 10), and it is what every framework computes. By convention we minimise rather than maximise, so the training loss is the negative log-likelihood (NLL), usually averaged over examples:

Plug in numbers. Three examples with three classes each. The model's raw scores go through softmax to give probabilities, and each line below is one step above.

The idea

  1. 3 examples: raw scores (logits) per class, and the true class
  2. p_true ← softmax(scores)[true class]
  3. logs ← ln of each p_true; total ← their sum
  4. loss ← −total ÷ n
  5. compare: always guessing 1/3, and always right
"""Negative log-likelihood by hand: loss = -(1/n) * sum_i ln p(y_i | x_i).
Three examples, three classes. The model gives raw scores (logits); softmax
turns them into probabilities."""
import math

# ── 1. Three examples: logits from the model, and the true class ─────────
LOGITS = [[2.0, 1.0, 0.1],               # example 1
          [0.5, 2.5, 0.0],               # example 2
          [1.0, 0.0, 0.0]]               # example 3
TRUE = [0, 1, 2]                         # index of the correct class each


def softmax(z):
    """exp of each score, divided by the total: positive, sums to 1."""
    e = [math.exp(a) for a in z]
    return [a / sum(e) for a in e]


# ── 2. p(y_i | x_i; w): the probability given to the right answer ────────
p_true = []
for z, t in zip(LOGITS, TRUE):
    probs = softmax(z)
    p_true.append(probs[t])
    print(f"probs = {[round(a, 3) for a in probs]}  true class {t}  "
          f"p = {probs[t]:.4f}")

# ── 3. ln of each, then sum over the examples ────────────────────────────
logs = [math.log(p) for p in p_true]
print("ln p     =", [round(a, 4) for a in logs])
total = sum(logs)
print(f"sum ln p = {total:.4f}   (this is ln L, the log-likelihood)")

# ── 4. Average, and flip the sign: the loss ──────────────────────────────
n = len(logs)
loss = -total / n
print(f"loss     = -(1/{n}) * ({total:.4f}) = {loss:.4f}")

# ── 5. Reference points ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
print(f"guessing 1/3 every time: loss = {-math.log(1 / 3):.4f}")
print(f"always right (p = 1):    loss = {-math.log(1.0) + 0.0:.4f}")
probs = [0.659, 0.242, 0.099]  true class 0  p = 0.6590
probs = [0.111, 0.821, 0.067]  true class 1  p = 0.8214
probs = [0.576, 0.212, 0.212]  true class 2  p = 0.2119
ln p     = [-0.417, -0.1967, -1.5514]
sum ln p = -2.1652   (this is ln L, the log-likelihood)
loss     = -(1/3) * (-2.1652) = 0.7217
guessing 1/3 every time: loss = 1.0986
always right (p = 1):    loss = 0.0000

Gaussian noise gives squared error

Suppose the labels are the model's prediction plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ: y = f(x; w) + ε with ε ~ N(0, σ2). Then p(y | x; w) = (2πσ2)−1/2 exp(−(y − f)2 / (2σ2)), and its negative log is

−ln p(y | x; w) = ½ ln(2πσ2) + (y − f(x; w))2 / (2σ2).

Sum over the data. The first term does not involve w, so for a fixed σ minimising the NLL over w means minimising ∑(yi − f(xi; w))2, the sum of squared errors. Least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. The factor 1/(2σ2) rescales the gradient but does not move the minimum, which is why you can fix σ = 1 in practice. And if you let σ be learned too, setting the derivative of the NLL with respect to σ to zero gives σ̂2 = (1/n) ∑ (yi − fi)2: the MLE of the noise variance is the mean squared residual. The script checks both claims on 200 noisy points from the line y = 2x + 1, fitting the slope, intercept and a free σ by gradient descent on the NLL and comparing with closed-form least squares.

"""Gaussian noise gives squared error: fit a line by least squares and by minimising the negative log-likelihood, with sigma learned too."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(13)
n = 200
x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, n)
y = 2.0 * x + 1.0 + rng.normal(0, 0.7, n)          # true line y = 2x + 1, noise sd 0.7
X = np.column_stack([x, np.ones(n)])

# (a) least squares in closed form (normal equations)
w_ls = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
resid = y - X @ w_ls
print(f"least squares      : slope {w_ls[0]:.4f}, intercept {w_ls[1]:.4f}")
print(f"rms residual       : {np.sqrt(np.mean(resid ** 2)):.4f}")

# (b) gradient descent on the negative log-likelihood, learning (slope, intercept, log sigma)
def nll(params):
    w, b, log_s = params
    s2 = np.exp(2 * log_s)
    r = y - (w * x + b)
    return np.sum(0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * s2) + r ** 2 / (2 * s2))

def num_grad(f, p, eps=1e-6):
    g = np.zeros_like(p)
    for i in range(len(p)):
        d = np.zeros_like(p); d[i] = eps
        g[i] = (f(p + d) - f(p - d)) / (2 * eps)
    return g

p = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0])
for step in range(3000):
    p -= 0.002 * num_grad(nll, p)
print(f"NLL minimiser      : slope {p[0]:.4f}, intercept {p[1]:.4f}, sigma {np.exp(p[2]):.4f}")
print(f"NLL at the optimum : {nll(p):.3f}")

# The gradient of the NLL in w is the gradient of the sum of squares divided by sigma^2.
s2 = np.exp(2 * p[2])
g_nll = num_grad(nll, np.array([1.5, 0.5, p[2]]))[:2]
g_sse = X.T @ (X @ np.array([1.5, 0.5]) - y)
print("gradient of NLL at (1.5, 0.5):", np.round(g_nll, 3))
print("gradient of SSE / sigma^2    :", np.round(g_sse / s2, 3))
least squares      : slope 1.9951, intercept 0.9432
rms residual       : 0.7249
NLL minimiser      : slope 1.9951, intercept 0.9432, sigma 0.7249
NLL at the optimum : 219.433
gradient of NLL at (1.5, 0.5): [-267.499 -192.323]
gradient of SSE / sigma^2    : [-267.499 -192.323]

Both routes land on slope 1.9951 and intercept 0.9432, and the learned σ is 0.7249, exactly the root-mean-square residual printed above it. The last two lines show the gradient identity directly: the NLL's gradient with respect to the weights equals the sum-of-squares gradient divided by σ2, to every digit shown. (The true noise standard deviation was 0.7; 0.7249 is what 200 samples gave.)

MLE is not always unbiased

One caution about σ̂2. The mean squared residual divides by n, and the maximum-likelihood estimate of a Gaussian variance is systematically too small, because the residuals are measured from a mean that was itself fitted to the same data, and so they are artificially tight. The correction is to divide by n − 1 instead (Bessel's correction, which NumPy exposes as ddof=1). The size of the effect is a clean factor (n − 1)/n:

"""The maximum-likelihood estimate of a variance is biased low: divide by n and you under-estimate; divide by n-1 and the bias disappears."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
true_var = 4.0
for n in (3, 5, 10, 50):
    data = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(true_var), size=(200_000, n))
    mle = data.var(axis=1, ddof=0)           # divides by n: the Gaussian MLE
    unbiased = data.var(axis=1, ddof=1)      # divides by n - 1
    print(f"n = {n:>2}: mean of MLE {mle.mean():.3f}   mean of n-1 version {unbiased.mean():.3f}   (true {true_var})   MLE factor (n-1)/n = {(n - 1) / n:.3f}")
n =  3: mean of MLE 2.662   mean of n-1 version 3.993   (true 4.0)   MLE factor (n-1)/n = 0.667
n =  5: mean of MLE 3.199   mean of n-1 version 3.999   (true 4.0)   MLE factor (n-1)/n = 0.800
n = 10: mean of MLE 3.600   mean of n-1 version 4.000   (true 4.0)   MLE factor (n-1)/n = 0.900
n = 50: mean of MLE 3.920   mean of n-1 version 4.000   (true 4.0)   MLE factor (n-1)/n = 0.980

For n = 3 the MLE averages 2.66 against a true 4.0 (a factor 0.667 low); by n = 50 it is within 2%. In machine learning n is usually large, so this rarely bites a weight estimate, but it matters in small-sample statistics (chapter 15) and as a reminder that "maximum likelihood" promises good behaviour as n grows, not perfection at every n.

Other noise, other losses

If squared error is the Gaussian assumption, what do other assumptions give? The recipe is mechanical: write the density, take −ln, drop constants.

  • Laplace noise (density proportional to exp(−|r|/b), more mass near zero and in the tails than a Gaussian): −ln p gives |y − f|, the absolute error. The MLE of the centre of a sample is its median, not its mean.
  • Student-t noise (heavy tails controlled by a parameter ν, the Cauchy of chapter 12 when ν = 1): −ln p gives ((ν + 1)/2) ln(1 + r2/ν), a loss that grows only logarithmically for large residuals, so wild outliers lose their power.
  • Huber loss is quadratic for small residuals and linear beyond a threshold δ. It is not exactly the likelihood of a textbook distribution (it is the likelihood of noise that is Gaussian in the middle and Laplace in the tails), but it is the standard practical compromise, introduced by Peter Huber in 1964.

The right-hand panel is the most informative one. The slope of the loss at a point is how hard that point pulls the fit. Under squared error the pull grows without limit with the residual, so one point a hundred units away pulls a hundred times harder than a typical one. Under absolute error every point pulls with the same unit force, the definition of a median. The script finds the best location for 31 measurements around 10 (clean), and then again after adding three wild values (25, 27 and 30), under each loss. The grid search is crude but exact enough for one parameter.

"""Different noise models, different best estimates: location of a sample with and without outliers."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
clean = rng.normal(10.0, 1.0, 31)
dirty = np.concatenate([clean, [25.0, 27.0, 30.0]])     # three wild measurements

def argmin_grid(f, data, lo=0.0, hi=40.0, steps=8001):
    grid = np.linspace(lo, hi, steps)                    # a grid search is plenty for one parameter
    return grid[np.argmin([f(m, data) for m in grid])]

def sq(m, d):   return np.sum((d - m) ** 2) / 2                                  # Gaussian NLL, up to constants
def ab(m, d):   return np.sum(np.abs(d - m))                                      # Laplace NLL, up to constants
def huber(m, d, delta=1.0):
    r = np.abs(d - m)
    return np.sum(np.where(r <= delta, 0.5 * r ** 2, delta * (r - 0.5 * delta)))
def student(m, d, nu=3.0):                                                        # Student-t NLL (scale 1), up to constants
    return np.sum((nu + 1) / 2 * np.log1p((d - m) ** 2 / nu))

print(f"{'loss (noise model)':<24} {'clean':>8} {'with 3 outliers':>16}")
for name, f in (("squared (Gaussian)", sq), ("absolute (Laplace)", ab), ("Huber (hybrid)", huber), ("Student-t, nu = 3", student)):
    print(f"{name:<24} {argmin_grid(f, clean):>8.3f} {argmin_grid(f, dirty):>16.3f}")
print(f"{'mean':<24} {clean.mean():>8.3f} {dirty.mean():>16.3f}")
print(f"{'median':<24} {np.median(clean):>8.3f} {np.median(dirty):>16.3f}")
loss (noise model)          clean  with 3 outliers

Three outliers out of 34 points move the squared-error answer from 9.85 to 11.39 (the mean has no defence) and barely move the others (absolute: 9.89 to 9.995, which is the median in both cases; Student-t: 9.84 to 9.87). Which behaviour is right depends on what the outliers are. If they are measurement glitches, the robust losses are right. If they are real events that you must not ignore (a genuine fraud, a genuine spike in load), the mean's sensitivity is a feature. The loss is a statement about the world, and you can test the statement by looking at the residuals.

Bernoulli and categorical labels give cross-entropy

Now the labels are not numbers with noise but yes/no answers or classes. A model that outputs a logit z (any real number) for a binary label turns it into a probability with the sigmoid p = σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z) and claims y ~ Bernoulli(p): the label is 1 with probability p and 0 with probability 1 − p. The likelihood of one label is py (1 − p)1−y, so

−ln p(y | x) = −[ y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p) ].

This is binary cross-entropy. For K classes, the network outputs K logits z and the softmax, pk = ezk / ∑j ezj, gives a categorical distribution; if the true class is t, the likelihood is pt and the loss is −ln pt = −zt + ln ∑j ezj. This is cross-entropy loss (chapter 14 explains the name). It looks only at the probability given to the correct class: the loss is 0 when that probability is 1 and grows without bound as it approaches 0, so a model is punished far more for being confident and wrong than for being unsure.

Differentiating, the logit gradients are strikingly simple. For the softmax form, ∂L/∂zi = −1[i = t] + ezi/∑j ezj, that is

∇z L = p − et = (predicted probabilities) − (one-hot target).

The binary case is the same result: with p = σ(z) and σ′(z) = p(1 − p), the chain rule gives (−y/p + (1 − y)/(1 − p)) p(1 − p) = p − y. The gradient is the prediction error, with no extra factors. This is the "p − y" that appears at the start of every backpropagation derivation for classifiers (A2, chapter 2). The script verifies both gradients against finite differences (the check from chapter 8) and then shows why the loss is always computed from logits rather than from probabilities.

"""Cross-entropy from logits: the gradient is (predicted - observed), checked against finite differences, plus the stable log-softmax."""
import math
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z): return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))

# Binary case. Loss = -[y ln p + (1-y) ln(1-p)] with p = sigmoid(z).
def bce(z, y):
    p = sigmoid(z)
    return -(y * math.log(p) + (1 - y) * math.log(1 - p))

z, y, eps = 0.8, 1, 1e-6
numeric = (bce(z + eps, y) - bce(z - eps, y)) / (2 * eps)
print(f"binary:  dL/dz numeric {numeric:.6f}   p - y = {sigmoid(z) - y:.6f}")

# Multiclass case. Loss = -log softmax(z)[target].
def log_softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max()                                  # stability: shift by the max (chapter 11)
    return z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum())

def ce(z, t): return -log_softmax(z)[t]

z = np.array([2.0, -1.0, 0.5, 0.0]); t = 2
analytic = np.exp(log_softmax(z)) - np.eye(4)[t]
numeric = np.array([(ce(z + eps * np.eye(4)[i], t) - ce(z - eps * np.eye(4)[i], t)) / (2 * eps) for i in range(4)])
print("softmax: analytic gradient softmax(z) - onehot:", np.round(analytic, 6))
print("         numeric gradient                      :", np.round(numeric, 6))

# Why the stable form matters: logits of 1000 overflow the naive formula.
big = np.array([1000.0, 0.0, 0.0])
with np.errstate(over="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
    naive = np.exp(big) / np.exp(big).sum()
print("\nnaive softmax of [1000, 0, 0]       :", naive)
print("exp(log_softmax) of [1000, 0, 0]    :", np.exp(log_softmax(big)))
print("cross-entropy if the target is 1    :", ce(big, 1), "(exactly 1000: the loss is finite and linear)")

Analytic and numeric gradients agree to all six digits shown. The second half is the practical lesson of chapter 11: computing the softmax naively on logits of size 1000 overflows to nan, while working in log space (subtract the maximum, then use log-sum-exp) returns a clean loss of exactly 1000 for a target the model considers impossible. That is why PyTorch's cross_entropy takes raw logits and does the softmax internally; passing it probabilities and taking the log yourself is a common bug.

Why not squared error for classification?

Nothing stops you from training a classifier with squared error on the probability, (p − y)2. It is a legitimate loss, and it is the Gaussian likelihood applied to a quantity that is not Gaussian. The practical failure is in its gradient. With p = σ(z) the chain rule gives ∂L/∂z = 2(p − y) p (1 − p), which has the sigmoid's derivative p(1 − p) as a factor. When the model is confidently wrong, p(1 − p) is nearly 0 and the gradient vanishes, just when the model most needs to correct itself.

"""Why classifiers use cross-entropy, not squared error: the gradient with respect to the logit when the model is confidently wrong."""
import math

def sigmoid(z): return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))

print("true label y = 1; model logit z; p = sigmoid(z)")
print(f"{'z':>5} {'p':>8} {'|dCE/dz| = 1-p':>16} {'|dMSE/dz| = 2p(1-p)^2':>24}")
for z in (-8, -6, -4, -2, 0, 2):
    p = sigmoid(z)
    print(f"{z:>5} {p:>8.4f} {1 - p:>16.4f} {2 * p * (1 - p) ** 2:>24.5f}")

   -2   0.1192           0.8808                  0.18496
    0   0.5000           0.5000                  0.25000
    2   0.8808           0.1192                  0.02503

At z = −6 the model gives the true class a probability of 0.0025. The cross-entropy gradient is 0.9975, close to its maximum, and pushes hard. The squared-error gradient is 0.0049, 200 times weaker. Cross-entropy matches the sigmoid so exactly that the awkward factor cancels. This is a general principle: the likelihood that fits the output distribution gives the cleanest gradient.

The recipe, in one picture

The pattern is now clear, and it is how you choose a loss for a new problem: decide what distribution describes the labels given the inputs, make the network output that distribution's parameters (with whatever transformation keeps them valid: a sigmoid for a probability, a softmax for a probability vector, an exponential for a positive rate), and minimise the negative log-probability of the observed labels.

Language models: the same loss, one token at a time

A language model is the categorical case applied repeatedly. By the chain rule of probability (chapter 12), the probability of a text x1, …, xT is ∏t p(xt | x<t), so the negative log-likelihood of a text is the sum over positions of each token's cross-entropy loss, and the training loss is its average per token:

loss = −(1/T) ∑t ln p(xt | x<t; w).

When a model reports "loss 2.3", that is 2.3 nats per token. Because the loss is the average of −ln p, the geometric mean of the probabilities the model gave to the real next tokens is e−2.3 ≈ 0.10: on average the model put about 10% of its probability on the correct token. Chapter 14 turns this loss into perplexity and bits per character. Fine-tuning (A3, chapter 10) is the same loss restricted to the tokens you want the model to learn to produce, by masking the others out.

The simplest possible language model makes the recipe fully concrete, because its MLE can be written down without any optimisation. A bigram model says each character depends only on the previous one, so p(b | a) is a table with one row per context a. Maximising the likelihood subject to each row adding to 1 gives, by a short argument (see the box), the intuitive answer: the MLE is the relative frequency, p(b | a) = count(a, b) / count(a).

Go deeper optional: the MLE of a categorical distribution is its frequencies

For one row of the table, with counts n1, …, nK and probabilities p1, …, pK that must add to 1, we maximise ∑k nk ln pk. Add a Lagrange multiplier λ for the constraint: ∂/∂pk [ ∑ nj ln pj − λ(∑ pj − 1) ] = nk/pk − λ = 0, so pk = nk/λ. The constraint ∑ pk = 1 forces λ = ∑ nk = n, hence pk = nk/n. The coin example is the case K = 2.

The next script trains such a model on a 152-character toy corpus by counting, evaluates it on a held-out sentence, and exposes the weak point of pure maximum likelihood.

"""Maximum likelihood for a character bigram language model: counting is the MLE, zero counts are a disaster, and a prior fixes them."""
import math
from collections import Counter

train = ("the cat sat on the mat. the dog sat on the log. a cat and a dog sat in the sun. "
         "the sun set and the cat ran to the dog. the dog ate the bone on the mat.")
test = "a bird flew over the hill. the cat ate fish."
alphabet = sorted(set(train + test))
V = len(alphabet)

def counts_of(text):
    c = Counter(zip(text, text[1:]))
    return c, Counter(text[:-1])

pair, single = counts_of(train)
def p_mle(a, b):               # maximum likelihood: relative frequency
    return pair[(a, b)] / single[a] if single[a] else 0.0
def p_smooth(a, b, k=1.0):     # add-k smoothing = MAP under a symmetric Dirichlet prior (see text)
    return (pair[(a, b)] + k) / (single[a] + k * V)

def nll_per_char(text, p):
    total = 0.0
    for a, b in zip(text, text[1:]):
        q = p(a, b)
        total += -math.log(q) if q > 0 else math.inf
    return total / (len(text) - 1)

print(f"alphabet size {V}, training characters {len(train)}, test characters {len(test)}")
print(f"train NLL per char, MLE      : {nll_per_char(train, p_mle):.3f} nats")
print(f"test  NLL per char, MLE      : {nll_per_char(test, p_mle):.3f} nats")
unseen = [(a, b) for a, b in zip(test, test[1:]) if pair[(a, b)] == 0]
print(f"bigrams in test never seen in training: {len(unseen)}, e.g. {unseen[:3]}")
for k in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0):
    print(f"test  NLL per char, add-{k:<4}: {nll_per_char(test, lambda a, b: p_smooth(a, b, k)):.3f} nats")
print(f"uniform guess over {V} symbols   : {math.log(V):.3f} nats")
alphabet size 21, training characters 152, test characters 44
train NLL per char, MLE      : 1.032 nats
test  NLL per char, MLE      : inf nats
bigrams in test never seen in training: 22, e.g. [('b', 'i'), ('i', 'r'), ('r', 'd')]
test  NLL per char, add-0.01: 3.379 nats
test  NLL per char, add-0.1 : 2.692 nats
test  NLL per char, add-1.0 : 2.554 nats
uniform guess over 21 symbols   : 3.045 nats

On the training text the model scores 1.03 nats per character, far below the 3.05 nats of a uniform guess over the 21 characters. But on held-out text containing letter pairs it never saw (22 of them, such as "bi", "ir", "rd"), the MLE assigns probability 0 to them, and the loss is infinite: one unseen pair makes the whole text impossible. This is overfitting in miniature: the MLE reproduces the training data perfectly and says nothing sensible about anything else. The practical repair is smoothing: pretend you saw every pair a little. Adding 1 to each count gives a finite test loss of 2.55, already better than uniform, although too little smoothing (0.01) is worse. Where does that "pretend counts" idea come from? From a prior, which is next.

Priors, MAP and regularisation

Maximum likelihood treats the data as the only evidence. Bayes' rule (chapter 12) lets us add what we knew before the data: the posterior p(w | data) is proportional to the likelihood times a prior p(w). Taking logs and keeping only what depends on w,

ln p(w | data) = ln p(data | w) + ln p(w) + constant.

Maximising this is maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation: the NLL plus a penalty, −ln p(w). Choose a prior and out comes a regulariser:

  • Gaussian prior wj ~ N(0, τ2): −ln p(w) = ‖w‖2/(2τ2) + constant. With Gaussian noise of variance σ2, the negative log-posterior is (1/(2σ2)) [ ∑(yi − fi)2 + λ ‖w‖2 ] with λ = σ2/τ2. That is L2 regularisation, or ridge regression.
  • Laplace prior p(wj) ∝ exp(−|wj|/b): the penalty is ∑|wj|, the L1 penalty (lasso), which pushes small weights exactly to zero.
  • A Dirichlet prior on a categorical distribution gives the pseudo-counts of the bigram model: add-k smoothing is the MAP estimate under a symmetric Dirichlet with parameter k + 1.

The relation λ = σ2/τ2 says how to read a regularisation strength: more noise in the labels (larger σ) or a tighter belief that weights are small (smaller τ) both mean stronger regularisation. The script fits a degree-9 polynomial to 12 noisy points from a smooth curve, once by plain maximum likelihood and once by MAP with σ = 0.2 and τ = 0.5, and verifies that the gradient of the negative log-posterior vanishes at the ridge solution.

"""A Gaussian prior on the weights is an L2 penalty: MAP estimation equals ridge regression."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
def make(n):
    x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n)
    return x, np.sin(2.5 * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.2, n)
x_tr, y_tr = make(12)
x_te, y_te = make(400)
deg = 9
feats = lambda x: np.vander(x, deg + 1, increasing=True)       # 1, x, x^2, ..., x^9
A, A_te = feats(x_tr), feats(x_te)

sigma, tau = 0.2, 0.5          # noise sd and prior sd of each weight
lam = sigma ** 2 / tau ** 2    # the penalty strength the derivation predicts
print(f"noise sd {sigma}, prior sd {tau}  ->  lambda = sigma^2 / tau^2 = {lam:.3f}")

w_mle = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y_tr, rcond=None)[0]                            # maximum likelihood
w_map = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + lam * np.eye(deg + 1), A.T @ y_tr)        # maximum a posteriori

def neg_log_posterior(w):       # -log likelihood - log prior, constants dropped
    return np.sum((y_tr - A @ w) ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2) + np.sum(w ** 2) / (2 * tau ** 2)
def grad(w):
    return -A.T @ (y_tr - A @ w) / sigma ** 2 + w / tau ** 2

print(f"gradient of the negative log posterior at w_map: max |g| = {np.abs(grad(w_map)).max():.2e}  (zero, so ridge solves MAP)")
for name, w in (("MLE (no prior)", w_mle), ("MAP (Gaussian prior)", w_map)):
    tr = np.sqrt(np.mean((A @ w - y_tr) ** 2)); te = np.sqrt(np.mean((A_te @ w - y_te) ** 2))
    print(f"{name:<22} |w| = {np.linalg.norm(w):>9.2f}   train rmse {tr:.3f}   test rmse {te:.3f}")
noise sd 0.2, prior sd 0.5  ->  lambda = sigma^2 / tau^2 = 0.160
gradient of the negative log posterior at w_map: max |g| = 1.04e-14  (zero, so ridge solves MAP)
MLE (no prior)         |w| =  12142.51   train rmse 0.018   test rmse 42.994
MAP (Gaussian prior)   |w| =      1.84   train rmse 0.259   test rmse 0.234

The unregularised fit passes through the 12 points almost exactly (training error 0.018) using weights of total size 12,142, and is wildly wrong on new data (test error 42.99). The MAP fit accepts a worse training error (0.259, about the noise level) with weights of size 1.84 and a test error of 0.234, nearly the noise floor. The prior did not add information about the curve; it ruled out the absurd weight vectors that only a perfect fit to noise requires. The gradient check on the first line of output (about 10−14) confirms that "ridge regression" and "the mode of the posterior under a Gaussian prior" are the same computation. This is also how to understand weight decay in deep learning (chapter 10, A2, chapter 4): it acts as a Gaussian prior on the weights, with the caveat from chapter 10 that decoupled decay in AdamW is no longer literally an L2 penalty on the loss.

One honesty note. MAP is a point estimate: it returns the single most probable w and discards the rest of the posterior. Fully Bayesian methods keep the whole distribution, which is expensive for large networks and rarely done at the scale of this path. When someone says a neural-network regulariser "is Bayesian", they usually mean exactly this MAP correspondence and no more.

What maximum likelihood does well, and where it fails

MLE has strong credentials. It is consistent: given enough data from a model that contains the truth, it converges to the true parameters. It is efficient: among reasonable estimators it asymptotically has about the smallest variance possible, with the width of the likelihood peak (the curvature of the log-likelihood at the maximum, called the Fisher information) setting the error bars. The sharp curves for 1,000 flips in the figure are exactly this: more data, higher curvature, a narrower plausible range. Chapter 15 uses that observation to turn likelihoods into confidence intervals.

It also has three characteristic failures, each of which you have now seen.

  • Overfitting. With flexible models and little data, the likelihood is maximised by memorising (the degree-9 polynomial, the zero-count bigram). Priors, early stopping, held-out validation (A1, chapter 1) and more data are the cures.
  • Small-sample bias. The variance example above. It fades with n.
  • Misspecification. If the model family does not contain the truth, MLE converges to the closest member, with closeness measured by a specific quantity: the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the data distribution to the model. Maximising likelihood is the same as minimising that divergence. Chapter 14 proves it, and it is why every loss in this chapter has an information-theoretic reading, and why KL appears in post-training. A Gaussian fitted to skewed data returns the best Gaussian, not a warning that the data are skewed. Plot your residuals.

The counts row deserves a demonstration, because it shows the cost of ignoring the table. The script simulates request counts whose mean grows exponentially with load, then fits them two ways: a straight line by least squares (Gaussian noise, constant variance, linear mean) and a Poisson model with a log link, fitted by Newton's method on the Poisson NLL, which for counts y and mean μ is ∑(μi − yi ln μi) up to a constant.

"""Counts want a Poisson likelihood: fit requests-per-minute against load with Gaussian least squares and with Poisson maximum likelihood."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
x = rng.uniform(0, 3, 300)
rate_true = np.exp(-0.5 + 1.2 * x)                       # the true mean count grows exponentially with x
y = rng.poisson(rate_true)

# (a) ordinary least squares: assumes Gaussian noise of constant variance, and a straight line
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
w_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]

# (b) Poisson regression: mean = exp(X w); the negative log-likelihood is sum(mean - y * log(mean)) + const.
w = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(60):                                       # Newton's method on the NLL
    mu = np.exp(X @ w)
    g = X.T @ (mu - y)
    H = X.T @ (X * mu[:, None])
    w -= np.linalg.solve(H, g)
print(f"true (a, b)        : (-0.50, 1.20)")
print(f"Poisson MLE (a, b) : ({w[0]:.2f}, {w[1]:.2f})")
print(f"least squares line : intercept {w_ols[0]:.2f}, slope {w_ols[1]:.2f}")
for xv in (0.0, 0.5, 3.0):
    print(f"  at x = {xv}: true mean {np.exp(-0.5 + 1.2 * xv):6.2f}   Poisson fit {np.exp(w[0] + w[1] * xv):6.2f}   least-squares fit {w_ols[0] + w_ols[1] * xv:6.2f}")
true (a, b)        : (-0.50, 1.20)
Poisson MLE (a, b) : (-0.57, 1.23)
least squares line : intercept -3.16, slope 6.00
  at x = 0.0: true mean   0.61   Poisson fit   0.56   least-squares fit  -3.16
  at x = 0.5: true mean   1.11   Poisson fit   1.04   least-squares fit  -0.16

The Poisson fit recovers the true parameters (−0.57 and 1.23 against −0.5 and 1.2) and predicts 22.43 at x = 3 against a true 22.20. The least-squares line predicts a negative count (−3.16) at x = 0, and 14.84 at x = 3, because it is fitting an exponential curve with a straight line. Matching the likelihood to the data type fixed both. When a loss function misbehaves, ask what distribution it assumes before reaching for tricks.

One further property is worth knowing about the classification losses. Cross-entropy is a proper scoring rule: its expected value is minimised only when the model reports the true probabilities (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007, survey the idea). A model trained to minimise it is therefore rewarded for being calibrated, not merely for ranking examples correctly, which is why the probabilities of a well-trained classifier are meaningful at all, and why they become over-confident when a model is trained for too long (A1, chapter 15). A raw accuracy loss has no such property, and it has no useful gradient either.

Systems connection

The loss you train on and the number you monitor in production are different things, and the likelihood view tells you which mismatches to expect. Request latency is positive and heavy-tailed, so a Gaussian squared-error model on raw milliseconds is misspecified: the occasional 5-second request dominates the fit, exactly like the outliers in the widget, and the resulting "average" describes no real user. Fitting the logarithm of latency (a log-normal model) or a quantile loss at the p99 matches what the tail-latency chapter says users actually feel. The same applies to capacity forecasts for counts of requests (Poisson, as above) and to anomaly thresholds on a metric: a threshold set in "standard deviations" silently assumes the Gaussian noise model.

Key takeaways

  • The likelihood is the probability of the observed data as a function of the parameters. It is not a probability of the parameters. Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the data least surprising.
  • We maximise the log-likelihood: products underflow, sums do not, and the argmax is unchanged. The training loss is the negative log-likelihood, averaged over examples.
  • Gaussian noise gives squared error (and its learned variance is the mean squared residual); Laplace gives absolute error and a median; Student-t gives a loss robust to outliers; Bernoulli and categorical give binary and multiclass cross-entropy; Poisson gives the count loss.
  • For softmax with cross-entropy the gradient with respect to the logits is p − onehot, and for sigmoid with binary cross-entropy it is p − y. A loss matched to the output distribution has a clean gradient; squared error on a sigmoid goes quiet when the model is confidently wrong.
  • Compute classification losses from logits with log-sum-exp, never from probabilities, to avoid overflow and log of zero.
  • A language model's loss is the average negative log-probability per token; e−loss is the geometric-mean probability it gave the true next tokens.
  • MAP adds a prior: a Gaussian prior is L2 regularisation with λ = σ2/τ2, a Laplace prior is L1, a Dirichlet prior is add-k smoothing. MLE overfits when flexible models meet little data, and under a wrong model it finds the nearest member of the family in the KL sense (chapter 14).

Check your understanding

You flip a coin 20 times and see 5 heads. Which statement about the likelihood L(θ) = θ5(1 − θ)15 is correct?

  1. L(0.25) is the probability that the coin's true bias is 0.25
  2. L(0.25) is the probability of seeing exactly this sequence of flips if the true bias were 0.25, and it is the largest value L takes
  3. L(θ) must add to 1 when summed over all θ, since it is a probability
  4. L(0.5) is larger than L(0.25), because a fair coin is the most natural hypothesis
The likelihood is the probability of the data given a parameter, and it peaks at the observed frequency 5/20 = 0.25. It is not a probability of the parameter (first option) and does not integrate to 1 over θ (third). The fourth option imports a prior preference for fair coins; the likelihood alone knows nothing about that, and L(0.5) is far smaller than L(0.25).

Why do we maximise the log-likelihood rather than the likelihood itself?

  1. The log-likelihood has a different, better maximum
  2. The likelihood is not differentiable
  3. The log makes the model more accurate
  4. The log turns a product of many small probabilities into a sum, which avoids underflow and gives per-example gradients, while leaving the maximiser unchanged
Because the logarithm is strictly increasing, the arg max of ln L equals that of L, so nothing is lost (first option is false). The likelihood is differentiable but a product of thousands of probabilities underflows to 0.0 in floating point, as the coin script showed with 1,500 flips. The log does not change the model or its accuracy; it changes how the optimum is computed.

A sensor reading is usually accurate but about 1 in 50 readings is a wild glitch many times larger than the typical error. To estimate the true value from a batch of readings, which choice is best motivated by the likelihood view?

  1. A heavy-tailed noise model, such as Laplace or Student-t (median-like estimators), because the Gaussian model's squared error lets each glitch pull with force proportional to its size
  2. Squared error, because it is the standard loss and the central limit theorem makes all noise Gaussian
  3. Squared error with a larger learning rate, to compensate for the glitches
  4. Cross-entropy, because it is robust to outliers
Squared error is the Gaussian likelihood, and its pull grows with the residual, so rare large errors dominate the mean. A model with heavier tails (Laplace, Student-t, Huber) expects occasional glitches and discounts them. The central limit theorem is about averages of many small effects, not about glitches, which are rare large effects. A learning rate does not change what the loss minimises, and cross-entropy is for categorical labels.

A 3-class classifier outputs softmax probabilities (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and the true class is the second one. What is the gradient of the cross-entropy loss with respect to the three logits?

  1. (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)
  2. (−0.7, 0.8, −0.1)
  3. (0.7, −0.8, 0.1)
  4. (0, −1, 0)
The gradient is the predicted probabilities minus the one-hot target: (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) − (0, 1, 0) = (0.7, −0.8, 0.1). Gradient descent subtracts it, so the logit of the true class rises and the others fall. The first option forgets the target, the second has every sign reversed (it would increase the loss), and the fourth is the gradient with respect to the probabilities scaled wrongly and ignores the softmax coupling.

You fit ridge regression with a Gaussian prior on the weights of standard deviation τ, and the label noise has standard deviation σ. If you decide the prior should be twice as wide (τ doubled), what happens to the regularisation strength λ = σ2/τ2?

  1. It doubles, because a wider prior means more regularisation
  2. It falls to a quarter, because a wider prior is a weaker belief that weights are small
  3. It halves, because λ is proportional to 1/τ
  4. It is unchanged, because only σ enters the formula
λ = σ2/τ2, so doubling τ divides λ by 4. A wider prior permits larger weights, which is weaker regularisation. The formula involves the square of τ, ruling out "halves"; and τ clearly enters, ruling out "unchanged".

A language model's average training loss is 2.3 nats per token. Which statement is closest to the truth?

  1. The model predicts the correct next token 2.3 times out of 10
  2. The model assigns about 2.3% probability to the correct token on average
  3. The geometric mean of the probabilities it gave the correct next tokens is about e−2.3, roughly 0.10
  4. The model's accuracy is 77%, since 1 − 0.23 = 0.77
The loss is the average of −ln p over tokens, so e−2.3 ≈ 0.10 is the geometric-mean probability given to the true tokens. That is not the accuracy: a model can put 10% on the right token and still rank it first, or put 90% on it on some tokens and 1% on others. Nats are logarithmic units, so the loss cannot be read as a percentage or a count.
