Lab 5: A Statistics Toolkit for Experiments

Chapter 15 gave you the ideas: a number computed from a sample wobbles, an interval says by how much, a test asks whether a difference could be a fluke, power says whether the experiment could have seen the effect at all, and looking early breaks every guarantee. In this lab you write the tools yourself over plain Python lists: intervals for means and proportions, the bootstrap, permutation and sign-flip tests, sample-size and power calculators checked by simulation, and a simulator that shows peeking inflating false positives. Then you point those tools at themselves, measuring how often your own intervals deserve their name.

Why this lab matters

Almost every claim in applied AI is a measured difference: the new prompt scored two points higher, the fine-tuned model won 54% of comparisons, the ranker lifted click-through by 0.9%. Whether to believe it depends on a handful of small calculations, and in practice they are rarely done, or are done with a library call whose assumptions nobody checked. An interval that quietly assumes a bell-shaped distribution is applied to a heavy-tailed latency. A proportion near zero gets a formula that reports certainty from an empty sample. A dashboard is refreshed every morning until it turns green. Each of these fails silently, and each gives a confident number.

The habit this lab installs is testing a statistical procedure by simulation. Because you can generate data from a world where you know the truth, you can ask of any method: how often does your 95% interval contain the truth, how often does your test cry wolf when nothing is going on, how often does it catch an effect that is really there? Those three questions, answered with a few lines of seeded random numbers, are the same questions that A6 chapter 4 asks of an evaluation, that online experiments in A1 chapter 21 depend on, and that A7 chapter 16 needs when a system picks the best of many variants. The checker uses the idea against you in the best sense: it enumerates every shuffle of tiny data sets to get exact answers, and it holds your simulations to the error bars that a simulation of that size should have.

Pure Python, deterministic, a few seconds

Standard library only (math, random and itertools), Python 3.9 or newer; no NumPy. Every random function takes a seed and builds its own random.Random(seed), so a run can be replayed exactly. Because different correct programs consume random numbers in different orders, the statistical checks in check.py do not compare your output with a stored answer. They compare it with an exact answer (found by trying every permutation) or a theoretical rate, using a tolerance of several standard errors of the simulation. A failure prints its seed: python3 check.py solution 5 12 replays milestone 5 on seed 12 only. The full check takes about four seconds.

What you'll build

Notation follows chapters 12 and 15. A sample has n observations; a proportion is estimated as p̂ = k/n from k successes; the standard error of a mean is σ̂/√n; z is a statistic measured in standard errors; and α is the false-positive rate you are prepared to accept (0.05 throughout). The functions take plain lists and return plain numbers, tuples and dictionaries.

  1. Intervals: the sample variance, the standard error, the normal interval for a mean, and two intervals for a proportion: the simple Wald interval and the Wilson interval that repairs it near 0 and 1.
  2. The bootstrap: percentiles, resampling with replacement, an interval for any statistic, and the paired version that resamples questions rather than answers.
  3. Hypothesis tests: the two-proportion z-test, a permutation test for a difference of means and a sign-flip test for paired data, each checked against exhaustive enumeration.
  4. Power and the winner's curse: the sample-size formula, a simulation that confirms it, and a measurement of how much underpowered experiments exaggerate the effects they do find.
  5. Peeking: simulate experiments with no real effect, check them at several looks, and measure the false-positive rate of stopping at the first significant result. Then calibrate boundaries that repair it.
  6. Audit and report: a coverage function that tests an interval method against a known truth, and an A/B report with lift, interval, p-value, verdict, power and the sample size you would have needed.

Milestone 1: standard errors and intervals

Start with the formulas of chapter 15. A sample mean has a standard error, the typical size of its wobble from sample to sample, equal to σ̂/√n, where σ̂ is the sample's standard deviation. The estimate needs the unbiased sample variance, which divides the sum of squared deviations by n − 1 rather than n. A sample sits closer to its own mean than to the population's, so dividing by n runs low, and the n − 1 corrects it (chapter 12 covers variance and the standard error). For the eight values 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 the squared deviations from the mean 5 add up to 32, so the variance is 32/7 = 4.571, not 4.0.

A normal-approximation interval is estimate ± z × standard error, with z = 1.96 for 95%. For a general confidence level the multiplier is the quantile phi_inv(0.5 + conf/2): 0.975 for 95%, 0.95 for 90%. The same recipe works for a proportion, whose standard error is √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n); that is the Wald interval. Chapter 15 showed its weakness and a repair, the Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927), which you now write:

centre = (p̂ + z2/2n) / (1 + z2/n),    half-width = z √( p̂(1 − p̂)/n + z2/4n2 ) / (1 + z2/n).

The Wilson interval is what you get by asking which true rates p would make the observed count unsurprising, rather than centring a bell on the observed rate. It does not collapse when p̂ is 0 or 1, and it never leaves [0, 1]. The script below puts the two intervals side by side on four cases, then applies the mean interval to ten latencies:

Read the Wald column. For 2 successes in 100 the interval starts below zero, a rate that is impossible. For 0 of 50 and for 20 of 20 it is a single point: the standard error is exactly 0, so the interval claims certainty from a handful of observations. The Wilson interval says what a sensible person would: 20 out of 20 is compatible with a true rate as low as 84%, and 0 of 50 with one as high as 7%. This matters in practice whenever an evaluation reports "100% on 20 examples", or a safety check reports "no failures in 50 runs".

Common misconception

"A 95% interval means a 95% chance the truth is inside it." Once the interval is computed the truth is either inside or not. The 95% describes the procedure: repeat the whole experiment many times and about 95% of the intervals built this way contain the truth. Milestone 6 turns that sentence into a measurement, and you will see that for some procedures and sample sizes the real figure is 86%, not 95%.

def sample_variance(xs):
    """The unbiased sample variance: sum((x - mean)^2) / (n - 1)."""
    m = mean(xs)
    return sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1)

def std_error(xs):
    """The standard error of the mean: sqrt(sample variance / n), i.e. sigma-hat / sqrt(n)."""
    return math.sqrt(sample_variance(xs) / len(xs))

def mean_interval(xs, conf=0.95):
    """Normal-approximation interval for a mean: mean +/- z * standard error (z = 1.96 for 95%)."""
    z = phi_inv(0.5 + conf / 2)
    m, se = mean(xs), std_error(xs)
    return (m - z * se, m + z * se)

def wald_interval(k, n, conf=0.95):
    """The simple interval for a proportion: p-hat +/- z * sqrt(p-hat (1 - p-hat) / n)."""
    z = phi_inv(0.5 + conf / 2)
    p = k / n
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
    return (p - half, p + half)

def wilson_interval(k, n, conf=0.95):
    """The Wilson (1927) interval for a proportion, always inside [0, 1].

    centre = (p + z^2 / 2n) / (1 + z^2 / n)
    half   = z * sqrt(p (1 - p) / n + z^2 / 4n^2) / (1 + z^2 / n)
    """
    z = phi_inv(0.5 + conf / 2)
    p = k / n
    denom = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / denom
    return (max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half))

Milestone 2: the bootstrap

The formulas above exist for means and proportions. For the median latency, the 90th percentile, an F1 score or a ratio of two means, you replace the mathematics with computation (Efron, 1979): treat the sample as a stand-in for the population and redraw samples from it. One resample is n values drawn with replacement from your n values, so some appear twice and others not at all. Compute the statistic on each of B resamples, sort the B results, and read an interval off their percentiles.

Start with percentile. Given a sorted list and a q between 0 and 100, the position is (n − 1) × q/100, and when it falls between two elements you blend the neighbours in proportion. This is the default rule of NumPy's percentile, so for [10, 20, 30, 40] the 50th percentile sits at position 1.5 and equals 25. Then bootstrap_stats draws the B resamples (rng.choices(data, k=n) draws with replacement), and bootstrap_interval puts them together. Run it on 60 right-skewed latencies and compare with the formula where one exists:

For the mean, the bootstrap interval (81.4 to 118.7) has almost the same width as the formula's (37.3 against 37.6): where a formula exists, the bootstrap reproduces it (the skew in the data pulls the bootstrap interval slightly to the right of the symmetric one). The median needed no formula at all. And the 90th percentile has an interval twice as wide as the median's: tail statistics are estimated from the few extreme observations, which is why a p99 latency from a short test is not worth quoting to the millisecond (reliability chapter 4 covers how percentiles are measured and why they cannot be averaged).

The last lines show the most common use of the bootstrap in AI work: comparing two systems on the same questions. Model B fixes 14 of A's mistakes and breaks 4 of its successes; the two agree on 182 of 200 questions. Treated as two independent accuracies the difference of +5.0 points has an interval from −3.2 to +13.2, which includes zero. Resampling question indices, so that each question keeps both of its results together, gives +1.0 to +9.0. The agreeing questions carry no information about which model is better, and an unpaired analysis lets their noise in anyway. This is exactly the pairing argument of chapter 15, and it returns in A6 chapter 4.

Analogy: photocopying the crowd

You want to know how much a crowd's average height would vary between random groups of 60 people, but you can only measure one group. So you photocopy that group, treat the copies as the whole crowd, and draw new groups of 60 from the copies, again and again. The variation between those redrawn groups stands in for the variation between real groups.

Where it breaks down: the copies contain only the people you already measured. For the maximum, the bootstrap interval ends exactly at the sample's maximum, because no resample can contain anything larger; the checker tests this. A rare slow request that your sample never saw cannot be found by redrawing.

def percentile(sorted_xs, q):
    """The q-th percentile (0 to 100) of an already sorted list, by linear interpolation.

    The same rule as NumPy's default: position (n - 1) * q / 100, blending the two neighbours.
    """
    pos = (len(sorted_xs) - 1) * q / 100
    lo = int(math.floor(pos))
    hi = min(lo + 1, len(sorted_xs) - 1)
    frac = pos - lo
    return sorted_xs[lo] * (1 - frac) + sorted_xs[hi] * frac

def bootstrap_stats(data, stat, B, rng):
    """B values of stat(resample), each resample drawn WITH replacement and the same size as data."""
    n = len(data)
    return [stat(rng.choices(data, k=n)) for _ in range(B)]

def bootstrap_interval(data, stat, B=2000, conf=0.95, seed=0):
    """Percentile bootstrap interval for stat(data): the (1-conf)/2 and (1+conf)/2 percentiles."""
    boots = sorted(bootstrap_stats(data, stat, B, random.Random(seed)))
    alpha = 1 - conf
    return (percentile(boots, 100 * alpha / 2), percentile(boots, 100 * (1 - alpha / 2)))

def paired_bootstrap_interval(a, b, B=2000, conf=0.95, seed=0):
    """Interval for mean(b) - mean(a) when a[i] and b[i] are two systems on the SAME question i.

    Resample the question indices, so each question keeps both of its results.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n = len(a)
    diffs = []
    for _ in range(B):
        idx = [rng.randrange(n) for _ in range(n)]
        diffs.append(sum(b[i] - a[i] for i in idx) / n)
    diffs.sort()
    alpha = 1 - conf
    return (percentile(diffs, 100 * alpha / 2), percentile(diffs, 100 * (1 - alpha / 2)))

Milestone 3: hypothesis tests

An interval says how big an effect might be; a hypothesis test answers the narrower question "could this be a fluke?". State a null hypothesis H0 (no real difference), measure how far the data are from it, and report the p-value: the probability, if H0 were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Three tests cover most of what you will need.

The two-proportion z-test is the textbook formula for an A/B test on conversion. Under H0 the arms share one rate, so estimate it from the pooled data, pooled = (k1 + k2)/(n1 + n2), and divide the observed difference by its standard error under H0:

z = (p̂2 − p̂1) / √( pooled (1 − pooled) (1/n1 + 1/n2) ),    p = P(|Z| ≥ |z|).

The permutation test needs no formula and no bell curve. If the group labels do not matter, then any way of splitting the pooled values into groups of the original sizes is as likely as the one you saw. Shuffle the pooled values, split, recompute the difference of means, and count how often it is at least as large (in absolute value) as the observed one. With B shuffles the estimate is (count + 1)/(B + 1): the observed split is itself one of the permutations, so the p-value can never be exactly 0. For small data you can do better than sampling and try every split, which gives the exact p-value.

The sign-flip test is the paired version. Under H0 each per-question difference is as likely to be positive as negative, so flip the sign of each difference at random and recompute the mean. Differences of zero (both systems agree) never change the sum, which is why they contribute no evidence. The script runs the permutation test twice on two groups of five, once by enumerating every split and once by shuffling, and then the other two tests on examples from chapter 15:

from itertools import combinations
from solution import mean, permutation_test, sign_flip_test, two_proportion_z_test

x, y = [12, 15, 11, 14, 13], [16, 19, 14, 18, 17]
pooled, obs = x + y, abs(mean(y) - mean(x))
extreme = total = 0
for idx in combinations(range(10), 5):                       # every way to pick which 5 values form the first group
    first = [pooled[i] for i in idx]
    second = [pooled[i] for i in range(10) if i not in idx]
    total += 1
    extreme += abs(mean(second) - mean(first)) >= obs - 1e-12

There are 252 ways to choose which five of the ten values form the first group, and 6 of them give a difference of means as large as the observed 3.8, so the exact two-sided p is 6/252 = 0.0238. Twenty thousand random shuffles estimate it as 0.0237. The z-test reproduces the 0.0547 of chapter 15 for 790/8000 against 864/8000. And the last line is the paired comparison from the bootstrap example in the chapter, 26 questions where only B is right against 9 where only A is: the sign-flip test gives p = 0.006, the sign pattern of 35 informative questions being strongly lopsided. The 465 ties contribute nothing, as they should.

Common misconception

"p = 0.0238 means there is a 2.4% chance the null hypothesis is true." The p-value is the chance of data this extreme assuming H0, not the chance that H0 holds; turning it round is the same error as confusing P(positive test | healthy) with P(healthy | positive test) in chapter 12. And p = 0.055 is not evidence of "no effect": the interval for that A/B test ran from −0.02 to +1.87 points, wide enough to hold both nothing and an effect twice as large as the one seen.

def two_proportion_z_test(k1, n1, k2, n2):
    """Pooled two-proportion z-test of H0: both arms convert at the same rate.

    Returns (z, two_sided_p) with z = (p2 - p1) / sqrt(pooled (1 - pooled) (1/n1 + 1/n2)).
    """
    p1, p2 = k1 / n1, k2 / n2
    pooled = (k1 + k2) / (n1 + n2)
    se = math.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n1 + 1 / n2))
    z = (p2 - p1) / se
    return z, two_sided_p(z)

def permutation_test(x, y, B=5000, seed=0):
    """Two-sided permutation test for a difference in means.

    Shuffle the pooled values B times, split them into groups of the original sizes, and count
    how often |difference| is at least the observed one. Return (count + 1) / (B + 1): the +1
    counts the observed split itself as one of the permutations, so p is never 0.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    nx = len(x)
    obs = abs(mean(y) - mean(x))
    pooled = list(x) + list(y)
    count = 0
    for _ in range(B):
        rng.shuffle(pooled)
        d = mean(pooled[nx:]) - mean(pooled[:nx])
        if abs(d) >= obs - 1e-12:
            count += 1
    return (count + 1) / (B + 1)

def sign_flip_test(diffs, B=5000, seed=0):
    """Two-sided paired test: under H0 each per-question difference is as likely to be + as -.

    Flip the sign of every difference at random B times; return (count + 1) / (B + 1) where
    count is how often |mean of flipped diffs| >= |observed mean|.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    obs = abs(mean(diffs))
    count = 0
    for _ in range(B):
        m = sum(d if rng.random() < 0.5 else -d for d in diffs) / len(diffs)
        if abs(m) >= obs - 1e-12:
            count += 1
    return (count + 1) / (B + 1)

Milestone 4: power and the winner's curse

Power is the probability that an experiment detects an effect of a given size when the effect is real. A test that has power of 10% for the effect you care about is not a neutral instrument; it will usually return "not significant" whether or not the change helps. The convention is to design for 80% power. For a conversion test the argument in chapter 15 gives the sample size per arm directly:

n = (zα/2 + zpower)2 · ( p1(1 − p1) + p2(1 − p2) ) / (p2 − p1)2,

where zα/2 = 1.96 for a two-sided test at α = 0.05 and zpower = 0.8416 for 80% power; round up. The effect size is squared in the denominator, so halving the effect you want to see costs four times the users. A formula is a model, though, and you can check a model by simulation: draw many experiments from a world where you know the true rates, run your test on each, and count how often it rejects. If the formula is right, that fraction should be about 80% at the n it chose, and about α when there is no effect.

The simulation needs binomial counts. Drawing 15,000 Bernoulli trials per arm per experiment would be slow in pure Python, so the starter gives you binomial(rng, n, p), which jumps between successes with geometric gaps and costs about n·p random numbers instead of n. The numbers it returns are exact binomial draws.

The second half of the milestone is the winner's curse from chapter 15. Suppose the true lift is +0.5 points, but each arm has only 2,000 users, so power is under 10%. Most experiments fail to reach significance, and the few that do are precisely those where noise inflated the estimate, because only a large observed difference clears the bar. Average over all experiments and the estimate is fine; average over the winners and it is several times too large. Teams that ship what wins and report what was observed are quietly reporting selection effects (Gelman and Carlin, 2014, call the exaggeration a Type M error). Run both parts:

def sample_size(p1, p2, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """Users per arm (rounded UP to an integer) to detect p1 -> p2 with a two-sided z-test.

    n = (z_{alpha/2} + z_{power})^2 * (p1 (1 - p1) + p2 (1 - p2)) / (p2 - p1)^2
    """
    za, zb = phi_inv(1 - alpha / 2), phi_inv(power)
    var = p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2)
    return math.ceil((za + zb) ** 2 * var / (p2 - p1) ** 2)

def simulate_power(p1, p2, n, E=2000, alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    """Fraction of E simulated experiments (n users per arm, true rates p1 and p2) that reject H0."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(E):
        k1, k2 = binomial(rng, n, p1), binomial(rng, n, p2)
        if k1 + k2 in (0, 2 * n):
            continue                      # no variation at all: the test cannot reject
        if two_proportion_z_test(k1, n, k2, n)[1] < alpha:
            hits += 1
    return hits / E

def winners_curse(p1, p2, n, E=4000, alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    """Simulate E experiments and report what 'ship the significant ones' does to the estimate.

    A win is p < alpha with a POSITIVE observed lift. Returns a dict:
      power:                 fraction of experiments that are wins
      mean_lift_all:         average observed lift (p2-hat - p1-hat) over every experiment
      mean_lift_significant: average observed lift over the wins only (0.0 if there are none)
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    lifts, wins = [], []
    for _ in range(E):
        k1, k2 = binomial(rng, n, p1), binomial(rng, n, p2)
        lift = (k2 - k1) / n
        lifts.append(lift)
        if k1 + k2 not in (0, 2 * n) and lift > 0 and two_proportion_z_test(k1, n, k2, n)[1] < alpha:
            wins.append(lift)
    return {"power": len(wins) / E, "mean_lift_all": mean(lifts),
            "mean_lift_significant": mean(wins) if wins else 0.0}

Milestone 5: peeking

Every guarantee so far assumes one analysis, planned in advance, of a sample of fixed size. A dashboard that updates daily invites a different procedure: check the p-value each morning and stop the moment it falls below 0.05. Each check is another chance for noise to cross the line. The z statistic of an experiment with no effect behaves like a random walk that starts wild and slowly settles, and a random walk that is examined at many times will sooner or later leave any fixed band.

To measure this you need many A/A tests: experiments in which both arms are identical, so any "significant" result is by construction a false positive. Each simulated experiment runs to a planned n per arm, split into equal blocks, one per look. At look i the z statistic is computed from all the data so far (the counts accumulate across blocks), which is what makes the sequence a random walk and makes consecutive looks strongly correlated: look 2 contains all of look 1 plus as much again, so the correlation of their z values is 1/√2 = 0.71. A look that used only the newest block would be a different, independent test, and the checker detects that mistake by measuring this correlation.

You will write z_paths, the simulator, and the two rates that matter: the fraction of experiments that are significant at the final look only (end_only_rate) and the fraction that are significant at any look (peeking_rate, the rate for an analyst who stops at the first one). Then you repair the procedure with a stricter boundary used at every look. Two choices, both from group sequential testing: the Bonferroni boundary spends the 5% equally, zα/(2K) for K looks, which is safe but conservative because the looks are correlated; the calibrated, Pocock-style boundary is the constant c for which exactly 5% of the simulated A/A paths ever exceed it. That c is just the 95th percentile of the per-path maximum of |z|.

Systems connection

Peeking is a stopping-rule problem that appears wherever a decision is made from a stream. A canary release that is promoted the first time its error rate looks no worse than the baseline, an alert that auto-resolves when a metric dips below its threshold once, and a model comparison that ends when the leaderboard gap crosses an interval are all stopped by the data they are judging. Reliability chapter 2 defines an SLO over a fixed window, and the discipline is the same: a window or a stopping time that moves with the result invalidates the comparison. Case 5 follows what modern experimentation platforms do instead (always-valid p-values and intervals).

def z_paths(looks, n_final, p, E, seed):
    """E simulated A/A tests (both arms convert at p), each analysed at `looks` equally spaced looks.

    Returns a list of E lists of `looks` z statistics. Look i (1-based) uses the CUMULATIVE data
    of the first i blocks of n_final // looks users per arm, so the paths are random walks.
    If the standard error is 0 (everyone converted, or nobody), use z = 0.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    block = n_final // looks
    paths = []
    for _ in range(E):
        ka = kb = 0
        path = []
        for i in range(1, looks + 1):
            ka += binomial(rng, block, p)
            kb += binomial(rng, block, p)
            n = block * i
            pooled = (ka + kb) / (2 * n)
            se = math.sqrt(2 * pooled * (1 - pooled) / n)
            path.append((kb - ka) / n / se if se > 0 else 0.0)
        paths.append(path)
    return paths

def end_only_rate(paths, c):
    """Fraction of paths whose LAST z has |z| > c: one look, at the planned end."""
    return sum(1 for path in paths if abs(path[-1]) > c) / len(paths)

def peeking_rate(paths, c):
    """Fraction of paths where ANY look has |z| > c: stop at the first look that crosses."""
    return sum(1 for path in paths if any(abs(z) > c for z in path)) / len(paths)

def bonferroni_boundary(looks, alpha=0.05):
    """The z boundary that splits alpha equally over the looks: z_{alpha / (2 * looks)}."""
    return phi_inv(1 - alpha / (2 * looks))

def pocock_boundary(paths, alpha=0.05):
    """The constant boundary c whose peeking_rate on THESE paths is at most alpha.

    That is the (1 - alpha) quantile of the per-path maximum of |z|: the smallest c such that
    at most a fraction alpha of the paths ever exceed it.
    """
    maxima = sorted(max(abs(z) for z in path) for path in paths)
    k = math.ceil(round((1 - alpha) * len(maxima), 9)) - 1    # 0-based index of the (1 - alpha) quantile (round() guards float noise)
    return maxima[k]

Milestone 6: coverage audit and the experiment report

The last milestone turns the lab's method on its own tools. The coverage of an interval procedure is the fraction of repeated experiments in which the interval contains the true value. A 95% procedure should have coverage of 95%. You can measure it whenever you can generate data from a world with a known truth: coverage(interval_fn, sampler, truth, E, seed) draws E fresh samples with sampler(rng), builds an interval from each with interval_fn(sample), and counts how often it contains truth. It is the same loop as every other simulation in the lab, with the interval as the thing being tested.

def coverage(interval_fn, sampler, truth, E=1000, seed=0):
    """How often does the interval contain the truth? Repeat the experiment E times.

    sampler(rng) draws one fresh sample; interval_fn(sample) returns (lo, hi). Return the
    fraction of the E intervals with lo <= truth <= hi.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(E):
        lo, hi = interval_fn(sampler(rng))
        if lo <= truth <= hi:
            hits += 1
    return hits / E

def ab_report(k1, n1, k2, n2, mde, alpha=0.05):
    """Summarise a two-arm conversion experiment (control k1/n1, treatment k2/n2).

    `mde` is the smallest absolute lift worth detecting (0.012 means +1.2 points). Returns a dict:
      lift:     p2-hat - p1-hat
      interval: (lo, hi), lift +/- z * sqrt(p1(1-p1)/n1 + p2(1-p2)/n2), z for 1 - alpha
      z, p:     from two_proportion_z_test
      verdict:  "win" if p < alpha and lift > 0, "loss" if p < alpha and lift < 0, else "inconclusive"
      power:    the chance THIS design (n1, n2, baseline p1-hat) detects a true lift of exactly `mde`
      n_needed: users per arm for 80% power at `mde` from baseline p1-hat (sample_size)
    For the power, let q = p1-hat + mde and se = sqrt(p1(1-p1)/n1 + q(1-q)/n2), zc = z_{1-alpha/2}:
      power = phi(mde/se - zc) + phi(-mde/se - zc).
    """
    p1, p2 = k1 / n1, k2 / n2
    lift = p2 - p1
    zc = phi_inv(1 - alpha / 2)
    se = math.sqrt(p1 * (1 - p1) / n1 + p2 * (1 - p2) / n2)
    z, p = two_proportion_z_test(k1, n1, k2, n2)
    if p < alpha:
        verdict = "win" if lift > 0 else "loss"
    else:
        verdict = "inconclusive"
    q = p1 + mde
    se_alt = math.sqrt(p1 * (1 - p1) / n1 + q * (1 - q) / n2)
    power = phi(mde / se_alt - zc) + phi(-mde / se_alt - zc)
    return {"lift": lift, "interval": (lift - zc * se, lift + zc * se), "z": z, "p": p,
            "verdict": verdict, "power": power, "n_needed": sample_size(p1, q, alpha)}

Going further

  • A permutation test at scale. Shuffling 16,000 conversion outcomes 20,000 times is slow in pure Python. For 0/1 data the number of successes in the first group follows a hypergeometric distribution, so you can draw it directly. Implement that and check that it reproduces the permutation p-value of chapter 15 (0.056 for 790/8000 against 864/8000) in a fraction of a second.
  • Other bootstrap intervals. The percentile interval is the simplest. Implement the basic bootstrap (reflect the percentiles about the estimate) and compare their coverage with coverage on the skewed median problem. Efron and Tibshirani (1993) describe the improved BCa interval; what extra quantities does it need?
  • The cluster trap. Suppose 500 test questions come from 50 source documents, ten from each, and a model's correctness is correlated within a document. Resample the 500 questions as if independent and then resample the 50 documents; compare the interval widths. This is the clustered-questions problem of A6 chapter 4.
  • Many things at once. Simulate 20 A/A tests, report the best one, and measure how often the best of 20 is significant at 0.05 (about 64%: one minus 0.9520). Then add a Bonferroni correction and measure again. The same arithmetic applies to a system that tries many prompts and keeps the best (A7 chapter 16).
  • An always-valid test. Replace the constant boundary with one that grows with the square root of the sample size, or implement a mixture sequential probability ratio test (Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, 2017), and measure its false-positive rate over 100 looks and its power against the fixed-sample test.
  • Pocock against O'Brien and Fleming. The constant boundary spends the error evenly. The O'Brien-Fleming shape demands very strong evidence early and relaxes later. Choose a boundary of the form c/√(fraction of data seen), calibrate c with the same quantile trick, and compare the power of the two designs for a true lift of 1 point.

Systems connection

Two habits from this lab carry straight into engineering work. First, quote a percentile with its error bar: a p99 latency from a ten-minute test has an interval nearly as wide as the number, and reliability chapter 9 explains why the tail is where fan-out makes the real damage. Second, treat a random generator as an input: every simulation here takes a seed so that a surprising result can be replayed, the same discipline chapter 4 asks of every experiment. Studio 1, Design an Experiment That Can Fail, asks you to use sample_size, a stopping rule and a pre-registered decision on a design of your own.

What you learned

  • The standard error is σ̂/√n with the n − 1 variance; the Wald interval for a proportion fails near 0 and 1 (a single point for 20 of 20), and the Wilson interval does not.
  • The bootstrap gives an interval for any statistic by resampling with replacement; for paired comparisons, resample the questions so each keeps both systems' results. It cannot see beyond the sample.
  • A permutation test shuffles labels under H0 and reports (count + 1)/(B + 1); for small data you can enumerate every split and get the exact answer, which is how you test your own implementation.
  • Halving the effect you want to detect costs four times the users. Underpowered experiments miss real effects, and the few that are significant overstate them (4.7 times in the example).
  • Stopping at the first p < 0.05 across ten looks turns a 5% false-positive rate into about 19%. A constant boundary near 2.56 restores 5%; Bonferroni is safe but conservative because looks are correlated.
  • You can test any statistical procedure by simulating a world where the truth is known: measure its coverage, its false-positive rate and its power before trusting it on real data.

Check your understanding

A safety check runs a model on 50 adversarial prompts and sees 0 failures. What do the Wald and Wilson 95% intervals for the failure rate say?

  1. Both give an interval from 0 to about 7%, since 0 of 50 is 0%
  2. Wald gives 0 to 7% and Wilson gives exactly 0 to 0, because the data show no failures
  3. Wald gives the single point 0 to 0, claiming certainty; Wilson gives 0 to about 7%, the honest range of rates compatible with no failures in 50
  4. Neither can be computed when the count is 0
With p̂ = 0 the Wald standard error √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) is exactly 0, so its interval collapses to the point 0 however small the sample. The Wilson interval is built from the rates that would make 0 of 50 unsurprising, and its upper end is z2/(n + z2) = 0.0713. The first option credits Wald with Wilson's answer; the second swaps the two intervals.

Two models are scored on the same 400 questions. They agree on 360 of them. You build a bootstrap interval for the accuracy difference by resampling A's results and B's results separately, each with replacement. What goes wrong?

  1. The interval is too wide: it discards the pairing, so the 360 questions on which they agree add noise to the difference that the paired resampling would not have
  2. The interval is too narrow, because resampling twice gives more data
  3. Nothing: resampling each list is equivalent to resampling the questions
  4. The interval is biased towards zero, because with-replacement samples average out differences
Separate resampling treats the two accuracies as independent, but they are not: both models find the same questions easy. Resampling question indices keeps each question's two results together, so the common part cancels in the difference and only the 40 disagreements carry information. The checker catches this with b = a + 0.1: the paired interval is the single point 0.1, and independent resampling gives a wide one. The interval is not biased, only too wide.

For two groups of five values, 6 of the 252 possible splits give a difference of means at least as large as the one you observed. Which statement is correct?

  1. The probability that the groups really are the same is 6/252, about 2.4%
  2. The probability that the observed difference is due to chance is 97.6%
  3. The experiment has 97.6% power
  4. If the group labels had no effect, a split at least this extreme would come up about 2.4% of the time; it is the p-value, not the probability that H0 is true
A p-value is a conditional probability computed assuming H0: how often chance alone would produce data this extreme. It cannot be turned into the probability of H0 without a prior (chapter 12's confusion of P(positive | healthy) and P(healthy | positive)). Power is a different quantity, defined for a specific true effect before the experiment.

A baseline converts at 10%. The team computes that 14,749 users per arm give 80% power to detect a lift of 1 point. A product manager wants to detect a lift of 0.5 points instead. About how many users per arm does that need?

  1. About 29,500, twice as many
  2. About 57,800, four times as many
  3. About 118,000, eight times as many
  4. The same, because power is fixed at 80%
The effect size is squared in the denominator of n = (zα/2 + zpower)2(…)/(p2 − p1)2, so halving the effect multiplies the sample by four: sample_size(0.10, 0.105) is 57,760. Doubling is the common guess, from thinking the standard error falls as 1/n. It falls as 1/√n, so noise is halved only by quadrupling the data.

You simulate A/A tests with ten looks and compare two stopping boundaries that both keep the overall false-positive rate at or below 5%: Bonferroni (z = 2.81) and a calibrated constant (z = 2.56, the 95th percentile of the per-path maximum of |z|). Why is the calibrated boundary lower?

  1. Because Bonferroni was computed with the wrong alpha
  2. Because the calibrated boundary allows more than 5% false positives
  3. Because the looks share data and are positively correlated, so ten looks give fewer than ten independent chances to cross; Bonferroni assumes the worst case and over-corrects
  4. Because the calibrated boundary uses a larger sample
Look 2 contains all of look 1's users (the correlation is about 0.71), so the ten z values move together and a path that crosses at one look often crosses at its neighbours. The union of ten overlapping events has probability well below ten times one of them; Bonferroni adds the probabilities as if they never overlapped. The calibrated boundary hits 5.0% by construction, and Bonferroni's 2.3% is unused error budget that costs power.

A team runs many small experiments, each with about 7% power for the true effect, and ships whichever ones reach p < 0.05. The shipped changes average a measured lift of +2.3 points. What should they expect when they measure the real effect later?

  1. A much smaller lift, near the true value of about +0.5 points: selecting on significance picked the experiments where noise inflated the estimate
  2. About +2.3 points again, because the experiments were individually significant
  3. No lift at all, because underpowered experiments cannot find real effects
  4. A larger lift, because only the most promising changes reach significance
This is the winner's curse. With 7% power a result can clear the bar only if the observed lift is large, which happens mainly when noise pushed it up. The effects are real (so "no lift at all" is wrong) but the estimates of the winners are about 4.7 times too large in the lab's simulation. Averaged over all experiments the estimate is accurate; it is the filter that inflates it.
