Lab 3: The Optimiser Race

Chapter 10 explained what SGD, momentum, RMSProp, Adam and AdamW do and showed their results on two test surfaces. In this lab you write all five yourself, over plain Python lists, with the learning-rate schedules and gradient clipping that surround them in every real training run. Then you race them, each at its own best learning rate, and reproduce the table from the chapter number for number. After this lab, torch.optim.AdamW is a function whose every line you could have written.

Why this lab matters

The optimiser is the part of a training run that most often fails without announcing it. A learning rate a little above the stability limit gives nan at step 40,000. An Adam copied from another codebase with L2 regularisation in place of weight decay trains, but regularises the wrong weights. A schedule with no warmup diverges in the first hundred steps; a run with no clipping survives until one bad batch arrives. Each of these is a few lines of arithmetic, and each is easy to get wrong when you have only ever called the library version.

The habit this lab installs is testing an optimiser against its definition. You will check each update rule against an independent, unrolled formula (Adam's m written out as a weighted sum of every past gradient, for example), and you will check each property that the chapter claims: Adam's first step has the size of the learning rate whatever the gradient, AdamW's decay is the same fraction for a weight of 0.1 and a weight of 10, a clipped gradient keeps its direction. The same updates, scaled up, train every model in A2 and A3, and the optimiser state you create here is the extra memory that A3 chapter 2 budgets for.

Pure Python, deterministic, under a second

Standard library only (math and random), Python 3.9 or newer. The test surfaces are formulas, so no run depends on a random number except the noisy problem in milestone 6, which draws from random.Random(seed). A failure prints its seed or the exact inputs: python3 check.py solution 3 12 replays milestone 3 on seed 12 only. The independent oracles in check.py never use your classes. The full check takes about a fifth of a second, so run it after every edit.

What you'll build

Notation follows chapter 10. The weights are a list w, the gradient of the loss is g (∇L in the maths), and the learning rate is η (lr in code). An optimiser here is an object with one method, step(w, g), that returns the new weights as a fresh list and keeps whatever state it needs between calls: a velocity, a running average of squared gradients, a step counter. Because the state lives in the object, one object serves one run.

  1. Surfaces and SGD: the gradients of the narrow valley and of Rosenbrock's banana, plain gradient descent w ← w − ηg, and a loop that detects a blow-up. You see the stability limit η < 2/κ appear in numbers.
  2. Momentum: a velocity v ← βv + g, so successive gradients add up where they agree and cancel where they alternate.
  3. RMSProp and Adam: divide each coordinate's step by a running estimate of its own gradient size; add momentum and bias correction to get Adam.
  4. L2 penalty versus AdamW: the same weight decay done two ways, with a flat-loss experiment that shows why they differ.
  5. Schedules and clipping: warmup then cosine decay, warmup-stable-decay, and clipping by global norm.
  6. The race: choose each optimiser's learning rate by grid search, reproduce the chapter's table, then train a noisy problem with a schedule and with clipping.

Milestone 1: surfaces, SGD and the loop

An optimiser is only interesting on a surface that is hard to descend, so the lab uses three. You write the first two and are given the third.

The narrow valley is f(x, y) = ½(x2 + κy2) with κ = 100. It is a bowl with its minimum at the origin, and the second coordinate is 100 times steeper than the first. Its gradient is (x, κy). Rosenbrock's banana, f(x, y) = (1 − x)2 + 100(y − x2)2, is a curved narrow valley with its minimum, f = 0, at (1, 1); it has been a standard test of optimisers since Howard Rosenbrock proposed it in 1960. Write r = y − x2. By the chain rule (chapter 8) ∂r/∂x = −2x and ∂r/∂y = 1, so

∂f/∂x = −2(1 − x) − 400 x r,    ∂f/∂y = 200 r.

Derivatives written by hand are easy to get wrong, and check.py applies the habit from Lab 2: it compares your gradient with central differences of your own f at random points. If the two disagree, one of them has a slip, and the message tells you which numbers to compare.

The third surface, double_well, is f(x, y) = (x2 − 1)2 + ½y2, with two equally good minima at (−1, 0) and (1, 0) and a hump between them. Which minimum a method reaches depends on where it starts and how it moves, which makes it a good toy for the "Going further" section.

Now the step. Plain gradient descent moves every weight against its gradient: w ← w − ηg. On the valley the two coordinates do not interact, so each is multiplied by its own fixed factor every step: x by (1 − η) and y by (1 − ηκ). The run converges only if both factors have magnitude below 1, and the second is the harder one: 1 − ηκ > −1 means η < 2/κ. This is the stability limit from the chapter, and it is sharp. The script below runs 3,000 steps at six learning rates around the limit 2/κ = 0.02:

from solution import SGD, bowl, minimise

print("kappa = 100, so the stability limit is 2/kappa = 0.02; 3000 steps from (1, 1)")
print(f"{'eta':>7} {'1-100*eta':>10}  outcome")
for eta in (0.005, 0.01, 0.015, 0.0199, 0.02, 0.0205):
    t = minimise(SGD(eta), bowl, [1.0, 1.0], 3000)
    if t.diverged:
        outcome = f"diverged after {len(t.losses) - 1} steps"
    else:
        outcome = f"final loss {t.losses[-1]:.3e}"
    print(f"{eta:7.4f} {1 - 100 * eta:10.4f}  {outcome}")

At η = 0.0199 the factor on y is −0.99: the coordinate flips sign every step and still shrinks, slowly enough to be reached in 3,000 steps. At exactly 0.02 the factor is −1 and the iterate bounces between +1 and −1 for ever, a loss stuck at 50. At 0.0205 the factor is −1.05 and y grows by 5% a step until the loss passes 1012. Notice too that the best rate here is 0.015, not the largest stable one: the best fixed rate is the one that suits both directions as well as possible, and with a ratio of 100 between them no rate does well on both. That is the problem momentum and Adam attack.

The loop has one design decision. minimise(opt, fn, w0, steps) returns a Trace holding the final weights, the loss before the first step and after each step, and a diverged flag. It must stop and report when the loss is not finite or above 1012, rather than keep going: a diverging run overflows to inf, then nan, and a race that crashed or compared nan values on one bad learning rate would be useless. Treating a blow-up as a result (loss inf) is what lets milestone 6 search over rates that include bad ones.

Common misconception

"If the loss goes up, the gradient code must be wrong." It can be, and a gradient check settles it in a minute, but a loss that rises or oscillates is equally often a learning rate beyond the stability limit, which is what you just measured at 0.0205. The two causes need opposite fixes: one is a bug to find, the other a setting to lower. Check the gradient against finite differences first, then halve the rate.

def bowl(w, kappa=100.0):
    """The narrow valley f(x, y) = 0.5 * (x^2 + kappa * y^2): returns (f, [df/dx, df/dy])."""
    x, y = w
    return 0.5 * (x * x + kappa * y * y), [x, kappa * y]

def rosenbrock(w):
    """Rosenbrock's banana f(x, y) = (1 - x)^2 + 100 (y - x^2)^2, minimum 0 at (1, 1).

    Returns (f, [df/dx, df/dy]).
    """
    x, y = w
    r = y - x * x
    return (1 - x) ** 2 + 100 * r * r, [-2 * (1 - x) - 400 * x * r, 200 * r]

class SGD:
    """Plain gradient descent: w <- w - lr * g."""

    def __init__(self, lr):
        self.lr = lr

    def step(self, w, g):
        """Return the new weights: SGD(0.1).step([1.0, 2.0], [1.0, -1.0]) == [0.9, 2.1].

        Return a NEW list; do not modify w or g.
        """
        return [wi - self.lr * gi for wi, gi in zip(w, g)]

def minimise(opt, fn, w0, steps):
    """Run `steps` optimiser steps on fn from w0 and return a Trace.

    fn(w) returns (loss, gradient). Record the loss before the first step and after each
    step. If the loss is not finite or exceeds DIVERGED, stop: set diverged=True and make
    the last entry of `losses` math.inf.

    Example: on f(w) = w^2 with SGD(0.25) from [1.0], each step halves w, so the losses are
    [1.0, 0.25, 0.0625, ...] and `Trace.w` is the weights after the last step.
    Do not modify w0.
    """
    w = list(w0)
    losses = []
    for _ in range(steps + 1):
        f, g = fn(w)
        if not math.isfinite(f) or f > DIVERGED:
            losses.append(math.inf)
            return Trace(w, losses, True)
        losses.append(f)
        if len(losses) == steps + 1:
            break
        w = opt.step(w, g)
    return Trace(w, losses, False)

Milestone 2: momentum

The valley shows what is wrong with a single step size. Along the steep direction the iterate bounces from wall to wall, flipping the sign of y every step; along the gentle direction it creeps. Look at the gradients, though: the steep component alternates in sign (+, −, +, −) while the gentle component keeps the same sign step after step. If you keep a running sum of recent gradients, the alternating parts cancel and the consistent part adds up. That is momentum (the heavy-ball method, Polyak, 1964): keep a velocity v, add each new gradient to a decayed copy of it, and step along the velocity.

v ← βv + g,    w ← w − ηv,    β = 0.9 by default.

Unrolling the recurrence shows what v is: vt = gt + βgt−1 + β2gt−2 + …, an exponentially weighted sum of every past gradient, with an effective memory of about 1/(1 − β) = 10 steps. The check uses exactly this formula as its oracle. If the gradient is constant, the weights of the sum add to 1 + β + β2 + … = 1/(1 − β), so v settles at 10g and each step is ten times larger than plain SGD's. The script feeds a constant gradient of 1 to momentum with η = 0.01:

from solution import Momentum

opt, w = Momentum(0.01, 0.9), [0.0]
for t in range(1, 41):
    prev, w = w, opt.step(w, [1.0])             # the gradient is 1 at every step
    if t in (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40):
        print(f"step {t:2d}: the weight moved {prev[0] - w[0]:.5f}")
print("plain SGD with lr 0.01 would move 0.01000 every step; the limit is lr / (1 - beta) = 0.1")

step  2: the weight moved 0.01900

The step grows from 0.01 to 0.019, 0.04, 0.065 and settles at 0.1 (1 − 0.9t of the way there after t steps: 0.0985 by step 40). Two consequences matter in practice. The learning rate that suited SGD is too large once momentum is added, because the same η now means up to ten times the movement in a consistent direction; a typical tuned rate with β = 0.9 is several times smaller (0.0051 against 0.0189 on the valley, as you will see in the race). And the speed-up is not free: momentum gives the iterate inertia, so on a problem that did not need help it overshoots and oscillates, which is why chapter 10 found it slower than plain descent at κ = 10 and much faster at κ = 1,000.

The velocity is state, so the class stores it as self.v, created on the first call because only then do you know how many weights there are. Store it on the instance, not in a default argument or a class attribute: the check builds two optimisers and makes sure they do not share a velocity.

Solution spoiler 
class Momentum:
    """Heavy-ball momentum, as in chapter 10: v <- beta * v + g, then w <- w - lr * v."""

    def __init__(self, lr, beta=0.9):
        self.lr, self.beta, self.v = lr, beta, None

    def step(self, w, g):
        """The velocity starts at zero. With lr 0.1, beta 0.9 and g = [1, 2] twice from [0, 0]:
        v = [1, 2] then [1.9, 3.8], so w goes [-0.1, -0.2] then [-0.29, -0.58]."""
        if self.v is None:
            self.v = [0.0] * len(w)
        self.v = [self.beta * vi + gi for vi, gi in zip(self.v, g)]
        return [wi - self.lr * vi for wi, vi in zip(w, self.v)]

Milestone 3: RMSProp and Adam

Momentum smooths the direction. A second idea fixes the scale. If one weight's gradient is typically a hundred times larger than another's, a single learning rate is wrong for at least one of them. The remedy is to divide each coordinate's gradient by a running estimate of its own typical size. RMSProp (Tieleman and Hinton, 2012, in a lecture slide, never a paper) keeps an exponential moving average of the squared gradient, s ← βs + (1 − β)g2, and steps by ηg/(√s + ε). An exponential moving average is a weighted average whose weights fade geometrically into the past; here β = 0.9 gives a memory of about ten steps. The small constant ε = 10−8 keeps the division finite when s is near 0.

Look at the first step to see what this does. Starting from s = 0, s = 0.1g2, so the step is ηg/(0.316|g| + ε), about 3.16η in the downhill direction whatever the size of g. A gradient of 0.001 and a gradient of 1,000 move the weight by the same amount. The step length is set by the learning rate and not by the gradient, which is the whole point, and also the reason RMSProp with a fixed rate hops around a minimum at a distance of order η instead of settling into it.

Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014, Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization) combines the two ideas: momentum's average of the gradient for the direction, RMSProp's average of the squared gradient for the scale. It keeps two numbers per weight,

m ← β1m + (1 − β1)g,    v ← β2v + (1 − β2)g2,

with β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999. Both start at 0, which biases them low early on: after one step m is 0.1g and v is only 0.001g2. The fix is bias correction: divide by (1 − βt), where t counts steps from 1. At t = 1 that is 0.1 and 0.001, which restores m̂ = g and v̂ = g2 exactly. The update is

w ← w − η m̂ / (√v̂ + ε).

Two properties follow, and the check tests both. The first step is ηg/(|g| + ε), about η times the sign of g. And with a constant gradient, the corrected averages equal the gradient at every t, so every step has size η exactly: a handy test, because it fails at once if the correction is missing or t starts at 0. Gradients that differ by a factor of a million produce identical first steps, as the script shows (SGD and momentum move in proportion to the gradient; RMSProp moves 3.16η; Adam moves η):

This is why Adam is forgiving across weights of very different scale (an embedding next to a layer norm) and why its learning rate means something different from SGD's: it is roughly a step length in weight units, typically 10−4 to 10−3 for transformers.

Common misconception

"Adam needs no tuning because it adapts the step size." It adapts the step size per weight relative to gradient scale, which removes one tuning problem (badly scaled gradients) and leaves the main one. The learning rate still decides whether training works: the same bounded-step property that makes Adam robust makes it hop around the minimum if η is too large. On Rosenbrock in the race below, Adam's best rate sits at the edge of the grid, 0.5, which tells you the search was not wide enough, not that Adam is magic.

class RMSProp:
    """s <- beta * s + (1 - beta) * g^2 (per coordinate); w <- w - lr * g / (sqrt(s) + eps)."""

    def __init__(self, lr, beta=0.9, eps=1e-8):
        self.lr, self.beta, self.eps, self.s = lr, beta, eps, None

    def step(self, w, g):
        """Per coordinate. The first step is lr * g / (sqrt(0.1) |g| + eps): about 3.16 * lr in the
        downhill direction, whatever the size of g. Keep eps OUTSIDE the square root."""
        if self.s is None:
            self.s = [0.0] * len(w)
        self.s = [self.beta * si + (1 - self.beta) * gi * gi for si, gi in zip(self.s, g)]
        return [wi - self.lr * gi / (math.sqrt(si) + self.eps) for wi, gi, si in zip(w, g, self.s)]

class Adam:
    """Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014): a momentum average m, a scale average v, bias-corrected.

    t <- t + 1;  m <- b1 m + (1 - b1) g;  v <- b2 v + (1 - b2) g^2
    m_hat = m / (1 - b1^t);  v_hat = v / (1 - b2^t);  w <- w - lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
    """

    def __init__(self, lr, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
        self.lr, self.b1, self.b2, self.eps = lr, b1, b2, eps
        self.m = self.v = None
        self.t = 0

    def step(self, w, g):
        """Per coordinate, with t counting steps from 1. After bias correction the first step is
        lr * g / (|g| + eps), about lr * sign(g); with a constant gradient EVERY step is."""
        if self.m is None:
            self.m, self.v = [0.0] * len(w), [0.0] * len(w)
        self.t += 1
        self.m = [self.b1 * m + (1 - self.b1) * gi for m, gi in zip(self.m, g)]
        self.v = [self.b2 * v + (1 - self.b2) * gi * gi for v, gi in zip(self.v, g)]
        c1, c2 = 1 - self.b1 ** self.t, 1 - self.b2 ** self.t          # bias corrections
        return [wi - self.lr * (m / c1) / (math.sqrt(v / c2) + self.eps)
                for wi, m, v in zip(w, self.m, self.v)]

Milestone 4: L2 penalty versus AdamW

Weight decay shrinks every weight a little on each step, w ← (1 − ηλ)w, which discourages large weights and acts as regularisation (A1, chapter 6). For plain SGD it is the same thing as adding the L2 penalty ½λ|w|2 to the loss, because that penalty's gradient is λw and the SGD step on it is exactly the shrink. The equivalence is the trap. It holds for SGD and fails for Adam.

To see why, follow the decay through Adam. If you add λw to the gradient (the L2 form), it goes into m and v and is divided by √v̂ with everything else. A weight with a large typical gradient has a large v̂, so its decay is divided by a large number and is nearly cancelled; a weight with a small gradient has a small v̂, and its decay is boosted. The regularisation each weight feels then depends on its gradient scale, not on the λ you chose. AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization, 2017; ICLR 2019) fixes this by taking the decay out of the gradient and applying it directly to the weights, outside the Adam machinery:

w ← w − η( m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) + λw ).

The decay term is ηλw, the same fraction of every weight, whatever its gradient. The two classes you write are tiny, and they differ in one line. Here is the experiment that shows the difference most cleanly: make the loss flat, so its gradient is 0, and let only the decay act on a small weight (0.1) and a large one (10).

from solution import AdamW, AdamL2

# The loss is flat (its gradient is 0), so only the weight decay acts on the weight.
for w0 in (0.1, 10.0):
    left = []
    for opt in (AdamW(0.01, 1.0), AdamL2(0.01, 1.0)):
        w = [w0]
        for _ in range(100):
            w = opt.step(w, [0.0])
        left.append(w[0] / w0)

AdamW shrinks both weights to 36.6% of their starting value, exactly (1 − 0.01)100: a decay in proportion to the weight, as the name promises. With the penalty inside the gradient, Adam sees g = λw, normalises it to a step of about η in weight units, and so subtracts about 0.01 per step from every weight: the small weight is driven to nearly 0 (it crosses zero at step 11 and hovers around it) while the large weight loses only 10% of its size. The same λ regularises small weights hard and large weights hardly at all. In a real network the gradients of the loss are present too, but the lesson carries over: in an adaptive optimiser, L2 regularisation and weight decay are different things. The framework you use makes the choice for you: in PyTorch, Adam(weight_decay=...) adds the penalty to the gradient, and AdamW(weight_decay=...) decouples it. Case 3 follows the history of that distinction.

One detail of convention. Loshchilov and Hutter's update, and PyTorch's, decay the weights you were given and then apply the Adam step, which is what the lab asks for. The listing in chapter 10 applies the decay to the weights after the Adam step; the two differ by a term of order η2λ, which is invisible in practice, and the zero-gradient experiment above gives the same numbers for both.

class AdamL2(Adam):
    """Adam with an L2 penalty: the penalty's gradient l2 * w is ADDED TO g before Adam sees it."""

    def __init__(self, lr, l2, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
        super().__init__(lr, b1, b2, eps)
        self.l2 = l2

    def step(self, w, g):
        """Adam's step on the gradient g + l2 * w. First step of AdamL2(0.1, 0.5) from [1, -2]
        with g = [0.5, 0.5]: Adam sees [1.0, -0.5], so w becomes [0.9, -1.9]."""
        # The decay now travels through m and v, so it is divided by sqrt(v_hat) like any gradient.
        return super().step(w, [gi + self.l2 * wi for wi, gi in zip(w, g)])

class AdamW(Adam):
    """AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter): decay the weights directly, outside the Adam machinery.

    w <- w * (1 - lr * weight_decay), using the weights you were GIVEN, then the ordinary Adam
    step with the unmodified gradient g.
    """

    def __init__(self, lr, weight_decay, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
        super().__init__(lr, b1, b2, eps)
        self.weight_decay = weight_decay

    def step(self, w, g):
        """First step of AdamW(0.1, 0.5) from [1, -2] with g = [0.5, 0.5]: decay to [0.95, -1.9],
        then Adam moves each coordinate 0.1 downhill: [0.85, -2.0]."""
        decayed = [wi * (1 - self.lr * self.weight_decay) for wi in w]
        adam = super().step(w, g)                         # w - lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
        return [a - (wi - d) for a, wi, d in zip(adam, w, decayed)]   # apply the decay on top

Milestone 5: schedules and clipping

A good learning rate is not constant over a run. Early on you want a large step to make progress; late in the run a large step makes the iterate jitter around the minimum instead of settling in it. Practice has converged on a learning-rate schedule: a function of the step number t that multiplies the peak rate, with these pieces.

  • Warmup. Ramp linearly from 0 to the peak over the first warmup steps. Early in training Adam's v̂ is estimated from very few gradients and the weights are random, so full-size steps can be destabilising; warmup lets the statistics settle first.
  • Cosine decay. After warmup, follow half a cosine wave from 1 down to a floor (often 10% of the peak). The formula is floor + (1 − floor) · ½(1 + cos(πp)), where p = (t − warmup)/(total − warmup) runs from 0 to 1. The total length must be chosen in advance.
  • Warmup-stable-decay (WSD). Hold the peak until decay_start, then decay linearly to the floor. Because the stable phase does not depend on the final length, a run can be extended or branched to make checkpoints at several budgets; it was described, for example, in the MiniCPM report (Hu et al., 2024).

Two edge cases deserve a thought. At warmup = 0 there is no ramp and the schedule must start at its peak, 1.0, which means the t / warmup branch must never be reached. And the cosine's progress p reaches exactly 1 at t = total, so the floor is hit on the last step and not before.

The second guard is gradient clipping. Occasionally a batch produces an enormous gradient: a rare example, a numerical glitch, a region of sharp curvature. One such step can throw the weights far from where they were and wreck a run. Clipping by global norm is the standard defence. Treat all the gradients of all the parameters as one long vector and compute its L2 norm, √(∑ gi2). If that exceeds a threshold c (often 1.0), multiply every gradient by the same factor c/norm. One common factor means the direction of the update is preserved and only its length is capped; clipping each tensor or each element separately would change the direction. A gradient already inside the limit must pass through untouched.

    print(f"max_norm {limit:4.1f}: norm {norm:.3f}, scale {scale:.4f}, result {[[round(x, 4) for x in g] for g in clipped]}")

def warmup_cosine(t, total, warmup, floor=0.1):
    """Learning-rate multiplier at step t: linear warmup 0 -> 1 over `warmup` steps, then a
    half cosine from 1 down to `floor` at step `total`."""
    if t < warmup:
        return t / warmup
    progress = (t - warmup) / (total - warmup)
    return floor + (1 - floor) * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

def wsd(t, total, warmup, decay_start, floor=0.1):
    """Warmup-stable-decay: warm up to 1, hold 1 until `decay_start`, then decay linearly to
    `floor` at step `total`."""
    if t < warmup:
        return t / warmup
    if t < decay_start:
        return 1.0
    return 1.0 - (1 - floor) * (t - decay_start) / (total - decay_start)

def global_norm(grads):
    """The L2 norm of ALL the gradients together: grads is a list of lists of floats."""
    return math.sqrt(sum(x * x for g in grads for x in g))

def clip_by_global_norm(grads, max_norm):
    """Scale every gradient by ONE common factor so the global norm is at most max_norm.

    Returns (clipped_grads, norm_before, scale), with scale = min(1, max_norm / (norm + 1e-6)).
    A gradient already within the limit passes through with scale exactly 1.0.
    Example: clip_by_global_norm([[3.0], [4.0]], 1.0) -> ([[0.6], [0.8]], 5.0, 0.2) up to the epsilon.
    Return new lists; do not modify grads.
    """
    norm = global_norm(grads)
    scale = min(1.0, max_norm / (norm + 1e-6))
    return [[x * scale for x in g] for g in grads], norm, scale

Milestone 6: the race

Which optimiser is best depends on the surface, and the learning rate usually matters more than the choice of optimiser. A race with one shared rate is therefore unfair, because each method has a different useful range: SGD's is bounded by 2/κ and Adam's step is in weight units. The fair comparison gives every optimiser its own best rate. best_lr(make, fn, w0, grid, steps) tries every rate in GRID (14 values evenly spaced in the logarithm from 10−4 to 0.5), runs steps steps, skips rates that diverge, and returns the rate with the lowest final loss. Your milestone-1 minimise is what makes skipping cheap.

The last function is train(opt, w0, grad_fn, steps, schedule=None, max_norm=None), the loop that real training code is a descendant of. def best_lr(make, fn, w0, grid, steps):
    """Try every learning rate in `grid` (make(lr) builds a fresh optimiser) and return
    (lr, final_loss) for the one with the lowest final loss. Rates that diverge are skipped;
    if all diverge return (None, math.inf). Ties go to the smaller rate."""
    best = (None, math.inf)
    for lr in grid:
        trace = minimise(make(lr), fn, w0, steps)
        if not trace.diverged and trace.losses[-1] < best[1]:
            best = (lr, trace.losses[-1])
    return best

def train(opt, w0, grad_fn, steps, schedule=None, max_norm=None):
    """Train with an optional learning-rate schedule and gradient clipping.

    grad_fn(w, t) returns (loss, gradient) at step t (t = 0, 1, ...). Remember base = opt.lr.
    At each step t: evaluate grad_fn at the current w, set opt.lr = base * schedule(t) (if a
    schedule is given), clip the gradient to max_norm by global norm (if given; count the
    steps where it was actually shortened), then take the optimiser step.

    Returns a dict: {"w": final weights, "losses": the loss seen at each step (length steps),
    "lrs": the learning rate used at each step, "clipped": number of clipped steps}.
    """
    w = list(w0)
    base = opt.lr
    losses, lrs, clipped = [], [], 0
    for t in range(steps):
        loss, g = grad_fn(w, t)
        if schedule is not None:
            opt.lr = base * schedule(t)
        if max_norm is not None:
            (g,), _, scale = clip_by_global_norm([g], max_norm)
            clipped += scale < 1.0
        losses.append(loss)
        lrs.append(opt.lr)
        w = opt.step(w, g)
    opt.lr = base
    return {"w": w, "losses": losses, "lrs": lrs, "clipped": clipped}

  • Gradient noise and batch size. Change noise in noisy_problem to mimic a batch four times larger (half the noise) and check that the final-loss floor drops by about a factor of four. Systems connection

Count what your optimisers store. SGD keeps nothing, momentum one number per weight, Adam two, and AdamW the same two. For a model with N parameters trained in 32-bit floats, that is 4N bytes for the weights, 4N for the gradients and 8N for Adam's m and v: 16N bytes before a single activation, which is 112 GB for a 7-billion-parameter model (A3, chapter 2). The optimiser is usually the largest single item in the training memory budget, which is why sharding it across devices (A3, chapter 3) and shrinking it are active topics. The fraction of steps clipped, the global gradient norm and the learning rate at each step are also the first three metrics worth putting on a training dashboard, in the same way that error rate and latency percentiles are the first metrics of a service (reliability, chapter 4): a run that is about to diverge usually shows it there before the loss does.

What you learned

  • Gradient descent on a quadratic multiplies each direction by 1 − ηλ; it is stable only for η < 2/λmax, and a ratio of 100 between curvatures forces a rate that is slow in the gentle direction.
  • Momentum is an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients: it multiplies consistent directions by 1/(1 − β) and cancels alternating ones, so it needs a smaller learning rate and helps most on ill-conditioned problems.
  • RMSProp and Adam divide by a running gradient scale, so their step is about η per weight whatever the gradient size; Adam's bias correction makes that exact from the first step.
  • Inside Adam an L2 penalty is rescaled by each weight's gradient history; AdamW decays every weight by the same fraction. They are the same only for SGD.
  • Schedules (warmup, then decay) and global-norm clipping wrap the optimiser; clipping scales all gradients by one factor so the direction survives.
  • Compare optimisers at their own best learning rate, treat a blow-up as a result, and distrust a verdict from two test functions.

Check your understanding

You run SGD with η = 0.025 on f(x, y) = ½(x2 + 100y2) from (1, 1). What happens?

  1. It converges slowly, because 0.025 is smaller than 1
  2. It converges, flipping the sign of y at every step
  3. It diverges: y is multiplied by 1 − 100 × 0.025 = −1.5 each step, even though x converges
  4. It bounces between the same two points for ever
The coordinates decouple. x is multiplied by 0.975 and shrinks; y is multiplied by −1.5, so its magnitude grows by half each step and the loss explodes. The stability limit is 2/κ = 0.02, set by the steepest direction. Flipping sign while converging needs a factor between −1 and 0 (a rate between 0.01 and 0.02); bouncing forever needs the factor to be exactly −1, a rate of exactly 0.02.

A training run uses Adam with η = 0.001 and a loss averaged over the batch. A refactor changes it to a loss summed over a batch of 1,000, so every gradient is 1,000 times larger. What is the effect on the first updates?

  1. Almost none: Adam divides by the gradient's running scale, so the steps stay about 0.001 per weight (SGD, in contrast, would take steps 1,000 times larger)
  2. The steps become 1,000 times larger, as with SGD, so training diverges
  3. The steps become √1,000 times larger, since Adam uses a square root
  4. The bias correction cancels the change only for the first step; later steps are 1,000 times larger
m̂ scales with the gradient and √v̂ scales with it too, so their ratio does not change (ε = 10−8 is negligible next to gradients of ordinary size). This scale invariance is a real property, and it also hides a pitfall: the loss scaling no longer changes Adam's steps, but it does change the meaning of gradient clipping thresholds and weight-decay settings that are tied to the gradient's size. The last option confuses the first step with all steps; bias correction only matters early, and the ratio is scale-free at every step.

Adam with an L2 penalty added to the gradient (not AdamW) is used on a network where some weights have large gradients and others tiny ones. Compared with AdamW at the same λ, which weights are decayed least?

  1. Those with tiny gradients, because there is little else to cancel the decay
  2. All weights are decayed equally, as both methods implement the same penalty
  3. The weights with the largest values, because the penalty is proportional to w
  4. Those with large gradients, because the penalty is divided by a large √v̂
The L2 term goes through m and v and is divided by √v̂ with the rest of the gradient, so its effect shrinks as the weight's gradient history grows; weights with small gradients are decayed the most. AdamW takes the decay outside the division, so all weights lose the same fraction ηλ per step. The flat-loss experiment shows the extreme case: Adam + L2 removed 99.7% of a weight of 0.1 and 10% of a weight of 10 in the same 100 steps.

A model has two parameter tensors whose gradients are [[12], [16]], a global norm of 20. You clip by global norm to 5. What do the clipped gradients look like?

  1. [[5], [5]]: every element is capped at 5
  2. [[3], [4]]: both multiplied by 5/20 = 0.25
  3. [[0.6], [0.8]]: rescaled to a unit vector
  4. [[12], [16]]: unchanged, because neither element exceeds 20
Global-norm clipping multiplies every gradient by the same factor, max_norm/norm = 0.25, so the result [[3], [4]] has norm exactly 5 and the same direction as before. Capping each element at 5 changes the direction (it would turn a 3:4 ratio into 1:1). Rescaling to unit norm would shrink every gradient to length 1, including small ones that are within the limit; clipping leaves in-limit gradients alone.

You tuned SGD at η = 0.1 on a smooth problem and it trains stably. You switch to momentum with β = 0.9 and keep η = 0.1. What should you expect?

  1. The same behaviour, slightly faster
  2. Slower training, because the velocity carries old information
  3. Steps up to ten times larger in a consistent direction, which may overshoot or diverge: the rate usually needs to be cut
  4. Nothing at all, because β only matters on ill-conditioned problems
With a steady gradient the velocity settles at g/(1 − β) = 10g, so the effective step is ten times SGD's (the momentum demo shows a step of 0.1 for η = 0.01). If the old rate was close to the stability limit, the new effective rate is far beyond it. In the race, momentum's best rate on the valley was 0.0051, about a quarter of SGD's 0.0189. It does not follow that momentum is slower: tuned, it is faster on ill-conditioned problems.
