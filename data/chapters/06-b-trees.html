B-Trees

Most of the SQL databases you will ever meet keep their data in a B-tree: a wide, shallow tree of fixed-size pages that finds any one of billions of keys by reading three or four pages. This chapter builds the structure from the disk up, watches pages split, and explains why a crash in the middle of a split is the reason databases keep a write-ahead log.

Why this matters

The log-structured engines of chapter 4 and chapter 5 are one answer to "how do I store sorted data on disk". The B-tree, invented in the early 1970s and still the default almost everywhere, is the other. When you create an index in PostgreSQL, MySQL or SQLite, you are building a B-tree. Knowing what a page is, why the tree is so shallow, and what an insert physically does lets you reason about index sizes, write costs, cache behaviour and why some workloads (Case 4) push teams to change databases.

Disks like pages

Back in the library of chapter 0, you do not fetch one word at a time from the shelf; you fetch a book. Storage devices work the same way. A spinning disk spends milliseconds moving its head to the right spot, and once it is there, reading a few more kilobytes costs almost nothing extra. An SSD is built from flash pages (a few KiB to 16 KiB, depending on the device) that it reads and programs as a unit. In both cases, asking for 1 byte costs about the same as asking for several thousand. This is the memory-hierarchy lesson from systems chapter 2: the unit of transfer between levels is a block, and the file system adds its own blocks on top (systems chapter 8).

So databases organise their files into fixed-size pages (also called blocks), and read and write whole pages. Typical sizes are 4 to 16 KiB. PostgreSQL uses 8 KiB by default, and InnoDB (MySQL's default engine) uses 16 KiB by default. SQLite defaults to 4 KiB. Everything below is built on one rule:

The unit of cost is the page. The cost of a lookup is roughly the number of pages it has to read from disk, not the number of keys it compares. A design that reads 3 pages beats one that reads 30, even if the first does more CPU work per page. Comparing keys inside a page already in RAM takes nanoseconds each; fetching a page from disk takes tens of microseconds on an NVMe SSD and milliseconds on a spinning disk.

What a B-tree looks like

A B-tree is a balanced search tree designed around that rule. Instead of two children per node (as in a textbook binary tree), each node is one whole page and has hundreds of children. Pages come in two kinds:

  • Internal pages hold sorted separator keys and child page numbers: P0, k1, P1, k2, P2, …. Everything under P0 is less than k1; everything under P1 is at least k1 but less than k2; and so on.
  • Leaf pages hold the actual keys, each with its value (the row, or a pointer to it).

The top page is the root. The number of children per internal page is the branching factor (or fan-out). Every leaf is at the same depth, which is what "balanced" means here and what makes lookup time predictable.

The arithmetic of a shallow tree

How many keys can a tree of a given depth hold? Take an 8 KiB page and 8-byte keys and child pointers. Each key-and-pointer pair takes about 16 bytes, so a page could hold up to about 8192 / 16 = 512 of them. Real pages are not packed full (a page must leave room to grow, and random inserts leave pages around 70% full on average), so call it a branching factor of about 358. Then:

Three levels hold roughly 358 × 358 × 358 ≈ 46 million keys, and four levels roughly 16 billion. A lookup in a table of tens of millions of rows reads about three pages; a lookup among billions reads about four. And because the root and the next level are tiny (the top two levels total only about 3 MiB), they stay cached in RAM, so in practice the disk is touched once or twice per lookup. Compare that with a balanced binary tree over 46 million keys, which is about 26 levels deep: 26 hops, and nearly every one a separate page.

Analogy: the library's shelf-end signs

Walk into a big library and you do not scan every shelf. A sign at the entrance sends you to a floor, a sign on the floor to an aisle, and a label on the aisle's end to a shelf. Three or four glances and you are looking at the right shelf, where you scan a few dozen books by hand. The signs are internal pages; the shelves are leaf pages; and the library is "wide" at each step: one sign points to dozens of aisles.

Where it breaks down: a library's shelves are fixed. A B-tree has to keep itself balanced while books are added and removed, by splitting full shelves in two, which is the next section.

Search: walk down from the root

Searching is a single descent. Read the root page. Binary-search its keys (cheap, it is in RAM now) to find which child covers your key. Read that child. Repeat until you reach a leaf, then search the leaf.

The cost is the height of the tree, always. There is no best case where the key happens to be near the front; there is no worst case where it is buried. That evenness is a feature.

Insert, page splits, and how the tree grows

To insert, search for where the key would be, and add it to that leaf, keeping the leaf sorted. If the leaf has room, you are done. If not, the page is full, and the page has to be split:

  1. Allocate a new page and move the upper half of the full leaf's keys into it.
  2. Tell the parent. Add a new separator key (the first key of the new page) and a pointer to the new page into the parent internal page.
  3. If the parent is now over-full, split it too, moving its middle key up one level, and so on.
  4. If the root splits, create a new root above the two halves. This is the only way a B-tree gets taller, and it happens at the top: every leaf moves down one level together, so the tree stays balanced.

Splits are rare: with a branching factor of hundreds, a given leaf splits once every few hundred inserts into it, and a parent splits a few hundred times less often. A root split is rarer still: it happens only a handful of times in a table's life. But when a split does happen, one logical insert has changed several pages, which matters for crashes, as we will see.

Simplified, on purpose

The playground keeps only keys, unique ones, and splits at fixed points. Real pages store variable-length keys, so a page is "full" when its bytes run out, not when it hits a key count; real engines also differ in where they split (some split unevenly when inserts arrive in order, to leave earlier pages packed). The mechanics (descend, insert, split, push a separator up, maybe grow the root) are the real ones.

B+ trees: values only in leaves, leaves linked

What we have been drawing is, strictly, a B+ tree, and it is the variant nearly every database means by "B-tree". Two choices define it:

  1. Values live only in leaves. Internal pages hold only separator keys and pointers. Because no values take up room, internal pages are as wide as they can be, which makes the tree shallower, and it makes every lookup end at a leaf, so every lookup costs the same.
  2. Leaves are linked to their neighbours. To scan a range (WHERE age BETWEEN 30 AND 40), descend once to find the first matching leaf, then follow the links to the right until you pass the end of the range. No climbing back to the parents.

The second point is why B+ trees are good at the thing hash indexes (chapter 4) cannot do: ordered access. "The next 100 orders after this one", "all users with a name starting with Sm" and ORDER BY all become sequential reads along the leaf level.

Go deeper: "B-tree" vs "B+ tree" optional

In the original B-tree, values could also live in internal nodes, so a lookup might finish early. That saves a page occasionally but makes internal pages narrower and the tree deeper, and it complicates range scans. Almost every modern system uses the B+ variant (or a close cousin) and still calls it a B-tree, as we do from here on unless the difference matters. Real implementations add refinements: PostgreSQL's btree, for example, links sibling leaves in both directions and keeps a "high key" and right-link in each page so concurrent readers can recover from a split they raced with (the Lehman and Yao design).

Deletes and merges, briefly

A delete finds the key's leaf and removes it. If the leaf becomes less than about half full, the textbook rule is to repair it: first try to borrow a key from a neighbouring sibling; if the sibling is also at the minimum, merge the two pages into one and delete the separator from the parent. The parent might in turn become under-full and merge with its sibling, and a root left with a single child is dropped, making the tree shorter. It is the exact mirror of a split.

Real engines are often lazier, because merging is complex and risky under concurrency. PostgreSQL, for example, does not merge half-empty index pages eagerly; it reclaims pages once they are entirely empty (VACUUM does that work), so an index that has seen a lot of deletes can stay larger than it needs to be until it is rebuilt. This is one reason "my index is bloated" is a real maintenance task.

In-place updates and why a crash is dangerous

An LSM tree never changes a file once written, which makes crashes easy to reason about. A B-tree does the opposite: it overwrites pages in place. That is fine until one logical operation needs several pages to change together.

Look back at the split in the diagram above. It writes three pages: the old leaf (now shorter), the new leaf, and the parent (new separator and pointer). They are three separate page writes, and the disk guarantees only that each one lands or does not; and as we saw in systems chapter 9, even a single page write can be torn if the page is larger than the unit the device writes atomically. Cut the power after two of the three writes and you have a corrupt tree: a new leaf that no parent points to (its keys are lost to searches), or a parent that points to a page that was never written, or a key that now exists in two places.

The write-ahead log

The standard fix is the pattern you met in systems chapter 9: a write-ahead log (WAL). Before changing any B-tree page, the database appends a record describing the change to a sequential log file and (at commit) calls fsync on it. Only then is the in-memory page changed. The data pages themselves can be written back later, whenever convenient.

After a crash, the database reads the log from the last checkpoint and redoes every logged change that did not reach the pages, so a half-finished split is finished from the log. Because the log is one sequential file written in order, it is cheap to fsync (and group commit from systems chapter 9 shares one fsync among many commits). The details of redo, undo and checkpoints belong to chapter 13. For now, remember what the WAL buys the B-tree: a multi-page change becomes durable with one sequential write, before any page is touched.

The cost is that every change is written twice: once to the log, once eventually to the page. That is a form of write amplification, which we tally in the comparison below. (Two further wrinkles: PostgreSQL, after each checkpoint, logs a full copy of a page the first time it changes, to survive torn pages; InnoDB has a "doublewrite buffer" for the same purpose. Both trade extra writes for safety.) Some B-tree engines, such as LMDB, avoid the log by never overwriting a page: they write new copies up to a new root and then swap one pointer, a copy-on-write design that echoes the atomic-rename trick from systems chapter 9.

The buffer pool

You cannot read three pages from disk for every query; the disk would be the bottleneck. Databases therefore keep a large cache of pages in RAM called the buffer pool (PostgreSQL: shared_buffers; InnoDB: the buffer pool). A page request first checks the pool: a hit costs nanoseconds to microseconds; a miss reads the page from disk, evicting another page if the pool is full.

Because the pool is smaller than the data, it needs an eviction policy: which page to drop when a new one is needed. Pure LRU (drop the least recently used) is the textbook choice, but a single large table scan would flush the whole useful cache, so real systems tweak it. PostgreSQL uses a "clock sweep" approximation; InnoDB uses an LRU list split into a "young" and an "old" region so that a scan's pages must prove themselves before displacing hot ones. A page that has been modified is dirty and must be written out (after its log record is durable) before it can be evicted. That ordering rule, log first, is what makes it the write-ahead log.

The OS page cache: caching twice

You already know the operating system has its own cache of file contents (systems chapter 8). When a database reads a page with an ordinary read(), the kernel keeps a copy in its page cache, and the database keeps another in its buffer pool: the same page cached twice, which is wasteful. This is called double buffering.

There are two schools. One lets the OS cache do some of the work and keeps the database's own pool moderate; PostgreSQL leans this way (the docs suggest starting its shared_buffers at roughly a quarter of RAM, leaving the rest to the OS cache). The other has the database bypass the OS cache with O_DIRECT, so reads and writes go straight between the buffer pool and the device and the pool is the only cache; InnoDB is commonly run this way, with a large pool. Bypassing the OS gives the database full control of what stays cached and when writes happen, at the price of doing all the caching work itself. Neither is "right"; it is another place a database takes responsibility from the OS, much as it takes responsibility for durability.

Where do the rows live? Heap files vs clustered tables

A leaf holds a key and its "value", but what is the value? Two designs are common, and the difference shows up in real performance:

  • Heap file (PostgreSQL). A table is an unordered collection of heap pages, and rows are placed wherever there is room. Every index, including the primary key's, is a separate B-tree whose leaf entries point to a row by its physical location (page number and slot). One extra page read fetches the row.
  • Clustered / index-organised (InnoDB). The table is a B+ tree keyed by the primary key, and its leaves contain the full rows. If you define no primary key, InnoDB picks a suitable unique index or invents a hidden row id. A secondary index stores, instead of a location, the row's primary key; a lookup through it is two tree walks (secondary index, then the primary-key tree). SQLite also stores ordinary tables as a B-tree keyed by an integer row id.

Clustering makes primary-key range scans excellent: neighbouring keys are neighbouring rows in neighbouring leaf pages. It also means a primary key matters a great deal. A random primary key, such as a randomly generated UUID, sends each new row to a random leaf, so every insert hits a random page that may need to be read from disk, and pages split all over the tree; an ever-increasing key appends at the right edge, where the hot pages stay in the buffer pool. Heap tables avoid tying row placement to a key, but pay for it with the extra hop and with the issue that an update that moves a row can require touching every index (a point in the Uber case study, Case 4). The next chapter, Indexes, builds on all of this.

Analogy: shelf order vs the catalogue

In a library with a catalogue (a heap table plus indexes), books sit on shelves in the order they arrived, and the catalogue cards say "shelf 3, position 2". In a library that shelves books in strict author order (a clustered table), the shelves are the index; a card catalogue by subject is then a secondary index that points to authors, not to shelf positions.

Where it breaks down: a library cannot cheaply reshuffle its shelves when a new book arrives in the middle of the alphabet; a B-tree does exactly that with page splits, and the cost is real, which is why key choice matters.

Concurrency: latches on pages

Many queries walk the same tree at the same time, and some of them are modifying it. Without protection, a reader could land on a page in the middle of a split. A latch is a short-term lock on an in-memory structure such as a page in the buffer pool: take it, look or change, release it, typically within microseconds. (Do not confuse it with a transaction lock on a row, which can last as long as a transaction and which chapter 12 covers. Latches protect the data structure; locks protect the data.)

The classic technique is latch coupling (or "crabbing"): latch the root, latch the child you are about to enter, and only then release the parent. A writer can release ancestors early if the child is "safe", meaning it has room, so that no split can climb back to them. Without care, the root would be a bottleneck for every operation, so real B-trees use refinements: for example, designs with sibling links (like the Lehman and Yao scheme mentioned above) let a reader that raced with a split simply follow the link to the right and carry on. You do not need the details; you need the cost model: a B-tree under heavy concurrent writes pays for page latches and for waiting on the hot pages, usually the rightmost leaf.

B-tree vs LSM tree

Now you have both main designs. They start from the same constraint (disks like big sequential operations) and choose opposite compromises: the B-tree keeps data sorted in place and pays at write time with random page writes; the LSM tree (chapter 5) keeps recent writes in memory and in sequential files and pays later, in compaction and in extra work on reads.

There is no winner

Beware of any claim that "LSM is faster" or "B-trees are faster". The answer depends on read/write mix, key distribution, value size, hardware, compaction settings and what you measure (throughput, median latency or the 99.9th percentile). Both families have been tuned by decades of engineering. The useful skill is to know which costs each design pays and when, so you can predict where a workload will hurt.

Try it yourself

Try it yourself: the arithmetic, then a real B-tree

The first half of this script repeats the capacity arithmetic. The second half builds a real SQLite database with 200,000 rows and a secondary index, then asks SQLite's dbstat virtual table for every page of each tree. It needs only Python 3 (sqlite3 is built in; on a build without dbstat the second half will fail with "no such table", and you can skip it). It writes into a temp folder inside your current directory and removes it afterwards.

import sqlite3, os, tempfile, shutil, random

# Part 1: the arithmetic. 8 KiB pages, 8-byte keys, 8-byte child pointers, pages ~70% full.
page, key, ptr, fill = 8192, 8, 8, 0.7
fanout = int(page // (key + ptr) * fill)
print(f"fan-out per page is about {fanout}")
for levels in range(1, 5):
    print(f"{levels} level(s): about {fanout**levels:>14,} keys")

# Part 2: a real B-tree. SQLite's dbstat table lists every page of every tree.
d = tempfile.mkdtemp(dir=".")
con = sqlite3.connect(os.path.join(d, "t.db"))
con.execute("CREATE TABLE t (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, k INTEGER)")
con.execute("CREATE INDEX idx_k ON t(k)")
random.seed(1)
con.executemany("INSERT INTO t VALUES (?, ?)", [(i, random.randint(0, 10**9)) for i in range(1, 200_001)])
con.commit()
print("\npage size:", con.execute("PRAGMA page_size").fetchone()[0], "bytes")
print(f"{'tree':<8}{'pages':>7}{'internal':>10}{'leaf':>7}{'levels':>8}")
q = """SELECT name, count(*), sum(pagetype='internal'), sum(pagetype='leaf'),
              max(length(path) - length(replace(path, '/', '')))
       FROM dbstat WHERE name IN ('t', 'idx_k') GROUP BY name"""
for r in con.execute(q):
    print(f"{r[0]:<8}{r[1]:>7}{r[2]:>10}{r[3]:>7}{r[4]:>8}")
con.close(); shutil.rmtree(d)

Read the table. The 200,000-row table is a B+ tree of 636 pages: 633 leaves and just 3 internal pages (the root and two below it), and the index is the same shape. Both are three levels deep, so any lookup reads three pages. SQLite's pages are 4 KiB with a branching factor of a couple of hundred, so your fan-out arithmetic is the right order of magnitude. Distributed systems connection

Three ideas from this chapter turn up at the scale of whole clusters. First, the WAL is also the replication stream: PostgreSQL's streaming replication ships the write-ahead log to standbys, which replay it, and Amazon's Aurora (Case 8) goes further and sends only log records to its storage layer. We pick this up in chapter 15. Second, sorted keys make ranges natural to shard: a table partitioned by key range puts a small B-tree (or LSM tree) on each shard, and a range query touches only the shards whose ranges overlap (chapter 16). Third, the page is a unit of failure: checksums per page detect corruption, and a page that is torn on one replica can be repaired from another, a theme from the replication chapters in networking chapter 18.

Key takeaways

  • Storage is read and written in pages (4 to 16 KiB; Postgres 8 KiB, InnoDB 16 KiB by default). The cost of a lookup is the number of pages it touches.
  • A B-tree has a branching factor in the hundreds, so it is 3 to 4 levels deep for millions to billions of keys. Search is one root-to-leaf walk, always the same length.
  • Inserts fill a leaf; a full leaf splits, pushing a separator into the parent; a root split adds a level at the top. Deletes borrow or merge (many engines are lazy about it).
  • B+ trees keep values in leaves and link the leaves, so range scans and ordering are cheap.
  • Pages are updated in place, and a split changes several pages, so a crash mid-split corrupts the tree. A write-ahead log makes the change durable first and lets recovery redo it.
  • The buffer pool caches pages with an eviction policy; it overlaps the OS page cache (double buffering) unless the database uses O_DIRECT. Hot upper levels of the tree live in RAM.
  • Heap tables (Postgres) point from indexes to rows; clustered tables (InnoDB) store rows in the primary-key tree. B-trees pay at write time for fast, predictable reads; LSM trees do the reverse.

Check your understanding

A table has about 50 million rows and its index has a branching factor of roughly 350. About how many pages does a lookup through the index read in the worst case?

  1. About 26, as for a balanced binary tree
  2. About 4: the tree is 3 or 4 levels deep, and 3503 is already around 43 million
  3. About 350, one per child of the root
  4. One, since the root page holds the whole index
Each level multiplies capacity by the branching factor, so a few levels suffice. Three levels hold about 43 million keys and four hold billions, and the upper levels are usually cached, so the real disk reads are fewer still.

A leaf page is full and you insert a new key into it. What happens?

  1. The insert fails and the client must retry later
  2. The tree is rebuilt from scratch
  3. The key is added at the end of the file and merged later
  4. The leaf splits in two, a separator key is added to the parent, and the parent splits too if it is full
Splitting is local: only the pages along the path can be affected. If the split climbs all the way and splits the root, a new root is created and the whole tree grows one level taller, keeping every leaf at the same depth.

Why does a B-tree need a write-ahead log when pages are updated in place?

  1. A split changes several pages, and a crash after only some of them are written would leave the tree corrupt; the log lets recovery redo the whole change
  2. Because RAM is not fast enough to hold the pages
  3. Because B-tree pages cannot be read without a log
  4. To make range scans faster
Single-page writes can be torn, and multi-page changes can be half-applied. The log makes the intent durable with one sequential write before the pages are touched, so recovery can finish the job.

Why do B+ trees link their leaf pages together?

  1. To let writers skip the buffer pool
  2. To make point lookups need fewer levels
  3. To allow a range scan to move sideways from leaf to leaf after one descent, without climbing back up to the parents
  4. To store duplicate copies of each key for safety
The links do not change the cost of a point lookup. They make ordered access cheap: find the first key in the range, then follow the chain until the range ends.

In the InnoDB design, what does a secondary index entry store instead of a row location?

  1. A copy of the entire row
  2. The row's primary key, which is then looked up in the primary-key tree (where the rows live)
  3. The page number of the root of the table
  4. Nothing; secondary indexes are scanned in full
The table is itself a B+ tree keyed by the primary key, so a secondary index points to a row by that key. A lookup through a secondary index therefore walks two trees. (PostgreSQL's heap design instead stores a physical row location.)

A team notices that every small logical write to their B-tree database causes a whole 8 KiB page and a WAL record to be written. Which description fits best?

  1. Write amplification from in-place page updates, one reason write-heavy systems sometimes prefer an LSM tree
  2. A bug: B-trees should only write the changed bytes
  3. Read amplification, which the buffer pool eliminates
  4. The normal cost of an LSM tree's memtable
Disks are written in pages, so changing a few bytes rewrites a page, plus the log. LSM trees have write amplification too (from compaction), so the right question is which kind costs less for the workload, not which design has none.
