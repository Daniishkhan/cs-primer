Choosing a Database & Where Next

You now know how databases store bytes, find them, protect them from crashes and from each other, copy them and split them. This last chapter turns that into a habit: a short list of questions that picks storage for a workload, a warning about the cost of picking too many, and a map of where to go from here.

Why this matters

"Which database should we use?" is one of the most expensive questions a team answers, because the choice outlives the people who made it. It is also a question people answer by fashion. The skill is to answer it from the workload: what the queries look like, how correct the data must be, how big it will get, and who will be paged when it breaks. Everything you learned in this course exists to make that answer a reasoned one.

Start from the workload, not the product

Four lenses cover almost every decision. Ask them in this order, and write the answers down before you open a vendor's web page.

1. Access patterns

What are the actual queries? Lookups by key? Range scans? Joins across entities? Aggregates over millions of rows? Free-text search? Read-heavy or write-heavy? Chapters 1, 7, 8 and 9 are about this: the shape of the question decides the shape of the structure (B-tree, LSM tree, column store, inverted index).

2. Consistency needs

What happens if two users act at once, or a reader sees stale data? Money and inventory need real transactions and the right isolation level (chapters 10 to 12). A "likes" counter or a recommendation list can tolerate being a little wrong or late (chapter 15).

3. Scale

How much data, how many reads and writes per second, now and in two years? Do the arithmetic (the studios practise it). Most systems are far smaller than people fear, and a single well-tuned machine goes surprisingly far. If you do outgrow one machine, chapters 14 to 16 are the toolbox.

4. Operations

Who runs it, restores it, upgrades it, and understands it at 3 a.m.? Backups you have actually tested, monitoring, failover, the team's existing skills, managed-service options. A technically perfect database nobody on the team can operate is a bad choice.

The default: a relational database

Unless you have a specific reason not to, start with PostgreSQL (or another mature relational database such as MySQL). That is not loyalty; it is arithmetic about risk.

  • It gives you real transactions and constraints, so the hard correctness problems of chapters 10 and 11 are handled by someone else's well-tested code.
  • Its query language lets you ask questions you did not plan for. Requirements change; a flexible query layer absorbs that change.
  • With the right indexes (chapter 7), it handles a great many workloads on one machine. Read replicas (chapter 15) take you further. Postgres can also store JSON documents, run full-text search and keep a time-series table, which is often good enough that you never need a second system.
  • The tooling, the documentation, the hosting options and the pool of people who know it are enormous.

Choosing something else should require a reason you can state in one sentence, ideally with a number in it. "It's web scale" is not a reason. "We ingest 200,000 measurements a second and need to delete anything older than 30 days cheaply" is.

Common misconception

"NoSQL scales and SQL does not." Scale is about the access pattern and the architecture, not the query language. Some relational systems are sharded across many machines (chapter 16), and some non-relational stores are single-node. The honest trade is usually flexibility of queries and strength of transactions versus ease of spreading data across machines. Choose by what your workload needs, not by label.

Polyglot persistence, and what it costs

Real systems rarely use only one store. A shop might hold orders in Postgres, product search in a search engine, sessions in Redis, and sales reports in a warehouse. This is called polyglot persistence: each store does the job it is best at. It is often the right design, and chapter 19 showed the mechanism that makes it safe: one source of truth, with every other store a derived copy fed from its log.

But every store you add is a bill that arrives monthly:

  • Consistency. Copies lag and can drift. You need the outbox or CDC machinery, idempotent consumers and a way to detect and repair divergence.
  • Operations. Each system has its own deployment, backups, upgrades, monitoring, security and failure modes. The on-call engineer must understand all of them.
  • Skills. Fewer people know five systems well than know one.
  • Joins disappear. Once data lives in two systems, a question spanning both has to be answered in application code or in the pipeline.
  • Failure combinations. The system is now only as available as its dependencies in series (networking ch. 15).

Analogy: a kitchen with specialist gadgets

A good knife, a pan and an oven make nearly any meal. A garlic press, a spiraliser and a sous-vide machine each do one thing brilliantly, but you have to store them, clean them, and remember how they work. Add them when a specific dish demands it, not because the shop had a sale.

Where it breaks down: an unused kitchen gadget just sits there. An extra data store keeps running, keeps being paged about and keeps drifting from the others even when nobody is looking at it.

A practical rule: add a specialised store when you can state the measurable problem it solves, and when you are ready to own the pipeline that feeds it.

Key takeaways

  • Decide from four lenses: access patterns, consistency needs, scale and operations. Write the numbers down.
  • Default to PostgreSQL or another relational database. Leaving it needs a reason you can state in one sentence, with a number in it.
  • Each specialised store is a trade: column stores for analytics, search engines for ranked text, time-series databases for metrics, key-value stores for lookups at speed, partitioned LSM stores for scale by key, graph databases for relationship queries.
  • Polyglot persistence works when there is one source of truth and every other store is derived from its log. Every extra store costs consistency work, operations, skills and failure combinations.
  • "NoSQL scales, SQL does not" is a slogan, not an analysis.

Check your understanding

A small team is starting a new product with orders, customers and payments, a few hundred requests per second at peak and no unusual query types. Which starting point is most defensible?

  1. A document store, because it will scale better later
  2. PostgreSQL, adding a specialised store only when a measured need appears
  3. One store per entity, so each can be tuned
  4. A graph database, because orders relate to customers
Relational databases give transactions, constraints and flexible queries, and handle far more than this load on one machine. Specialised stores should be added for a specific, measurable reason, not in anticipation of one.

Which workload is the best reason to add a column store beside your main database?

  1. Updating a single customer's address
  2. Looking up a session by its token in under a millisecond
  3. Storing user-uploaded images
  4. Computing monthly revenue by region over several billion order rows
Aggregates that read a few columns across billions of rows are exactly what columnar layout and compression speed up. Single-row updates and fast key lookups are what row stores and key-value stores are for.

Your product search lives in a separate search engine, fed from the main database. What is the most important property to remember about it?

  1. It is a derived copy that may lag the source of truth, so you need a reliable feed (outbox or CDC) and a way to rebuild it
  2. It is the source of truth for product prices
  3. It makes the main database unnecessary
  4. It is always perfectly in sync, because both are written in the same transaction
A search index is a derived structure. It is only as fresh as its feed, and because it is derived it can be rebuilt by replaying the data. A transaction does not span the database and the search engine, which is why chapter 19's patterns exist.

A colleague proposes adding a fifth type of datastore "because it's the best tool for one small feature". What is the strongest argument for pausing?

  1. New databases are always slower than old ones
  2. Specialised stores cannot store data durably
  3. Each extra store adds a pipeline to keep consistent, plus operations, skills and failure modes, so it needs a measurable problem to justify the cost
  4. Polyglot persistence is impossible to make consistent
Polyglot persistence is workable and often right, but every store has a continuing cost. The test is whether a measurable problem justifies it and whether the team is ready to own the feed that keeps it correct.
