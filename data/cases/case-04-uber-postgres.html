Why Uber Moved from Postgres to MySQL

In July 2016 an Uber engineer published a post explaining why much of Uber's data layer had moved from PostgreSQL to MySQL. It is one of the best public tours of how storage-engine internals (tuple versions, index pointers, the shape of the replication log) turn into operational pain. It is also one company's view, of one version, at one moment, and the Postgres community answered it. This page reads the post closely and then fairly.

The pattern in one sentence

Storage-engine design choices are workload choices: how a database versions rows, what its indexes point at, and what its replication log describes decide the cost of updates, of replicas and of upgrades, so the right engine depends on your read/write mix, your index count and your operational constraints.

The cast

The mechanisms are in chapter 6 (B-trees, heap files versus clustered tables), chapter 12 (MVCC) and chapter 15 (what gets shipped to followers, and upgrades). The facts about Uber's experience are from the post (by Evan Klitzke, 26 July 2016).

Uber's early stack
A monolithic Python backend on Postgres. It later moved to microservices and new data platforms. Many former Postgres workloads now run on Schemaless, a sharding layer built on MySQL, and some specialised cases use Cassandra.
Postgres (as analysed)
The analysis is mainly of Postgres 9.2. The author says the on-disk design had not changed significantly since 8.3, then nearly ten years old. Rows are immutable tuples, each addressed by a ctid, which represents its physical location on disk. Indexes map key values to ctids.
The write-ahead log (WAL)
Postgres's log for crash recovery. Streaming replication ships it to replicas, which apply it continuously. It describes changes at the level of on-disk tuples and offsets.
InnoDB (MySQL)
The storage engine Uber moved to. Secondary indexes store primary-key values, not physical pointers. The row is updated in place, and old versions go to a rollback segment.

Claim 1: write amplification

The post's example is a users table: a primary key id, plus first, last and birth_year, with a primary-key index, an index on (first, last) and an index on birth_year. Change one person's birth year from 780 to 770. In Postgres the old tuple is immutable, so the update adds a new tuple with a new ctid, links the old one to it, and every index must hold an entry for the new version. The post counts at least four physical writes for this change: the new tuple, and one in each of the three indexes. Each is also written to the WAL. Two of those index writes are unnecessary, since the primary key and name did not change. The post says a table with a dozen indexes would push an update of one indexed column into all twelve.

InnoDB, the post says, updates fixed-size fields like birth_year in place, copying the old version to the rollback segment, and updates only the indexes that cover the changed columns.

Step through

One UPDATE in two designs

  1. Before the update. Left: a heap tuple at ctid (0,1) and three indexes that each point at that location. Right: the table is stored clustered by primary key, and the secondary indexes hold primary-key values.
  2. UPDATE birth_year = 770. Left: a new tuple appears and each of the three indexes gets a new entry, including the two that did not change. Right: the row changes in place, the old version moves to the undo log, and only the birth_year index changes. The price on the right is that a lookup through a secondary index needs two searches.

Common misconception: "Postgres rewrites every index on every update"

The post's example updates an indexed column. Postgres has an optimisation, heap-only tuples (HOT), for updates that touch no indexed column. The official documentation gives two conditions: the update does not modify any column referenced by the table's indexes (summarising indexes aside), and there is enough free space on the page holding the old row. If both hold, new index entries are not needed. Uber's example fails the first condition, which is the case the post is about.

Claim 2: replication ships a very detailed log

Postgres streaming replication ships the WAL, which records physical changes to tuples and offsets. A caught-up replica matches the master byte for byte (so tools like rsync can repair a stale one). The post's complaint is volume. The write amplification above appears in the WAL too. That is manageable within a data centre but expensive across a country: Uber ran a master in a West Coast colocation and replicas on the East Coast for disaster recovery. Cascading replication cut the cross-country link to one replica, but verbosity stayed a problem, and archiving the WAL to a storage web service could not keep up at peak traffic.

The post also tells a corruption story. During a master promotion, Uber hit a Postgres 9.2 bug in which replicas followed timeline switches incorrectly and misapplied some WAL records, so rows that should have been inactive stayed active. A query could return both versions of the same row. The damage differed from replica to replica, was hard to measure, and affected only a few rows per database, but the team feared index corruption because replication is physical. They found the bug, confirmed the promoted master was clean, and re-synced the replicas from a fresh snapshot, slowly, since only a few could leave the pool at once. MySQL, the post says, offers statement-based, row-based and mixed replication; its binary log is more compact, and typical failures are a skipped or double-applied statement rather than a corrupted index structure.

Claim 3: replica MVCC

Postgres replicas are byte-for-byte copies, and the post says they do not provide true replica MVCC. A long-running transaction on a replica can block WAL application; Postgres then kills the transaction after a timeout, and replicas can lag the master by seconds. The author's example is code that sends an email receipt inside an open transaction, and ORMs can hide transaction boundaries. With MySQL's logical replication, the post says replicas can offer true MVCC, so reads do not block replication.

Claim 4: upgrades

Physical replication cannot cross major releases: 9.2 and 9.3 cannot replicate to each other. The post describes the upgrade procedure: shut down the master, run pg_upgrade in place (many hours with no traffic), restart, take a new snapshot (many more hours), restore each replica and wait for catch-up. Uber completed the 9.1 to 9.2 upgrade, but the post says it took so many hours they could not afford to repeat it, and a later upgrade would have been even lengthier, leaving legacy instances on 9.2 when 9.5 was the current release. The post mentions pglogical, a logical-replication layer for 9.4 and later that allows cross-release upgrades with less downtime, but notes that it was not in mainline Postgres and was unavailable for older releases. By contrast, the post says, MySQL's replication format rarely changes across versions, so Uber upgrades one replica at a time and then promotes one, with near-zero downtime.

Other points the post makes

  • Buffer pool versus page cache. Postgres relies on the operating system's page cache. On Uber's largest replicas with 768 GB of memory, only about 25 GB was resident to Postgres processes, leaving over 700 GB to the page cache, and each access involves lseek and read system calls. InnoDB's userspace buffer pool avoids those and permits a custom LRU. (Compare chapter 6's buffer pool section.)
  • Connections. MySQL uses a thread per connection; Postgres a process per connection. Uber saw trouble past a few hundred active Postgres connections and used pgbouncer.
  • Vacuum. The post says purgeable rows are directly available in InnoDB's rollback segment, while Postgres autovacuum must scan entire tables to find deleted rows.

The pattern: engine internals shape operations

Notice that three of the four claims are not about speed in a benchmark; they are about operations: how much data crosses a WAN, whether you can run a long report on a replica, whether you can upgrade without a day of downtime. That is the real lesson. A storage engine is a bundle of linked decisions: immutable versions + physical index pointers + physical log in Postgres, versus in-place update + undo log + primary-key pointers + logical log in InnoDB. Each bundle makes some things cheap and others costly.

Analogy: two ways to correct a library catalogue

Postgres is like a library that never erases a catalogue card: a correction is a new card, and every index drawer (by author, by year, by title) gets a new card pointing to the new shelf spot. Old cards are thrown out by a cleaner who comes round later. InnoDB's library corrects the book's record in place and files the old text in a back room; the drawers hold the book's accession number, so only a drawer whose key changed needs touching. Finding a book through such a drawer means looking up the number, then fetching it: two trips.

Where it breaks down: real engines can avoid some of that work (see the HOT note above), and the "cleaner" in Postgres is autovacuum, whose cost depends heavily on workload and configuration.

Reading the post fairly

The post is an engineering argument from the people who ran the systems, and its numbers are Uber's. It is also a snapshot: it analyses mainly Postgres 9.2, and says the main design dated back to 8.3. Postgres is not static, and a company is not obliged to be balanced when writing about its own migration.

The Postgres community's reaction (labelled as such)

I read the pgsql-hackers mailing-list thread about the post (July to August 2016; I read the first part of a long thread). It is a developer discussion, not an official project statement, and views differed. Participants included Josh Berkus, Tom Lane, Robert Haas, Stephen Frost and Bruce Momjian.

  • Where people agreed with Uber. Berkus wrote that the replication limitations were real, and gave his own account of write amplification for a small, heavily indexed, frequently updated table: HOT does not help when many indexes are involved. Haas said he had seen bloat cause severe regressions, sometimes manageable only with careful tuning and aggressive autovacuum. The thread also discussed ideas for reducing index traffic, such as indexes keyed by primary key.
  • Where they pushed back. Berkus noted the post glossed over MySQL's own weaknesses and Uber's other circumstances. Frost wrote that the post omitted details, that the corruption bug was quickly fixed, that logical replication is not inherently safer, and that pg_upgrade --link addressed the upgrade problem. Lane doubted it was a disaster, expecting VACUUM to hold bloat to a small multiple of table size, with long transactions as the main risk. Several participants argued that statement-based replication (which they said Uber used) has failure modes of its own.

A separate pgsql-advocacy thread reflected the same split. Berkus said the databases are full of trade-offs. I did not find, and do not claim, a formal Postgres project response.

Trade-offs and lessons

Index count multiplies write cost

Under the heap-and-ctid design, every indexed table pays per index on most updates. Fewer, better indexes help on either engine. The cost of one more index is not one write but one per update.

Physical vs logical logs

A physical log is simple to apply and gives exact copies, but is bulky and ties replicas to the same version and layout. A logical log is compact and more flexible but has failure modes of its own (the thread's participants said so).

Upgrades are a design requirement

If you cannot upgrade your database online, you stop upgrading it. The post describes Uber stuck on 9.2. Whether you can roll through versions replica by replica should be a selection criterion.

Your workload is not theirs

Uber's tables were large, indexed and update-heavy, with replicas across a continent. General observation: a read-mostly or append-mostly workload may never feel these costs, and a migration is a major cost in itself.

Try it yourself

Count the writes

This script is a tiny model of the two designs. It counts physical writes for one UPDATE and encodes the two HOT conditions from the Postgres documentation. It is a model of the ideas, not of either engine's code, and it touches no files.

def pg_style(n_indexes, changed_indexed_col, page_has_space=True):
    """New tuple version each update; every index maps key -> physical location (ctid)."""
    if not changed_indexed_col and page_has_space:
        return 1 + 0              # heap-only tuple (HOT): no new index entries (per the Postgres docs)
    return 1 + n_indexes          # new tuple + a new entry in every index

def innodb_style(n_indexes_on_changed_cols):
    """Row updated in place (old version goes to the undo log); secondary indexes hold the primary key."""
    return 1 + n_indexes_on_changed_cols

print("table with a primary key and 2 secondary indexes (3 indexes), UPDATE birth_year (indexed):")
print("  heap + ctid indexes :", pg_style(3, True))
print("  PK-pointer indexes  :", innodb_style(1))
print("12 indexes, UPDATE one indexed column:")
print("  heap + ctid indexes :", pg_style(12, True))
print("  PK-pointer indexes  :", innodb_style(1))
print("12 indexes, UPDATE a column NO index covers, space on the page:")
print("  heap + ctid, with HOT:", pg_style(12, False, True))
print("  heap + ctid, page full:", pg_style(12, False, False))

Output when run:

table with a primary key and 2 secondary indexes (3 indexes), UPDATE birth_year (indexed):
  heap + ctid indexes : 4
  PK-pointer indexes  : 2
12 indexes, UPDATE one indexed column:
  heap + ctid indexes : 13
  PK-pointer indexes  : 2
12 indexes, UPDATE a column NO index covers, space on the page:
  heap + ctid, with HOT: 1
  heap + ctid, page full: 13

The first block reproduces the post's "at least four writes". The last block is the other half of the story: with HOT and room on the page, the same table can avoid all twelve index writes, which is why a fair reading of the post notes that its example updates an indexed column. If you run Postgres, the documentation says the pg_stat_all_tables view lets you monitor HOT and non-HOT updates on your own tables, and lowering a table's fillfactor makes free space on the page more likely. The model is mine and deliberately crude: it ignores page pruning, vacuum and index page splits.

Distributed systems connection

The replication argument is chapter 15's "what actually gets shipped" section in a real company. Physical log shipping couples a replica to the leader's exact on-disk format; logical replication decouples them, which is what makes rolling upgrades possible and also what lets a replica serve its own consistent reads. The same idea returns whenever data crosses a boundary: change-data-capture streams (a later chapter) are a logical log too. And the cross-country bandwidth bill is the networking lesson of earlier courses: bytes shipped between regions cost money and time.

Key takeaways

  • Uber's 2016 post argued four things about Postgres 9.2 for its workload: write amplification from immutable tuples and ctid-based indexes, bulky physical replication, weak replica MVCC, and hard major-version upgrades.
  • InnoDB updates in place, keeps old versions in a rollback segment, and points secondary indexes at the primary key, at the cost of two searches for a secondary lookup.
  • HOT updates avoid new index entries when no indexed column changes and the page has room, so the post's example is the case where it does not apply.
  • The Postgres community discussion agreed on some points (index-update cost, bloat pain) and disputed others (corruption, logical versus physical replication, upgrade difficulty).
  • This is one company's view at one point in time: use it to learn the mechanisms, not to pick a database by brand.

Check your understanding

In the post's example, why does changing birth_year require at least four writes in Postgres?

  1. Postgres writes each column separately
  2. The immutable old tuple is replaced by a new tuple, and each of the three indexes needs an entry pointing to the new ctid
  3. The WAL must be copied four times
  4. Autovacuum runs after each update
One new tuple plus one entry in each of the primary-key, (first, last) and birth_year indexes. The first two index writes are unnecessary because those columns did not change, which is the post's point.

What is the trade-off of InnoDB's secondary indexes pointing at the primary key?

  1. They cannot be used for range scans
  2. They are larger than the table
  3. They must be rebuilt after every update
  4. A lookup through a secondary index needs two searches, but updates that do not change the key need no index change
The post calls the extra search a slight disadvantage. In exchange, moving or updating a row does not invalidate pointers in secondary indexes.

Which condition must hold for a HOT update, according to the Postgres documentation?

  1. No column referenced by the table's indexes is modified (summarising indexes aside), and the page has enough free space for the new row version
  2. The table has no primary key
  3. The update must be the first in the transaction
  4. All replicas are synchronous
Those are the two conditions the documentation gives. When both hold, new index entries are not needed.

What is the fairest way to use this case study?

  1. As proof that MySQL is faster than Postgres
  2. As proof that Postgres cannot scale
  3. As an account of how one company's workload, on one version, met specific engine trade-offs, to be weighed against the community's replies and your own workload
  4. As a recommendation never to use physical replication
The post analyses mainly Postgres 9.2 for Uber's workload, and community members disputed parts of it. The transferable part is the mechanisms.
