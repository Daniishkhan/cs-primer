43 Seconds That Took a Day: GitHub, 2018

On 21 October 2018 a network link between two GitHub sites dropped for 43 seconds. Automation reacted exactly as configured, moved the MySQL primaries to the other coast, and in doing so created two copies of the truth. Putting that right took 24 hours and 11 minutes. This is the clearest real-world picture of what the failover section of the replication chapters warns about.

The pattern in one sentence

Automatic failover trades a short outage for a risk of split brain: if a leader is demoted on the strength of a partition, and the old leader had acknowledged writes the new one never received, then both sides hold writes the other lacks, and no clean way back exists.

The cast

The roles come from chapter 15 (the "Failover" section) and networking chapter 18 (single-leader replication). The details below are from GitHub's own post-incident analysis.

MySQL clusters
GitHub ran multiple clusters, ranging from hundreds of gigabytes to nearly five terabytes, each with up to dozens of read replicas. Data was split across clusters by function. Applications sent writes to each cluster's primary and usually read from replicas.
Orchestrator
The tool that managed the MySQL topologies and automated failover. It used Raft consensus, so its own nodes had to agree on who was leader. The post stresses that its configuration had to match what the application tier could support.
The US East Coast data center
Home of the primaries when the incident began, behind a network hub on the East Coast.
The US West Coast data center
Held replicas, and Orchestrator nodes that could vote. The post describes it forming a quorum with East Coast public-cloud nodes.
The application tier
Wrote to whichever server Orchestrator said was the primary. It did exactly that, immediately, once connectivity returned.

What happened, step by step

At 22:52 UTC (all times are UTC, as in the post), maintenance to replace failing 100G optical equipment cut connectivity between the East Coast network hub and the primary East Coast data center. Connectivity came back after 43 seconds. The next few minutes decided the next day.

Step through

From a 43-second blip to two primaries

  1. Normal. The East Coast holds the primaries; replicas, including ones on the West Coast, copy from them. Replication is not instantaneous, so a replica is always slightly behind.
  2. 22:52: the link drops. Maintenance on optical equipment cuts connectivity between the East Coast hub and the primary East Coast data center. It will be back in 43 seconds, but nobody knows that yet.
  3. Orchestrator acts. The East Coast Orchestrator node, via Raft, deselects itself as leader. The West Coast and East Coast public-cloud nodes form a quorum and fail clusters over to primaries in the West Coast data center.
  4. Connectivity returns, and the application writes immediately. The application tier starts writing to the new West Coast primaries as soon as it can reach them. Some East Coast writes had not replicated west.
  5. Two histories. Both sites now contain writes the other lacks, so failing back safely is not possible. GitHub's choice was to protect the data.

Common misconception: "the failover failed"

Orchestrator did what it was configured to do. The post says Orchestrator's actions "behaved as configured", and names cross-country latency as a major contributor to the damage. The lesson is less "the tool had a bug" than "the configured policy did not match what the rest of the system could tolerate".

The decision: integrity over speed

The post puts the discovery at 23:02, when first responders found cluster topologies they did not expect: the Orchestrator API showed only West Coast servers. By 23:13 database engineers learned that the West Coast clusters had taken about 40 minutes of application writes. Sending East Coast traffic across the country to those primaries would have left the service unusable for many users because of cross-country latency, so the team chose to fail forward and protect the data. At 23:19 they paused webhook delivery and GitHub Pages builds, and the post says plainly that data integrity took priority over usability and recovery speed.

The recovery plan, posted at 00:05 on 22 October, was to restore from backups, synchronise replicas, fall back to a stable topology, and then resume queued jobs. Backups ran every four hours and lived in remote cloud blob storage. The post says restoring several terabytes took hours (transfer, decompression, checksumming, preparation and loading) and that the team had never fully rebuilt a cluster from backup before.

The long tail

Several things stretched the day. At 06:51 several East Coast clusters were restored and replicating from the West Coast, but pages that wrote across the country loaded slowly, and a two-hour estimate was posted based on linear interpolation of replication telemetry. By 11:12 all primaries were back on the East Coast, yet dozens of replicas still lagged by hours, so users saw inconsistent data. Replication catch-up followed a power-decay curve, not the straight line the estimate had assumed, and rising load slowed it further. At 13:15 the team provisioned more read replicas in the East Coast public cloud to spread read load. Replicas were in sync at 16:24 and the team failed back to the original topology.

Even then the queue had to drain: over five million hook events and 80,000 Pages builds. About 200,000 webhook payloads had exceeded an internal TTL (time to live) and were dropped. Processing was paused and the TTL was raised. At 23:03 everything pending was processed and the status returned to green.

The pattern: failover needs a policy about divergence

In chapter 15 you saw that failover has hazards: the new leader may be missing the last acknowledged writes, and the old leader may come back and still believe it is the leader. GitHub's incident is the first hazard in the real world. Writes acknowledged on the East Coast primaries had not replicated west when the West was promoted. Once the application wrote to the new primaries, there were two histories. This is split brain in its quiet form: not two leaders accepting writes at the same moment, but a sequence that produced two sets of writes.

Analogy: the ledger and its photocopy

A shop's head office keeps the master ledger and sends a photocopy to the branch every few minutes. The phone line drops. The branch manager, assuming head office is gone, makes the photocopy the master and keeps trading. Head office, still open, keeps writing in the original. When the line comes back, each book contains sales the other does not. Neither can simply be thrown away.

Where it breaks down: a shop can compare two paper ledgers line by line. For a database with millions of interdependent rows, the post says the team was still analysing which writes could be reconciled automatically and which needed user outreach.

Trade-offs and lessons

Availability versus integrity

The team could have kept serving users from the West Coast primaries at the cost of cross-country latency, or paused and restored. They chose to protect data. No user data was lost, according to the post, though reconciliation of a few seconds of writes was still in progress at the time of publication. One of the busiest clusters had 954 writes in the affected window.

Policy must match topology

One of GitHub's follow-up actions was to change Orchestrator configuration to prevent primary promotion across regional boundaries. A failover that is safe between racks is a different decision when it crosses a continent.

Test the recovery you depend on

The post says the team had never fully rebuilt a cluster from backup before. Their follow-ups include proactive testing of design assumptions and fault injection and chaos engineering, to validate failure scenarios before they affect users.

Estimates need a model

Linear extrapolation of replication progress was wrong. GitHub's own words, summarised: its estimates were based on backlog processing rates and did not account for all variables, and it committed to more accurate updates.

What this page does not claim

It does not say that automatic failover is a mistake, or that GitHub's design was careless; the post describes GitHub pursuing an active/active/active multi-data-center design to tolerate losing a whole facility. General industry pattern, not from the post: teams that choose manual failover trade a longer outage for a human judging whether the old primary was really lost, and teams that choose automatic failover often add rules such as "do not promote across regions" and fencing so the old leader cannot accept writes.

Try it yourself

Model two primaries in twenty lines

This script models the situation in the post in miniature: a west primary promoted from a slightly stale replica, new writes landing there, and then a diff of the two histories. It is read-only (nothing is written to disk) and uses only the standard library. The data is made up.

east = [("e1", "alice.email", "a@x.org"), ("e2", "issue#7.state", "open"),
        ("e3", "issue#7.title", "Crash"), ("e4", "issue#7.state", "closed"),
        ("e5", "pr#3.state", "merged")]
replicated_to_west = 3                      # replication was a little behind
west = list(east[:replicated_to_west])      # west promoted from this prefix
west += [("w1", "issue#7.state", "reopened"), ("w2", "pr#9.state", "open")]

ids_e, ids_w = {w[0] for w in east}, {w[0] for w in west}
print("only on east:", sorted(ids_e - ids_w))
print("only on west:", sorted(ids_w - ids_e))

def final_state(log):
    s = {}
    for _, k, v in log: s[k] = v
    return s

e, w = final_state(east), final_state(west)
for k in sorted(set(e) | set(w)):
    if e.get(k) != w.get(k):
        print(f"{k}: east={e.get(k)!r} west={w.get(k)!r}")
print("safe to just point everything back at east?", ids_w <= ids_e)

Output when run:

only on east: ['e4', 'e5']
only on west: ['w1', 'w2']
issue#7.state: east='closed' west='reopened'
pr#3.state: east='merged' west=None
pr#9.state: east=None west='open'
safe to just point everything back at east? False

Notice the first conflict: the same issue is closed on one side and reopened on the other. Last-write-wins would silently pick one. Change replicated_to_west to 5 and the only on east line comes out empty: west holds everything east had, plus two newer writes, so one history simply extends the other and nothing is lost by carrying on from west. The danger is exactly the gap.

For your own MySQL or Postgres setups, the read-only question to ask is "how far behind can a replica be right now?" In MySQL, SHOW REPLICA STATUS (older versions: SHOW SLAVE STATUS) reports lag; in Postgres, pg_stat_replication on the primary reports how far each standby has received, flushed and replayed.

Distributed systems connection

Failover is a consensus problem wearing operational clothes: the cluster must agree on who the leader is, and the old leader must stop acting as one. Orchestrator used Raft among its own nodes (networking chapter 20), but agreeing on a new leader does not by itself recover writes the old leader acknowledged and the new one never saw. General pattern: synchronous or semi-synchronous replication (chapter 15) shrinks that gap at the cost of write latency, and fencing tokens stop a stale leader from writing. The post does not say whether GitHub's replication was asynchronous, but the incident shows the general cost of that gap: usually harmless, until a failover turns it into a day.

Key takeaways

  • A 43-second partition triggered an automated promotion of West Coast primaries; East Coast writes that had not replicated west meant both sites held writes the other lacked.
  • The team prioritised data integrity over usability and recovery speed, and restored from backups; the incident lasted 24 hours and 11 minutes.
  • Failover policy must match the topology: GitHub's follow-up was to prevent primary promotion across regional boundaries.
  • Recovery you have never rehearsed (a full rebuild from backup) is recovery of unknown duration, and estimates from a straight line can be badly wrong.
  • Asynchronous replication leaves a gap; failover is the moment that gap becomes visible.
