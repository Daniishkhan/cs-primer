Aurora: The Log Is the Database

A traditional database writes the same change several times in several forms: the log, the data page, a safety copy of the page. Amazon's Aurora, described in a 2017 SIGMOD paper, asks what would happen if the database sent only the log across the network and let a storage service do the rest. The answer reshapes crash recovery, replication and how many copies you can afford to keep.

The pattern in one sentence

Disaggregated storage: if the network is your bottleneck, stop shipping what can be recomputed. Ship the redo log, and let the storage layer rebuild pages from it, in parallel and in the background.

The cast

This case builds on chapter 13 (write-ahead logging, redo, checkpoints, torn pages), chapter 15 (replicas and lag) and chapter 6 (pages and the buffer pool). For the quorum arithmetic, see networking chapter 18. All numbers below are from the paper, "Amazon Aurora: Design Considerations for High Throughput Cloud-Native Relational Databases" by Alexandre Verbitski and nine co-authors, SIGMOD 2017, and are the authors' own measurements.

The database instance
Per the paper, each instance still contains "most of the components of a traditional kernel": query processor, transactions, locking, buffer cache, access methods and undo management. What moves out is redo logging, durable storage, crash recovery and backup/restore.
The storage service
A multi-tenant, scale-out service built for Aurora, running on a fleet of storage nodes with attached SSDs.
Segment and protection group
The volume is cut into fixed-size segments, currently 10 GB in the paper. Each is replicated six ways into a protection group (PG): six segments, two in each of three availability zones.
Availability zone (AZ)
A subset of a region, isolated from most faults such as power, networking and flooding, and linked to other AZs by low-latency links.
LSN, VCL, VDL
The log sequence number orders redo records. The volume complete LSN (VCL) is the highest LSN for which storage can guarantee all earlier records are present. The volume durable LSN (VDL) is the highest consistency-point LSN (a record the database tags as a safe place to truncate) at or below the VCL. Commits wait for it (below).

What happened, step by step

The sequence follows the paper's description of a write, then of failures. The six boxes are the six copies of one protection group.

Step through

A write, then an availability-zone failure

  1. Only the log crosses the network. The primary sends redo log records, batched by the protection group they belong to, to all six segments. The paper stresses that "no pages are ever written from the database tier, not for background writes, not for checkpointing, and not for cache eviction."
  2. Four of six is durable. Each storage node queues the record and, as the paper puts it, persists it on disk and acknowledges. The write is hardened when four of the six reply, and commits are acknowledged asynchronously once the VDL has passed the transaction's commit LSN. A slow node is just a slow node: the other four are enough.
  3. Storage does the rest in the background. Nodes organize the records, notice gaps, gossip with peers to fill them, coalesce the log into data pages, stage the log and pages to S3, garbage collect old versions and check CRCs. The paper says only the first two steps (receive and persist) are in the foreground path.
  4. An entire AZ fails. Four copies remain. With a write quorum of 4, writes continue; reads need 3, so they do too.
  5. One more failure (AZ plus one node). Three copies remain: enough for the read quorum of 3, so no data is lost and the volume can be repaired, but not for the write quorum of 4. The paper calls this "AZ+1": the design tolerates it without losing data, and tolerates losing any two copies, including an AZ, while still writing. Repairing segments quickly (below) is what restores the write quorum.

The pattern explained

Why only the log?

The paper's argument is that "the central constraint in high throughput data processing has moved from compute and storage to the network". In a mirrored MySQL setup, one logical write turns into many physical ones. The paper lists the redo log, the binary log archived to S3 for point-in-time restore, the modified data pages, a second temporary write of each page (the double-write that prevents torn pages, see chapter 13), and metadata files. It adds that several of those steps (steps 1, 3 and 5 in its figure) are sequential and synchronous, so latency is additive and jitter is amplified.

Aurora's key move is to treat a page as a cache of the log. Traditional databases already generate a redo record for every change and apply it to the in-memory page. In Aurora, the log applicator runs in the storage tier. In the paper's words, "the log is the database, and any pages that the storage system materializes are simply a cache of log applications." Materialising pages in the background is entirely optional for correctness. It just avoids rebuilding a page from its full chain of changes on each read.

The six-copy quorum

The classic choice is three copies with write and read quorums of two. The paper argues that is inadequate: an AZ failure is a correlated failure of every disk in that AZ, and on top of it there is always background noise of individual failures. With three copies, an AZ loss plus a single other failure leaves you unable to tell whether the last copy is current. Aurora uses six copies, two per AZ, with V = 6, write quorum 4, read quorum 3. The quorums overlap (4 + 3 > 6) and two writes always overlap (4 > 6/2), the same rules as in networking chapter 18. The paper gives the consequences: lose an AZ plus one node and you keep read availability; lose any two nodes, including a whole AZ, and you keep write availability.

Segments make repair fast

Failures are unavoidable, so the paper focuses on shrinking the window in which a second failure could hurt. Small 10 GB segments are the unit of repair, and the paper says a 10 GB segment "can be repaired in 10 seconds on a 10Gbps network link". To lose quorum you would need two such failures within that window plus the failure of an AZ containing neither.

No quorum reads, short recovery

You might expect a 3-of-6 read for every page fetch. The paper says the database does not use read quorums under normal circumstances: it tracks which segments are complete up to its read point and reads from one of them. Quorum reads are needed on recovery, when that runtime state is lost. Crash recovery is spread across ordinary processing, because storage applies the log continuously. The paper says an Aurora database can recover "generally under 10 seconds" even after a crash while processing more than 100,000 write statements per second. Up to 15 read replicas can mount the same storage volume, receive the log stream too, and the paper reports they typically lag the writer by 20 ms or less.

The measured claim

The paper reports that over the 30 minutes Aurora sustained 35 times more transactions than mirrored MySQL, with 7.7 times fewer I/Os per transaction on the database node, "despite amplifying writes six times", and that each storage node sees unamplified writes, which gives 46 times fewer I/Os to process at that tier. Treat these as the authors' benchmark for one workload in 2017, run by the vendor, not a guarantee for your application. The paper's other benchmarks, such as write-only throughput tables, are not reproduced here.

Analogy: the master score and the printed pages

Imagine a choir whose music is printed. The old way: each time the conductor edits a bar, a fresh printed page of the whole piece is couriered to six cities. The new way: send six cities only a one-line edit instruction, and each city's print shop updates its own pages overnight, or on demand if a singer asks. The edit list is the score; the printed pages are a convenience.

Where it breaks down: an edit list needs strict ordering and no gaps, so Aurora's storage nodes gossip to fill missing records and the engine tracks completeness with LSNs.

Trade-offs and lessons

Spend storage to save network

Six copies sounds wasteful, yet the paper argues that writing less data lets you replicate aggressively. The saving is in what you ship, not in what you keep.

Tied to the log format

General pattern: storage that understands the redo format cannot be a generic block device. The design couples engine and storage; the paper describes a purpose-built service for Aurora.

Foreground small, background large

Only receive-and-persist sit on the commit path. Coalescing, garbage collection and scrubbing can wait, and the paper notes that, for example, garbage collection need not run while a node is busy with foreground writes unless the disk is nearly full.

Single writer

The paper describes a single writer with up to 15 read replicas sharing the volume. Scaling writes across multiple writers is a different problem; this paper does not address it.

What this page did not read

A 2018 SIGMOD follow-up on avoiding distributed commits and quorum reads exists. It was not read for this page, so nothing here depends on it. Aurora has also evolved since 2017, and the paper notes Aurora was then based on the MySQL 5.6 code base. Check current AWS documentation before assuming any detail still holds.

Try it yourself

Pages as a cache of the log, and quorum arithmetic

This script is a sketch with made-up sizes (the 100-byte record size is an assumption, not from the paper). It shows a storage node applying a log into pages, then checks the 4-of-6 and 3-of-6 quorums against AZ failures.

# --- Part 1: what crosses the network for one row update -------------------
PAGE = 16 * 1024          # a typical InnoDB page size (ch. 6)
REDO = 100                # an ASSUMED size for one redo record: illustrative only
print("page-shipping engine sends ~%d bytes; log-shipping engine sends ~%d bytes" % (PAGE, REDO))

# --- Part 2: a storage node materialises pages from the log ----------------
class StorageNode:
    def __init__(self): self.log = []; self.pages = {}
    def receive(self, lsn, page, delta):   # foreground: append + ack
        self.log.append((lsn, page, delta)); return "ack"
    def coalesce(self):                    # background: apply log to pages
        for lsn, page, delta in sorted(self.log):
            self.pages[page] = self.pages.get(page, 0) + delta
        self.log.clear()
    def read(self, page):                  # a read applies any pending records first
        self.coalesce(); return self.pages.get(page, 0)

n = StorageNode()
for lsn, (p, d) in enumerate([("A", 5), ("B", 2), ("A", -1)], start=1): n.receive(lsn, p, d)
print("page A as of the log:", n.read("A"), " page B:", n.read("B"))

# --- Part 3: which failures can a 4-of-6 write / 3-of-6 read quorum survive? -
copies = [(az, i) for az in "ABC" for i in (0, 1)]
def alive(down): return [c for c in copies if c not in down]
az_down = {c for c in copies if c[0] == "C"}
for label, down in [("one AZ down", az_down),
                    ("one AZ + 1 node", az_down | {("A", 0)}),
                    ("one AZ + 2 nodes", az_down | {("A", 0), ("B", 0)})]:
    n_up = len(alive(down))
    print("%-18s copies up=%d  write(4)=%-5s read(3)=%s" % (label, n_up, n_up >= 4, n_up >= 3))
print("Vw+Vr > V:", 4 + 3 > 6, "  Vw > V/2:", 4 > 6 / 2)
page-shipping engine sends ~16384 bytes; log-shipping engine sends ~100 bytes
page A as of the log: 4  page B: 2
one AZ down        copies up=4  write(4)=True  read(3)=True
one AZ + 1 node    copies up=3  write(4)=False read(3)=True
one AZ + 2 nodes   copies up=2  write(4)=False read(3)=False

The last line printed is Vw+Vr > V: True Vw > V/2: True. Try changing the quorum to 3 copies with 2 and 2 and re-asking what one AZ plus one node does to it. That is the paper's argument against 2/3 quorums.

Distributed systems connection

Aurora re-uses the ideas of the whole course: a write-ahead log as the single source of truth (chapter 13), replication where the log is the unit shipped (chapter 15), and quorum voting with overlapping read and write sets (networking chapter 18). Notice that, per the paper, it avoids a chatty protocol like two-phase commit between the six copies for each write: the single writer allocates LSNs and tracks acknowledgements, and storage nodes fill gaps by gossip. Having one writer makes that simplification possible.

Key takeaways

  • The paper's thesis is that the network, not compute or storage, is the bottleneck in a cloud database.
  • Aurora ships only redo log records to storage; storage nodes apply the log and materialise pages, so pages are a cache of the log.
  • Six copies in three AZs, write quorum 4, read quorum 3, survive an AZ failure plus one more node without data loss.
  • 10 GB segments are repaired quickly (10 seconds on a 10 Gbps link, per the paper), shrinking the window for a second failure.
  • Normal reads go to one up-to-date segment, not a quorum, and recovery is mostly continuous background work.
