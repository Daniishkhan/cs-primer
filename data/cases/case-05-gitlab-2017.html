The Backups That Weren't: GitLab, 2017

On 31 January 2017, an engineer working late at night, on a database that was already in trouble, deleted the data directory of the wrong server. That was a human error that any tired person might make. The deeper story is what happened next: the team reached for their backups and found that the ones they relied on were not there, not working, or not the right kind of backup at all.

The pattern in one sentence

An untested backup is not a backup: a replica, a snapshot and a scheduled dump each protect against different failures, and only a restore you have actually performed, and timed, proves that you can get your data back.

The cast

This case builds on chapter 13 (backups, replicas and point-in-time recovery) and chapter 15 (leaders, followers and replication lag). Everything about GitLab below comes from its own postmortem, published on 10 February 2017.

db1 (the primary)
According to the postmortem, GitLab.com ran one primary PostgreSQL server, db1.cluster.gitlab.com, that handled all the load. The post calls it a single point of failure.
db2 (the secondary)
One hot-standby, db2.cluster.gitlab.com, which the post says existed only for failover.
WAL segments
PostgreSQL's write-ahead log, cut into files (chapter 13). A secondary stays current by streaming this log. If the primary deletes segments before the secondary has received them, the secondary cannot catch up. GitLab.com did not use WAL archiving, so the secondary had to be rebuilt from scratch with pg_basebackup.
The staging database
At about 17:20 UTC an engineer had loaded an LVM snapshot of production into staging to test connection pooling. That copy turned out to be the thing that saved the day.
The engineer
The postmortem's root cause analysis points at missing procedures and missing ownership as well as at the mistaken command.

What happened, step by step

All times are UTC on 31 January 2017, as given in the postmortem.

Step through

From a load spike to an empty primary

  1. A load spike (about 19:00). The postmortem says database load increased, probably from spam, and that part of it came from a background job removing a GitLab employee's data after the account was flagged for abuse and scheduled for removal by mistake.
  2. Replication falls behind (about 23:00). The primary removed WAL segments the secondary had not yet received. With no WAL archiving, the only fix was to rebuild the secondary with pg_basebackup, which first needs an empty data directory.
  3. Rebuild attempts. pg_basebackup hung without output. The team raised max_wal_senders from 3 to 32, then had to lower max_connections from 8000 to 2000 so PostgreSQL would restart. Around 23:30 the engineer decided to clear the secondary's data directory and try again.
  4. The wrong server. The command was run on the primary, db1. In the postmortem's words, the engineer "terminated the process a second or two after noticing their mistake", but around 300 GB had already been removed.
  5. No data on either host. db2's directory had been emptied as part of the rebuild, db1's by mistake. The team went looking for backups and, per the post, "the process of both finding and using backups failed completely" (next section).
  6. Recovery from staging. They chose the staging copy, taken about 6 hours before the outage, over a much older option. The data directory was copied back to production, which the post says took about 18 hours because staging used slow disks, then webhooks and sequences were fixed up.

Four copies, none of them a working backup

The postmortem lists the procedures GitLab.com had in place and why each one did not save them. The first two were real backups that were not working. The last two were never backups at all.

The pg_dump story is worth reading closely. The post explains that the cron job ran on an application server with no PostgreSQL data directory, so it fell back to the 9.2 binary, and a major-version mismatch makes pg_dump fail. The failure notices went out as email, and the post says they were rejected because they were not DMARC-signed. So the backup failed, and the alarm that should have said so failed too. The root cause analysis puts the underlying reason plainly: because there was no ownership, "nobody was responsible for testing this procedure".

The pattern: replicas, backups and point-in-time recovery

This section is the general pattern, not a claim about GitLab beyond what is cited above. Four things are easy to confuse, and chapter 13 separates them:

Replica

A live copy that tracks the primary, for availability and read scaling (chapter 15). It faithfully replays mistakes: a bad DELETE or DROP TABLE reaches it within moments. It protects against a dead machine, not against a wrong command.

Backup

A separate copy from a past moment, held somewhere the primary cannot damage. It protects against mistakes, corruption and loss of the whole site. It is only as recent as its last run.

Point-in-time recovery

A base backup plus an archive of every WAL segment since. You replay the log up to a chosen moment, such as just before the bad command. The postmortem's remediation list includes point-in-time recovery and continuous archiving for PostgreSQL.

Restore test

The only step that proves the other three. Measure two numbers: how much data you lose (the recovery point) and how long the restore takes (the recovery time). GitLab's snapshot was about 6 hours old, and copying it back took about 18.

Analogy: the fire drill

A smoke alarm with no battery and a fire exit nobody has ever opened look identical to working ones until the day you need them. A backup is a fire exit: you only know it works if you have walked through it, on a quiet day, with a stopwatch.

Where it breaks down: a fire drill does not change the building, whereas a restore drill may reveal that the data itself is corrupt, which is exactly the kind of thing you want to learn early.

Their response, and the transparency

The postmortem lists remediations rather than blaming a person. Among them: sane max_connections, point-in-time recovery, hourly LVM snapshots of production databases, Azure disk snapshots of production, automated testing of restoring database backups, clearer replication documentation and runbooks, evaluating pgbarman and WAL-E, and assigning an owner for data durability. It also says it would rather make it clearer which host you are on than stop engineers from running commands, arguing that aliasing rm would not protect against corruption or other ways to lose data.

GitLab also worked in public. The post says a public Google document tracked progress, and the recovery was streamed on YouTube with a peak of about 5,000 viewers. (The post links that document. This page does not rely on it.)

Inconsistent figures in the source

The postmortem's overview says the lost data runs from 17:20 to 00:00 UTC, while its data-loss section says 17:20 to 23:30 UTC. Both start at the staging snapshot. It also gives the scale as at least 5,000 projects and comments and roughly 700 new user accounts. Git repositories and wikis were unavailable but not lost. This page quotes the figures as the post gives them.

Trade-offs and lessons

Silence is not success

A job that reports only failures, by a channel that can itself fail, looks perfect when it is broken. General pattern: alert on the absence of a fresh, verified backup, not on the presence of an error email.

Make dangerous steps hard to misfire

Making it clearer which host you are on, as the post proposes, is cheaper than hoping nobody ever types the wrong command at midnight. General pattern: confirmations that show the hostname, and separate prompts per environment.

Backups need an owner

The post's root cause analysis ends with no one owning the procedure. A shared responsibility is often nobody's.

Keep the recovery point and time in view

Taking hourly snapshots shrinks the data you can lose. Practising restores shrinks the hours you wait. They are separate dials.

Try it yourself

A replica copies mistakes; a backup remembers

This script uses only Python's built-in sqlite3 and a temporary directory. It makes a "replica" and a backup, applies a mistaken delete to the primary and replica, then performs the restore test.

import sqlite3, os, tempfile

d = tempfile.mkdtemp()
prim = sqlite3.connect(os.path.join(d, "primary.db"))
prim.execute("CREATE TABLE issues(id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, title TEXT)")
prim.executemany("INSERT INTO issues(title) VALUES (?)", [("issue %d" % i,) for i in range(1, 101)])
prim.commit()

repl = sqlite3.connect(os.path.join(d, "replica.db"))
prim.backup(repl)                      # initial copy of the replica

snap = sqlite3.connect(os.path.join(d, "backup-0100.db"))
prim.backup(snap)                      # a point-in-time backup, kept apart

def both(sql):                         # "replication": same change on both
    for c in (prim, repl):
        c.execute(sql); c.commit()

count = lambda c: c.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM issues").fetchone()[0]
both("DELETE FROM issues WHERE id > 50")          # the mistake
print("primary rows:", count(prim))
print("replica rows:", count(repl), " <- replication copied the mistake")
print("backup rows :", count(snap), " <- the old copy still has it")

restored = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
snap.backup(restored)
ok = restored.execute("PRAGMA integrity_check").fetchone()[0] == "ok" and count(restored) == 100
print("restore test passed:", ok)
for f in os.listdir(d): os.remove(os.path.join(d, f))
os.rmdir(d)

Output on my machine:

primary rows: 50
replica rows: 50  <- replication copied the mistake
backup rows : 100  <- the old copy still has it
restore test passed: True

Now do the same thinking for a database you run: when was its last restore test, and how long did it take? If you cannot answer, you have found your first task.

Distributed systems connection

Replication is the first tool people reach for in a distributed setting, and it is the wrong tool for this job. A replica is a second place where the same write must succeed, so it multiplies availability but also propagates every logical error. The two ideas compose: replicas keep you up through a machine failure, while backups with a log archive let you travel back in time. See networking chapter 18 for replication and consistency, and the failover story in the GitHub 2018 case.

Key takeaways

  • A load spike led to replication lag, a manual rebuild of the secondary, and a data directory deleted on the primary by mistake.
  • The postmortem lists four mechanisms: pg_dump, Azure snapshots, LVM snapshots and replication. None gave them a usable recent backup. The recovery used a staging copy about 6 hours old.
  • Replicas are for availability, backups are for mistakes, and point-in-time recovery needs a base backup plus a log archive.
  • Silent failures and unowned procedures hide broken backups. Practise restores and time them.
  • GitLab published a candid postmortem and streamed the recovery.

Check your understanding

Why could the replica not be used to recover from the deleted data directory?

  1. Replicas cannot store 300 GB
  2. Replication was meant for failover, and in this case the secondary had been wiped too while it was being rebuilt
  3. PostgreSQL does not support replicas
  4. The replica was in a different time zone
The postmortem says replication was for failover rather than disaster recovery, and both hosts had been wiped. More generally, a replica copies mistakes such as deletes.

What went wrong with the daily pg_dump backup?

  1. It ran only on Sundays
  2. It filled the disk
  3. It used PostgreSQL 9.2 tools on a 9.6 database, so it failed, and the failure emails were rejected
  4. It backed up the wrong database on purpose
The job ran where no data directory existed, defaulted to 9.2, and failed. The notifications were not DMARC-signed and were rejected, so nobody knew.

What made point-in-time recovery impossible for GitLab.com at the time?

  1. There was no WAL archiving, and PITR is on the remediation list
  2. PostgreSQL has no write-ahead log
  3. The engineer chose not to use it
  4. It requires a Windows server
The post says GitLab.com did not use WAL archiving, and lists point-in-time recovery and continuous archiving as a remediation. PITR replays an archived log on top of a base backup.

Which action would best have exposed the broken backups before the outage?

  1. Adding more replicas
  2. Renaming the servers
  3. Aliasing rm
  4. Regularly restoring a backup into a scratch environment and checking it
The post lists automated testing of database backup recovery as a follow-up, and says nobody was responsible for testing the procedure. A restore test would have found an empty bucket on the first run.
