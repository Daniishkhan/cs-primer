Sharding Postgres at Figma

Figma's whole product's metadata, such as permissions, files and comments, began life in one Postgres database. Two engineering posts, from 2023 and 2024, tell how it was carved up: first by moving groups of tables to their own databases, then by splitting individual tables across many shards behind a query proxy. It is a good case study in doing something dangerous in small, reversible steps.

The pattern in one sentence

Shard in two moves: first decide where every row logically lives and route queries accordingly, and only then move the data physically, so that each step can be tested and rolled back separately.

The cast

This case applies chapter 16 (shard keys, hot spots, cross-shard queries, routing) and chapter 7 (indexes: a shard key is, in effect, the one index the whole system leans on). Facts below are from Figma's two posts.

The starting point
Per the 2023 post, in 2020 most of Figma's metadata lived in one large Amazon RDS database whose peak CPU reached "upwards of 65%", with database traffic growing roughly 3x a year. The 2024 post says that by late 2022 the stack included caching, read replicas and about a dozen vertically partitioned databases.
Vertical partitioning
Moving whole groups of tables into their own databases. The 2024 post notes its limit: "the smallest unit of partitioning is a single table."
Horizontal sharding
Splitting one table's rows across several databases by a shard key.
Colo (colocation)
The post's name for a group of related tables that share a sharding key and physical layout. In the post's words, tables within a colo "support cross-table joins and full transactions when restricted to a single sharding key".
DBProxy
A Go service the post describes between the application and PgBouncer. It handles load shedding, observability, transactions, topology management and query routing, and contains the query engine.

Step through

From one database to shards behind a proxy

  1. One big database. In 2020 the metadata lived in a single large RDS Postgres. The 2023 post lists the early fixes: a bigger instance (r5.12xlarge to r5.24xlarge), multiple read replicas, new databases for new use cases, and PgBouncer for connection pooling.
  2. Vertical partitioning. Figma moved groups of tables into separate databases, choosing tables by impact (moving them should shift a significant share of the workload) and isolation (not strongly connected to other tables). Each move used logical replication and took, per the post, roughly 30 seconds of partial availability. The last one, in October 2022, moved 50 tables. But a single table with billions of rows cannot be split this way.
  3. Logical sharding first. The 2024 post says the team "separated the concept of 'logical sharding' at the application layer from 'physical sharding' at the Postgres layer". Queries are routed through DBProxy by shard key, while the data still sits in one database. The post notes that rolling back logical sharding when bugs appeared "was a simple configuration change".
  4. Physical sharding. Only when the logical layer is trusted is data copied to other databases and traffic rerouted. The first horizontally sharded table shipped in September 2023, and the post reports that the failover caused "only ten seconds of partial availability on database primaries".

The pattern explained

Why not a distributed database?

The 2024 post says the team looked at distributed SQL systems (CockroachDB, TiDB, Spanner, Vitess) and NoSQL, and decided against them: switching would need a complex data migration, the team had deep in-house expertise running RDS Postgres, and it favoured known low-risk solutions. They chose to build horizontal sharding on top of their existing RDS Postgres, deliberately supporting a smaller feature set. The post also says the team plans to reassess the in-house approach against NewSQL and managed options once it has bandwidth. That is Figma's reasoning in 2024, not a general verdict.

Shard keys and colos

Chapter 16 stresses that the shard key is the decision you cannot easily undo. Figma chose keys per group of tables, with examples such as UserID, FileID and OrgID, and put tables that share a key into a colo so joins and transactions within one key stay inside one shard. For routing, the post says "we decided to use the hash of the sharding key": it avoids hot spots that auto-incrementing or timestamp-prefixed IDs would create, at the cost of making range scans on the shard key less efficient.

DBProxy and the query language

The post says single-shard queries are routed directly, and queries without a shard key become scatter-gather, which is costly, so the supported language is restricted. The team used production data to choose a subset covering "the most common 90% of queries": point and range scans are allowed, and joins only within a colo on the sharding key.

Rolling out safely

The post describes several layers of caution. A shadow planning framework ran against live traffic to see which query patterns would work under candidate sharding schemes. Shadow reads sent live reads through the new views to compare correctness and performance. Load tests found the views added minimal overhead in most cases, under 10% in the worst. Sharded reads and writes were enabled gradually behind feature flags. Logical and physical sharding being separate meant a bug found at the logical stage was a configuration rollback, not a data migration.

Analogy: rearranging the library

The library has one enormous room (our running library). First you put up signs saying which aisle each book would go in, and have staff follow the signs while the books stay where they are. If the signs are wrong, you take them down. Only when the signs are trusted do you actually carry shelves into a second building.

Where it breaks down: books do not change while you carry them. Rows are being written throughout, which is why Figma needed logical replication to keep the copy current.

Trade-offs and lessons

You give up database guarantees

The 2024 post says horizontal sharding removes some ACID guarantees: foreign keys and global unique indexes are no longer enforced by Postgres, and partial commit failures must be handled in the application. Cross-shard atomic transactions were deliberately not supported.

Restrict the query language

Figma picked a query language covering the most common 90% of queries rather than everything. As a general consequence, queries outside that subset must be rewritten or pay the scatter-gather price.

Do the easy cut first

Vertical partitioning bought runway. The 2023 post says it also lost cross-table atomic transactions, foreign key validation and joins across partitions, and that routing complexity "scales multiplicatively" as databases and clients grow.

Unfinished work is honest work

The post lists what is still open: sharded schema updates, globally unique IDs, cross-shard transactions, distributed unique indexes, an ORM that supports sharding, and automated reshards.

Read with care

These are Figma's choices for Figma's workload and constraints. Another company with different queries could reasonably choose Vitess, Citus or a managed distributed database. This page makes no claim that one is better.

Try it yourself

Logical shards, physical moves

This toy hashes keys to a fixed number of logical shards and keeps a routing table to physical databases. It is a sketch of the idea, not Figma's code. It uses only Python's standard library.

import hashlib

N_LOGICAL = 8                              # logical shards: fixed up front
def logical_shard(shard_key: str) -> int:  # stable hash (not Python's salted hash())
    return int(hashlib.sha256(shard_key.encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % N_LOGICAL

routing = {s: "db-A" for s in range(N_LOGICAL)}   # everything on one physical database
def route(k): return routing[logical_shard(k)]

keys = ["user:%d" % i for i in range(1000)]
def spread():
    out = {}
    for k in keys: out[route(k)] = out.get(route(k), 0) + 1
    return dict(sorted(out.items()))

before_map = {k: logical_shard(k) for k in keys}
print("before:", spread())
for s in range(4, 8): routing[s] = "db-B"          # 'physical' move of 4 logical shards
print("after :", spread())
print("keys whose logical shard changed:", sum(before_map[k] != logical_shard(k) for k in keys))

def query_by_key(k):  return {route(k)}
def query_by_email(): return set(routing.values())      # no shard key: ask everyone
print("lookup by shard key hits :", sorted(query_by_key("user:42")))
print("lookup by other column hits:", sorted(query_by_email()))
before: {'db-A': 1000}
after : {'db-A': 487, 'db-B': 513}
keys whose logical shard changed: 0
lookup by shard key hits : ['db-A']
lookup by other column hits: ['db-A', 'db-B']

Moving data changed only the routing table; no key changed its logical shard. Now try N_LOGICAL = 2 and then moving shard 1: with so few logical shards, how even can the split be? That is why logical shard counts are chosen generously.

Distributed systems connection

A fixed number of logical partitions, mapped onto machines by a small routing table, is the same idea you meet in consistent hashing and in partitioned log systems (chapter 19 on partitions and keys). The table is itself shared state that every router must agree on, so changing it is a coordination problem. The post says topology updates propagate in under a second. Compare the replication and failover story in chapter 15, which each shard still needs.

Key takeaways

  • Figma's growth moved it from one RDS Postgres to about a dozen vertical partitions, then to horizontal sharding for tables too large to split any other way.
  • Colos group tables that share a shard key, so joins and transactions stay inside one shard.
  • Logical sharding (routing) was separated from physical sharding (moving data), making early steps reversible by configuration.
  • DBProxy supports a restricted query language; queries without a shard key scatter to all shards.
  • Sharding costs you cross-shard transactions, foreign keys and global unique indexes unless you rebuild them in the application.

Check your understanding

Why was vertical partitioning not enough for Figma's largest tables?

  1. Postgres cannot have more than one database
  2. The smallest unit of vertical partitioning is a whole table, so a single huge table cannot be split
  3. It required downtime of several hours each time
  4. It made reads slower on every table
The 2024 post says the smallest unit of partitioning is a single table. Horizontal sharding splits one table's rows across databases.

What does separating logical from physical sharding buy?

  1. Routing can be tested and rolled back with configuration before any data moves between databases
  2. It removes the need for a shard key
  3. It makes cross-shard transactions atomic
  4. It lets each query touch every shard cheaply
The post says rolling back logical sharding was a simple configuration change. Physical rollback is more complicated.

What happens to a query that lacks the shard key?

  1. It is rejected by Postgres
  2. It is sent to a random shard
  3. It is cached forever
  4. It is scattered to all shards and the results gathered, which is costly
Per the post, single-shard queries are routed directly while queries missing a shard key need scatter-gather. Chapter 16 covers the same cost in general.

Which guarantee does the post say horizontal sharding weakens?

  1. Durability of committed single-row writes
  2. The ability to run SELECT
  3. Foreign keys, global unique indexes and cross-shard atomic transactions
  4. Hash functions
The post says foreign keys and global unique indexes are no longer enforced by Postgres, and cross-shard atomic transactions were deliberately not supported.
