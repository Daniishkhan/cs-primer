Design a Payments Ledger

A wallet balance is just a number, and the whole job of the system is to keep that number true: never negative, never double-counted, and always explainable. Where the URL shortener could shrug off a lost click, this design may not lose a cent. That changes almost every choice.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing the core ledger of a wallet app: users hold balances and send money to each other.

  • Balances must never be wrong and never negative.
  • Every change to a balance must be auditable: you can say exactly why it is what it is.
  • Peak load is 5,000 transfers per second.
  • Mobile clients retry on timeouts, often without knowing whether the first attempt worked.
  • One currency, in whole cents. Ignore card networks, fees and foreign exchange for now.

Your notes

Keep notes as you go: decisions, numbers, open questions. Plain text is fine; try sketching boxes with characters, as in an interview whiteboard.

1Requirements and invariants

Before any boxes: what must always be true, no matter what fails or how requests interleave? Write the invariants as statements you could check with a query. What is out of scope? How is this different from the URL shortener in how wrong it is allowed to be?

Compare with a worked answer

In a ledger, the requirements are really invariants: facts that stay true across every state of the database. Good ones are precise enough to turn into a query:

  1. Conservation: a transfer creates no money. The sum of all changes in the ledger is zero (money enters and leaves only through a designated "outside world" account).
  2. No overdraft: no user account balance is below zero, at any instant a reader could observe.
  3. Explainability: every account's balance equals the sum of its entries, and every entry belongs to a transfer.
  4. At-most-once effect: one user intention (one tap on "send") moves money at most once, however many times the request is delivered.
  5. Atomicity: a transfer either happens completely (debit and credit) or not at all.
  6. Immutability: history is never edited. A mistake is fixed by a new, opposite entry.
  7. Durability: once we tell a user "sent", the transfer survives a crash (chapter 13) and a failover (chapter 15).

Out of scope: multiple currencies, foreign exchange, fees, card networks and settlement with banks, pending holds, chargebacks. Naming what you leave out is part of a good answer.

The trade you are making: compare the URL shortener (studio 1), where the sensible stance was "fast and slightly stale is fine, losing a click is fine". Here correctness beats latency and availability. If the system cannot be sure, it should refuse the transfer, not guess. This is a consistency-first design (the "C" end of the trade-offs in networking ch. 18), and that explains the rest: a single leader, transactions, constraints, and a willingness to retry.

2Back-of-the-envelope numbers

Estimate row writes per second at peak, how much history you accumulate per year, how big the hot data (balances) is, and whether one database machine could possibly handle the peak. State your assumptions.

Compare with a worked answer
  • Row writes at peak: each transfer writes 1 transfer row, 2 ledger entries, 2 balance updates and 1 outbox row (stage 6): about 6 row writes. 5,000 × 6 = ~30,000 row writes/s, all inside 5,000 small transactions per second. Each transaction must reach durable storage before we acknowledge (systems ch. 9), though the database batches concurrent commits into shared fsyncs (group commit).
  • Average vs peak: assume the average is about a fifth of the peak: 1,000 transfers/s. That is 86 M transfers a day, about 31 billion a year, and double that in entries.
  • History size: an entry row is small (ids, signed amount, timestamp), perhaps 100 bytes with its indexes. 63 B entries × 100 B ≈ 6 TB per year of entries, plus transfers. And we must keep it: audit demands it. So: partition entries by time, keep recent partitions on fast storage, archive old ones to cheap storage and a column store for analysis (chapter 9).
  • Hot data: balances. With, say, 50 million accounts at ~50 bytes each, that is about 2.5 GB. It fits in RAM easily. The hot set is tiny; the history is huge.
  • Can one machine do 5,000 transactions/s? On modern hardware with fast SSDs, a well-tuned relational database can run several thousand small write transactions per second, so 5,000 is plausible but with little headroom, especially with synchronous replication. Hence: start with one primary, design it so we can partition later, and test it rather than assume.
  • Contention is the other number: a popular merchant account might receive thousands of payments a second, all updating one balance row. A single row can be updated by only one transaction at a time, so that row's throughput is bounded by commit time. We come back to this in stage 7.

What worries me is not volume, it is correctness under concurrency and failure, plus hot rows.

3The API, and idempotency keys

Design the "send money" call. A client sends it, the network drops the response, and the client sends it again. How does the server know whether these are the same intention or two different ones? What goes in the request, and what does a retry return?

Compare with a worked answer

The core problem is one you met in networking ch. 16: when the response is lost, the client cannot tell "it never arrived" from "it was done and the answer was lost". So the client will retry, and the server must make retrying safe. The standard tool is an idempotency key: a unique id the client generates once per intention (a random UUID made when the user taps "send") and reuses on every retry of that intention.

POST /transfers
Idempotency-Key: 7c9e6679-7425-40de-944b-e07fc1f90ae7
Content-Type: application/json

{ "from": 1, "to": 2, "amount_cents": 7000 }

201 Created   { "transfer_id": 4182, "status": "completed" }
422           { "error": "insufficient_funds" }
409           { "error": "idempotency_key_reused_with_different_request" }
  • Amounts are integers in the smallest unit (cents). Never floating point: 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3 in binary floating point.
  • Same key, same request: the server returns the original result (the same 201 and transfer id), without moving money again. That includes a stored failure such as 422, so a retry gets the same answer.
  • Same key, different request: a client bug. Return an error rather than guessing. Store a fingerprint of the request with the key to detect it.
  • Who generates the key? The client, once per user intention; not the server (it cannot know a retry is a retry) and not per attempt.
  • How long to keep keys? Retries happen within minutes or hours, not months. Keeping each key for a window such as a day or several days (then pruning) bounds the index size; document that a retry after the window is not protected.
  • Reads: GET /accounts/{id} for the balance and GET /accounts/{id}/entries for the statement. Both are safe to repeat.

Idempotency is also what lets the client use aggressive retry policies with backoff and jitter (retry storms) without risking duplicates: the server, not the client, absorbs the danger.

4The data model

How do you record money so that every balance can be explained? Sketch the tables. Is a balance a stored number, a computed one, or both? What does "append-only" mean for your tables, and how do you fix a mistake?

Compare with a worked answer

Accountants solved this centuries ago with double-entry bookkeeping: every movement of money is recorded as at least two entries that sum to zero, a debit from one account and a credit to another. Money is never created or destroyed in a transfer, only moved. In database terms:

entry idtransferaccountamount (cents)
10141821 (Alice)-7000
10241822 (Bob)+7000

The tables are transfers (one row per user intention, carrying the idempotency key), entries (the immutable signed amounts, at least two per transfer) and accounts (current balance). Money enters and leaves the system through a special outside-world account (a deposit is a transfer from it, a withdrawal a transfer to it), which is allowed to go negative; that is how the global invariant "all entries sum to zero" stays true.

  • Entries are the truth. A balance is by definition the sum of an account's entries. If the balance and the entries ever disagree, the entries win.
  • The balance column is a cache, with a check. Summing millions of entries on every read would be slow, so we keep a stored balance, updated in the same transaction as the entries. It is a derived value, exactly the idea of chapter 19, except we derive it synchronously because the invariant (no overdraft) needs the current value. A reconciliation job (stage 7) recomputes it from entries to catch any disagreement. Alternatives: purely derived (always correct, slow at scale; mitigate with periodic checkpoint balances), or purely stored (fast, but nothing to check against: a bug silently corrupts money).
  • Append-only and immutable. Application code never updates or deletes an entry. To enforce that, grant the application's database role INSERT and SELECT on entries but not UPDATE or DELETE. If a transfer was wrong, the fix is a reversal: a new transfer with opposite entries, referencing the original. The history then shows the mistake and its correction, which is what an auditor wants. (Optionally, chain entry hashes so tampering is detectable.)
  • Constraints do the policing. CHECK (amount > 0) on transfers, foreign keys from entries to transfers and accounts, UNIQUE on the idempotency key, and CHECK (balance >= 0) on user accounts. The database refuses states that break the rules, even if a bug in the application tries (chapter 10, the "C").
  • Indexes: entries by (account_id, id) for statements and reconciliation; transfers by idempotency key (unique). Partition entries by time (stage 2).

5Concurrency control

Two transfers debit the same account at the same moment, each valid alone but not together. Name the anomalies that threaten the invariants. Then compare: (a) SELECT ... FOR UPDATE with a consistent lock order, (b) serializable isolation with retries, (c) a conditional UPDATE ... WHERE balance >= x. Pick one and justify it.

Compare with a worked answer

The threats (chapter 11):

  • Lost update. Alice has $100. Two $70 transfers each read 100, each decide "enough", each write 30. Two debits of $70 happened, but the balance fell by only $70: $70 of money was created from nothing. (Both transfers passed the "enough money" check against the same stale number.)
  • Write skew. The invariant spans several rows, so two transactions that each check it and each write a different row can both pass. For example: a rule that the sum of a user's two wallets must stay non-negative, or "no more than $1,000 sent per day" checked by summing today's transfers. Snapshot isolation does not catch these (the doctors-on-call example).
  • Deadlock. Transfer A to B and a simultaneous B to A each lock their source first, then wait for the other's row (systems ch. 13).

The options:

OptionHow it protectsCosts
(a) SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, orderedLock both account rows first (ORDER BY id), then read balances, check, write. The lock makes the read-then-write atomic. Taking locks in one global order (lowest id first) prevents the A to B vs B to A deadlock.Two round trips; locks are held while application code thinks. Good when the rule needs several rows to be examined consistently. Hot rows serialise.
(b) Serializable + retryThe database detects any outcome no serial order could produce, aborts one transaction, and you retry it (Postgres uses SSI, chapter 12). Handles write skew automatically.Every code path must catch the serialisation failure and retry. Abort rate rises with contention, and a hot account can make retries pile up. Some false-positive aborts.
(c) Conditional updateUPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 7000 WHERE id = 1 AND balance >= 7000. One statement: the database locks the row, evaluates the condition against the latest committed balance, and either applies the change (1 row affected) or does nothing (0 rows). A concurrent debit waits for the lock, then re-checks.Only protects an invariant that lives in one row. The rowcount must be checked. Cannot express multi-row rules.

My pick: (c), the conditional update, inside one transaction, with the two row updates issued in ascending account-id order. Our headline invariant ("a balance never goes below zero") lives in a single row, so a single atomic statement enforces it: no lost update is possible (the arithmetic happens inside the database, not in the app) and no overdraft is possible. It takes one round trip, holds the row lock only until commit, runs fine at Read Committed, and needs no retry loop for correctness. Lock ordering removes the deadlock. It also fails safe: if the conditional update affects zero rows, we roll back and answer "insufficient funds".

I would reach for (a) or (b) only for the multi-row rules: the daily limit, or a combined-wallet rule. There, serializable with a retry loop is the least error-prone; or materialise the conflict by locking a single per-user "limits" row with FOR UPDATE. Notice the general principle: put the invariant where one atomic statement can check it, and use heavier isolation only for what cannot be.

Here is the whole thing running in SQLite (a single-writer database, so BEGIN IMMEDIATE stands in for the row locks; the SQL statements themselves are the ones you would use in Postgres):

import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:", isolation_level=None)      # we issue BEGIN/COMMIT ourselves
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE accounts (
  id      INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  balance INTEGER NOT NULL,                         -- cents; a cached, derived value
  CHECK (balance >= 0 OR id = 0)                    -- id 0 is the 'outside world' account
);
CREATE TABLE transfers (
  id       INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT,
  idem_key TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE,                    -- the client's idempotency key
  src INTEGER NOT NULL, dst INTEGER NOT NULL, amount INTEGER NOT NULL CHECK (amount > 0)
);
CREATE TABLE entries (                              -- immutable, append-only
  id          INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT,
  transfer_id INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES transfers(id),
  account_id  INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES accounts(id),
  amount      INTEGER NOT NULL                      -- negative = debit, positive = credit
);
INSERT INTO accounts VALUES (0, 0), (1, 0), (2, 0);
""")

def transfer(idem_key, src, dst, amount):
    if src == dst:
        return "rejected: same account"
    db.execute("BEGIN IMMEDIATE")
    try:
        try:
            cur = db.execute("INSERT INTO transfers (idem_key, src, dst, amount) VALUES (?,?,?,?)",
                             (idem_key, src, dst, amount))
        except sqlite3.IntegrityError:                # same key seen before: a retry
            db.execute("ROLLBACK")
            return "duplicate (no change)"
        tid = cur.lastrowid
        for acct in sorted((src, dst)):               # fixed lock order: lowest account id first
            if acct == src and src != 0:
                n = db.execute("UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - ? WHERE id = ? AND balance >= ?",
                               (amount, src, amount)).rowcount
                if n == 0:
                    db.execute("ROLLBACK")
                    return "rejected: insufficient funds"
            elif acct == src:
                db.execute("UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - ? WHERE id = 0", (amount,))
            else:
                db.execute("UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + ? WHERE id = ?", (amount, dst))
        db.execute("INSERT INTO entries (transfer_id, account_id, amount) VALUES (?,?,?)", (tid, src, -amount))
        db.execute("INSERT INTO entries (transfer_id, account_id, amount) VALUES (?,?,?)", (tid, dst, amount))
        db.execute("COMMIT")
        return "ok"
    except Exception:
        db.execute("ROLLBACK")
        raise

print("fund alice   :", transfer("k1", 0, 1, 10_000))   # $100.00 from the outside world
print("alice -> bob :", transfer("k2", 1, 2, 7_000))     # $70.00
print("client retry :", transfer("k2", 1, 2, 7_000))     # same key: no second debit
print("overdraw     :", transfer("k3", 1, 2, 7_000))     # only $30.00 left
print("balances     :", db.execute("SELECT id, balance FROM accounts ORDER BY id").fetchall())
print("entries sum  :", db.execute("SELECT SUM(amount) FROM entries").fetchone()[0])
bad = db.execute("""SELECT a.id FROM accounts a
                    LEFT JOIN (SELECT account_id, SUM(amount) s FROM entries GROUP BY account_id) e
                           ON e.account_id = a.id
                    WHERE a.balance != COALESCE(e.s, 0)""").fetchall()
print("reconcile    :", "all balances match their entries" if not bad else bad)
fund alice   : ok
alice -> bob : ok
client retry : duplicate (no change)
overdraw     : rejected: insufficient funds
balances     : [(0, -10000), (1, 3000), (2, 7000)]
entries sum  : 0
reconcile    : all balances match their entries

Read the output. The first retry of k2 found the key already in transfers, so the unique constraint stopped a second debit and the balance stayed correct. The overdraw failed because the conditional UPDATE touched zero rows, rolling back the transfer row too. Alice's $100 became $30 and Bob's $70; account 0 (the outside world) is at -$100 because that is where the money came from; entries sum to zero; and the reconciliation query agrees that every cached balance equals its entries. One deliberate gap: this version does not remember rejected requests under their key. A production system would, so a retry of a rejected transfer returns the same rejection rather than succeeding later after a deposit.

6Architecture

Draw the system. How many databases? Where do replicas fit, and which reads may use them? Why might sharding by account be tempting, and what breaks when a transfer crosses shards? How do notifications and other systems learn about completed transfers without a dual write?

Compare with a worked answer
  • A single-region primary. One leader takes every write, so the ledger has a single, serial order of changes: the simplest possible story for correctness. At 5,000 transfers per second, a single primary is plausible (stage 2). Run it with a synchronous replica in the same region: the commit is acknowledged only after the replica has the log, so failover loses no acknowledged transfer (chapter 15; the price is a little commit latency). Add asynchronous cross-region replicas for disaster recovery, understanding that failing over to those can lose the last few moments, which for money needs a deliberate decision (the GitHub case is a lesson in that).
  • Which reads can use replicas? Statements, history pages and reports: yes, stale by a moment is fine. The "can I pay this?" decision: no replica ever decides this. The conditional update on the primary makes the decision, so a stale balance on a screen cannot cause an overdraft.
  • Why sharding by account is tempting. The data is naturally keyed by account, and sharding by account (chapter 16) would scale writes horizontally and shrink the hot set per machine. But a transfer touches two accounts, which may live on two shards, and then a single database transaction can no longer make the debit and the credit atomic. You need two-phase commit across shards (slow, and blocks on a failed coordinator) or a saga: debit the source and record a pending credit, then credit the destination, with compensating steps if something fails. Both make correctness much harder, and they are a preview of what later courses cover. The advice: don't shard until you must; vertical scale, partitioned history tables and read replicas buy a great deal of room. If forced, shard by account but keep most transfers local, and treat cross-shard transfers as a slower, carefully tested path.
  • Notifications without a dual write. "Alice paid Bob" should trigger a push notification, a fraud check and a warehouse update. If the API server wrote the transfer and then called a notification service, a crash between them would leave a completed payment with no notice (chapter 19). Instead, insert a TransferCompleted row into an outbox table inside the same transaction. A relay publishes it to Kafka with at-least-once delivery, and consumers dedupe by transfer id.

7Failure modes and trade-offs

Walk through the failures: the client retries; the server crashes between debit and credit; a user reads their balance from a lagging replica; a bug quietly corrupts a balance; one merchant account receives thousands of payments a second. What stops each one, and how would you detect a failure that nothing stopped?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Double-spend via retries. The client's request times out and it sends the same transfer again, possibly while the first is still running. The UNIQUE constraint on the idempotency key, in the same transaction as the money movement, handles every ordering: if the first has committed, the second finds the key and returns the original result; if both run at once, the second insert waits on the first's lock and then fails with a unique violation (the code above turns that into "duplicate"). Because the key and the entries commit atomically, there is no moment where the money moved but the key is missing, or the reverse.
  • Crash mid-transfer. The server dies after debiting Alice but before crediting Bob. Because the debit, the credit, the entries and the outbox row are one transaction, atomicity means the database either applies all of it or none (chapter 10), and write-ahead logging makes recovery undo or redo correctly after a crash (chapter 13). The client, which does not know the outcome, retries with the same key, and learns the truth: either "already done" or a fresh, clean attempt.
  • Replica-lag reads. A user sends $70 and the app immediately fetches the balance from an asynchronous replica, which has not applied the commit yet: the screen shows the old balance. That is a UX bug, not a money bug, because the primary decided the transfer. Fixes: read the user's own balance from the primary right after a write, or return the new balance in the transfer response, or track the log position of the user's last write and only use a replica that has reached it (chapter 15, read-your-writes).
  • Hot accounts. A big merchant receiving thousands of payments a second all update one balance row. Each transaction holds that row's lock until it commits, so the row becomes a serial bottleneck and the queue behind it grows. Remedies: split the merchant's balance into N sub-accounts and pick one at random per payment (the true balance is their sum, read when needed); or batch credits; or credit via the entries only and apply the balance asynchronously, for accounts where the no-overdraft rule does not apply (the merchant can only receive).
  • Deadlocks. Lock ordering prevents the common A to B vs B to A case, but other paths can still deadlock. The database detects deadlocks and kills one transaction: the application must retry it. Another reason every transfer is idempotent.
  • Bugs and corruption. Nothing in the design makes bugs impossible, so build detection. Reconciliation jobs run continuously against a replica or the warehouse: (1) the sum of all entries must be zero; (2) for every account, the cached balance must equal the sum of its entries (the query in the demo above, run incrementally by checkpoint); (3) no user balance is negative; (4) every transfer has balanced entries; (5) our totals match the external world: bank and payment-provider statements, compared daily. Any mismatch raises an alert and a human investigates. Fix by reversal entries, never by editing history.
  • Audits. Because entries are immutable and every one points to a transfer with a key, a timestamp and (in a real system) the actor, "why is this balance what it is?" is answered by a query. Keep backups that you have actually restored (GitLab 2017), and consider shipping the change stream to write-once storage for tamper-evident records.

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"Correctness is the product here, so I'd state the invariants first: money is conserved, no balance goes negative, every change is explained, and each user intention is applied at most once. The model is a double-entry ledger: an immutable, append-only entries table where every transfer is a debit and a credit summing to zero, and a cached balance on each account updated in the same transaction and checked by reconciliation. The API takes a client-generated idempotency key with a unique constraint, committed atomically with the money movement, so retries after timeouts are safe. For concurrency, the no-overdraft rule lives in one row, so I'd use a conditional update, balance = balance - x WHERE balance >= x, in a single transaction with updates ordered by account id to avoid deadlock; I'd use serializable isolation with retries only for multi-row rules like daily limits. One primary with a synchronous replica handles 5,000 transfers a second, and I'd avoid sharding until forced, because cross-shard transfers need two-phase commit or sagas. Notifications go through a transactional outbox. And since I can't prove there are no bugs, reconciliation jobs continuously check that entries sum to zero and every balance equals its entries, with fixes made by reversal entries."

Notice the shape: name the invariants → choose the data model that makes them checkable → choose the cheapest mechanism that enforces them → name the failure modes → add detection for what slips through.

Key takeaways

  • Start from invariants you can express as queries. In a ledger, correctness beats latency and availability.
  • Double-entry plus an append-only entries table makes every balance explainable. A stored balance is a cache that must be checked against the entries.
  • Idempotency keys with a unique constraint, committed in the same transaction as the effect, make retries safe.
  • Put each invariant where one atomic statement can enforce it (the conditional update). Use serializable or locks only for rules that span rows. Order your locks.
  • One primary with synchronous replication goes a long way. Sharding by account turns every cross-shard transfer into a distributed-transaction problem, so delay it.
  • Use the outbox for events, never a dual write.
  • You cannot prove there are no bugs. Build reconciliation and audits, and fix with reversals, never edits.
