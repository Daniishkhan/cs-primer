Design a URL Shortener's Storage

Someone pastes a long link and gets back sho.rt/aZ3kQ9x. Billions of clicks later, every one of those short codes must still lead to the right place, fast. It looks like the easiest design question there is, which is exactly why it is asked so often: all the interesting decisions are in the storage and the read path.

How a design studio works

This page is a design problem, not a chapter. At each stage there's a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer isn't the only right design; it's one strong one with its reasoning. A rubric at the end lets you check whether you covered what an experienced engineer would. Being wrong first and then seeing why is the point of the exercise.

📐 The brief

You're designing the storage and read path of a URL-shortening service, in the spirit of the well-known public ones.

  • 100 million new short links per month.
  • Reads outnumber writes 100 to 1: every link is created once and followed many times.
  • Links never expire unless the owner deletes them.
  • A redirect must have a p99 latency under 50 ms (measured at your servers, not counting the user's own network).
  • Users may optionally choose a custom alias (sho.rt/summer-sale) instead of a generated code.
  • Keep it for 5 years of growth. The click-analytics dashboard is a separate product; you only need to make sure it does not hurt redirects.

Your notes

Keep notes as you go: decisions, numbers, open questions. Plain text is fine; try sketching boxes with characters, as in an interview whiteboard.

1Clarify the requirements

What would you ask before designing? Which requirements are functional and which are quality targets? What is the hard part of this problem: write throughput, read latency, storage size, or something else? What would be a poor answer to "what happens to an old link?"

Compare with a worked answer

Functional: create a short link for a long URL; redirect a short code to its long URL; optionally pick your own alias; delete a link. Quality: redirect p99 under 50 ms; never send someone to the wrong place; links durable for years.

Good questions to ask out loud:

  • Can a link be edited? Assume no: the short code always maps to one long URL, which keeps caching simple. Delete is allowed.
  • Is the same long URL always given the same code? Not required. Two users shortening the same page get two links (it keeps ownership and analytics separate, and avoids a lookup on every create).
  • How bad is a stale redirect? After a delete, a few seconds of the link still working is tolerable; a link that was just created returning 404 is not (the user will click it right away).
  • How bad is losing a link? Very. A short link is printed on posters and baked into emails. Unlike a cache entry, this data must be durable and backed up (chapter 13).
  • What about abuse? Shorteners are attractive to spammers and phishers. We'll treat this as a requirement, not an afterthought (stage 7).

The hard part is not volume, it is the shape: a huge, permanent, read-mostly key-to-value map that must answer in milliseconds. The write rate is tiny; the dataset is large; reads are skewed toward a minority of links. That points directly at "a good key scheme, a cache, and a store that is easy to partition by key".

2Back-of-the-envelope numbers

Estimate: writes per second (average and peak), reads per second, storage after 5 years (assume about 500 bytes per link including indexes), and how much cache you need for the hot set. Which number worries you most?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Writes: 100 M per month ÷ (30 days × 86,400 s ≈ 2.6 M s) ≈ 39 writes/s on average. Plan for a peak of perhaps 5×, so ~200 writes/s. A single database leader handles that without effort.
  • Reads: ×100 ≈ 3,900 reads/s on average (about 333 M redirects a day). Allow a 3 to 5× peak: ~15,000 reads/s. Not heroic, but a single database serving it uncached would be at its limits with tail latency suffering. The cache earns its place here.
  • Links in 5 years: 100 M × 12 × 5 = 6 billion.
  • Storage: 6 B × ~500 B ≈ 3 TB of data and indexes (each record is the short code, the long URL averaging maybe 100 to 200 bytes, an owner id, timestamps and flags). With three copies for replication (chapter 15), roughly 9 TB. That is large for "one box" operations (backups, restores, index maintenance), though not impossible, so design so it can be split by key.
  • Hot set for the cache: reads are heavily skewed: a minority of links (viral posts, popular campaigns) get most of the clicks. Suppose the links actually clicked on a given day are about 20% of the 333 M daily clicks, so ~67 M distinct links. A cache entry holds only the code and the long URL, perhaps 150 bytes with overhead, so 67 M × 150 B ≈ 10 GB. That fits in RAM on one machine; use several for capacity and failure tolerance. (The 20% is an assumption you would verify from real traffic.)
  • Bandwidth: a redirect response is a few hundred bytes. 15,000 × ~500 B ≈ 7.5 MB/s. Negligible.

What worries me is storage and operability, not throughput. Throughput is easy once a cache absorbs the hot set. The 3 TB of permanent data, and keeping its tail latency low as it grows, is the long-term concern.

The status codes are HTTP's (networking ch. 10). Things to defend in an interview:

  • Validate the URL. Accept only http and https schemes (never javascript:), cap its length (a common practical limit is around 2,000 characters), and normalise it. Check it against a blocklist of known-bad domains.
  • Validate the alias. Restrict to letters, digits, hyphen; a sensible length range; and reserve words like api, admin and links that would collide with your own routes.
  • Retries. A client that times out and retries a create might make two links. For random or counter codes that is harmless (just a wasted code). For a custom alias the retry returns 409 if the first attempt really succeeded, which is confusing but safe. If you want to be tidy, accept an Idempotency-Key header (studio 2 builds this properly).
  • Authentication and rate limits on create, so one account cannot mint millions of links. The redirect path is public and must stay as light as possible.

4The data model

What is the primary key? What columns does a link have? What queries does the service run, and which need an index beyond the primary key? Does this want a relational, a key-value or a document store?

Compare with a worked answer

The hot query is a single point lookup: code → long URL. The code is the primary key. Around it sits a little metadata:

CREATE TABLE links (
  code        TEXT PRIMARY KEY,        -- 'aZ3kQ9x' or a custom alias
  long_url    TEXT NOT NULL,
  owner_id    BIGINT NOT NULL,
  created_at  TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
  is_custom   BOOLEAN NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE,
  deleted_at  TIMESTAMP                -- NULL = live; soft delete lets us answer 410
);
CREATE INDEX links_by_owner ON links (owner_id, created_at);  -- "show my links"
  • The primary key is the access path. Redirect = a B-tree (or LSM) lookup by code (chapter 6). Nothing else sits on the hot path.
  • One secondary index for "show me my links" (chapter 7). It costs write amplification on every create, but writes are ~40/s, so it is cheap. If the index were also keyed by code across shards, it would become a scatter-gather query: a reason to keep owner listings separate or to shard the index by owner (we return to this in stage 6).
  • No joins at all. There is nothing relational about "code to URL". That is why a key-value or wide-column store is a natural fit, and why it shards cleanly by key.
  • Soft delete. Marking a link deleted rather than removing the row lets us answer 410 Gone, stops that code from being reissued to someone else (a security issue: an old link in an email must not suddenly go to a stranger's site), and keeps an audit trail.

Which store? Either works, and the reasoning is the point. A replicated relational database (Postgres, MySQL) gives you the unique constraint, mature tooling and a familiar operating model; 3 TB is within its reach, and in the spirit of chapter 20 it is the sensible default. A partitioned key-value or wide-column store gives easier horizontal scaling by key, at the cost of weaker transactions and fewer ad-hoc queries, neither of which this workload needs much. My pick: start relational, designed so that the primary key is the shard key, and move to many shards when the data or operations demand it.

5Key generation

How will you generate the short code? Compare (a) hash the URL and truncate, (b) a counter converted to base62, with ranges leased to each server, and (c) random codes. How long must the code be for 6 billion links? What does each scheme cost in collisions, coordination and guessability?

Compare with a worked answer

The length arithmetic. Base62 uses 62 symbols: 0-9, A-Z, a-z, so each character carries just under 6 bits and a code of length L can name 62L links. (Dropping + and / from base64 keeps the code URL-safe.) We need to name 6 billion links:

import math
ALPHABET = "0123456789ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZabcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz"

def b62(n, width=7):
    out = ""
    while n:
        n, r = divmod(n, 62)
        out = ALPHABET[r] + out
    return out.rjust(width, "0")

# Counter ranges: each app server leases a block of 1,000 ids from a shared counter.
class Counter:                      # stands in for one row in a database, or a ZooKeeper-style service
    def __init__(self): self.next = 0
    def lease(self, n):
        start, self.next = self.next, self.next + n
        return start, start + n
shared = Counter()
class Server:
    def __init__(self, name): self.name, self.lo, self.hi = name, 0, 0
    def new_id(self):
        if self.lo == self.hi:
            self.lo, self.hi = shared.lease(1000)      # one round trip per 1,000 links
        self.lo += 1
        return self.lo - 1

My pick: random 7-character codes with insert-and-retry on conflict. There is no coordination service to run or lose, nothing sequential to enumerate, and the collision rate (under 0.2% at the end of year five, lower before) costs an occasional second insert. If a team needs zero collisions and likes predictability for sharding or sorting, counter ranges (b) are a good alternative; I would scramble the id. Hashing (a) mostly makes sense if you want deduplication of identical URLs, which our requirements do not.

Common misconception

"Check whether the code exists, then insert it." That is a race (chapter 11): two servers can both see "not found" and both insert. Let the unique constraint be the check: just insert, and treat a unique-violation error as "pick another code". The database serialises the conflict for you.

Custom aliases live in the same key space, so they use the same constraint. One more detail: because a generated code could equal someone's custom alias, generated codes also go through insert-and-retry. A single unique key space makes the problem disappear.

6Architecture and the read path

Draw the path of a redirect from the browser to the data and back. Where does the cache sit, and how do you keep it correct? Should you answer 301 or 302, and what does that choice do to analytics and to CDNs? How will you split 3 TB of data across machines?

Compare with a worked answer

The read path, step by step. The request reaches a stateless app server (any server can handle any request; scale by adding more behind a load balancer, networking ch. 14). It asks the cache for code. On a hit (we aim for 95% or better) it returns the redirect: a sub-millisecond lookup plus a network hop inside the data centre. On a miss it reads the owning shard, puts the result in the cache with a TTL, and replies. This is plain cache-aside (chapter 14). Roughly: hit in a couple of milliseconds; miss in perhaps ten. Even with a few percent of misses, p99 is comfortably inside 50 ms, provided tail effects (below) are controlled.

Keeping the cache honest. Links never change, so the cache only has to deal with creation and deletion. On create, write the new mapping to the cache as well as the database, so that the creator's first click does not miss (and does not hit a lagging replica; chapter 15). On delete, delete the cache entry as well; keep a short TTL (minutes to an hour) as a safety net so a failed invalidation fixes itself.

301 or 302? (networking ch. 10.) A 301 Moved Permanently tells browsers, CDNs and proxies the redirect may be remembered, so repeat visits may never reach us. That cuts load and latency dramatically and makes a CDN in front of us effective. The price: we lose click counts for every cached repeat, and a deleted or changed link keeps working for anyone who has the 301 cached. A 302 Found (or 307) is temporary: every click comes to us, so analytics are exact and delete is immediate, at the price of full traffic and one extra round trip for every click. Many real services choose 302 when they sell analytics. A middle path is 301 or 302 with a short Cache-Control: max-age, which lets an edge absorb bursts but bounds the staleness. My pick: 302 for tracked links; 301 with a modest max-age for untracked ones. State the trade-off, whichever you choose.

Sharding by key. With 3 TB growing toward 9 TB of replicated data, split the table (chapter 16). Because every hot query is "by code", code is the natural shard key: shard = hash(code) mod N, so a lookup touches exactly one shard and a random code spreads load evenly with no hot partition from key order. Two practical details. First, do not map directly to N physical servers: use many logical shards (say 256) mapped to physical machines, so adding a machine moves whole logical shards rather than rehashing everything (networking ch. 19). Second, anything that is not a lookup by code is awkward: "show me all of one user's links" would have to ask every shard. Keep a small separate index keyed by owner (maintained by the same outbox/CDC machinery as chapter 19) or accept a scatter-gather query for that rare, non-latency-critical page.

7Failure modes and trade-offs

What happens when one link goes viral? When someone enumerates codes? When the analytics volume dwarfs the redirects? When two users want the same alias at the same moment? What happens when the cache dies?

Compare with a worked answer
  • Hot links and stampedes. A viral link might receive thousands of requests per second for the same code. The cache serves it well, but if that entry expires (or the cache node restarts), thousands of requests miss at once and slam one shard: a cache stampede (chapter 14). Defences: request coalescing so only one request per code goes to the database while the others wait; TTL jitter so entries do not all expire together; a small in-process cache on each app server for the very hottest codes; and, if 301s are acceptable for that link, letting the CDN absorb the burst. Also negative caching: cache "no such code" for a short while, or a scanner hitting random missing codes sends every request through to the database ("cache penetration").
  • Cache failure. If a cache node dies, its share of traffic goes to the shards. 15,000 reads/s of mostly-cacheable reads is within what the shards could carry for a while, but p99 will suffer. Use several cache nodes so one failure loses only a fraction, warm replacements gradually, and make the database tier able to take a degraded period.
  • Guessable IDs and abuse. Sequential codes let anyone enumerate every link, including private ones shared by email. Random codes remove the sequence, but remember the density: about 1 in 590 random codes is live, so scanning still finds links. The real defences are rate limits per client on both create and redirect misses, not treating a short code as a secret, and offering an unlisted-link option with longer codes for sensitive links. Separately, spam and phishing: scan submitted URLs against known-bad lists, re-scan periodically (a good URL today can turn bad), let people report links, and keep a way to disable a code immediately (a flag in the row plus a cache purge).
  • Analytics write load. At 15,000 clicks/s peak, doing UPDATE links SET clicks = clicks + 1 on every redirect would turn the redirect path into a write path, hammer hot rows (a viral link means thousands of writers on one row; see lock contention in chapter 12) and tie p99 to the slowest write. Instead emit a small click event (code, time, coarse client info) asynchronously to a log, and let a separate pipeline aggregate it for dashboards (chapter 19). If the pipeline falls behind, dashboards lag but redirects are unaffected. Losing a few click events on a crash is acceptable for statistics; losing a link is not. That asymmetry is why they are separate systems.
  • Custom-alias uniqueness. Two users request summer-sale in the same millisecond. A "check then insert" lets both pass. The fix is the primary key / unique constraint: both insert, exactly one succeeds, the other gets a unique-violation and you return 409. Because the alias is the shard key, both inserts land on the same shard, so one database enforces the constraint with no cross-shard coordination. (If aliases were a separate global table sharded differently, uniqueness would need a distributed transaction: another reason to keep one key space.)
  • Replica lag. If redirects read from replicas to scale, a link created a moment ago might 404 there (chapter 15). Writing to the cache on create hides this for the creator; for everyone else, treat a 404 from a replica as "try the leader once" before answering 404.
  • Region loss and durability. Replicate each shard across failure domains, back up and restore-test (as the GitLab case shows, an untested backup is a hope, not a backup).

Saying it in an interview

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"This is a read-heavy key-value problem: about 40 writes a second, about 4,000 reads, 6 billion links over five years, roughly 3 TB. The write rate is trivial, so the design is about the key, the cache and the shape of the data. Each link gets a random 7-character base62 code, giving 3.5 trillion possibilities and a collision rate under a fifth of a percent at the end; collisions and custom aliases are both handled by the primary-key unique constraint, never check-then-insert. Redirects go through a cache-aside layer of about 10 GB and fall back to a relational store sharded by hash of the code, replicated for durability. I would answer 302 where we want exact click counts and a 301 with a short max-age otherwise, and I'd send click events to an event log rather than updating counters in the redirect path. The failure modes I'd watch are stampedes on viral links, scanners hitting missing codes, and a stale replica returning 404 for a fresh link."

Notice the shape: name what dominates → choose the key and the access path → explain the trade-off → name the failure modes → say how it scales.

Key takeaways

  • Size the problem first. Here writes are tiny; the dataset is big, permanent and read-mostly, so the key scheme, the cache and partition-friendliness decide the design.
  • Seven base62 characters name 3.5 trillion links. Random codes avoid coordination and sequence, and a unique constraint turns the rare collision into a retry.
  • Never check-then-insert for uniqueness. Let the database's constraint arbitrate, including for custom aliases.
  • Cache-aside plus a stampede defence carries the read path. 301 versus 302 trades load and latency against analytics and control.
  • Shard by the access key. Anything not keyed that way (listing a user's links) becomes a separate index or a scatter-gather query.
  • Keep analytics off the critical path: asynchronous events and a separate pipeline.
