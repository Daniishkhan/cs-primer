Lab 3: A PII Redaction & Audit Pipeline

The restricted-environments chapter said that retention, access and what is sent to a model at all are design decisions, and the security-review chapter said that a log sample showing what is recorded and what is redacted belongs in the evidence pack. In this lab you build the machinery behind both statements: detectors for identifiers that tell a real card number from a look-alike, name and place rules, a vault that swaps each value for a stable token, a policy that releases values only to the roles allowed to see them, a hash-chained audit log that notices tampering, and a measurement of what all of it misses. Then you wrap it around a model so that the provider never sees a raw identifier.

Why this lab matters

Sooner or later, in almost every customer engagement that touches people, someone asks two questions in the same breath: "Does the model provider see our customers' names?" and "Who looked at them, and how would you know?" An engineer who answers with a policy document gets a second meeting. An engineer who can show a pipeline, the prompt that was actually sent, the log of who revealed what, and a measured miss rate on a hand-labelled sample gets a pilot. This lab builds that pipeline in about 300 lines and, more usefully, makes you confront its limits in numbers. Rules find structured identifiers almost perfectly and names only mostly; the names they miss are not random; and some identifying details (the only chemist in the village) are not identifiers at all. The honest deliverable is not "we redact PII" but "we redact these kinds at this measured recall, and these are the cases we know we miss".

Invented data, no model, no network

Every person, card, account and phone number in corpus.jsonl (30 short customer messages, with hand-labelled gold spans) is invented; the card and account numbers are test numbers that pass the public checksums. Nothing calls a real model: the "model" in milestone 6 is a scripted stand-in built on the shared llm.py, which repeats the placeholders it is shown so that you can see what a real model would be handed and what it would hand back. Time in the audit log is a counter, so a run is repeatable. The only randomness is in seeded property checks, and a failure prints its seed so you can replay it: python3 check.py solution 2 7 runs milestone 2 with seed 7. The whole check takes a few hundredths of a second. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only.

State of play, October 2026

Three rules of thumb the lab leans on, each with the caveat that rules change and none of this is legal advice. First, the GDPR defines pseudonymisation in Article 4(5) as processing personal data so that it can no longer be attributed to a specific person without additional information, provided that information is kept separately under technical and organisational measures (text read on gdpr-info.eu, a convenience copy of the Regulation). Pseudonymised data is still personal data: the token vault of milestone 3 reduces risk, it does not take the system out of scope. Second, the US HIPAA Safe Harbor method of de-identification lists 18 kinds of identifier to remove (see the healthcare chapter), which is why milestone 3 generalises dates to a year instead of tokenising them. Third, payment card numbers are covered by the PCI DSS, whose requirements include logging access to cardholder data; the standard is on a 4.x line and several requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025, but I read this through secondary summaries rather than the standard itself, so check the current version and the exact requirement numbers with the customer's assessor. The checksum algorithms are older and stable: Hans Peter Luhn filed US patent 2,950,048, "Computer for Verifying Numbers", in 1954 (granted 1960), and the IBAN check is the ISO 13616 mod-97 scheme.

The brief

A retail bank wants an assistant that summarises customer messages for its support staff. The messages are full of personal data. The bank's rules are plain, and you turn them into tests:

  • The model provider must never see a raw identifier (a name, card, account number, email address, phone number or place). It sees placeholders such as [PERSON_1].
  • The same person must get the same placeholder wherever they appear, so that the model can still tell that two mentions are one customer.
  • A member of staff may see the real value behind a placeholder only if their role allows that kind of value and they state a recorded reason. Nobody may ever see a card number again.
  • Every attempt, allowed or denied, is logged, and the log must make after-the-fact editing detectable. The log itself must contain no personal data.
  • Dates are not needed at full precision, so they are generalised to the year and are not recoverable.
  • If anything that still looks sensitive survives redaction, the message is stopped before it reaches the model.

You have the 30-message corpus with gold labels, and you finish by measuring your own detector on it. Seven of its 47 names and places are ones your rules do not know. That gap is the point.

What you'll build

Six milestones:

  1. Structured identifiers: a Luhn check for cards, a mod-97 check for IBANs, and finders for emails, phone numbers, cards, accounts and dates that use them.
  2. Names, places and resolution: title, full-name and greeting rules, a place gazetteer, and one rule for what happens when detections overlap.
  3. Tokens and a vault: stable placeholders for the same value across spellings, year-only generalisation for dates, and a restore that can be limited to some kinds.
  4. Policy and audit: who may reveal what for which recorded reason, and a log whose entries are chained by hash so that edits, deletions and reordering show up.
  5. Measurement: exact-match precision and recall by kind with intervals, and a stricter question: which values would have leaked?
  6. The pipeline: a tripwire that stops messages that still look sensitive, and the function that puts detection, redaction, a model, policy and log together.

Milestone 1: structured identifiers, and the real from the look-alike

Some personal data has a shape. An email address has an at-sign and a domain; a phone number is a country code or a leading zero followed by eight to eleven digits; a date is a day, a month and a year. A regular expression (a pattern language for matching text; the starter gives you the patterns in PATTERNS) finds candidates for all of these in one pass. But a pattern alone cannot tell a card number from any other long number, and the difference matters in both directions. Miss a card and you leak it. Redact every sixteen-digit number and you black out order references, policy numbers and tracking codes that the support agent needs.

Card numbers, IBANs and many other identifiers carry a check digit: a digit or two computed from the rest, so that a typing slip is detected. The checksum does not prove a number belongs to anyone. It proves the number is well formed, which is exactly what a detector needs: a way to say "this long number is probably a card" without a database.

The Luhn check for cards

Working from the right-hand end, double every second digit (if doubling gives more than 9, subtract 9, which equals adding the two digits), add everything up, and the number is valid if the total is a multiple of ten. For the invented card 4539 8997 2074 3225, the undoubled digits (from the right: 5, 2, 4, 0, 7, 9, 9, 5) sum to 41, the doubled ones (3, 3, 7, 2, 9, 8, 3, 4 become 6, 6, 5, 4, 9, 7, 6, 8) sum to 49, and 41 + 49 = 90. Change one digit and the total stops being a multiple of ten: Luhn catches every single-digit error and nearly all swaps of adjacent digits. It was designed to catch accidents, and it offers no protection against anyone who wants to make up a valid number. It also means that about one in ten arbitrary sixteen-digit strings passes, so a checksum cuts false alarms by a factor of ten, not to zero.

The mod-97 check for IBANs

An IBAN (international bank account number) is two letters (the country), two check digits and up to 30 more letters and digits. To validate it: move the first four characters to the end, replace each letter by a number (A is 10, B is 11, up to Z at 35), read the whole thing as one large integer and check that dividing by 97 leaves remainder 1. Python integers have no size limit, so this is three lines. The published example GB29 NWBK 6016 1331 9268 19 passes; change its last digit and it does not.

A CARD or IBAN candidate counts only if its checksum passes. Do not resolve overlaps yet: the digits inside an IBAN can themselves look like a phone number, and milestone 2 decides what to do about that.

def luhn_valid(number):
    """True if `number` passes the Luhn checksum used by payment cards. Spaces and dashes are
    ignored; fewer than 13 or more than 19 digits is not a card, so False.

    luhn_valid("4539 8997 2074 3225") -> True;  luhn_valid("4539 8997 2074 3226") -> False."""
    digits = [int(c) for c in number if c.isdigit()]
    if not 13 <= len(digits) <= 19:
        return False
    total = 0
    for i, d in enumerate(reversed(digits)):
        if i % 2 == 1:                       # every second digit from the right is doubled
            d *= 2
            if d > 9:
                d -= 9                       # same as adding the two digits of the product
        total += d
    return total % 10 == 0


def iban_valid(value):
    """True if `value` is a well-formed IBAN with correct check digits (ISO 13616 mod 97).
    Spaces are ignored and case does not matter. Shape: two letters, two digits, then 11 to 30
    letters or digits. Move the first four characters to the end, turn each letter into a number
    (A=10 ... Z=35), read the result as one big integer, and require it to leave remainder 1
    when divided by 97.

    iban_valid("GB29 NWBK 6016 1331 9268 19") -> True;  the same with the last digit changed -> False."""
    s = re.sub(r"\s", "", value).upper()
    if not re.fullmatch(r"[A-Z]{2}\d{2}[A-Z0-9]{11,30}", s):
        return False
    rearranged = s[4:] + s[:4]
    number = "".join(str(int(c, 36)) for c in rearranged)     # int('A', 36) == 10
    return int(number) % 97 == 1


def find_structured(text):
    """All EMAIL, PHONE, CARD, IBAN and DATE spans in `text`, as Span(start, end, kind), sorted.

    Use the regexes in PATTERNS to find candidates. A CARD candidate counts only if luhn_valid
    accepts it, and an IBAN candidate only if iban_valid accepts it; the rest count as found.
    Overlaps are NOT resolved here (milestone 2 does that)."""
    spans = []
    for kind, pattern in PATTERNS.items():
        for m in pattern.finditer(text):
            if kind == "CARD" and not luhn_valid(m.group()):
                continue
            if kind == "IBAN" and not iban_valid(m.group()):
                continue
            spans.append(Span(m.start(), m.end(), kind))
    return sorted(spans)

Milestone 2: names, places and one answer for every character

Names have no checksum and no fixed shape. The approaches, in rising cost: a list of known names and places (a gazetteer), hand-written rules around cues such as titles and greetings, and a trained named-entity recogniser (NER), a model that labels words as person, place or organisation from context. The guardrails chapter shows how to measure such classifiers, and tools such as Presidio package all three. This lab uses the first two, because they are small enough to read in full and because their failures are instructive.

Three name rules, applied to every message:

  • Title. A title (Mr, Mrs, Ms, Miss, Mx, Dr, Prof) followed by a capitalised word is a surname ("Dr Sutton"). If that word is a known first name and another capitalised word follows, the name is both ("Mrs Aoife O'Neill"). The title itself is not part of the name.
  • Full name. A known first name followed by a capitalised word ("Tom Reilly"). Case matters: "grace period" is not a name; "Will you call" is not a name either, because "you" is lower case.
  • Greeting. A greeting ("Hi", "Dear", "Thanks,") followed by a known first name ("Hi Priya"). This finds the first name that stands alone.

Places are whole-word matches against the list in PLACES.

Rules overlap. "Dr Sutton lives in Sutton" holds the doctor and the town, which are the same spelling in different roles. "Dear Daniel Moreau" is matched by the full-name rule (both words) and the greeting rule (the first name alone). Newcastle sits inside Newcastle upon Tyne. Redaction needs one answer for every character, so the lab asks you to write resolve: consider spans longest first, break ties by the order of KINDS (which puts the more specific, checksum-validated kinds first), and keep a span only if it overlaps nothing already kept. This is the same greedy idea as the "longest match wins" rule in lexers and in the matching step of some tokenisers (see Tokenisation).

Analogy: a highlighter and a ruler

Think of marking a printed letter with highlighters of different colours. You may mark the same words twice, in different colours, or overlap two marks by accident. Before you black anything out, you lay a ruler along each line and decide for every character which single mark counts. resolve is the ruler: it does not find anything new, it makes the marks consistent.

Where it breaks down: a highlighter mark is only a mark, but a wrongly resolved span is an action: if the longer, wrong span wins, the shorter correct one is lost and a value may leak.

Your task

Write find_names(text) (PERSON spans from the three rules, distinct and sorted), find_places(text) (PLACE spans, including overlapping matches), resolve(spans) (non-overlapping, sorted, longest first with KINDS as the tie-break, input unchanged), and detect(text), which combines the finders and resolves.

The check tries each rule on hand-written strings (including the awkward ones: lower-case "grace period", "Will you", a surname the lists do not know, a full stop after "Dr."), the place word-boundary cases, hand-built overlaps for resolve, then 30 seeded random span sets against five properties (sorted, non-overlapping, idempotent, nothing dropped without a covering span, the longest span always kept). It finishes by requiring that your rules find at least 38 of the 47 gold names and places in the corpus exactly.

Hints try without first
  • Build the regular expressions once at module level. A capitalised word with an apostrophe or hyphen is [A-Z][\w'’-]*, and \w already matches letters such as the accented ones in "Wiśniewski".
  • For the title rule use two capture groups, and take m.end(2) as the end only if group 1 is in FIRST_NAMES; otherwise take m.end(1). Report group positions, not m.start(), so the title is excluded.
  • The full-name and title rules both find "Aoife O'Neill" in "Mrs Aoife O'Neill". Return sorted(set(spans)) so duplicates collapse.
  • resolve: sorted(set(spans), key=lambda s: (-(s.end - s.start), KINDS.index(s.kind), s.start)), then keep each span only if s.end <= k.start or s.start >= k.end for every kept k. Sort the result.

_WORD = r"[A-Z][\w'’-]*"                      # a capitalised word, apostrophes and hyphens allowed


_FIRST = "|".join(sorted(map(re.escape, FIRST_NAMES)))


_TITLE_RE = re.compile(rf"\b(?:{'|'.join(TITLES)})\.?\s+({_WORD})(?:\s+({_WORD}))?")


_FULL_RE = re.compile(rf"\b(?:{_FIRST})\s+{_WORD}")


_GREET_RE = re.compile(rf"\b(?:{'|'.join(GREETINGS)}),?\s+({_FIRST})\b")


def find_names(text):
    """PERSON spans from three rules. The span never includes a title or a greeting.

    A. TITLE rule: a title from TITLES (optional full stop) then a capitalised word. If that word
       is in FIRST_NAMES and another capitalised word follows, the name is both words ("Mrs Aoife
       O'Neill" -> "Aoife O'Neill"); otherwise only the first ("Dr Sutton" -> "Sutton").
    B. FULL-NAME rule: a word from FIRST_NAMES followed by a capitalised word ("Tom Reilly").
    C. GREETING rule: a greeting from GREETINGS, an optional comma, then a word from FIRST_NAMES
       ("Hi Priya", "Thanks, Marcus"): the span is the first name alone.
    Rules may produce overlapping spans (rules A and B both find "Aoife O'Neill"); return the
    distinct spans, sorted. Case matters: "Grace period" is not a name."""
    spans = []
    for m in _TITLE_RE.finditer(text):
        end = m.end(2) if m.group(2) and m.group(1) in FIRST_NAMES else m.end(1)
        spans.append(Span(m.start(1), end, "PERSON"))
    for m in _FULL_RE.finditer(text):
        spans.append(Span(m.start(), m.end(), "PERSON"))
    for m in _GREET_RE.finditer(text):
        spans.append(Span(m.start(1), m.end(1), "PERSON"))
    return sorted(set(spans))


def find_places(text):
    """PLACE spans: every whole-word, case-sensitive occurrence of a name in PLACES, sorted.
    Overlapping matches (Newcastle inside Newcastle upon Tyne) are all returned; resolve() picks."""
    spans = []
    for place in PLACES:
        for m in re.finditer(r"\b" + re.escape(place) + r"\b", text):
            spans.append(Span(m.start(), m.end(), "PLACE"))
    return sorted(spans)


def resolve(spans):
    """One kind per character. Given possibly overlapping, possibly duplicated spans, return a
    sorted list of non-overlapping spans: consider spans longest first (ties: earlier kind in
    KINDS, then earlier start) and keep a span only if it overlaps nothing kept so far.
    Touching spans (one ends where the next starts) do not overlap. The input is not changed."""
    order = sorted(set(spans), key=lambda s: (-(s.end - s.start), KINDS.index(s.kind), s.start))
    kept = []
    for s in order:
        if all(s.end <= k.start or s.start >= k.end for k in kept):
            kept.append(s)
    return sorted(kept)


def detect(text):
    """Every PII span in `text`, resolved: structured, names and places combined."""
    return resolve(find_structured(text) + find_names(text) + find_places(text))

Milestone 3: tokens and a vault

Detecting is half the job; the other half is what you replace the value with. Deleting it ("[REDACTED]") is safe but destroys information the model needs: two mentions of one customer become indistinguishable, and a summary that says "the customer called the customer" is useless. A token here is a placeholder that stands for a value: [PERSON_1] (not the same thing as the sub-word tokens a language model reads, though the model will see each placeholder as a few of those). A vault is the store that remembers which value each token stands for. The pair gives you reversible redaction: the model sees tokens; an authorised person can swap the values back.

Two design choices carry the lesson:

  • Same value, same token. "Aoife O'Neill" and "AOIFE O'NEILL" should be one person; a phone number written with and without spaces should be one number. That needs a normal form: normalise(kind, value) lower-cases names, keeps only digits and a plus sign in phone numbers, and so on. The vault is keyed by (kind, normal form) and returns the first spelling it saw. The key includes the kind, because a place and a person can share a spelling.
  • Some things must not be reversible. A date of birth or an exact date of a transaction is more identifying than most people expect, and the model does not need it. The HIPAA Safe Harbor method, which the healthcare chapter discusses, keeps only the year. Here DATE is in GENERALISE: "14 March 2025" becomes [DATE-2025] and nothing is stored, so no later request can bring it back.

Common misconception

"The data is tokenised, so it is anonymous." It is not: whoever holds the vault, or can guess from context, can reverse it, and the GDPR treats pseudonymised data as personal data for exactly that reason. Tokens reduce who can see what; they do not remove the obligations. Two further leaks survive tokenisation. The pattern of tokens can identify (a message with [PERSON_1] and [PLACE_1] and a unique event), and a stable token is a stable identifier across messages, which is useful to the model and to anyone who gets the tokenised data. The vault is the crown jewel: in a real system it is encrypted, access-controlled and audited like the database it stands in for (the Data Protection & Privacy chapter covers keys, deletion and residency).

Your task

Write normalise(kind, value), Vault.token_for(kind, value) (counters per kind, rising from 1 in order of first appearance; lookup is given), redact(text, spans, vault) (resolve the spans first; generalise kinds in GENERALISE; tokenise the rest) and restore(text, vault, allow=None) (put values back in one pass, only for the kinds in allow if it is given, leaving unknown tokens untouched).

The check tests normal forms for every kind, token numbering and reuse across spellings, one exact redaction of a message holding two phone numbers and a repeated first name, duplicated and unsorted span lists, the restore variants (everything, one kind, nothing, unknown token), a value that itself looks like a token (restore must not re-scan it), and then the full round trip on every corpus message your detector finds in full: restore(redact(text)) must equal the text with its dates generalised.

Hints try without first
  • token_for: key = (kind, normalise(kind, value)); if it is new, increment a per-kind counter, build f"[{kind}_{n}]" and record both directions (key to token, token to (kind, value)).
  • redact: walk resolve(spans) left to right with a position pointer, appending the untouched text between spans and the replacement for each. Building a list and joining is easier than editing the string in place.
  • The year for a generalised date is re.search(r"\d{4}", value).group(). Compute it before building the f-string, because older Pythons do not allow backslashes inside an f-string expression.
  • restore: TOKEN_RE.sub(function, text) does one pass by construction, and the function returns m.group() unchanged whenever the token is unknown or not allowed.

def normalise(kind, value):
    """The form in which two mentions count as the same value.

    EMAIL, PERSON, PLACE: lower-cased (casefold) with runs of whitespace collapsed to one space.
    PHONE: only digits and '+' kept.  CARD: digits only.  IBAN: no whitespace, upper case.
    Any other kind: unchanged."""
    if kind in ("EMAIL", "PERSON", "PLACE"):
        return " ".join(value.casefold().split())
    if kind == "PHONE":
        return re.sub(r"[^\d+]", "", value)
    if kind == "CARD":
        return re.sub(r"\D", "", value)
    if kind == "IBAN":
        return re.sub(r"\s", "", value).upper()
    return value


class Vault:
    """Maps tokens to real values. Tokens look like [PERSON_1]: the kind, then a counter that
    starts at 1 for each kind and rises in order of first appearance. The same normalised value
    always gets the same token, so a model can still tell that two mentions are one person.
    In a real system the vault is an encrypted store behind its own access control; here it is
    a dictionary."""

    def __init__(self):
        self._token_of = {}      # (kind, normalised value) -> token
        self._entry = {}         # token -> (kind, first surface form seen)
        self._count = {}         # kind -> how many tokens issued

    def token_for(self, kind, value):
        """The token for `value`, issuing a new one the first time its normalised form is seen.
        The first surface form seen is what lookup() later returns."""
        key = (kind, normalise(kind, value))
        if key not in self._token_of:
            n = self._count.get(kind, 0) + 1
            self._count[kind] = n
            token = f"[{kind}_{n}]"
            self._token_of[key] = token
            self._entry[token] = (kind, value)
        return self._token_of[key]

    def lookup(self, token):
        """(kind, value) for a token this vault issued, else None. (GIVEN)"""
        return self._entry.get(token)

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._entry)


def redact(text, spans, vault):
    """`text` with every span replaced. The spans may overlap or repeat: pass them through
    resolve() first. A span whose kind is in GENERALISE is replaced by "[KIND-YYYY]" with the
    four-digit year found inside it (nothing is stored); every other span becomes
    vault.token_for(kind, the text it covers)."""
    out, pos = [], 0
    for s in resolve(spans):
        out.append(text[pos:s.start])
        value = text[s.start:s.end]
        if s.kind in GENERALISE:
            year = re.search(r"\d{4}", value).group()
            out.append(f"[{s.kind}-{year}]")
        else:
            out.append(vault.token_for(s.kind, value))
        pos = s.end
    out.append(text[pos:])
    return "".join(out)


def restore(text, vault, allow=None):
    """Put real values back. Every token in `text` that the vault issued is replaced by its
    value, except that when `allow` is a set of kinds, only tokens of those kinds are replaced.
    Unknown tokens and anything else are left exactly as they are. One pass: restored values
    are never searched for tokens again."""
    def swap(m):
        entry = vault.lookup(m.group())
        if entry is None or (allow is not None and entry[0] not in allow):
            return m.group()
        return entry[1]
    return TOKEN_RE.sub(swap, text)

Milestone 4: who may look, and a log that notices tampering

A vault that anyone can open is a database of personal data with extra steps. Access has to be decided per request, from three facts: who is asking (a role), what they ask for (a kind of value) and why (a recorded purpose). The lab's POLICY is a small table:

RoleMay revealReasoning
support_agentPERSON, PLACE, PHONENeeds to greet and call the customer; has no need for account numbers.
fraud_analystPERSON, PLACE, PHONE, EMAIL, IBANNeeds to contact the customer and trace the account.
auditornothingReads the log to check what others did; never needs the values.
(everyone)never CARDNobody downstream of the redactor has a reason to read a full card number.

This is least privilege (give each role the least access that lets it do its job) applied to values rather than to files; the authorisation chapter covers the general model. Purposes are drawn from a fixed set of codes (dispute, fraud_review, complaint, audit), not typed as free text. That is a deliberate privacy decision: a free-text "reason" field is exactly where someone types "customer Aoife O'Neill was shouting about her card", which would put personal data into the log you built to avoid holding it.

A log you can trust a little further

An audit log is the record of who did what and when. Its value depends on nobody being able to edit it quietly. A cheap and very effective structure is a hash chain: each entry stores the hash (a fixed-length fingerprint, here SHA-256) of the entry before it, and its own hash covers its content and that link. Change any entry and its hash no longer matches; delete or reorder entries and the links break; recompute one hash to hide an edit and the next entry's stored link no longer matches. The idea is older than blockchains; see Schneier and Kelsey, "Secure audit logs to support computer forensics" (1999), for the security argument.

The chain has one gap that the milestone makes you handle. If an attacker deletes the newest entries, what remains is a shorter but perfectly consistent chain. Only a copy of the latest hash kept somewhere else (in a different system, printed in a monthly report, or signed and timestamped by a third party) can reveal the cut. So verify takes an optional expected_head. The same reasoning is why the security-review chapter asks for a log sample and the retention terms together: a log kept by the party being audited needs an anchor outside that party's control.

Your task

Write AuditLog.append(...) (an entry with exactly the keys listed in its docstring, prev set to the current head, hash from the given _digest), AuditLog.verify(expected_head=None) (the index of the first untrustworthy entry, or None) and reveal(text, vault, user, role, purpose, log, policy): for each distinct token in the text decide with the first reason that applies (unknown_token, unknown_role, bad_purpose, kind_not_allowed, ok), log it, and return the text with only the allowed tokens restored.

The check builds logs and then damages them: it edits entry 3, deletes entry 2, swaps entries 1 and 2, edits and re-hashes entry 2 (still caught, one entry later), cuts the newest entry (invisible to verify() alone, caught with the saved head) and forges the last entry with a recomputed hash (caught only by the head). For reveal it runs one message past three roles and checks the exact restored text, the exact log rows, that no raw value appears anywhere in the serialised log, that a free-text purpose is refused and not copied, and that the policy argument is really read.

class AuditLog:
    """An append-only list of access records in which each entry carries the hash of the one
    before it, so changing, deleting or reordering an earlier entry breaks every hash after it.
    Time is a counter (1, 2, 3 ...) so a run is repeatable; a real log uses a trusted clock."""

    def __init__(self):
        self.entries = []

    def head(self):
        """The hash of the newest entry, or GENESIS when the log is empty. (GIVEN)"""
        return self.entries[-1]["hash"] if self.entries else GENESIS

    def append(self, user, role, action, token, kind, outcome, reason, purpose):
        """Add one entry and return it. The entry is a dict with exactly these keys:
        seq (1-based position), time (equals seq), user, role, action, token, kind, outcome,
        reason, purpose, prev (the previous entry's hash, or GENESIS) and hash (_digest of all
        the other keys)."""
        entry = {"seq": len(self.entries) + 1, "time": len(self.entries) + 1, "user": user, "role": role,
                 "action": action, "token": token, "kind": kind, "outcome": outcome, "reason": reason,
                 "purpose": purpose, "prev": self.head()}
        entry["hash"] = _digest(entry)
        self.entries.append(entry)
        return entry

    def verify(self, expected_head=None):
        """Index (0-based) of the first entry that cannot be trusted, or None if the log is intact.
        Entry i is bad if its 'prev' is not the hash of entry i - 1 (GENESIS for i = 0) or its
        'hash' is not _digest(entry). If every entry is fine and expected_head is given but is not
        the last hash, the tail was cut off: return len(entries), the index of the first missing
        entry."""
        prev = GENESIS
        for i, e in enumerate(self.entries):
            if e["prev"] != prev or e["hash"] != _digest(e):
                return i
            prev = e["hash"]
        if expected_head is not None and expected_head != prev:
            return len(self.entries)
        return None


def reveal(text, vault, user, role, purpose, log, policy=POLICY):
    """Show `user` the real values behind the tokens in `text`, as far as the policy allows, and
    log every decision. Returns the text with the allowed tokens restored.

    For each DISTINCT token in `text`, in order of first appearance, decide and call
    log.append(user, role, "reveal", token, kind, outcome, reason, clean_purpose(purpose)) with kind
    None for an unknown token, outcome "allowed" or "denied", and the first reason that applies:
      unknown_token   the vault never issued it
      unknown_role    `role` is not a key of `policy`
      bad_purpose     `purpose` is not in PURPOSES
      kind_not_allowed  the kind is not in policy[role]
      ok              allowed
    Never put a real value into the log."""
    seen = []
    for m in TOKEN_RE.finditer(text):
        if m.group() not in seen:
            seen.append(m.group())
    allowed_kinds = set()
    for tok in seen:
        entry = vault.lookup(tok)
        kind = entry[0] if entry else None
        if entry is None:
            reason = "unknown_token"
        elif role not in policy:
            reason = "unknown_role"
        elif purpose not in PURPOSES:
            reason = "bad_purpose"
        elif kind not in policy[role]:
            reason = "kind_not_allowed"
        else:
            reason = "ok"
        if reason == "ok":
            allowed_kinds.add(kind)
        log.append(user, role, "reveal", tok, kind, "allowed" if reason == "ok" else "denied", reason, clean_purpose(purpose))
    return restore(text, vault, allow=allowed_kinds)

Milestone 5: how much does the detector miss?

Everything so far is rules and goodwill until it is measured. The method is the one from the evaluation-plan chapter: a hand-labelled sample (the 30 messages, each with gold spans), counts with denominators, and an interval. For a span detector the counts are:

  • a true positive: you predicted a span, and the identical (start, end, kind) triple is in the gold labels;
  • a false positive: you predicted a span that is not in the gold labels;
  • a false negative: a gold span you did not predict exactly.

Precision is true positives over everything you predicted; recall is true positives over everything there was to find. For redaction the two errors are not symmetrical. A false positive blacks out something harmless and costs readability. A false negative leaves a person's data in the text, and costs the reason for building the pipeline. Recall is the number to watch, and the Wilson interval from Lab 1 tells you how little 7 or 23 labelled spans can establish.

Exact-match scoring also hides something. Suppose the rules find "Tadeusz" but not "Wiśniewski" in "Mr Tadeusz Wiśniewski" (the surname is not in a list, and the title rule joins two words only after a known first name). Exact matching counts one false positive and one false negative, which sounds like a near miss. In the redacted text, though, the surname is sitting there in the clear. So the milestone asks for a second, blunter number: a gold span has leaked if any of its characters is covered by no predicted span. That is closer to what a privacy reviewer cares about, and it is what evaluate lists.

Your task

Write score_spans(gold, pred) (counts per kind by exact match), prf(tp, fp, fn) (precision, recall and F1 with the stated conventions for empty denominators) and evaluate(docs, detector), which returns per-kind and overall statistics with the Wilson interval on recall, the list of leaked gold spans in document order, and the number of indirect identifiers that survive redaction.

The check covers hand-built triples (a span with the wrong end is both a false positive and a miss), the three empty-denominator conventions, a perfect and a partial detector on two toy messages (including the partly covered name), the interval, and then your own detector on the corpus: exact precision and recall for the five structured kinds, PERSON precision at least 0.90 and recall at least 0.75, PLACE recall at least 0.90, five to nine leaked spans, all of them names or places, and all four indirect identifiers still present.

Hints try without first
  • Turn both lists into sets of plain tuples (tuple(x) makes a Span and a list compare equal), then true positives are the intersection, false positives are predicted minus gold, false negatives are gold minus predicted.
  • A character set per message makes the leak test easy: covered.update(range(s[0], s[1])) for each predicted span, then a gold span leaks if any i in its range is missing.
  • For the indirect identifiers, redact the text with a fresh Vault() and test phrase in redacted.
Solution spoiler 
def score_spans(gold, pred):
    """Exact-match counts per kind. `gold` and `pred` are lists of (start, end, kind) triples
    (lists or Spans). A predicted span is a true positive if the identical triple is in gold.
    Returns {kind: {"tp": ., "fp": ., "fn": .}} for every kind in KINDS (zeros if unseen)."""
    g = {tuple(x) for x in gold}
    p = {tuple(x) for x in pred}
    out = {k: {"tp": 0, "fp": 0, "fn": 0} for k in KINDS}
    for x in p:
        out[x[2]]["tp" if x in g else "fp"] += 1
    for x in g:
        if x not in p:
            out[x[2]]["fn"] += 1
    return out


def prf(tp, fp, fn):
    """(precision, recall, f1). precision = tp / (tp + fp), or 1.0 when nothing was predicted;
    recall = tp / (tp + fn), or 1.0 when there was nothing to find; f1 = their harmonic mean,
    or 0.0 when both are 0."""
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else 1.0
    recall = tp / (tp + fn) if tp + fn else 1.0
    f1 = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall) if precision + recall else 0.0
    return precision, recall, f1


def evaluate(docs, detector):
    """Run `detector(text) -> spans` over every doc (docs as from load_corpus) and report.

    Returns {"kinds": {kind: {tp, fp, fn, precision, recall, f1, recall_lo, recall_hi}},
             "ALL": the same keys summed over kinds,
             "leaked": [(doc id, start, end, kind), ...],
             "indirect": (surviving, total)}.
    (recall_lo, recall_hi) is wilson(tp, tp + fn). A gold span has LEAKED if some character of it
    is not covered by any predicted span (a partial hit still leaves part of the value in the
    redacted text); list the leaks in document order, then start order. `indirect` counts the
    phrases in each doc's "indirect" list that are still present in the text after redact()
    with a fresh Vault, against the total number of such phrases."""
    totals = {k: {"tp": 0, "fp": 0, "fn": 0} for k in KINDS}
    leaked, surviving, total = [], 0, 0
    for doc in docs:
        pred = detector(doc["text"])
        for kind, c in score_spans(doc["spans"], pred).items():
            for key in c:
                totals[kind][key] += c[key]
        covered = set()
        for s in pred:
            covered.update(range(s[0], s[1]))
        for s, e, kind in sorted(doc["spans"]):
            if any(i not in covered for i in range(s, e)):
                leaked.append((doc["id"], s, e, kind))
        redacted = redact(doc["text"], pred, Vault())
        for phrase in doc["indirect"]:
            total += 1
            surviving += phrase in redacted

    def finish(c):
        p, r, f = prf(c["tp"], c["fp"], c["fn"])
        lo, hi = wilson(c["tp"], c["tp"] + c["fn"])
        return dict(c, precision=p, recall=r, f1=f, recall_lo=lo, recall_hi=hi)

    kinds = {k: finish(c) for k, c in totals.items()}
    allc = {key: sum(c[key] for c in totals.values()) for key in ("tp", "fp", "fn")}
    return {"kinds": kinds, "ALL": finish(allc), "leaked": leaked, "indirect": (surviving, total)}

Milestone 6: a tripwire, and the pipeline around a model

You now have a detector with a known miss rate. What should happen to the messages it gets wrong? Any single check has blind spots, so serious systems use defence in depth: a second, independent check that is allowed to be crude and over-sensitive because its only job is to stop the flow. Here that is tripwire, run on the already redacted text. It knows nothing about names. It looks for what should no longer be there: a run of nine or more digits, an at-sign, or something shaped like an IBAN whether or not its checksum passes. If it fires, the message is not sent to the model: the pipeline logs a blocked entry and raises LeakBlocked. This is failing closed (when in doubt, stop), the opposite of the default of most code.

The corpus holds two messages built to trigger it. Message m06 contains a policy number that looks like a card but fails Luhn, so the detector rightly leaves it alone and the tripwire stops it. Message m17 holds an IBAN with a bad check digit. Both are false alarms in the sense that the strings are not real card or account numbers, and both are the right outcome for a pilot, because the cost of a human glancing at two messages is small and the cost of a leaked number is not. In production you would route blocked messages to a review queue, and the block rate would be one of the numbers on your dashboard.

The remaining part is wiring. answer redacts the message, applies the tripwire, calls the model with only the redacted text, and then passes the model's reply through reveal for the asking user's role. Two details matter. The model's reply can contain a placeholder that was never issued (models invent things); reveal leaves it alone and logs it as unknown_token. And every model call goes through one function, which is the natural place for the controls an evidence pack must show: what is sent, what is blocked, who saw what.

Your task

The check runs tripwire on clean and dirty strings (short numbers, amounts and order references must pass). It then pushes all 30 corpus messages through answer with a support-agent role and requires: exactly m06 and m17 blocked, with the reason codes shown above; the model called for none of the blocked messages; no gold identifier of any message you detect in full appearing anywhere in what the model was sent, in the replies' placeholders, or in the audit log; one block entry per block; a log that verifies. Then a message shown to an agent, to an auditor, and to a model that invents [PERSON_99], and a tampered log.

Hints try without first

  • iban_shape: two capitals, two digits, then at least two groups of four capitals or digits, with optional spaces, as in \b[A-Z]{2}\d{2}(?: ?[A-Z0-9]{4}){2,}\b.
  • answer: the model gets [{"role": "user", "content": safe}] and system=SYSTEM. Use text_of(reply) to get the reply text.
  • Log the block with action "blocked", token and kind None, outcome "blocked" and the joined codes as the reason, before raising.

def tripwire(text):
    """A deliberately over-sensitive second check run on text that has ALREADY been redacted.
    Returns a sorted list of reason codes (empty means clean):
      long_digits   a run of 9 or more digits, optionally separated by single spaces or dashes
      at_sign       an '@' anywhere
      iban_shape    two capital letters, two digits, then two or more groups of four capitals or
                    digits (checksum NOT tested: a look-alike still trips it)
    It must not trip on tokens such as [PHONE_1] or generalised dates such as [DATE-2025]."""
    codes = []
    if re.search(r"\d(?:[ -]?\d){8,}", text):
        codes.append("long_digits")
    if "@" in text:
        codes.append("at_sign")
    if re.search(r"\b[A-Z]{2}\d{2}(?: ?[A-Z0-9]{4}){2,}\b", text):
        codes.append("iban_shape")
    return sorted(codes)


class LeakBlocked(Exception):
    """Raised when text that should be clean still looks sensitive. Carries the reason codes."""


def answer(model, vault, log, doc, user, role, purpose, policy=POLICY):
    """Summarise `doc` with `model` without the model ever seeing a raw identifier.

    1. spans = detect(doc); safe = redact(doc, spans, vault).
    2. codes = tripwire(safe). If there are any: log.append(user, role, "blocked", None, None,
       "blocked", ",".join(codes), clean_purpose(purpose)) and raise LeakBlocked(codes). The model is not called.
    3. Ask the model: model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": safe}], system=SYSTEM).
    4. masked = text_of(reply); shown = reveal(masked, vault, user, role, purpose, log, policy).
    Returns {"prompt": safe, "masked": masked, "reply": shown}."""
    safe = redact(doc, detect(doc), vault)
    codes = tripwire(safe)
    if codes:
        log.append(user, role, "blocked", None, None, "blocked", ",".join(codes), clean_purpose(purpose))
        raise LeakBlocked(codes)
    resp = model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": safe}], system=SYSTEM)
    masked = text_of(resp)
    shown = reveal(masked, vault, user, role, purpose, log, policy)
    return {"prompt": safe, "masked": masked, "reply": shown}

Going further

  • A recogniser from a model. Add a third finder that asks a model to list people and places, and score it with evaluate on the same corpus. Does it help the names that the lists miss? What does it cost per message, and what must be redacted before sending a message to the recogniser itself?
  • Keyed pseudonyms. Replace the counter with a keyed hash (HMAC with a secret key) of the normal form, so that tokens are stable across vaults and need no lookup table for matching. What can an attacker who holds the tokens but not the key do, and what can they do if the key leaks?
  • Deletion on request. A person asks to be forgotten. Delete their vault entries and show that the tokens in old model outputs no longer restore. Why is deleting log entries the wrong answer, and what do the data-protection chapter's ideas of crypto-shredding and per-subject keys change?
  • Per-tenant vaults. Two business units share the pipeline. Give each its own vault and policy and write the test that proves a token issued to one cannot be revealed by the other (compare multi-tenancy and isolation).
  • Redact the reply as well. A model can output a raw identifier it was never given (from its training data, or by inventing one). Run detect and the tripwire on replies too, and decide what to do when they fire.
  • Date shifting. Instead of the year only, shift every date in a message by one random offset per customer, so intervals between events survive. What does this keep for the model, and what risk does it add?
  • Compare with a library. Run the same corpus through an open-source recogniser such as Presidio (see the toolbox) and compare recall by kind. Which of the differences are about the library and which are about the labels you wrote?

Systems connection

The hash chain is an append-only log with integrity checking, a close cousin of the logs studied in logs and exactly-once; its missing-tail problem is why a separately held copy of the head matters. Role, purpose and kind form an authorisation decision of the kind built in the authorisation-engine lab, and the "log every decision, including denials" rule is straight from detection and security incidents. The tripwire is a sanity check on an output boundary, and the shape of the answer to "what should we do when it fires?" (stop, log, route to a human) is the same as for a failed canary or a poison-pill message in a queue. Data minimisation, which is the principle under all of this, is developed in Privacy & Data Handling for AI. Chapters 13 and 15 give the reasoning; this lab is the code.

What you learned

  • Structured identifiers have checksums: a Luhn or mod-97 check separates a real card or account from a look-alike, and cuts false alarms without a database. It proves a string is well formed, not that it belongs to anyone.
  • Names and places need lists, rules or models, and they fail in a pattern: the names your lists do not know are not random. Measure recall by kind, with intervals, on a labelled sample of the customer's own data.
  • Resolve overlapping detections with one explicit rule before redacting, so every character has exactly one answer.
  • Reversible tokens keep the model useful (the same person gets the same placeholder) and keep values in a vault; they are still personal data. Generalise what the model does not need (dates to a year), and make it irreversible.
  • Release values per request by role, kind and a coded purpose; log allowed and denied attempts, and keep free text and values out of the log.
  • A hash chain makes edits, deletions and reordering visible; the newest entries need an anchor held elsewhere.
  • Add a crude second check that fails closed. Report what you cannot catch, including indirect identifiers, instead of claiming that "PII is removed".

Check your understanding

A 16-digit order number in a support message happens to fail the Luhn check. The detector therefore leaves it alone. Which statement about this design is most accurate?

  1. It is a bug: any 16-digit number should be redacted to be safe
  2. It is safe, because a number that fails Luhn can never be a card number
  3. It is a sensible trade-off: the check removes most look-alikes, but a number that is mistyped or not a card of a scheme that uses Luhn could still slip through, so a separate tripwire is a good second check
  4. It shows that checksums are useless for detection
Luhn is designed to catch typing slips, so a number that fails it is very likely not a valid card, and about nine in ten arbitrary numbers fail. That cuts false alarms sharply. It does not prove the number is harmless (a customer might mistype their own card number in a message), which is why the pipeline adds an independent, crude tripwire. "Redact every 16-digit number" over-redacts references the agent needs, and "useless" ignores the tenfold reduction.

The detector finds "Tadeusz" but not "Wiśniewski" in "Mr Tadeusz Wiśniewski". Exact-match scoring reports one false positive and one false negative. What does the leak measure add?

  1. It shows the surname is still in the redacted text, so the person is partly exposed even though exact matching suggests a near miss
  2. It shows that precision is the more important metric here
  3. It shows the detector is perfect on every other kind
  4. It removes the need for a labelled sample
A partly covered span counts as a miss for exact match but also counts as a leak: some characters of the value survive into the text sent on. For redaction, recall and leakage are the numbers that matter, because a miss exposes a person, while a false positive costs only readability. A labelled sample is still needed to compute any of it.

An auditor asks whether the audit log can be trusted. Entries are hash-chained. Someone with write access deletes the five newest entries and leaves the rest untouched. What does verify() with no argument report, and what fixes the gap?

  1. It reports index 0, because the head changed
  2. It reports the first deleted entry, because every deletion breaks the chain
  3. It reports nothing wrong, and nothing can fix this
  4. It reports nothing wrong, because the remaining chain is consistent; keeping the latest hash somewhere the writer cannot change (and checking it with expected_head) detects the cut
Each entry links to the one before it, so deleting a tail leaves a shorter but valid chain. Deleting from the middle would break a link. An externally held head hash (printed in a report, written to another system or signed by a third party) turns the cut into a mismatch.

A colleague proposes recording the user's free-text reason for each reveal ("customer rang about a disputed payment, name is Aoife") for better audits. What is the strongest objection?

  1. Free text is slower to write than a code
  2. Free text will contain personal data, defeating the aim of a log that holds none, and cannot be checked mechanically; a fixed set of purpose codes keeps the log clean and comparable
  3. Hash chains cannot cover strings of variable length
  4. Auditors prefer shorter entries
People put names, accounts and stories into free-text fields. The log would become a second store of the data the vault protects, with weaker controls. Codes are also easy to count and review. Hash chains work on any string, and speed or length is not the issue.

The model's reply contains the placeholder [PERSON_99], which the vault never issued. What should reveal do?

  1. Raise an exception and discard the whole reply
  2. Replace it with a random name so the sentence reads well
  3. Leave it as it is and log an unknown_token denial: nothing is revealed, the oddity is on record, and the reply can still be used
  4. Ask the vault to issue it, since the model produced it
An invented placeholder reveals nothing, but it is evidence the model is not following the contract, so it should be recorded, and the text can be shown with the placeholder intact. Raising loses a useful reply; inventing a name fabricates data; letting a model's output create vault entries would let a model write to the store of record.

Your evaluation shows PERSON recall of 0.79 with a 95% interval of 0.62 to 0.90 on 29 gold names, and the five unrecognised people have names from outside the first-name list's region. What is the best statement for the customer report?

  1. "On a 30-message sample, names were found in 23 of 29 cases (about 79%, interval 62% to 90%). Misses were concentrated in names not on our list, so recall is likely lower for some customer groups; a larger recogniser and a re-measurement are planned."
  2. "The pipeline removes personal names."
  3. "Recall is 79%, which is good enough, so no further work is needed."
  4. "The interval is wide, so no claim can be made."
The first statement gives count, denominator, interval, the pattern in the errors and the next step. The second claims more than the data show. The third turns a measurement into a verdict without a threshold agreed in advance. The fourth is too timid: a wide interval is itself a finding, and the pattern in the misses is informative.
