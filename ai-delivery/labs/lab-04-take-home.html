Lab 4: A Take-Home, End to End

The take-home chapter listed what a strong submission contains: a restated brief, a baseline before any framework, evidence with intervals, an error analysis that names the first failure, and honest limitations. This lab lets you do all of it, in miniature, against a reviewer who is a program. You get a corpus of three short annual reports and twelve example questions. A hidden set of forty more decides whether you pass. You build a restatement that survives a mechanical review, a retrieval baseline, an answer that cites its page and whose numbers tie to that page, a rule that refuses what the reports do not say, an evaluation with bootstrap intervals against the baseline, and the memo, whose every figure is checked against the report or the page it claims to come from.

Why this lab matters

A take-home is the compressed version of the job: a vague brief, a clock, and a reviewer deciding whether to trust you with a customer. Reviewers read dozens of submissions, and what separates them is rarely the model. It is whether the candidate wrote down what they assumed, measured a baseline, reported a number with its uncertainty, looked at the failures, and said plainly what the submission does not show. This lab makes each of those a function with a test, so that you practise the whole shape once, cheaply, before doing it under a real clock. Its most useful result is uncomfortable and typical: the "better" system wins overall only because it refuses questions it cannot support, it is worse than the baseline on the questions it should answer, and the interval on its advantage includes zero. A submission that reports that sentence honestly is stronger than one that reports 85% and stops.

Hidden tests, a stand-in model, no network

The three reports in corpus.json (18 pages) are invented, as are the companies, people and figures. dev_questions.json holds twelve questions with answers and gold pages for you to work with. hidden_tests.json holds forty more, and check.py plays the reviewer that uses them. The file is on disk so that the check is reproducible, but if you read it before you finish you measure your memory, not your system: tune on the dev questions, then see what happens on the others. No model is called over a network. The "reader" is a deterministic extractive stand-in built on the shared llm.py: it picks the context sentence that shares the most content words with the question, so it cannot invent a figure, but it can pick the wrong sentence, year or company, as a real model can. Randomness (bootstrap resampling) is seeded, and a failure prints its seed: python3 check.py solution 5 3 runs milestone 5 with seed 3. The check takes about a quarter of a second. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only.

State of play, October 2026

What the chapter could verify about take-homes is thin, so the lab takes only the stable parts. What you'll build

Six milestones:

  2. A baseline: BM25 over pages with the formula spelled out, recall at k, and a baseline system that quotes the best sentence of the top page and never refuses.

Milestone 1: the requirements restatement

The first deliverable in the chapter's list is a one-page restatement: what you believe the customer needs, the assumptions you made, and the questions you would ask if you could. It exists because briefs are incomplete on purpose. Writing the restatement first costs half an hour and prevents the most common failure, which is a well-built answer to a question nobody asked (see requirements under ambiguity).

A program cannot judge whether a restatement is good. It can check that the parts that are easy to skip are present, which is surprisingly useful, because skipped parts are the main defect. The lab's checker demands six headings (Goal, Inputs, Assumptions, Questions for the customer, Out of scope, Success measures), at least eight words under each, at least three assumptions, at least three questions that end in a question mark, a success measure containing a number, no leftover TODO and no more than 350 words. Each of those is a lesson in miniature. A question that does not end in a question mark is usually a statement of belief in disguise. A success measure without a number cannot fail. A page over 350 words is not a page.

def check_restatement(text):
    """Problems found in a requirements restatement (an empty list means it passes).

    `text` is markdown with '## ' headings (use sections()). For each heading in REQUIRED_HEADINGS,
    in that order (headings are matched ignoring case), report in this order and skip the rest of that
    heading's checks after the first problem marked (stop):
      'missing heading: <Heading>'                           (stop)
      'empty section: <Heading>'    fewer than 8 words in its body (bullet markers do not count)  (stop)
      Assumptions:                  'too few assumptions: <n>'   fewer than 3 bullet lines ('- ' or '* ')
      Questions for the customer:   'too few questions: <n>' for fewer than 3 bullets, then for each bullet
                                    that does not end with '?': 'question without ?: <the bullet text>'
      Success measures:             'success measures need a number' if no digit appears in the body
    After the headings, two whole-document checks: 'placeholder left: TODO' if the word TODO appears
    anywhere, and 'too long: <n> words' if the whole text has more than 350 words (one page).
    <Heading> is spelled as in REQUIRED_HEADINGS."""
    sec = sections(text)
    problems = []

    def words(lines):
        return [w for line in lines for w in re.sub(r"^[-*]\s+", "", line).split()]

    for h in REQUIRED_HEADINGS:
        body = sec.get(h.casefold())
        if body is None:
            problems.append(f"missing heading: {h}")
            continue
        if len(words(body)) < 8:
            problems.append(f"empty section: {h}")
            continue
        bullets = [re.sub(r"^[-*]\s+", "", ln) for ln in body if re.match(r"^[-*]\s+", ln)]
        if h == "Assumptions" and len(bullets) < 3:
            problems.append(f"too few assumptions: {len(bullets)}")
        if h == "Questions for the customer":
            if len(bullets) < 3:
                problems.append(f"too few questions: {len(bullets)}")
            problems += [f"question without ?: {b}" for b in bullets if not b.rstrip().endswith("?")]
        if h == "Success measures" and not re.search(r"\d", " ".join(body)):
            problems.append("success measures need a number")
    if "TODO" in text:
        problems.append("placeholder left: TODO")
    n = len(text.split())
    if n > 350:
        problems.append(f"too long: {n} words")
    return problems

Milestone 2: a baseline you can beat

The chapter's second deliverable is "a baseline before any framework: the simplest thing that could work". If your system cannot beat it, that is the finding. Here the baseline has two parts: a search over pages and a way to turn the top page into an answer without a model.

Search: BM25 over pages

A page is the unit that the question cites, so it is the unit to retrieve. BM25 is the standard keyword ranking function, derived in the BM25 chapter. Each distinct query term t adds to a page's score

idf(t) × tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 × (1 − b + b × dl / avgdl))

where tf is the term's count on the page, dl is the page's length in tokens, avgdl is the average length over all pages, and idf(t) = ln(1 + (N − df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)) with N pages of which df contain the term. With k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75, a rare term on a short page scores high and repetition saturates. A tiny example makes it concrete: with three pages "apple pie", "apple apple tart" and "pie crust crust crust", the word apple is on 2 of 3 pages, so its idf is ln(1 + 1.5/2.5) = 0.470; on the first page (length 2 against an average of 3) it scores 0.470 × 2.2 / (1 + 1.2 × 0.75) = 0.544.

The index sees each page as its report's title followed by the page text (context(chunk)). That is a deliberate choice: many pages never name their company, and the question always does. The given tokenize lower-cases, drops stop words, keeps numbers like 6,840 whole and applies a light stemmer. The constructor is given; you write idf, score and search.

The answer without a model

The baseline takes the top-ranked page, picks the sentence that shares the most content words with the question (best_sentence, given) and cites the page. It never refuses. This is the "keyword search plus a rule" that the chapter recommends, and it is stronger than it sounds.

class BM25Index:
    """BM25 over page chunks, each represented by tokenize(context(chunk)).
    Parameters k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75. For a query, each DISTINCT query term t scores
        idf(t) * tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 * (1 - b + b * dl / avgdl))
    where tf is the term's count in the page, dl the page's length in tokens and avgdl the mean
    length over all pages, and idf(t) = ln(1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)) with N pages of which df
    contain t. A page's score is the sum over the query's distinct terms."""

    def __init__(self, chunks, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
        self.chunks, self.k1, self.b = list(chunks), k1, b
        self.tokens = [tokenize(context(c)) for c in self.chunks]
        self.tf = [Counter(t) for t in self.tokens]
        self.avgdl = sum(len(t) for t in self.tokens) / len(self.tokens)
        self.df = Counter(term for tf in self.tf for term in tf)

    def idf(self, term):
        """ln(1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)); a term in no page has df = 0."""
        n, df = len(self.chunks), self.df.get(term, 0)
        return math.log(1 + (n - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))

    def score(self, query, i):
        """BM25 score of page number `i` (an index into self.chunks) for the query string."""
        tf, dl = self.tf[i], len(self.tokens[i])
        total = 0.0
        for term in set(tokenize(query)):
            f = tf.get(term, 0)
            if f:
                total += self.idf(term) * f * (self.k1 + 1) / (f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * dl / self.avgdl))
        return total

    def search(self, query, k=3):
        """The top `k` pages as [(score, Chunk)], best first, only pages with score > 0.
        Ties are broken by (doc, page) in ascending order."""
        scored = [(self.score(query, i), c) for i, c in enumerate(self.chunks)]
        scored = [(s, c) for s, c in scored if s > 0]
        scored.sort(key=lambda sc: (-sc[0], sc[1].doc, sc[1].page))
        return scored[:k]


def recall_at_k(questions, index, k):
    """(found, total): of the ANSWERABLE questions, how many have their gold page among the top k
    results of index.search(question['question'], k). A question's gold pages are [[doc, page]]."""
    found = total = 0
    for q in questions:
        if not q["answerable"]:
            continue
        total += 1
        got = {(c.doc, c.page) for _, c in index.search(q["question"], k)}
        found += any(tuple(p) in got for p in q["pages"])
    return found, total


def baseline_answer(index, question):
    """The baseline system: no model, no abstention. Take the top-ranked page (k = 1), answer with
    its best sentence (best_sentence) and cite that page. Returns the result dict used everywhere:
      {"question", "answer", "cites": [(doc, page)], "hits": [(doc, page)], "abstained": False,
       "reason": None, "problems": []}
    If the search finds nothing at all, answer None and abstain with reason "no_hits"."""
    hits = index.search(question, 1)
    if not hits:
        return {"question": question, "answer": None, "cites": [], "hits": [], "abstained": True, "reason": "no_hits", "problems": []}
    chunk = hits[0][1]
    _, sentence = best_sentence(question, chunk.text)
    return {"question": question, "answer": sentence, "cites": [(chunk.doc, chunk.page)], "hits": [(chunk.doc, chunk.page)],
            "abstained": False, "reason": None, "problems": []}

Milestone 3: an answer with a page, and a check that the figures tie

Now the model. The reader receives a prompt with the retrieved pages, each on its own line labelled [doc p.N], and the question. It replies with a sentence ending in the citation it used, or with NOT_FOUND. The prompt format is part of the contract because the stand-in reader parses it; a real model needs a prompt like it too, and you would write it with the same care. Look at what the check asks for: the exact text of the prompt, byte for byte. That is a good habit, because a prompt is code, and an accidental edit to it is a change in behaviour.

Three small parsing jobs follow. parse_reply splits a reply into the answer text and a list of (doc, page) citations (with the page as an integer) and treats exactly NOT_FOUND as no answer. numbers_in extracts the numbers from a text, with thousands commas removed, so that "5,420" and "5420" are the same. citation_problems uses these two to reject answers that cannot be trusted: no citation at all, a citation to a page that was never in the prompt (models do invent citations), and a figure in the answer that is on none of the cited pages.

Common misconception

"If the figure is on the cited page, the answer is right." The tie check proves the answer is faithful to the page: it did not invent the number. It cannot prove the page is relevant. Ask for a company's dividend in 2022 and a reader that quotes this year's dividend sentence with the right page will pass every tie check and still be wrong. Faithfulness and correctness are different properties, and the evaluation needs both: a gold answer for correctness, and a citation check for traceability. (The grounded-citations chapter and fact-checking and citation verification go further, to claims and entailment.)

def build_prompt(question, hits):
    """The prompt for the reader. `hits` is the list of (score, Chunk) from search. Exactly:

    Answer the question using only the context below. Cite the page you used as [doc p.N]. If the context does not contain the answer, reply NOT_FOUND.<newline>
    <newline>
    Context:<newline>
    one line per hit, in rank order:  [doc p.N] page text<newline>
    <newline>
    Question: <question>

    (no trailing newline). Use chunk.text, not the title."""
    lines = [f"[{c.doc} p.{c.page}] {c.text}" for _, c in hits]
    return ("Answer the question using only the context below. Cite the page you used as [doc p.N]. "
            "If the context does not contain the answer, reply NOT_FOUND.\n\nContext:\n"
            + "\n".join(lines) + f"\n\nQuestion: {question}")


CITE_RE = re.compile(r"\[([a-z0-9_]+) p\.(\d+)\]")


def parse_reply(reply):
    """(answer, cites) from a reader's reply. Citations look like [doc p.N]; they are removed from
    the answer text, which is then stripped, and returned as [(doc, N)] with N an int, in order.
    A reply whose stripped text is exactly NOT_FOUND gives (None, []).
    parse_reply("Revenue was 5. [fernwood p.1]") -> ("Revenue was 5.", [("fernwood", 1)])"""
    if reply.strip() == "NOT_FOUND":
        return None, []
    cites = [(d, int(p)) for d, p in CITE_RE.findall(reply)]
    return CITE_RE.sub("", reply).strip(), cites


NUM_RE = re.compile(r"(?<![A-Za-z0-9])\d[\d,]*(?:\.\d+)?")


def numbers_in(text):
    """Every number in `text`, as a string without thousands commas, in order of appearance.
    A number is digits with optional commas and an optional decimal part, not glued to a letter or
    another digit on the left (so 'h36' holds none). numbers_in("5,420 GWh, up 14.5% from 4910") -> ['5420', '14.5', '4910'];
    a full stop that ends a sentence is not part of the number: numbers_in("It was 21.") -> ['21']."""
    return [m.group().replace(",", "") for m in NUM_RE.finditer(text)]


def citation_problems(answer, cites, chunks):
    """Reasons an answer cannot be trusted, as a list of strings (empty means it ties out).
    `chunks` are the pages that were put in the prompt. In this order:
      'no_citation'                                  if cites is empty
      'cite_not_in_context: <doc> p.<N>'             for each cite that is not one of `chunks`
      'number_not_on_cited_page: <n>'                for each number of the answer (numbers_in) that appears
                                                     on none of the cited pages' text (only cites that ARE in context count)
    A number is checked once however often it appears in the answer."""
    problems = []
    if not cites:
        problems.append("no_citation")
    by_key = {(c.doc, c.page): c for c in chunks}
    for doc, page in cites:
        if (doc, page) not in by_key:
            problems.append(f"cite_not_in_context: {doc} p.{page}")
    on_pages = set()
    for key in cites:
        if key in by_key:
            on_pages.update(numbers_in(by_key[key].text))
    seen = set()
    for n in numbers_in(answer or ""):
        if n not in on_pages and n not in seen:
            problems.append(f"number_not_on_cited_page: {n}")
        seen.add(n)
    return problems


def answer_question(model, index, question, k=3):
    """Retrieve, ask the reader, parse, check. Steps: hits = index.search(question, k);
    reply = model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": build_prompt(question, hits)}], system=SYSTEM);
    (answer, cites) = parse_reply(text_of(reply)). Returns the result dict:
      {"question", "answer", "cites", "hits": [(doc, page)] of all hits, "abstained": answer is None,
       "reason": "model_not_found" if abstained else None,
       "problems": citation_problems(answer, cites, chunks of the hits) (an abstention has none),
       "raw": the reply text}"""
    hits = index.search(question, k)
    resp = model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": build_prompt(question, hits)}], system=SYSTEM)
    raw = text_of(resp)
    answer, cites = parse_reply(raw)
    problems = citation_problems(answer, cites, [c for _, c in hits]) if answer is not None else []
    return {"question": question, "answer": answer, "cites": cites, "hits": [(c.doc, c.page) for _, c in hits],
            "abstained": answer is None, "reason": "model_not_found" if answer is None else None,
            "problems": problems, "raw": raw}

Milestone 4: knowing when not to answer

The brief said "when the reports do not contain the answer, it must say so rather than guess". A model asked a question the context cannot answer often answers anyway, because the most similar sentence is right there (see grounding, abstention and confidence). The stand-in reader has one weak refusal built in: it says NOT_FOUND if the best sentence shares fewer than two content words with the question. That catches questions about nothing in the reports and misses the dangerous ones, where a nearby sentence overlaps well enough.

So the system needs its own gate, and the one this lab uses is deliberately simple and checkable: the things a question names must exist in what you cite. A question about "Harbourline Freight's revenue in 2024" names a company and a year. If the cited page (its title and text) does not contain "2024", the question is asking about something the source does not cover, and no sentence the reader found can be a correct answer. required_terms pulls out the named things: capitalised words that are not the first word of the question and not question words, and four-digit numbers. missing_terms checks them against the cited chunks. answer_or_abstain chains the pieces: take the model's answer; refuse as ungrounded if its citations fail the milestone-3 checks; refuse as missing:... if a named thing is absent; otherwise answer. The withheld answer is kept as draft, because the error analysis will want to read what the system nearly said.

This rule has a failure mode that it cannot fix, and a good submission says so. The year 2022 appears on Harbourline's first page (as last year's profit), so a question about the 2022 dividend passes the gate and is answered from the wrong year. Rules on surface features catch absence, not mismatch. You can see the effect by swapping in an over-eager reader: the check does exactly that, using ExtractiveReader(min_overlap=1), and requires that your rule, not the reader's caution, refuses the five questions that name a year, a place or a company that is not in the cited pages.

CAPS_STOP = {"What", "How", "Which", "Who", "When", "Where", "Why", "Does", "Did", "Is", "Are", "Was", "Were", "The", "In", "On", "By", "For", "Of", "A", "An"}


def required_terms(question):
    """The things a question names that the source must contain, lower-cased, without duplicates,
    in order of first appearance: every word beginning with a capital letter that is not the first
    word of the question and not in CAPS_STOP, and every four-digit number. Words are runs of
    letters (an apostrophe plus s, as in Harbourline's, is dropped); '&' is ignored.
    required_terms("What was Calder & Moss's net cash in 2024?") -> ['calder', 'moss', '2024']"""
    seen = []
    for i, m in enumerate(re.finditer(r"[A-Za-z]+(?:['’][A-Za-z]+)?|\b\d{4}\b", question)):
        w = re.sub(r"['’]s$", "", m.group())
        if w.isdigit():
            term = w
        elif i > 0 and w[0].isupper() and w not in CAPS_STOP:
            term = w.lower()
        else:
            continue
        if term not in seen:
            seen.append(term)
    return seen


def missing_terms(question, chunks):
    """The required_terms of `question` that occur in none of the `chunks` (use tokens of
    context(chunk), lower-cased, without stemming; a term must match a whole word). Same order."""
    have = set()
    for c in chunks:
        have.update(re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", context(c).lower()))
    return [t for t in required_terms(question) if t not in have]


def answer_or_abstain(model, index, question, k=3):
    """The full system. Start from answer_question. Then, if it did not already abstain:
      1. if it has problems -> abstain with reason "ungrounded";
      2. else take the chunks it cites; if missing_terms(question, those chunks) is non-empty ->
         abstain with reason "missing:" + the terms joined by ",".
    An abstention returns the same dict with "answer": None, "cites": [], "abstained": True, the
    reason set, and a new key "draft" holding the answer that was withheld (None if the reader had
    none). A non-abstention has "draft": None."""
    res = answer_question(model, index, question, k)
    res["draft"] = None
    if res["abstained"]:
        return res
    reason = None
    if res["problems"]:
        reason = "ungrounded"
    else:
        by_key = {(c.doc, c.page): c for c in index.chunks}
        miss = missing_terms(question, [by_key[key] for key in res["cites"]])
        if miss:
            reason = "missing:" + ",".join(miss)
    if reason:
        res.update(draft=res["answer"], answer=None, cites=[], abstained=True, reason=reason)
    return res

Milestone 5: evaluation with intervals

A number without an interval, on a set of forty, is a story. The evaluation chapter's recipe (evaluation plans customers trust) applies unchanged. First, decide what correct means before looking at results. Here an answerable question is correct only if the answer contains the gold figure and at least one cited page is a gold page: the right number on the wrong page is an untraceable claim and scores zero. An unanswerable question is correct only if the system refused. Second, report counts with denominators and a Wilson interval for the headline (given). Third, because the baseline is run on the same forty questions, compare them by pairing: resample whole questions, keeping each question's two outcomes together, and take the interval of the difference. Pairing matters because both systems find the same questions easy, so most of the question-to-question variation cancels out of the difference.

The percentile bootstrap, in the exact form the lab fixes so that two people get the same interval: with rng = random.Random(seed), each of reps repetitions draws n indices with rng.randrange(n), takes the mean (or, for the paired version, the mean difference on the same indices), the repetitions are sorted, and the interval is the entries at positions int(0.025 * reps) and int(0.975 * reps).

def normalise_text(s):
    """Lower case, thousands commas inside numbers removed (6,840 -> 6840), runs of whitespace
    collapsed to one space, stripped."""
    s = re.sub(r"(?<=\d),(?=\d)", "", s.lower())
    return " ".join(s.split())


def grade(q, result):
    """Grade one result against its question. Returns a dict:
      {"id", "answerable", "abstained", "answer_ok", "cite_ok", "correct"}
    answer_ok: the gold answer (normalise_text) occurs inside the normalised answer text (False if
               there is no answer or the question is unanswerable).
    cite_ok:   at least one cited (doc, page) is one of the gold pages (False if none is cited).
    correct:   for an answerable question, not abstained and answer_ok and cite_ok;
               for an unanswerable question, abstained."""
    answerable = q["answerable"]
    abstained = bool(result["abstained"])
    answer_ok = bool(answerable and result["answer"] and normalise_text(q["answer"]) in normalise_text(result["answer"]))
    gold = {tuple(p) for p in q["pages"]}
    cite_ok = any(tuple(c) in gold for c in result["cites"])
    correct = (not abstained and answer_ok and cite_ok) if answerable else abstained
    return {"id": q["id"], "answerable": answerable, "abstained": abstained,
            "answer_ok": answer_ok, "cite_ok": cite_ok, "correct": correct}


def bootstrap_ci(values, reps=2000, seed=7):
    """95% percentile bootstrap interval (lo, hi) for the mean of `values` (numbers or booleans).
    rng = random.Random(seed); for each of `reps` repetitions draw n = len(values) indices with
    rng.randrange(n), one call per draw, and take the mean of those values. Sort the means; lo is
    means[int(0.025 * reps)] and hi is means[int(0.975 * reps)]. Empty input raises ValueError."""
    n = len(values)
    if n == 0:
        raise ValueError("no values")
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    means = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        means.append(sum(values[rng.randrange(n)] for _ in range(n)) / n)
    means.sort()
    return means[int(0.025 * reps)], means[int(0.975 * reps)]


def paired_bootstrap(a, b, reps=2000, seed=7):
    """(diff, lo, hi): the mean of a minus the mean of b on the full sample, and the 95% percentile
    bootstrap interval for that difference. a and b are parallel lists scored on the SAME items.
    rng = random.Random(seed); each repetition draws n indices with rng.randrange(n), one call per
    draw, and uses the SAME indices for both lists: the repetition's difference is
    sum(a[i] - b[i] for the drawn i) / n. Sort; lo = diffs[int(0.025 * reps)], hi = diffs[int(0.975 * reps)].
    Unequal lengths or empty lists raise ValueError."""
    n = len(a)
    if n == 0 or n != len(b):
        raise ValueError("a and b must be non-empty and the same length")
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    diffs = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        idx = [rng.randrange(n) for _ in range(n)]
        diffs.append(sum(a[i] - b[i] for i in idx) / n)
    diffs.sort()
    return sum(a) / n - sum(b) / n, diffs[int(0.025 * reps)], diffs[int(0.975 * reps)]


def make_report(graded, base_graded):
    """The numbers of the evaluation, from the graded items of the system and of the baseline
    (parallel lists from grade(), same questions in the same order). Returns:
      n, correct, accuracy                    over all questions
      wilson: (lo, hi)                        wilson(correct, n)
      boot: (lo, hi)                          bootstrap_ci of the 0/1 'correct' values (default reps and seed)
      answerable_n, answerable_correct        questions with a gold answer, and how many were correct
      unanswerable_n, abstained_right         questions with no answer, and how many were abstained on
      false_abstain                           answerable questions the system abstained on
      baseline_correct, baseline_accuracy
      diff, diff_lo, diff_hi                  paired_bootstrap(system, baseline) on the 0/1 'correct' values."""
    sys_ok = [int(g["correct"]) for g in graded]
    base_ok = [int(g["correct"]) for g in base_graded]
    n, correct = len(graded), sum(sys_ok)
    diff, dlo, dhi = paired_bootstrap(sys_ok, base_ok)
    return {
        "n": n, "correct": correct, "accuracy": correct / n,
        "wilson": wilson(correct, n), "boot": bootstrap_ci(sys_ok),
        "answerable_n": sum(g["answerable"] for g in graded),
        "answerable_correct": sum(g["correct"] for g in graded if g["answerable"]),
        "unanswerable_n": sum(not g["answerable"] for g in graded),
        "abstained_right": sum(g["correct"] for g in graded if not g["answerable"]),
        "false_abstain": sum(g["abstained"] for g in graded if g["answerable"]),
        "baseline_correct": sum(base_ok), "baseline_accuracy": sum(base_ok) / n,
        "diff": diff, "diff_lo": dlo, "diff_hi": dhi,
    }

Milestone 6: the first failure, and a memo whose numbers tie

The chapter's error-analysis deliverable: "read a sample of failures, name the first thing that went wrong in each, count the categories, and say which one to fix first". The method matters: name the first thing in the pipeline's own order, not the most dramatic. A wrong answer whose gold page was never retrieved is a retrieval problem, however wrong the sentence looks. The lab's categories are checked in this order: false_abstain (it refused an answerable question), retrieval_miss (no gold page among the hits), wrong_sentence_same_page, wrong_page_cited, citation_mismatch, and for unanswerable questions should_have_abstained.

The memo then has to say what you found without overclaiming. Its numbers should all come from the report, and any figure it quotes from a report should be on the page it cites. That is mechanical enough to check, so the lab does: check_memo verifies that the memo has its five sections, that every number in the Result and Baseline sections is one the report supports (counts, percentages, interval ends, to the printed precision), that every failure category and count appears, that the counts add up to the number of failures, that any line citing [doc p.N] has all its other numbers on that page, and that the limitations are not a single line. Write the memo as a function of the report, and rendering it twice gives the same text; write it by hand, and a figure will eventually drift from the table it came from.

def first_failure(q, graded, result):
    """Name the FIRST thing that went wrong for one question, or None if it was correct. Check in
    this order (the pipeline's own order) and return the first that applies:
      answerable question:
        'false_abstain'              the system abstained
        'retrieval_miss'             no gold page among result['hits']
        'wrong_sentence_same_page'   cite_ok but not answer_ok
        'wrong_page_cited'           not answer_ok (and not cite_ok)
        'citation_mismatch'          answer_ok but not cite_ok
      unanswerable question:
        'should_have_abstained'      the system answered anyway"""
    if graded["correct"]:
        return None
    if not q["answerable"]:
        return "should_have_abstained"
    if graded["abstained"]:
        return "false_abstain"
    gold = {tuple(p) for p in q["pages"]}
    if not any(tuple(h) in gold for h in result["hits"]):
        return "retrieval_miss"
    if not graded["answer_ok"]:
        return "wrong_sentence_same_page" if graded["cite_ok"] else "wrong_page_cited"
    return "citation_mismatch"


def error_table(categories):
    """Count a list of failure categories (ignore None). Returns [(category, count)] sorted by
    count descending, then category name ascending."""
    c = Counter(x for x in categories if x)
    return sorted(c.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))

    (question id, (doc, page) of the page involved, category). Percentages have one decimal place. Exactly these
    sections in this order, each a '## ' heading:

    # Take-home memo: document QA over three annual reports
    ## Result            "The system was correct on C of N hidden questions (P%), 95% Wilson interval LO% to HI%,
                          95% bootstrap interval LO% to HI%." and a second line "It answered AC of AN answerable
                          questions correctly and abstained correctly on UC of UN unanswerable ones."
    ## Baseline          "The retrieval-only baseline was correct on BC of N (BP%). The paired difference is
                          +D.D points (95% bootstrap interval +L.L to +H.H)." (signed with a sign, one decimal; points = 100 x)
    ## Error analysis    "Failures by first cause: " then "category count" pairs joined by ", " and a full stop

        "The answers are extracted sentences, not written prose, so fluency is not tested. "

def check_memo(memo, rep, errors, corpus):
    """Problems found in a memo (an empty list means it passes).

    Use sections() with MEMO_SECTIONS, in that order:
      'missing section: <Name>'                for each required section that is absent (spelled as in MEMO_SECTIONS)
      For Result and Baseline, for each line: every number in it (numbers_in, skipping any that lie inside a
        [doc p.N] citation) must be within 0.051 of a value in report_values(rep); else
        'untied number: <n>'  (<n> as numbers_in gave it).
      For Error analysis: for each (category, count) in `errors`, the text of the section must contain
        '<category> <count>' or else 'missing error row: <category> <count>'; if sum of counts != n - correct:
        'error counts sum to <s>, report has <f> failures'.
        Every line there that contains a citation [doc p.N] must have each of its other numbers on the cited
        page of `corpus` (the load_corpus list), else 'number not on cited page: <n> [<doc> p.<N>]';
        a citation to a page that does not exist is 'unknown page: <doc> p.<N>'. Lines with no citation
        have every number checked against the counts in `errors`, n, and n - correct, else 'untied number: <n>'.
      'limitations too thin' if the Limitations section has fewer than 25 words.
    Report problems in that order: missing sections, then per section in the order above."""
    sec = sections(memo)
    problems = []
    for name in MEMO_SECTIONS:
        if name.casefold() not in sec:
            problems.append(f"missing section: {name}")
    allowed = report_values(rep)

    def strip_cites(line):
        return CITE_RE.sub(" ", line)

    for name in ("Result", "Baseline"):
        for line in sec.get(name.casefold(), []):
            for tok, val in _tokens_with_values(strip_cites(line)):
                if not any(abs(val - a) <= 0.051 for a in allowed):
                    problems.append(f"untied number: {tok}")
    if "error analysis" in sec:
        lines = sec["error analysis"]
        body = "\n".join(lines)
        for cat, count in errors:
            if f"{cat} {count}" not in body:
                problems.append(f"missing error row: {cat} {count}")
        total, failures = sum(c for _, c in errors), rep["n"] - rep["correct"]
        if total != failures:
            problems.append(f"error counts sum to {total}, report has {failures} failures")
        counts = {float(c) for _, c in errors} | {float(rep["n"]), float(failures)}
        pages = {(d["doc"], p["page"]): p["text"] for d in corpus for p in d["pages"]}
        for line in lines:
            cites = [(d, int(p)) for d, p in CITE_RE.findall(line)]
            nums = _tokens_with_values(strip_cites(line))
            if cites:
                for doc, page in cites:
                    if (doc, page) not in pages:
                        problems.append(f"unknown page: {doc} p.{page}")
                        continue
                    on_page = set(numbers_in(pages[(doc, page)]))
                    for tok, _ in nums:
                        if tok not in on_page:
                            problems.append(f"number not on cited page: {tok} [{doc} p.{page}]")
            else:
                for tok, val in nums:
                    if val not in counts:
                        problems.append(f"untied number: {tok}")
    if "limitations" in sec and len(" ".join(sec["limitations"]).split()) < 25:
        problems.append("limitations too thin")
    return problems

Systems connection

The grading harness is a regression test suite with a hidden half, and the discipline around it (tune on dev, run the sealed set once) is the same as holding back a validation environment from developers. BM25 over pages is the inverted-index machinery of search engines (specialised stores). The refusal gate is a precondition check at a service boundary: reject requests that name things the service does not hold, before doing work. The trade-off between a cautious and an eager reader is the precision and recall trade-off of every alerting system, and the paired bootstrap is the same trick as comparing two builds on the same requests (compare the canary analysis in the reliability course). Chapters 5, 6 and 18 give the reasoning; this lab is the code.

What you learned

  • A restatement is a deliverable that can be checked mechanically for the parts people skip: assumptions, questions, out-of-scope, a measurable success criterion.
  • Build the baseline first. A page-level BM25 plus the best sentence is strong, and the system must be compared with it on the same questions.
  • Make the model's output parseable and checkable: a fixed prompt, citations in a fixed form, and a test that every figure is on a cited page. That proves faithfulness, not relevance.
  • Refusal is a design decision with two error types. A rule that checks the named things against the source is simple and testable, and it cannot catch a plausible answer from the wrong year.
  • Report counts, denominators and intervals; compare with a paired bootstrap. A gain whose interval includes zero is a result to state plainly. Break the result down: here the system loses on answerable questions and wins on refusals.
  • Name the first failure in pipeline order, count the causes, and generate the memo from the report so its numbers cannot drift.

Check your understanding

On 40 hidden questions the system is right on 34 and the baseline on 29. On the 30 answerable ones the baseline is right 29 times and the system 25. What is the most accurate reading?

  1. The system is better on every kind of question
  2. The system's overall lead comes from refusing unanswerable questions; on answerable ones it is worse, so the next work should reduce wrong refusals
  3. The baseline is better, because it is right on more answerable questions
  4. Nothing can be said without more questions
The totals hide a split. The baseline never refuses, so it scores zero on the ten unanswerable questions, which is where the system gains; on the answerable thirty it loses four. Calling the system better everywhere, or the baseline better because of one slice, each ignores half of that. More questions would narrow the interval, but the split already tells you where to work.

The paired bootstrap interval for the system's advantage is −2.5 to +30.0 points. Which sentence should the memo contain?

  1. "The system is significantly better than the baseline."
  2. "The system is worse than the baseline."
  3. "The system and baseline are identical."
  4. "The system scored 12.5 points higher on these 40 questions; the data are compatible with anything from a small loss to a large gain, so the gain is not established. Gains of about this size would need many more questions to resolve."
The point estimate is +12.5 but the interval includes zero, so "significantly better" overclaims. "Worse" or "identical" claims knowledge the interval does not give. The fourth states the number, the uncertainty and what would settle it.

An answer to "What was the dividend per share in 2022?" quotes the 2023 dividend sentence and cites the right page. The tie check passes. Why, and what catches the problem?

  1. The tie check only proves the figure is on the cited page, not that the page answers the question; grading against a gold answer catches it
  2. The tie check is broken and must be fixed
  3. The citation is wrong, which the tie check should have caught
  4. Nothing can catch this
The figure is on the page, so the answer is faithful to its source, but it answers a different question. Correctness needs a gold answer for the intended question, and the evaluation grades against it. A rule on named things cannot see it either, because 2022 does appear on that page.

You set the reader to need only one shared content word instead of two. Wrong refusals fall from 5 to 1 but four unanswerable questions are now answered. Which choice is best?

  1. Always take the setting with the higher total, here 35 against 34
  2. Choose the setting that does best on the hidden set
  3. Choose on dev data and the customer's stated costs of a wrong answer against a wrong refusal; report the hidden result once, with both error counts
  4. Average the two settings
The totals differ by one question, well inside the noise, and the error types are different. Which matters more is a cost question, and it belongs in the restatement. Selecting on the hidden set uses up the test; averaging settings is not meaningful.

Why compare the system and baseline with a paired bootstrap that resamples whole questions rather than bootstrapping each system separately and subtracting?

  1. It is faster
  2. Both systems face the same questions, so questions that are easy or hard for both cancel out of the difference; keeping each question's two outcomes together gives a narrower and honest interval
  3. It guarantees the interval excludes zero
  4. Separate bootstraps are not possible with 0/1 data
Resampling each system separately throws away the fact that the same items were scored twice, which usually inflates the interval for the difference. Pairing makes no promise about zero. Separate bootstraps are possible but less efficient.

Your restatement checker passes a document in which every assumption is "the data is fine". What does that show about mechanical checks of this kind?

  1. The checker is wrong and should read the text more deeply
  2. Mechanical checks are pointless
  3. They catch omissions (missing headings, no numbers, unfinished placeholders) cheaply but not quality, so a human or a rubric still reviews content; passing is necessary, not sufficient
  4. Assumptions should be allowed to be empty
The checker's job is the part that is easy to skip. Whether an assumption is a real decision needs judgement. Dismissing the check wastes its value; weakening it to allow empty assumptions defeats the point of the deliverable.
