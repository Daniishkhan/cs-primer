Lab 2: A Cost & Latency Model

The cost chapter said the unit that matters is the cost of an accepted task, not of a token, and choosing models said that pay-per-token and self-hosting cross at a volume you can compute. In this lab you build the calculator behind both: the price of a call with caching, a task with retries, routing and batching, a latency percentile that shows how retries set the tail, the cheapest design that clears a quality bar and a latency target, the break-even against running your own GPUs, and finally a net present value with a sensitivity sweep that says which assumption the whole case depends on.

Why this lab matters

A customer's finance partner will ask three questions in the first meeting. What does one task cost? What happens at ten times the volume? And which of your assumptions, if it turns out wrong, kills the case? An engineer who answers with a spreadsheet nobody can rerun loses the room at the second question. An engineer who has a small, tested model, whose every number is an input and whose output can be recomputed in a second, can change an assumption live and show the answer move. This lab builds that model. Its most useful result is a surprise it will show you rather than tell you: in a pipeline with a human reviewer, the model bill is a few per cent of the cost of a task, so the "cheapest" design on the token bill is not the cheapest per accepted task, and the assumption that decides the project's value is not a price at all.

Pure arithmetic, with every assumption on the page

Nothing here calls a model, a clock or the network. Prices, speeds, token counts, review times and the measured quality of each candidate design are illustrative assumptions chosen to be plausible in shape, not any vendor's list. The functions read module-level constants (PRICE, RETRY, REVIEW_SHARE and so on) when they run, so you can change a constant and rerun, and the check does exactly that to test you. The only randomness is in seeded property checks, and a failure prints its seed so you can replay it: python3 check.py solution 3 7 runs milestone 3 with seed 7. The whole check takes a fraction of a second. Python 3.9 or newer, standard library only.

State of play, October 2026

Two billing mechanisms in the lab are real and widely offered, and their numbers change. Anthropic's prompt-caching documentation (read 14 October 2026) states that 5-minute cache writes cost 1.25 times the base input price, 1-hour writes 2 times, and cache reads 0.1 times, with a footnote that some models have lower read multipliers. Its batch-processing documentation describes asynchronous processing at a 50% cost reduction. The lab uses 1.25, 0.1 and 0.5 as CACHE_WRITE, CACHE_READ and BATCH. Other providers offer similar mechanisms with different multipliers, minimum prompt lengths and cache lifetimes, and per-token prices differ by provider and model tier and change often. Before you use any number in a real proposal, read the current price list; the code is built so that is a five-minute edit.

The brief

You are the engineer on the claims-letter assistant of the cost chapter. Each letter goes through two model calls in sequence: extract (read the letter, 1,500 fixed instruction tokens plus 6,000 tokens of letter, write 300 tokens of structured fields) and draft (1,500 variable tokens of context after an 800-token fixed prefix, write a 450-token reply). Six per cent of tasks repeat every call after a format failure. A reviewer checks 35% of outputs for 4 minutes at a loaded 48 dollars an hour. The sponsor has three constraints: the work must meet a quality bar, an interactive reply must come back within a target, and the finance partner wants to know whether to buy tokens or buy GPUs. You have seven candidate designs and a gold set of 300 cases on which each was scored, using the evaluation kit of Lab 1.

What you'll build

Six milestones:

  1. The price of one call: input and output tokens at per-million prices, with the static prefix served from a prompt cache.
  2. The price of a task: sum the calls, route a share to a small model, repeat failed calls, discount the batched share, then add the reviewer.
  3. Latency and the retry tail: time to first token plus generation, calls in sequence, and a weighted percentile.
  4. The cheapest design that clears the bar: cost per accepted task, a quality gate on the interval's low end and a latency gate, and a winner.
  5. Pay per token or host: fixed fleet cost, capacity, fleets bought whole, and the break-even volume.
  6. Net present value and sensitivity: discounted cash flow, a tornado of one-at-a-time swings, a seeded chance of loss and a bisection for the break-even input.

Milestone 1: the price of one call

Providers price by the token, the unit of text a model reads and writes, quoted in dollars per million, with output tokens costing several times as much as input tokens. A call costs (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price) / 1,000,000. For the extract call on the mid tier ($3 in, $15 out per million, illustrative): (7,500 × 3 + 300 × 15) / 1,000,000 = 0.0270 dollars. On the small tier ($0.15 and $0.60) the same call costs 0.0013, twenty times less.

Prompt caching changes the input side. Many calls begin with the same long, fixed instructions (here 1,500 tokens), and a provider can keep the processed prefix and serve it again at a fraction of the price. The first time a prefix is stored you pay a premium (CACHE_WRITE, 1.25 times the input price); each later read costs a fraction (CACHE_READ, 0.1 times). If cached_share is the share of requests whose prefix is already in the cache, the prefix costs prefix × price × (cached_share × 0.1 + (1 − cached_share) × 1.25), and the variable tokens (the letter itself) are always billed in full. With 90% of requests hitting the cache, the extract call's input falls from 22,500 to 18,967.5 token-dollars-per-million and the call from 0.0270 to 0.0235: a 13% saving, not the 90% the headline implies, because only 1,500 of the 7,500 input tokens are cacheable. Caching rewards long, repeated prefixes, not prompts dominated by each new document.

One detail is a trap. cached_share == 0 in this model means caching is off, so the prefix is billed at the plain input price. A cache that is on but almost never hit (cached_share = 0.01) pays the 1.25 write premium on nearly every request and costs more than no cache. The check tests exactly that, because "turn on caching" is a change that must be measured, not assumed. (Real providers also have a minimum prefix length below which nothing is cached and a time-to-live after which entries expire; the lab folds both into the single hit share you supply.)

def call_cost(model, prefix, var, out, cached_share=0.0):
    """USD for one call to `model` (a key of PRICE) with `prefix` static input tokens, `var` variable
    input tokens and `out` output tokens. Prices are per million tokens.

    cached_share == 0: no caching, input = (prefix + var) * input price.
    cached_share > 0 : the static prefix is billed at CACHE_READ x price for the cached share and
                       CACHE_WRITE x price for the rest; the variable tokens are billed in full.
    Output tokens are billed at the output price either way.
    call_cost("mid", 1500, 6000, 300) -> 0.0270   (7,500 x 3 + 300 x 15, per million)"""
    pin, pout = PRICE[model]
    if cached_share > 0:
        inp = prefix * (cached_share * CACHE_READ + (1 - cached_share) * CACHE_WRITE) * pin + var * pin
    else:
        inp = (prefix + var) * pin
    return (inp + out * pout) / 1e6

Milestone 2: the price of a task

A task is the unit the customer thinks in: one letter processed. It may involve several calls, so the cost of a task is the sum over the calls, adjusted by three levers, each of which the cost chapter introduced:

  • Routing. A share small_share of tasks goes to the small model, the rest to the main one. The expected cost is the mix: (1 − s) × cost on the main model + s × cost on the small one. It is only a saving if the small model's quality holds on the work it is sent, which milestone 4 checks.
  • Retries. A share RETRY of tasks repeats every call after a format failure, so the whole sum is multiplied by (1 + RETRY).
  • Batching. Requests that can wait are billed at half price. If batch_share of tasks wait, the cost is multiplied by 1 − batch_share × (1 − BATCH).

A design is a dictionary with optional keys model (default "mid"), small_share, cached and batch_share. The chapter priced four designs; your task_cost must reproduce its table: all-mid 0.0431 dollars a task, with caching 0.0373, with 60% routed to the small model 0.0160, and with half the tasks batched 0.0120.

The reviewer is the other cost of the task. review_cost() is REVIEW_SHARE × REVIEW_MIN / 60 × RATE: 0.35 × 4 minutes at 48 dollars an hour is 1.12 dollars, twenty-six times the model bill of the all-mid design. total_cost(design) adds the two. The shape of that sum drives the rest of the lab.

def task_cost(design):
    """Model cost in USD of one task under `design`, a dict with optional keys
    model='mid', small_share=0.0, cached=0.0, batch_share=0.0.

    Sum over CALLS of: (1 - small_share) x the cost on `model` + small_share x the cost on 'small'
    (both with the `cached` share); multiply by (1 + RETRY) for repeated calls; then batching
    takes (1 - BATCH) off the cost of the batch_share of tasks:  x (1 - batch_share x (1 - BATCH))."""
    model = design.get("model", "mid")
    s = design.get("small_share", 0.0)
    cached = design.get("cached", 0.0)
    total = 0.0
    for _name, prefix, var, out in CALLS:
        big = call_cost(model, prefix, var, out, cached)
        small = call_cost("small", prefix, var, out, cached)
        total += (1 - s) * big + s * small
    total *= 1 + RETRY
    return total * (1 - design.get("batch_share", 0.0) * (1 - BATCH))

def review_cost():
    """USD of human review per task: REVIEW_SHARE x REVIEW_MIN minutes at RATE USD an hour."""
    return REVIEW_SHARE * REVIEW_MIN / 60 * RATE

def total_cost(design):
    """USD per task including the reviewer: task_cost(design) + review_cost()."""
    return task_cost(design) + review_cost()

Milestone 3: latency and the retry tail

The user waits for the whole task, and a streamed answer has two parts. The time to first token (TTFT) is the wait before anything arrives, dominated by reading the prompt. Generation then proceeds at a steady tokens per second rate, so a call takes ttft + out / tps seconds (the cost, latency and quality chapter explains why output tokens dominate). For a 300-token extract on the mid tier at 0.5 seconds and 90 tokens a second that is 0.5 + 3.33 = 3.83 seconds. The two calls run one after the other, so path_latency("mid") is the sum of both: 9.33 seconds; for small 4.25 and for large 18.47.

Users do not experience the average. The metrics chapter and tail latency explain why a service is judged on a high percentile: the p95 is the latency that 95% of tasks beat. Here is the surprise this milestone is built to show. If 6% of tasks repeat every call, those tasks take twice as long, and 6% is more than the 5% that p95 leaves for the slow tail. So the p95 of an all-mid design is the doubled path, 18.7 seconds, though the typical task takes 9.3. Cut the retry rate to 5% or below and the p95 drops to 9.3 seconds without touching the model. The tail is set by the retries, which is a prompt-and-parsing problem, not a model problem.

The percentile of a mixture is a small calculation. A task goes through the small model with probability small_share, otherwise the main model; and independently runs twice with probability RETRY. That gives four outcomes, each a latency and a probability. Sort them by latency, accumulate the probabilities, and the q-quantile is the first latency at which the running total reaches q (the weighted nearest-rank rule). Note that the order is by latency, not by path: with 60% routed to small, the doubled small path (8.5 seconds) comes before the mid path (9.33 seconds), and the cumulative probability reaches 0.95 only at the mid path. Allow 1e-9 for floating-point error when you compare the running total with q. Batched tasks wait hours by design and are not interactive, so they are ignored; a design that batches everything has no interactive latency and raises ValueError.

  • Build the outcomes as (latency, probability) tuples: for each of (small path, weight s) and (main path, weight 1 − s), add one with weight w * (1 - RETRY) at latency lat and one with w * RETRY at 2 * lat. Sort the list; tuples sort by latency first.

Solution spoiler 
def call_latency(model, out):
    """Seconds for one call: time to first token plus out / tokens-per-second (SPEED[model])."""
    ttft, tps = SPEED[model]
    return ttft + out / tps

def path_latency(model):
    """Seconds for one task that goes through `model`: the calls run one after another, so add the
    call latencies of all CALLS."""
    return sum(call_latency(model, out) for _name, _p, _v, out in CALLS)

def latency_percentile(design, q):
    """The q-quantile (0 < q <= 1) of INTERACTIVE task latency under `design`, in seconds.

    A task goes through 'small' with probability small_share, else through design['model'] (default
    'mid'). With probability RETRY it runs twice (latency doubles). Build the weighted outcomes
    (latency, probability), sort by latency, and return the first latency whose cumulative probability
    reaches q (weighted nearest-rank; allow 1e-9 for float error). Batched tasks are not interactive and
    are ignored: batch_share >= 1 raises ValueError."""
    if design.get("batch_share", 0.0) >= 1:
        raise ValueError("a fully batched design has no interactive latency")
    s = design.get("small_share", 0.0)
    big = path_latency(design.get("model", "mid"))
    small = path_latency("small")
    outcomes = []
    for lat, w in ((small, s), (big, 1 - s)):
        outcomes.append((lat, w * (1 - RETRY)))
        outcomes.append((2 * lat, w * RETRY))
    outcomes.sort()
    cum = 0.0
    for lat, w in outcomes:
        cum += w
        if cum >= q - 1e-9:
            return lat
    return outcomes[-1][0]

Milestone 4: the cheapest design that clears the bar

Cost alone picks the cheapest wrong answer. Each candidate in CANDIDATES has a design and a measured result on the same 300-case gold set (k correct of n), and the sponsor's two gates are a quality bar and a latency target. Put the evaluation kit to work: a candidate passes quality if the low end of the 95% Wilson interval of k/n reaches the bar (the same rule as Lab 1; wilson is given), and passes latency if its p95 is within the limit.

Among the survivors, the winner is not the one with the lowest model bill but the lowest cost per accepted task: total_cost(design) / accepted, where accepted is the share of outputs that end in an accepted result (here the candidate's k/n). A task that fails still cost money, including the reviewer's four minutes. The cost chapter drew this as the final box of its first figure; here it decides.

With a bar of 85% and a p95 limit of 20 seconds, the seven candidates come out as the table in the "Look under the hood" section shows. Routing 60% of tasks to the small model cuts the model bill by 57% but drops accuracy to 86.3% (low end 82%), under the bar. All-large is the most accurate (96.3%) and fails the latency target, because its retries double an 18-second path. All-small is cheap and not good enough. Four designs pass both gates (A, B, D and E), with model bills from 0.0431 dollars down to 0.0200, and B, all-mid with a warm cache, wins on cost per accepted task at 1.258 dollars, ahead of E at 1.271 and D at 1.279. The routed designs lose 2.3 points of accuracy, and each rejected output wastes a reviewer's time. That is the chapter's warning made concrete: once a person is in the loop, saving cents on the model while losing accuracy costs dollars.

def cost_per_accepted(design, accepted):
    """USD per ACCEPTED task: total_cost(design) / accepted, where `accepted` is the share (0 < a <= 1)
    of outputs that end in an accepted result. A task that fails still cost money."""
    if not 0 < accepted <= 1:
        raise ValueError("accepted must be in (0, 1]")
    return total_cost(design) / accepted

def choose(candidates, bar, p95_limit):
    """Pick the winner among candidates (dicts with name, design, k, n).

    A candidate passes QUALITY if the LOW end of the 95% Wilson interval of k/n is >= bar, and passes
    LATENCY if latency_percentile(design, 0.95) <= p95_limit seconds. Among those passing both, the winner has
    the lowest cost_per_accepted(design, k / n); ties go to the alphabetically first name.

    Returns {'name', 'cost', 'rejected'}: the winner's name and cost per accepted task, and
    `rejected`, a dict name -> sorted list of the gates failed (['latency'], ['quality'] or both).
    If nothing passes, name and cost are None."""
    rejected, best = {}, None
    for c in candidates:
        failed = []
        if wilson(c["k"], c["n"])[0] < bar:
            failed.append("quality")
        if latency_percentile(c["design"], 0.95) > p95_limit:
            failed.append("latency")
        if failed:
            rejected[c["name"]] = sorted(failed)
            continue
        cost = cost_per_accepted(c["design"], c["k"] / c["n"])
        if best is None or (cost, c["name"]) < (best[1], best[0]):
            best = (c["name"], cost)
    return {"name": best[0] if best else None, "cost": best[1] if best else None, "rejected": rejected}

Milestone 5: pay per token or run your own GPUs?

The choosing-models chapter set out the shape of the decision: an API is a variable cost (nothing at zero volume, linear in use), and a self-hosted model is mostly a fixed cost (GPUs paid for by the hour whether busy or not, plus the people to run them). Variable wins at low volume, fixed at high volume, and the crossing is a formula. Five small functions compute it, with the chapter's assumed numbers (all illustrative): two 80 GB GPUs at 2.50 dollars an hour for a 730-hour month, plus 1,500 dollars of an engineer's time for patching and upgrades, gives selfhost_fixed = 5,150 dollars a month. Six requests a second per GPU at 40% average utilisation gives selfhost_capacity = 6 × 2 × 730 × 3,600 × 0.4 = 12,614,400 requests a month. An API request of 3,000 input and 400 output tokens costs 0.005 dollars on a mid-tier API and 0.015 on a frontier API, so the breakeven_volume is 5,150 / 0.005 = 1,030,000 requests a month against the first and 343,333 against the second.

The chapter stopped at the capacity of one fleet. Real capacity comes in steps: a thirteenth million requests needs a second fleet, not a fraction of one. monthly_selfhost buys fleets whole, ceil(volume / capacity) of them, at least one even at zero volume, and cheapest_option compares all the options at a volume: each API at volume × per-request price against the fleets. The step function matters for a decision at the edge: at 12 million requests a month one fleet serves everything for 5,150 dollars, and at 13 million the bill doubles to 10,300, which is still far below the API's 65,000. Utilisation, not price, is what makes hosting efficient, and the experiment below shows that halving the GPU price moves the break-even while doubling the utilisation moves the capacity.

def selfhost_fixed(gpu_hour, gpus, ops_month, hours=HOURS):
    """USD a month for one self-hosted fleet: GPUs for every hour of the month, plus the operations share."""
    return gpu_hour * gpus * hours + ops_month

def selfhost_capacity(req_per_s_per_gpu, gpus, utilisation, hours=HOURS):
    """Requests one fleet can serve in a month: rate x GPUs x seconds in the month x average utilisation."""
    return req_per_s_per_gpu * gpus * hours * 3600 * utilisation

def monthly_selfhost(volume, fixed, capacity):
    """USD a month to serve `volume` requests: fleets are bought whole, so ceil(volume / capacity) fleets
    (at least one, even at zero volume) times `fixed`."""
    return max(1, math.ceil(volume / capacity)) * fixed

def breakeven_volume(fixed, api_per_request):
    """Requests a month at which paying per request costs as much as one fleet: fixed / api_per_request."""
    return fixed / api_per_request

def cheapest_option(volume, api, fixed, capacity):
    """The cheapest way to serve `volume` requests a month. `api` maps an API's name to its USD per request;
    self-hosting is the option named 'self-hosted' costing monthly_selfhost(volume, fixed, capacity).
    Returns (name, monthly USD); ties go to the alphabetically first name."""
    options = {name: volume * per for name, per in api.items()}
    options["self-hosted"] = monthly_selfhost(volume, fixed, capacity)
    name = min(sorted(options), key=lambda n: options[n])
    return name, options[name]

Milestone 6: net present value, sensitivity and chance of loss

The last question is the sponsor's: does the project pay back, and what could make it fail? The given project_flows(x) turns one set of input values into a list of cash flows: year 0 is the one-off cost (build, integration, security review, training), and each of the next three years is the counted saving minus the model bill and the fixed running cost. The counted saving is where the case is won or lost: tasks handled × minutes freed per task (the manual time minus the reviewer's) × the hourly rate × realisation, the share of freed time that turns into something the customer can count, such as a hire not made or a backlog cleared. The cost chapter's warning applies: saved minutes are not saved money.

Four functions turn that into answers. npv(flows, rate) is the net present value: each year's flow divided by (1 + rate)year, so money next year counts a little less than money now (year 0 is not discounted). The given model has DISCOUNT = 8%. At the most likely inputs the NPV is about 2.63 million dollars.

tornado(f, inputs) answers "which assumption matters most?". Each input has a (low, most likely, high) range. Holding every other input at its most likely value, evaluate f at each input's low and high, and rank the inputs by the absolute swing. It is a one-at-a-time sweep, so it misses interactions, but it is the fastest way to find what to measure first.

chance_of_loss(f, inputs, n, seed) answers "how often does it lose money?". Draw every input from a triangular distribution (a simple shape with a minimum, a most likely value and a maximum), evaluate f, and count the share of draws below zero. To make the result reproducible, the docstring fixes the draw: one random.Random(seed), inputs in sorted-name order, rng.triangular(low, high, mode) for each (note the argument order: Python takes low, high, mode). And break_even_input uses bisection to find the value of one input at which f crosses zero, keeping the other inputs at their modes: "how many minutes must the work take by hand today for the project to break even?"

def npv(flows, rate):
    """Net present value: flows[t] discounted by (1 + rate) ** t, so year 0 is not discounted."""
    return sum(f / (1 + rate) ** t for t, f in enumerate(flows))

def tornado(f, inputs):
    """One-at-a-time sensitivity. `f` maps a dict of input values to a number; `inputs` maps name ->
    (low, mode, high). With every other input at its mode, evaluate f at each input's low and high.

    Returns a list of (name, f_at_low, f_at_high, swing) with swing = abs(f_at_high - f_at_low),
    sorted by swing, largest first (ties by name)."""
    base = {k: v[1] for k, v in inputs.items()}
    rows = []
    for name, (lo, _mode, hi) in inputs.items():
        a = f(dict(base, **{name: lo}))
        b = f(dict(base, **{name: hi}))
        rows.append((name, a, b, abs(b - a)))
    return sorted(rows, key=lambda r: (-r[3], r[0]))

def chance_of_loss(f, inputs, n, seed):
    """Share of n random draws for which f(x) < 0. Each draw takes every input from a triangular
    distribution: x[name] = rng.triangular(low, high, mode), one shared random.Random(seed), names in sorted order."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    losses = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        x = {name: rng.triangular(inputs[name][0], inputs[name][2], inputs[name][1]) for name in sorted(inputs)}
        if f(x) < 0:
            losses += 1
    return losses / n

def break_even_input(f, inputs, name, lo, hi, tol=1e-6):
    """The value of input `name` (others at their modes) where f crosses zero, found by bisection between
    lo and hi. f(lo) and f(hi) must have opposite signs (otherwise raise ValueError). Stop when hi - lo < tol
    and return the midpoint."""
    base = {k: v[1] for k, v in inputs.items()}
    g = lambda v: f(dict(base, **{name: v}))
    glo, ghi = g(lo), g(hi)
    if glo * ghi > 0:
        raise ValueError("f does not change sign between lo and hi")
    while hi - lo >= tol:
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        gm = g(mid)
        if (gm < 0) == (glo < 0):
            lo, glo = mid, gm
        else:
            hi, ghi = mid, gm
    return (lo + hi) / 2

Common misconception

"Use the cheapest model that scores well enough, and the bill will fall." The model's price is the lever that is easiest to pull and often the smallest. In the pipeline above, a fourfold cut to the model bill saves 0.03 dollars a task against a reviewer who costs 1.12, and the sensitivity sweep below ranks the model price last of ten inputs, with a swing of 15,000 dollars on a project worth 2.6 million. The assumption with the largest swing, realisation, is not on any price list at all. Optimise the biggest line of the cost first, and measure the assumption that decides the case before polishing the ones that do not.

Systems connection

Percentiles of a mixture, the doubling effect of retries and the way a 6% slow path owns the p95 are the tail-latency story of tail latency at scale, applied to model calls. Fleets bought whole and utilisation as the lever are capacity planning (load testing and capacity planning, and the estimation drills of Lab 1 in System Design). Cost per accepted task is cost per unit of good work, the same idea as a goodput in a retrying system. The decision between caching, routing and batching also appears as a design question in Designing with ML & LLMs. Chapters 7 and 14 give the reasoning; this lab is the code.

What you learned

  • Price a call from tokens and per-million prices; caching saves only the cacheable prefix, and a cache that is rarely hit costs more than none.
  • Price a task, not a call: sum the calls, mix the routed tiers, add retries, discount what can wait, then add the human who checks the output. With a reviewer in the loop the model bill can be a few per cent of the task.
  • Latency is a distribution. A retry share above 5% sets the p95 at the doubled path; compute percentiles of the mixture and fix the tail at its cause.
  • Choose with gates first (the interval's low end against the quality bar, the p95 against the target), then by cost per accepted task. Which design wins depends on whether a person checks the output.
  • Fixed versus variable cost crosses at a volume you can compute; fleets come in whole steps, and the quality gate must be passed before the cost question counts.
  • Present a case with a range and a sensitivity sweep. Find the assumption that decides it, and the value at which the answer flips, before arguing about the others.

Check your understanding

Caching is turned on for a pipeline whose prompts are 7,500 input tokens, of which a 1,500-token prefix is identical every time. The provider's cache read price is 10% of the input price. What saving on the input bill is the best estimate, at a 90% hit rate?

  1. About 90%, because 90% of requests hit the cache
  2. About 50%, because half of the prefix is read cheaply
  3. About 16%: only the 20% of tokens that are the static prefix can be cached, and the misses pay a write premium
  4. None, because caching never helps with long documents
The prefix is 1,500 of 7,500 tokens, so even free reads could save at most 20% of the input; at a 90% hit rate with a 1.25 write premium on the misses the prefix costs 0.215 of its plain price, a saving of about 16% of input. The headline discount applies to the cacheable part only. Caching does help here, just modestly, and it helps most when the repeated prefix is most of the prompt.

A pipeline retries every call in 6% of tasks, and the p95 latency target is met at a 5% retry rate. Which change is most likely to cut the p95 at no cost to quality?

  1. Fix the cause of format failures so retries fall below 5%, for example with a stricter output schema
  2. Switch to a model that generates tokens 10% faster
  3. Report the median instead, which already meets the target
  4. Add a second retry so that failures are less likely to remain
With 6% of tasks taking double the time, the 95th percentile falls in that slow group; below 5% it falls in the normal group and drops by half. A 10% faster model only takes 10% off the doubled path. Quoting the median hides the tail the target was written to control, and an extra retry adds a still slower group.

Design X has a model bill of 0.020 dollars a task and 87% accepted outputs. Design Y costs 0.038 and has 92% accepted. Review costs 1.12 a task in both. Which is cheaper per accepted task, and what does it show?

  1. X, because its model bill is nearly half of Y's
  2. They are equal to within a cent, so choose on convenience
  3. X, because review cost is the same, so only the model bill differs
  4. Y, at about 1.26 against 1.31: the extra accepted outputs are worth more than the saving on tokens, because the reviewer is the larger cost
X: (0.020 + 1.12) / 0.87 = 1.31. Y: (0.038 + 1.12) / 0.92 = 1.26. The review cost is paid on every task whether or not the output is accepted, so a lower accepted share raises the cost of each accepted one. Comparing model bills alone ignores that. The difference is five cents, not under a cent.

Self-hosting costs 5,150 dollars a month for one fleet that serves up to 12.6 million requests; the API costs 0.005 dollars a request. At 14 million requests a month, what should the model say?

  1. Self-hosting at 5,150, since the fixed cost does not change with volume
  2. Self-hosting at 10,300, because a second fleet is needed; the API would cost 70,000
  3. The API, because 14 million is above the single-fleet capacity
  4. It cannot say: capacity is a hard limit
Fleets come in whole units, so 14 million needs ceil(14 / 12.6) = 2 fleets at 10,300 dollars. The API at 14 million requests costs 70,000. Fixed costs are fixed only within a step of capacity, and being over capacity means buying another step, not abandoning the option.

A sensitivity sweep ranks the model's price tenth of ten inputs, with a swing of 15,000 dollars on an NPV of 2.6 million, and "share of freed time that becomes money" first, with a swing of 3.8 million. The customer asks you to spend the next week on cheaper prompts. What is the best response?

  1. Agree: every saving counts and prompts are easy to change
  2. Refuse: price never matters
  3. Spend the week finding out how much freed time the customer's finance team will actually count, because that assumption decides the case, and note that prompt cost can wait
  4. Cut the quality bar so a cheaper model can be used
The sweep says where the uncertainty that matters lives. Price still matters in a fully automated, high-volume system (the reviewer-free run of the lab flips the winner), but in this project it moves the NPV by 0.6%. Lowering the quality bar to save tokens trades a small saving for risk to the case itself.

The same project, with 3 minutes of manual work per task instead of 11, shows a chance of loss near 99%. What is the right reading?

  1. The model is not accurate enough for short tasks
  2. The project depends on the work being long enough for the freed minutes to outweigh the fixed build and running costs, which is a fact about the task and not a defect of any design
  3. The discount rate is too high
  4. The simulation needs more draws
On a 3-minute task the reviewer's 1.4 expected minutes cut most of the freed time, and the fixed costs (a 715,000 dollar build and 171,000 a year) are spread over the same volume.
