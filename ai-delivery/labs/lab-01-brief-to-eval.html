Lab 1: From Brief to Evaluation Kit

The requirements chapter said a vague goal becomes a number both sides sign, and the evaluation-plans chapter said that number must come from the customer's own cases, with an interval around it and a rule written before the first run. In this lab you build that kit in code: you clean a pool of customer documents, draw a stratified gold set, seal a test half, measure how well the labellers agree, score three systems on the metrics the customer chose, turn the scores into accept, reject or "not enough evidence", and audit a kit for the mistakes that make a number worthless.

Why this lab matters

The first week of an applied AI project ends with a sponsor asking "so, how good is it?", and the engineer who answers well is rarely the one with the cleverest model. It is the one who can say: "On 84 sealed cases drawn from your own claims, it is right 88% of the time, and given only 84 cases the true figure could be anywhere from 79% to 93%, so against the 80% bar we agreed the honest verdict is not yet, and here is how many more cases would settle it." Every sentence of that answer is a function in this lab: an interval, a sealed split, a measured agreement between labellers, a critical-error count, a three-outcome rule. None is hard once it exists. Each one is easy to get subtly wrong (a leaked near-duplicate, a point estimate compared with a bar, a slice of 6 cases reported as if it meant something), and a customer's risk team will find the mistake before you do. Build the kit once, with tests that catch those mistakes, and you can carry it into every project and into the take-home studios.

Deterministic, offline and fast: the data are fixed files

The 304 documents, the labels and the predictions of three systems are fixed files (pool.jsonl, predictions.json), generated once with a seeded script and shipped with the lab; they are synthetic, and nothing in them is a real person's claim. No model is called, nothing touches the network, and no clock is read. Random draws inside your functions come from a random.Random(seed) whose use the docstrings fix exactly, so two people get the same gold set. The random cases inside check.py are seeded too, and a failure prints its seed so you can replay it: python3 check.py solution 1 17 runs milestone 1 with seed 17. The whole check takes about a second. Python 3.9 or newer (the lab uses math.comb), standard library only. An optional last section shows how to score a real model instead of the stored predictions.

The brief

A regional insurer sends you this, and you have a week before the kick-off meeting. It is invented for the lab, but every part of it is the kind of thing a real brief says.

"Our first-line handlers read a first notice of loss (an email, a scanned form, a phone note or an adjuster's report) and decide: covered, not covered, or refer to a specialist. We want an assistant that proposes the decision so the handler only has to confirm it. A claim we wrongly approve costs us the most, and we cannot get that money back. We would like to pilot if it is no worse than our handlers. We have about 300 notices from the last six months that two handlers labelled independently, and our senior panel settled their disagreements."

Read it as the requirements chapter taught you. "Proposes the decision" is a classification with three labels. "Wrongly approve" names the critical error: the system says covered when the senior panel says not_covered or refer. "No worse than our handlers" is a non-inferiority claim against a baseline that must be measured, not assumed. "About 300 notices" is the pool you will build the gold set from, and the sentence about two handlers is a gift: it means label agreement can be measured. What the brief does not say is how good is good enough, how many cases are needed to know, or what happens when the answer is "cannot tell". You write the plan that does.

That plan is the SPEC dictionary at the top of the starter file. Every number in it is an illustrative assumption standing in for a customer's signed figures:

What you'll build

Six milestones, each adding a group of functions:

  1. How far to trust a number: the Wilson interval for a count of right answers, and the number of cases a given precision needs.
  2. Clean the pool, sample by stratum: Jaccard similarity of word shingles, greedy near-duplicate removal, and a quota sample that anyone can reproduce.
  3. Seal, agree, adjudicate: a stratified dev and test split, Cohen's kappa between the two labellers, and final labels with a log of every disagreement.
  4. The metrics the customer agreed to: a confusion matrix, accuracy, the critical-error rate, expected cost per case, and accuracy per slice with an interval.
  5. Verdicts on intervals: accept, reject or inconclusive; a paired comparison with the current process; non-inferiority.
  6. Audit the kit and decide: catch a leak, a tiny slice, weak agreement and a point-estimate rule, then run the signed plan on the sealed half.

Milestone 1: how far to trust a number

Every figure in the kit is a count of right answers out of some number of cases, and a count out of 84 is a sample, not the truth. If the model were run on a different 84 notices it would score a little differently. The confidence interval is the range of true accuracies that are plausible given what you saw: if you repeated the whole measurement on fresh random cases many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true accuracy. The evaluation-plans chapter explained why the interval, and not the point estimate, is what a verdict should rest on; the statistics-for-evals chapter derives it. Here you write it.

Use the Wilson score interval (E. B. Wilson, 1927). With k right out of n, p = k/n and z = 1.96:

centre = (p + z2/2n) / (1 + z2/n),   half-width = z √(p(1 − p)/n + z2/4n2) / (1 + z2/n)

The textbook alternative, p ± z√(p(1 − p)/n), has a fatal habit: with zero errors it reports a width of zero, as if no errors in 10 cases proved the system never fails. Wilson does not. With 0 critical errors in 10 cases its upper end is 27.8%, which is the honest reading: you have not yet seen enough to say the rate is below one in four. It also never leaves the range 0 to 1. For the slice from the chapter, 14 right out of 16, it gives 64% to 97%.

The second function runs the formula backwards. Before collecting any cases you can ask how many you need for an interval of half-width h around an accuracy near p: n = z2 p(1 − p) / h2, rounded up. Planning at p = 0.9 (a system you hope is good), ± 3 points needs 385 cases and ± 2 points needs 865. Precision is expensive because the width shrinks with the square root of the count: four times the cases for half the width. Our signed plan promised ± 8 points, which needs 55 cases, and the sealed half will have 84.

Your task

Write wilson(k, n, z=1.96), returning (lo, hi) (and (0.0, 1.0) for n == 0, because with no cases you know nothing), and cases_needed(half_width, p=0.9, z=1.96), returning an int.

Hints try without first

  • Do not use p +/- z*sqrt(p(1-p)/n). It is wrong at 0 and at n, and the check tests exactly those edges.
  • cases_needed is a single line with math.ceil. At 0.08 the raw value is 54.02, so the answer is 55: always round the sample size up.
Solution spoiler 
def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    """Wilson score interval (lo, hi) for k successes in n trials.

    wilson(14, 16) -> (0.640..., 0.965...);  n == 0 -> (0.0, 1.0).
    """
    if n == 0:
        return 0.0, 1.0
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half)

def cases_needed(half_width, p=0.9, z=1.96):
    """Cases needed for a 95% interval of +/- half_width around an accuracy near p.

    ceil(z^2 p (1-p) / half_width^2):  cases_needed(0.03) -> 385.
    """
    return math.ceil(z * z * p * (1 - p) / (half_width * half_width))

Milestone 2: clean the pool and sample by stratum

A pool of customer documents is not yet a gold set. Two things must happen first, and both are about the future reader's trust in the number.

Remove near-duplicates. Customers resend emails ("Re: " in front, "sent again in case the first copy was lost" at the end) and scanners rescan forms. If a document and its twin land on opposite sides of the dev and test split, the model (or the prompt you tuned on dev) has effectively seen the test case, and the score is inflated by a leak. The cure is to compare documents by what they say and keep one of each cluster. A fast way to compare is the set of shingles: overlapping runs of k consecutive words (here 3). The Jaccard similarity of two sets is the size of their intersection over the size of their union, 0 for nothing in common and 1 for identical. Take "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog today" (10 words, 8 shingles) and the same sentence with " again" added (9 shingles, 8 of them shared): the Jaccard similarity is 8/9, about 0.89. Two different claims written from the same template share the template's shingles but not the details, and in this pool the most similar pair of different claims reaches only 0.59, while every planted resend is above 0.82, so a threshold of 0.70 separates them cleanly. (Real data is messier; you choose the threshold by reading pairs near it.) The rule you implement is greedy and order-independent: walk the documents in id order, and keep one only if its similarity to every document already kept is below the threshold.

Sample by stratum, not at random. A simple random sample of 290 documents would be 40% email and contain only a handful of scanned forms from the awkward "worry" list. A stratum is a group you want represented on purpose. Here stratum_of (given) names each document by its type and whether the experts flagged it, such as scanned_form/worry, and QUOTAS says how many to draw from each: more than the natural share of the worry cases, because those are rare in a random sample and decisive for trust (the chapter's advice to oversample the cases that worry the experts). A quota larger than the stratum takes the whole stratum, which is a shortfall worth knowing about: the pool holds only 10 scanned worry cases, and the quota asks for 10.

The draw must be reproducible, because a customer will ask "which 168 cases are in the gold set?" and expect the same answer next month. So the docstring pins the procedure exactly: strata in sorted name order, members sorted by id, one shared random.Random(seed), rng.sample for each. Two engineers following it get identical sets, whatever order the pool arrives in.

Your task

Write jaccard(a, b) (two empty sets count as identical, 1.0), dedupe(docs, threshold) (greedy, by id, returns the kept documents sorted by id, leaves the input untouched) and stratified_sample(docs, quotas, seed) (the exact draw above, sorted by id; strata without a quota are left out).

def jaccard(a, b):
    """|a & b| / |a | b| for two sets; two empty sets count as identical (1.0)."""
    if not a and not b:
        return 1.0
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b)

def dedupe(docs, threshold=0.70):
    """Drop near-duplicates. Walk the documents in id order and keep one only if its
    shingle Jaccard with EVERY document already kept is below `threshold`.
    Returns the kept documents sorted by id; the input list is not modified."""
    kept, kept_sh = [], []
    for d in sorted(docs, key=lambda d: d["id"]):
        s = shingles(d["text"])
        if all(jaccard(s, t) < threshold for t in kept_sh):
            kept.append(d)
            kept_sh.append(s)
    return kept

def stratified_sample(docs, quotas, seed):
    """Sample `quotas[stratum]` documents from each stratum (see stratum_of).

    Determinism contract (so two people get the same set): work through strata in sorted
    name order; inside a stratum sort the members by id, then draw
    random.Random(seed).sample(members, k) with ONE generator shared across strata.
    A quota larger than the stratum takes the whole stratum. Documents in strata with
    no quota are left out. Returns the sample sorted by id."""
    by = {}
    for d in docs:
        by.setdefault(stratum_of(d), []).append(d)
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    out = []
    for name in sorted(quotas):
        members = sorted(by.get(name, []), key=lambda d: d["id"])
        out += rng.sample(members, min(quotas[name], len(members)))
    return sorted(out, key=lambda d: d["id"])

Milestone 3: seal a test half, measure agreement, adjudicate

Seal the test half. The gold set is split into a development half, which you may look at, tune prompts on and argue about, and a sealed test half, which nobody consults until the final run. The split is stratified so that both halves keep the mix: every stratum contributes about test_fraction of its members to test. The rule for the count is int(n * f + 0.5) (round half up, not Python's banker's rounding), forced into 1 .. n − 1 for any stratum with two or more members so that neither half is empty, and a stratum of one stays in dev. The shuffle uses one shared random.Random(seed) over id-sorted members, again so the split can be rebuilt. In a real project the person who holds the sealed half is often not the person tuning the system, and the plan fixes the seed before anyone sees a score: a team that tries five seeds and reports the friendliest has run five tests, and the "Try it yourself" section below shows how far five seeds spread.

Measure agreement. Two handlers labelled each notice independently. If they agree on 80% of the cases that sounds good, but with three labels and a majority of covered, two people guessing in proportion to the label frequencies would agree quite often by chance alone. Cohen's kappa (Jacob Cohen, 1960) corrects for that: kappa = (po − pe) / (1 − pe), where po is the observed agreement and pe the agreement expected if each labeller kept their own label frequencies but chose independently. A tiny worked case: labeller A says x, x, y, y and B says x, y, y, y. They agree on 3 of 4, so po = 0.75. A uses x half the time and B a quarter, so pe = 0.5 × 0.25 + 0.5 × 0.75 = 0.5, and kappa = (0.75 − 0.5) / (1 − 0.5) = 0.5. Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for no better than chance. The conventional reading (Landis and Koch, 1977) calls 0.61 to 0.80 "substantial" agreement, which is where the plan's floor of 0.60 comes from; a number below it means the labelling instructions are unclear, and the human-labels chapter covers how to fix them. Agreement also sets a ceiling: if the experts themselves disagree on one case in five, no system can be scored more precisely than the labels allow.

Adjudicate. Where the two handlers differ, the senior panel decides, seeing both answers, and the decision is logged. The final label is the agreed label when the handlers agree and the adjudicator's label when they differ. The log (who disagreed, what they said, what was decided) is what answers a later challenge, and what makes a correction after the fact visible and not silent.

def split_dev_test(items, test_fraction, seed):
    """Split into (dev, test), stratified, so that both halves keep the mix.

    For each stratum (sorted by name): sort members by id, shuffle a copy with the shared
    random.Random(seed), and give the first n_test to the test half, where
    n_test = int(len(members) * test_fraction + 0.5), forced into 1 .. len-1 when the
    stratum has 2 or more members (a stratum of 1 goes to dev). Both lists sorted by id."""
    by = {}
    for d in items:
        by.setdefault(stratum_of(d), []).append(d)
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    dev, test = [], []
    for name in sorted(by):
        members = sorted(by[name], key=lambda d: d["id"])
        rng.shuffle(members)
        n_test = int(len(members) * test_fraction + 0.5)
        if len(members) >= 2:
            n_test = min(max(n_test, 1), len(members) - 1)
        else:
            n_test = 0
        test += members[:n_test]
        dev += members[n_test:]
    return sorted(dev, key=lambda d: d["id"]), sorted(test, key=lambda d: d["id"])

def cohens_kappa(a, b):
    """Cohen's kappa for two equal-length label lists: (po - pe) / (1 - pe), where po is the
    observed agreement and pe the agreement expected if each labeller kept their own label
    frequencies but chose independently. If pe == 1 (both always say the same single label)
    return 1.0. Raise ValueError for empty or unequal lists."""
    if not a or len(a) != len(b):
        raise ValueError("kappa needs two equal-length, non-empty label lists")
    n = len(a)
    po = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    pe = sum((a.count(l) / n) * (b.count(l) / n) for l in set(a) | set(b))
    if pe == 1:
        return 1.0
    return (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

def adjudicate(rows):
    """Final labels from two independent labellers plus an adjudicator.

    Each row has label_a, label_b and label_adj. If a == b the final label is that label.
    If they differ, the final label is label_adj; a disagreement with label_adj None raises
    ValueError('<id> needs adjudication'). Returns (final, log): final maps id -> label, and
    log lists one dict {id, a, b, final} per disagreement, in input order."""
    final, log = {}, []
    for r in rows:
        if r["label_a"] == r["label_b"]:
            final[r["id"]] = r["label_a"]
        else:
            if r.get("label_adj") is None:
                raise ValueError(f"{r['id']} needs adjudication")
            final[r["id"]] = r["label_adj"]
            log.append({"id": r["id"], "a": r["label_a"], "b": r["label_b"], "final": r["label_adj"]})
    return final, log

Milestone 4: the metrics the customer agreed to

Accuracy treats every error alike, and the brief says they are not alike. Three functions give the customer's own view of the same predictions.

A confusion matrix counts decisions by (gold label, system decision), so you can see which mistakes are made: rows are what the panel decided, columns what the system said. Accuracy is the diagonal over the total. The critical error is a wrongly approved claim: the panel said not_covered or refer and the system said covered. It gets its own rate and its own bar because one wrongly paid claim is a serious incident, not a rounding error. And the expected cost per case weights each cell of the matrix by the customer's estimate of what that mistake costs, from the COST table (denying a valid claim 350 euros of complaint handling, wrongly paying a non-covered claim 1,800, skipping a case that needed a specialist 1,800 if approved and 600 if denied; all illustrative). The chapter built exactly this table for a model with 94.5% accuracy that was more expensive per claim than the handlers at 91.0%. Your score must reproduce that chapter's 52.10 euros from its published matrix, which the check does.

The last function answers "does it work for scanned forms?". slice_report groups the sealed documents by a key such as doc_type and gives each group its own accuracy and Wilson interval, plus a flag: a slice is reportable only if it has at least min_n cases. A slice of 6 cases at 100% has an interval from 61% to 100%. Reporting "100% on phone notes" from it would be a claim the data cannot support, and the plan says so in advance by fixing min_slice_n.

def confusion(gold, pred):
    """Nested counts m[gold_label][pred_label] over parallel lists, every cell present (0 if unseen),
    for the three LABELS."""
    m = {g: {p: 0 for p in LABELS} for g in LABELS}
    for g, p in zip(gold, pred):
        m[g][p] += 1
    return m

def score(gold, pred, costs=COST):
    """Score parallel lists of decisions against gold labels.

    Returns {n, correct, accuracy, critical, critical_rate, cost_per_case}.
    A critical error is a wrongly approved claim: gold 'not_covered' or 'refer' but the
    system said 'covered'. critical_rate is critical / n. cost_per_case is the mean of
    costs[gold][pred]. An empty list raises ValueError."""
    if not gold or len(gold) != len(pred):
        raise ValueError("score needs two equal-length, non-empty lists")
    n = len(gold)
    correct = sum(g == p for g, p in zip(gold, pred))
    critical = sum(g != "covered" and p == "covered" for g, p in zip(gold, pred))
    cost = sum(costs[g][p] for g, p in zip(gold, pred))
    return {"n": n, "correct": correct, "accuracy": correct / n, "critical": critical,
            "critical_rate": critical / n, "cost_per_case": cost / n}

def slice_report(docs, gold, pred, key, min_n):
    """Accuracy per slice. `docs` are documents, `gold` and `pred` map id -> decision.

    Returns {slice value: {n, correct, accuracy, lo, hi, reportable}} where lo, hi is the 95%
    Wilson interval and reportable is n >= min_n. The slice value is doc[key]."""
    groups = {}
    for d in docs:
        groups.setdefault(d[key], []).append(d["id"])
    out = {}
    for name, ids in groups.items():
        n = len(ids)
        k = sum(gold[i] == pred[i] for i in ids)
        lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
        out[name] = {"n": n, "correct": k, "accuracy": k / n, "lo": lo, "hi": hi, "reportable": n >= min_n}
    return out

Milestone 5: from numbers to a verdict

A pass mark applied to a point estimate makes the verdict depend on luck: the same system can land at 84% on one set of 84 cases and 88% on another (the experiment below shows it does). The rule in the plan is applied to the interval, and it has three outcomes, not two. For a bar to reach, such as accuracy: accept if the interval's low end reaches the bar (even on a pessimistic reading the system is good enough), reject if its high end falls below the bar (even on an optimistic reading it is not), otherwise inconclusive. For a limit not to exceed, such as the wrongly-approved rate, the mirror image: accept if the high end is at or under the limit, reject if the low end is over it, otherwise inconclusive. "Inconclusive" is a legitimate result and the plan says what follows from it: collect more cases, or narrow the claim. It is not a polite word for "fail", and it is not a licence to read the point estimate instead.

The second question, "is it better than what we do today?", is answered on the same items. Run both systems on every sealed case and sort the cases into four cells: both right, only A right, only B right, neither. Only the two "only" cells say which system is better; the cases where both agree add nothing to the comparison. With b cases where only A is right and c where only B is, out of n, the accuracy gap is (b − c)/n and its standard error is √(b + c − (b − c)2/n) / n (the variance of a paired difference of proportions, a form of McNemar's test). The interval is the gap ± z standard errors. Take the chapter's example, 200 cases, the model right alone on b = 15 and the handlers on c = 8: the gap is +3.5 points, the standard error is √(23 − 49/200)/200 = 0.0239 and the interval is −1.2 to +8.2. It includes zero, so the data do not show the model is better. The sign test says the same from the disagreements alone: if the systems were equally good, each of the 23 disagreements would favour either side with probability 1/2, and getting 15 or more on one side (or 8 or fewer on the other) has two-sided probability 2 × P(X ≥ 15), X ~ Binomial(23, 1/2), which is 0.21.

Finally, non-inferiority: the customer asked for "no worse than our handlers", not "better". Fix a margin (5 points in this plan) and ask whether the model is worse than the baseline by more than the margin. Accept if the paired interval's low end is above −margin, reject if its high end is below −margin (clearly worse by more than the margin), otherwise inconclusive. A system whose appeal is speed or cost can be worth deploying on that evidence even when it is not proven better. The example above passes a 3-point margin (low end −1.2) and not a 1-point margin.

Your task

def verdict(k, n, threshold, direction="min", z=1.96):
    """Three-outcome rule on the Wilson interval of k/n.

    direction 'min' (a bar to reach, such as accuracy): 'accept' if lo >= threshold,
    'reject' if hi < threshold, otherwise 'inconclusive'.
    direction 'max' (a limit not to exceed, such as the critical-error rate): 'accept' if
    hi <= threshold, 'reject' if lo > threshold, otherwise 'inconclusive'."""
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n, z)
    if direction == "min":
        return "accept" if lo >= threshold else "reject" if hi < threshold else "inconclusive"
    if direction == "max":
        return "accept" if hi <= threshold else "reject" if lo > threshold else "inconclusive"
    raise ValueError("direction must be 'min' or 'max'")

def paired_compare(a_ok, b_ok, z=1.96):
    """Compare system A with system B on the SAME items. a_ok and b_ok are parallel lists of
    booleans (was each system right on that item).

    Returns {n, both, a_only, b_only, neither, diff, lo, hi, p_sign}:
      diff = (a_only - b_only) / n, the accuracy gap A - B;
      (lo, hi) = diff -/+ z * se, with se = sqrt(a_only + b_only - (a_only - b_only)^2 / n) / n;
      p_sign = exact two-sided sign test on the disagreements (1.0 if there are none):
               min(1, 2 * P(X >= max(a_only, b_only))) for X ~ Binomial(a_only + b_only, 1/2)."""
    n = len(a_ok)
    both = sum(a and b for a, b in zip(a_ok, b_ok))
    a_only = sum(a and not b for a, b in zip(a_ok, b_ok))
    b_only = sum(b and not a for a, b in zip(a_ok, b_ok))
    neither = n - both - a_only - b_only
    diff = (a_only - b_only) / n
    se = math.sqrt(max(0.0, a_only + b_only - (a_only - b_only) ** 2 / n)) / n
    d = a_only + b_only
    if d == 0:
        p = 1.0
    else:
        k = max(a_only, b_only)
        p = min(1.0, 2 * sum(math.comb(d, i) for i in range(k, d + 1)) / 2 ** d)
    return {"n": n, "both": both, "a_only": a_only, "b_only": b_only, "neither": neither,
            "diff": diff, "lo": diff - z * se, "hi": diff + z * se, "p_sign": p}

def non_inferior(cmp, margin):
    """'accept' if the paired interval's low end is above -margin (no worse than the baseline by
    more than the margin), 'reject' if its high end is below -margin (clearly worse by more than the
    margin), otherwise 'inconclusive'. `cmp` is the dict from paired_compare."""
    if cmp["lo"] > -margin:
        return "accept"
    if cmp["hi"] < -margin:
        return "reject"
    return "inconclusive"

Milestone 6: audit the kit, then decide

A kit can look complete and still be unfit to settle anything. Before the sealed half is used, a second person (or a function) should run through the list of ways a gold set quietly goes wrong. audit_kit turns that list into code, and returns a sorted list of problem codes, empty if the kit passes:

CodeWhat went wrongWhy it matters
overlapThe same id is in dev and test.The "sealed" half is not sealed.
leakA dev and a test document have similarity at or above the dedupe threshold.A resent email on both sides inflates the score.
low_kappaLabeller agreement is under min_kappa.The labels are too noisy to score against.
underpoweredThe test half is smaller than cases_needed(half_width).The plan promised a precision the data cannot deliver.
small_slice:XSlice X has fewer than min_slice_n test cases.Reporting it separately would mislead.
point_ruleThe spec accepts on the point estimate.Luck decides the verdict.
no_baseline, no_criticalThe plan has no comparison with today's process, or no limit on the expensive error.Two questions the sponsor will ask are unanswerable.

The check plants each flaw into a copy of the real kit (a re-sent twin of a test document appended to dev, a kappa of 0.41, a plan promising ± 2 points, and so on) and expects exactly the right codes back, including all three of "leak", "low_kappa" and "point_rule" at once.

Then decide runs the signed plan on the sealed items. It computes three gates: accuracy (verdict on the correct count against accuracy_bar, direction "min"), critical (verdict on the wrongly-approved count against critical_limit, direction "max") and baseline (non_inferior on the paired comparison of the model against the baseline, with margin). The overall result is "reject" if any gate rejects, "accept" only if all three accept, and "inconclusive" otherwise. The asymmetry is deliberate: acceptance needs every gate, and one clear failure is enough to stop.

def audit_kit(kit):
    """Return a sorted list of problem codes found in a kit (an empty list means it passes).

    kit has dev and test (lists of documents), kappa and spec (like SPEC). Codes:
      'overlap'        a document id appears in both dev and test
      'leak'           a dev and a test document have shingle Jaccard >= spec['dedupe_threshold']
      'low_kappa'      kappa < spec['min_kappa']
      'underpowered'   len(test) < cases_needed(spec['half_width'])
      'small_slice:X'  the test half has fewer than spec['min_slice_n'] documents with
                       doc[spec['slice_key']] == X   (one code per such slice X)
      'point_rule'     spec['rule'] is not 'interval'
      'no_baseline'    spec has no 'baseline'
      'no_critical'    spec has no 'critical_limit'"""
    spec, dev, test = kit["spec"], kit["dev"], kit["test"]
    problems = []
    dev_ids = {d["id"] for d in dev}
    if any(d["id"] in dev_ids for d in test):
        problems.append("overlap")
    dev_sh = [shingles(d["text"]) for d in dev]
    thr = spec.get("dedupe_threshold", 0.70)
    if any(jaccard(shingles(t["text"]), s) >= thr for t in test for s in dev_sh):
        problems.append("leak")
    if kit["kappa"] < spec["min_kappa"]:
        problems.append("low_kappa")
    if len(test) < cases_needed(spec["half_width"]):
        problems.append("underpowered")
    counts = {}
    for d in test:
        counts[d[spec["slice_key"]]] = counts.get(d[spec["slice_key"]], 0) + 1
    problems += [f"small_slice:{name}" for name, c in counts.items() if c < spec["min_slice_n"]]
    if spec.get("rule") != "interval":
        problems.append("point_rule")
    if not spec.get("baseline"):
        problems.append("no_baseline")
    if "critical_limit" not in spec:
        problems.append("no_critical")
    return sorted(problems)

def decide(ids, gold, model, baseline, spec):
    """Run the signed plan on the sealed test items `ids`.

    gold, model and baseline map id -> decision. Returns {gates, overall, model, baseline, compare}:
      gates['accuracy']  verdict(correct, n, spec['accuracy_bar'], 'min')
      gates['critical']  verdict(critical, n, spec['critical_limit'], 'max')
      gates['baseline']  non_inferior(paired_compare(model right, baseline right), spec['margin'])
      overall            'reject' if any gate rejects, else 'accept' if all accept, else 'inconclusive'
      model, baseline    the score() dicts;   compare   the paired_compare dict."""
    g = [gold[i] for i in ids]
    m = score(g, [model[i] for i in ids])
    b = score(g, [baseline[i] for i in ids])
    cmp = paired_compare([model[i] == gold[i] for i in ids], [baseline[i] == gold[i] for i in ids])
    gates = {
        "accuracy": verdict(m["correct"], m["n"], spec["accuracy_bar"], "min"),
        "critical": verdict(m["critical"], m["n"], spec["critical_limit"], "max"),
        "baseline": non_inferior(cmp, spec["margin"]),
    }
    if "reject" in gates.values():
        overall = "reject"
    elif all(v == "accept" for v in gates.values()):
        overall = "accept"
    else:
        overall = "inconclusive"
    return {"gates": gates, "overall": overall, "model": m, "baseline": b, "compare": cmp}

Common misconception

"The model got 88% and the bar was 80%, so it passed." The 88% is one draw from the sampling distribution: a different, equally fair set of 84 sealed cases gives anything from 82% to 87% for this very model (see the experiment below), and the question the customer actually asked is about the true rate. A rule that compares the point estimate with the bar rewards the team that happens to get a lucky split, and punishes the team with an unlucky one. Compare the interval with the bar, say "inconclusive" when it straddles, and write down in the plan what you will do then.

Run it against a real model

Optional, and not used by check.py. The kit scores any dictionary from id to decision, so a real model drops in where load_predictions()["model"] was. The listing below uses the shared llm.py; set LAB_MODEL to anthropic:<model-id>, openai:<model-id> (with OPENAI_BASE_URL for any compatible server) or ollama:<name>. from llm import get_model, text_of
from solution import LABELS, SPEC, build_kit, decide, load_predictions

model = get_model()                       # reads LAB_MODEL; raises if it is unset
SYSTEM = ("You triage insurance first notices of loss. Reply with exactly one word: "
          "covered, not_covered or refer. Use refer if the facts are inconsistent, "
          "a third party is injured, or the claim is unusual.")

def propose(doc):
    reply = model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": doc["text"]}], system=SYSTEM, temperature=0)
    word = text_of(reply).strip().lower()
    return word if word in LABELS else "refer"       # anything unparseable goes to a human

kit = build_kit()
ids = [d["id"] for d in kit["test"]]                  # 84 calls, once, on the sealed half

Going further

  • Bootstrap the paired interval. Replace the closed-form interval in paired_compare with a paired bootstrap that resamples whole items (as in the chapter's script) and compare the two on the real kit. They agree to about a point; find a small-sample case where they do not.
  • Cohen's kappa against the adjudicated label. Compute each handler's kappa against the final label, and the current process's too. A baseline that is scored against labels it helped produce looks better than it is; how large is the effect in this pool?
  • A sequential plan. Instead of one sealed half of 84, stage the evaluation: 40 cases, then 40 more only if the verdict is inconclusive. What does stopping early do to the error rate of the final verdict, and how would you guard against it?
  • Cost-based acceptance. Add a fourth gate on expected cost per case with a bootstrap interval, and decide whether it should be an accept condition or a report line. The answer depends on who bears the costs in COST.
  • Rubrics for generated text. The same kit works for free-text outputs once each item has yes-or-no rubric criteria and a judge whose agreement with humans you have measured, as in Calibrate an LLM Judge.

Systems connection

The sealed test half is the evaluation analogue of a held-out environment: it only means something if nobody can tune against it, which is the same discipline as keeping production credentials out of development. Near-duplicate removal is the deduplication problem of any data pipeline, and shingling with Jaccard similarity is the idea behind the MinHash technique used at web scale (see specialised stores for how search systems handle near matches). A log of every label change, with who and why, is an audit trail of the kind detection and security incidents relies on. The three-outcome rule on an interval is the statistical core of a canary analysis (compare Automated Canary Analysis), and the paired comparison is the same trick as comparing two builds on the same requests. Chapters 2 and 6 give the reasoning; this lab is the code.

What you learned

  • Every metric is a count out of a sample. Report the Wilson interval, plan the sample size from the precision you promised, and treat a slice of 15 cases as a hint, not a result.
  • A gold set is built, not collected: remove near-duplicates before splitting, sample by stratum with quotas, make the draw reproducible, and seal a test half with a seed fixed in advance.
  • Measure label agreement with a chance-corrected statistic, adjudicate disagreements with a log, and remember agreement is lowest on exactly the cases that worry the experts.
  • Choose the metrics with the customer: accuracy, a separate critical-error rate with its own limit, and expected cost. Report them side by side.
  • Make verdicts on intervals with three outcomes. "Inconclusive" is a result, comes with a number of cases that would settle it, and is never replaced by reading the point estimate.
  • Compare with today's process on the same items, in pairs, against a non-inferiority margin. Audit the kit for leaks, thin slices and weak agreement before spending the sealed half.

Check your understanding

A gold set of 40 cases shows 0 critical errors for a model. The plan's limit is 5%. What does the Wilson interval say, and what is the right verdict?

  1. The interval is 0% to 0%, so accept: no errors were found
  2. The interval runs from 0% to about 8.8%; its high end is over the 5% limit, so the verdict is inconclusive and more cases are needed
  3. The interval runs from 0% to about 8.8%; the point estimate is under the limit, so accept
  4. The interval is undefined at zero errors, so the test cannot be run
Wilson does not collapse to zero width at zero errors: with 40 cases the upper end is about 8.8%, because zero errors in 40 does not rule out a rate of several per cent. The rule for a limit looks at the high end, which is above 5%, so the honest verdict is inconclusive. The point estimate is exactly what the interval rule refuses to use, and Wilson is defined for 0 and for n; it is the textbook formula that breaks there.

The pool contains an email and the same email resent with "Re:" in front. A random split puts one in dev and one in test. What is the effect and the cure?

  1. No effect: a model has never been trained on this insurer's data, so it cannot remember either copy
  2. The test half becomes harder; the cure is to move both copies to dev
  3. The labels disagree on the two copies; the cure is to adjudicate them
  4. The test score is inflated by a case that was effectively seen during development; the cure is to remove near-duplicates before splitting
The prompt, examples and thresholds you tune on dev are shaped by the dev copy, and the test copy then looks easy. That leak inflates the score without anyone intending it. Removing near-duplicates before the split (and auditing for pairs across the split afterwards) is the cure. Memorisation by the model is not even needed: your own tuning is the channel. Moving both copies to dev would also work for that pair but does not stop the next pair.

Two handlers agree on 85% of cases, but 90% of the cases are covered and both label most things covered. Why report kappa as well as agreement?

  1. Because when one label dominates, two labellers agree often by chance, and kappa removes the agreement chance alone would produce
  2. Because kappa measures whether the labellers are correct, which agreement cannot
  3. Because kappa is always lower than agreement, which keeps the plan conservative
  4. Because kappa tells you which labeller to drop
With a dominant label, two people guessing from the label frequencies would agree on about 81% of cases (0.9 × 0.9 + 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.82), so 85% observed agreement is barely better than chance and kappa is about 0.17. Kappa says nothing about correctness: two labellers can agree and both be wrong, which is why a senior panel adjudicates. It is not conservative by design, and it does not rank labellers.

On the same 200 cases, the new system is right alone on 15 and the current process right alone on 8. A colleague says "3.5 points better, ship it". What does the paired analysis add?

  1. Nothing: the 177 cases where both agree dominate the result
  2. It shows the new system is significantly better, because 15 is almost double 8
  3. It shows the gap's interval (about −1 to +8 points) includes zero and the sign test gives p = 0.21, so "better" is not shown, though it is compatible with "no worse than 3 points"
  4. It shows the new system is worse, because 8 cases are lost
Only the 23 disagreements carry information about which system is better, and 15 against 8 is not unusual for two equally good systems (a coin gives a split that lopsided about one time in five). The paired interval makes it precise and supports a non-inferiority claim, not a superiority claim. The agreeing cases show how easy the set is but do not change the comparison, and 8 lost cases are offset by 15 gained.

Your audit reports small_slice:scanned_form: 9 sealed scanned forms against a floor of 15. The sponsor asks "how does it do on scanned forms?". What is the best answer?

  1. Report the slice's accuracy; any number is better than none
  2. Say the sealed set holds too few scanned forms to report separately, show the wide interval if asked, and propose adding scanned forms to the gold set
  3. Drop scanned forms from the system's scope without telling anyone
  4. Merge scanned forms into another slice so the problem disappears
With 9 cases the interval is so wide (a 100% result still allows around 70%) that any figure would be a claim the data cannot support, which is why the plan fixed a floor in advance. The honest answer names the limit and the cheap fix. Quietly narrowing the scope or merging slices hides the weakness, and the first is a decision the customer should make.

The model passes the accuracy and critical-error gates but the non-inferiority gate against the current process is inconclusive. What should decide return overall, and why?

  1. "accept", because two of three gates accepted
  2. "reject", because one gate failed to accept
  3. "accept", because non-inferiority is only advisory
  4. "inconclusive": acceptance needs every gate to accept, and no gate rejected, so the plan cannot yet say yes or no
The plan was signed with three gates and the rule that all must accept; a majority is not a plan. A gate that is merely inconclusive is not a rejection: nothing has been shown to be wrong. The overall result is therefore inconclusive, with the baseline gate named as the one that needs more evidence. Calling it a rejection would overstate the evidence, and treating a gate as advisory after seeing the result is the move the plan exists to prevent.
