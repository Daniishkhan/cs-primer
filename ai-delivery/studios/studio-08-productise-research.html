Take-Home: Productise a Research Prototype

A university lab has published a paper and a repository that match supplier invoice lines to purchase-order lines. A freight company has read the paper and wants it running next quarter. Between a notebook that worked on the authors' laptop and a service people rely on lie a licence you must read, a result you must reproduce, a threshold that is really a business decision, and a conversation with a customer who expects a different answer.

How a take-home studio works

This page simulates a four to six hour take-home exercise of the kind described in Take-Homes, Portfolios & Interviews. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer shows the artefact an excellent submission would contain, with numbers from scripts that were run. It is one strong answer, not the only right one. The repository, the lab and the customer are invented. Because the repository does not exist, the scripts use small stand-ins for the research model and for the customer's data; what they demonstrate is the method and the shape of the findings, not the performance of any real system. Every price, time and cost-of-error figure is an assumption to replace with the customer's own numbers.

An email arrives from the head of accounts payable at Northgate Freight, a mid-sized logistics company:

"We read the paper from the Vance Lab on LineLink. They say it matches invoice lines to purchase-order lines with 99% accuracy. State of play, October 2026

Work on reproducibility in machine learning gives a vocabulary for this exercise. Pineau et al. (JMLR, 2021) report the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility programme, which paired a code submission policy, a community reproducibility challenge and a checklist, and defines reproducibility as obtaining similar results using the same code and data. Their programme addressed whether results hold with the authors' own resources; it does not answer whether they hold on your data, which is the question in this studio. I read a search summary of the paper, not the full text. Licence terms for models, weights and datasets differ widely and change; the table in stage 2 uses the well-known Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 terms as an example, and your submission must read the actual licence text in each case. Open-weight model licences in particular have their own restrictions that are not always the same as the licence on the code.

Common misconception

"The lab said we are welcome to use it, so we can." An email from a researcher is not a licence, and the researcher may not hold the rights: the university often owns the intellectual property, the data may belong to other parties, and a graduating student cannot grant what the institution holds. A customer who ships a product on weights licensed for research use takes on the legal risk, and so does the engineer who recommended it. Get permission in writing from whoever can give it, or avoid the dependency.

The reproduction

In the real exercise you run the repository on its published test split, then on a sample of Northgate's lines. Here, two small matchers on a generated world stand in for it. The "prototype stand-in" scores a line by how many three-letter chunks it shares with each candidate, which captures how a model trained on tidy data behaves when abbreviations and noise appear. The "baseline" is a plain word-overlap score. Both choose the best candidate on the purchase order, or decline when the score is below a threshold, which is tuned on benchmark-style data exactly as the paper would have done. The generator and the matchers are below.

def tok(s): return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())

def baseline_score(inv, prod):                # plain token overlap, supplier codes ignored: what you write in an hour
    inv_t = {w for w in tok(inv) if not re.fullmatch(r"[a-z]{2}\d{4}", w)}
    prod_t = set(tok(prod))
    return len(inv_t & prod_t) / (len(inv_t | prod_t) or 1)

LEARNED = {}                                  # abbreviation table the engineer learns from a few dozen labelled customer cases
def baseline2_score(inv, prod):
    inv_t = {w for w in tok(inv) if not re.fullmatch(r"[a-z]{2}\d{4}", w)}
    inv_t |= {LEARNED[w] for w in inv_t if w in LEARNED}
    prod_t = set(tok(prod))
    return len(inv_t & prod_t) / (len(inv_t | prod_t) or 1)

def grams(s, n=3):
    s = " " + " ".join(tok(s)) + " "; return Counter(s[i:i+n] for i in range(len(s)-n+1))
def proto_score(inv, prod):                   # stand-in for the repository's model: character 3-gram cosine
    a, b = grams(inv), grams(prod)
    dot = sum(a[g]*b[g] for g in a); na = math.sqrt(sum(v*v for v in a.values())); nb = math.sqrt(sum(v*v for v in b.values()))
    return dot/(na*nb) if na and nb else 0.0

def predict(case, score, thr):
    best = max(case["candidates"], key=lambda p: score(case["invoice"], PRODUCTS[p]))
    return best if score(case["invoice"], PRODUCTS[best]) >= thr else None

def tune(cases, score):
    return max((i/100 for i in range(5, 90, 1)), key=lambda t: sum(predict(c, score, t) == c["truth"] for c in cases))

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k/n; d = 1 + z*z/n; c = (p + z*z/(2*n))/d
    h = z*math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n))/d
    return c-h, c+h

    print(f"{name}: abstain threshold {thr:.2f} tuned on 400 benchmark-style cases")

print()

  • The claim does not transfer. On customer-style lines the same system gets 63.0% (57.4% to 68.3%). The intervals do not overlap: the drop is not luck.

3The gold set and the baselines

Describe how you would build the gold set from Northgate's invoices, what the labelling rule says about "not on the order", how large it must be, and what you try before any new model.

Compare with a worked answer

Follow evaluation plans customers trust: the gold set comes from the customer's own work, labelled by the people who do it, with a written rule.

  1. Source. 600 invoice lines drawn at random from the last three months, stratified by supplier so that the ten biggest suppliers are represented in proportion and the long tail by at least 60 lines in total.
  2. Labelling. Two clerks independently match each line to a purchase-order line or mark it "not on the order". Disagreements go to the senior clerk. Record agreement; if clerks agree on only 90% of lines, no system can honestly score above that.
  3. The rule for "not on the order". A line is "not on the order" if no purchase-order line on that order describes the same product at the same pack size, even if a similar product exists. Substitutions go to a human; the system must not match them.
  4. Split. 300 lines for development (tuning thresholds, building the abbreviation table) and 300 sealed for the final evaluation, touched once. This protects against the leakage described in leakage.

How large? Every system here is judged on 300 sealed lines. At 80% accuracy, 300 lines give an interval about ten points wide; to separate 80% from 85% you would need more, which is why the comparison between systems is made on the same lines (a paired comparison), where the difference is much easier to see. In a real engagement you would grow the set as the project went on.

Baselines before models. The first baseline, plain word overlap, took an hour: 80.7% on customer-style lines. The second learns a small table of the customer's abbreviations from labelled examples. The script below shows how accuracy grows with the number of labelled lines used, and the paired comparison against the prototype.

That is a statement about the method, and it should be phrased that way to the lab.

4Read the failures

For each system, count what goes wrong first in the failing cases on the sealed set. What do the two lists say about what to fix first?

Compare with a worked answer

This is error analysis: read the failures, name the first thing that went wrong, count the categories, fix the largest. Here the categories can be assigned by rule because the truth is known: matched a line not on the order, abstained on a line that was on the order, chose a sibling product (same family, different size or pack), or chose an unrelated product.

"""Error analysis: what is the FIRST thing that goes wrong in each failing customer-style case?"""
from collections import Counter
import s8_world as w
from s8_world import *
test_c = make_cases(3, "customer", 300)
train = make_cases(180, "customer", 80)                       # 80 labelled customer cases
w.LEARNED.update(learn(train)); thr2 = tune(train, w.baseline2_score)
thrp = tune(make_cases(1, "benchmark", 400), proto_score)
def family(i): return " ".join(PRODUCTS[i].split()[:2])
def kind(c, pred):
    if c["truth"] is None: return "matched a line that is not on the order (should have abstained)"
    if pred is None: return "abstained on a line that was on the order"
    if family(pred) == family(c["truth"]): return "chose a sibling product (same family, different size or pack)"
    return "chose an unrelated product"
for name, sc, thr in (("prototype stand-in", proto_score, thrp), ("baseline v2", w.baseline2_score, thr2)):
    fails = [(c, predict(c, sc, thr)) for c in test_c if predict(c, sc, thr) != c["truth"]]
    cnt = Counter(kind(c, p) for c, p in fails)

nm = [c for c in test_c if c["truth"] is None]
print(f"\nlines not on the order: {len(nm)} of 300 ({len(nm)/300:.0%}); prototype abstained correctly on {sum(predict(c, proto_score, thrp) is None for c in nm)}, baseline v2 on {sum(predict(c, w.baseline2_score, thr2) is None for c in nm)}")

  • 83% of the prototype's failures (92 of 111) are abstentions on lines that were on the order. It usually knew roughly what the line was and declined. Examples in the output: "padlck bras" and "pupose gen ltr 20 kit spill" abstained although they are plainly a padlock and a spill kit. The fix is to tune the abstain threshold on customer-style data, not to rebuild the model. Because it declined so often it was safe: only 3 failures were wrong matches.
  • Baseline v2 fails differently: 22 of 46 are sibling confusions (the 200mm tie instead of the 300mm tie), plus 8 matches of lines that were not on the order. These are the dangerous errors, because they look like successes. The fix is a rule: when two candidates are close, or when a size or pack token is missing, decline or ask for review.
  • Only 27 of 35 off-order lines were declined by baseline v2, against 32 for the prototype. The prototype is the safer system on that category.

Two systems with different failure profiles are worth combining: use the prototype's agreement as a second opinion, and route any disagreement to a clerk. Disagreement between two independent matchers is a cheap signal that a line is hard. It does not prove either is right.

Memo recommendation for the lab and the customer: fix the abstention threshold first (cheap, large effect on the prototype); then add rules for sibling products; then, once the licence is clear, retrain on Northgate's labelled lines and re-run this analysis on the sealed set.

5The threshold is a business decision

Northgate wants the system to do the matching. Using the error rates you measured, compare four options on cost per 1,000 lines: clerks alone, automatic matching, automatic matching with a stricter threshold, and a system that only suggests. State the assumptions, and say what you recommend.

Compare with a worked answer

The right question is not "how accurate is it?" but "what does it cost per line, counting both the review time and the cost of a mistake nobody notices?" (cost per task, not cost per token). The assumptions are listed in the script: 45 seconds to resolve a line by hand, $40 an hour, and $18 to find and fix one wrong match that went through unreviewed. The last figure is a guess; Northgate's finance team would replace it with real credit-note and dispute costs.

"""Quality, cost and latency per 1,000 invoice lines. Latency is measured here on CPU; prices and review times are assumptions."""
import time
import s8_world as w
from s8_world import *
test_c = make_cases(3, "customer", 300)
train = make_cases(180, "customer", 80)
w.LEARNED.update(learn(train)); thr2 = tune(train, w.baseline2_score)
thrp = tune(make_cases(1, "benchmark", 400), proto_score)
REVIEW_S, REVIEW_RATE, ERR_COST = 45, 40.0, 18.0   # seconds to resolve one line by hand, $ per hour of reviewer, $ to find and fix one wrong auto-match later
systems = (("human only (today)", None, None), ("baseline v2", w.baseline2_score, thr2), ("prototype stand-in", proto_score, thrp))
print(f"{'system':<22}{'auto-matched':>13}{'wrong & silent':>15}{'ms/line':>9}{'review $':>10}{'error $':>9}{'total $/1000':>14}")
for name, sc, thr in systems:
    if sc is None:
        print(f"{name:<22}{0:>13.1%}{0:>15.1%}{'-':>9}{1000*REVIEW_S/3600*REVIEW_RATE:>10.0f}{0:>9.0f}{1000*REVIEW_S/3600*REVIEW_RATE:>14.0f}")
        continue
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); preds = [predict(c, sc, thr) for c in test_c]; ms = (time.perf_counter()-t0)*1000/len(test_c)
    auto = sum(p is not None for p in preds)/300

6The interface is a contract

Define the service's request and response. What must a response carry so that a clerk, an auditor and the support engineer can each do their job? How do you version it?

Compare with a worked answer

A research notebook returns an answer. A product returns an answer, a reason, a confidence, a version and a way to say "I do not know" (the interface is a contract; see also evolving contracts for compatibility rules). The example below is the whole design in one response.

// request
{ "request_id": "inv-88213-l4",
  "invoice_line": { "text": "MV5820 cable tie black pk 100", "supplier_id": "MV", "qty": 40, "unit": "pk" },
  "po_lines": [ { "po_line_id": "PO-5521-03", "text": "cable tie black 200mm pack of 100", "qty": 40 },
                { "po_line_id": "PO-5521-04", "text": "cable tie black 300mm pack of 100", "qty": 40 } ] }

// response
{ "request_id": "inv-88213-l4",
  "model_version": "matcher-2026.10.2", "rules_version": "abbr-table-17",
  "decision": "needs_review",                      // "suggest" | "needs_review" | "not_on_order"
  "candidates": [
    { "po_line_id": "PO-5521-03", "score": 0.61, "evidence": ["black", "cable", "tie", "pack", "100"] },
    { "po_line_id": "PO-5521-04", "score": 0.59, "evidence": ["black", "cable", "tie", "pack", "100"] } ],
  "reason": "two candidates within 0.05; length missing from the invoice line" }
  • Decision, not just a score. Three values: suggest, needs_review, not_on_order. The service never returns "matched" without a human in the loop in the first release. The word makes the product's promise explicit.
  • Evidence and reason. The clerk sees why two candidates are close. This serves the stage-5 requirement to fight automation bias.
  • Versions on every response. The model and the abbreviation table are separate, versioned artefacts. When a dispute arises, the support engineer can reproduce the answer.
  • Idempotent by request id. The same request returns the same answer, so retries do not create duplicates (exactly-once and idempotency).
  • Compatibility. New fields are additive; a breaking change is a new path (/v2) with a deprecation date. The payables system will have been built against v1.
  • Errors the caller can act on. A timeout or malformed request returns an error, never a guess. The payables system must fall back to the clerk's normal workflow, so that an outage costs time, not payment errors.

The audit needs a sample size. The script computes how wide the interval is for a true precision of 96% at several weekly sample sizes, and how often an audit would flag a real problem (a true 90%). The rule for flagging is the strict one: only when the whole interval is below 95%.

"""How many labelled lines per week does the quality SLO need? Wilson interval half-width and detection chance. Seeded."""
import math, random
from s8_world import wilson
print("Weekly audit of n randomly sampled auto-matched lines. SLO: auto-match precision >= 95%")
print(f"{'n':>5}{'interval if true = 96%':>26}{'chance an audit flags a true 90%':>34}")
rng = random.Random(4)
for n in (50, 100, 200, 400, 800):
    k = round(.96*n); lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    flag = sum(wilson(sum(rng.random() < .90 for _ in range(n)), n)[1] < .95 for _ in range(4000))/4000   # flag only when the WHOLE interval is under 95%
    print(f"{n:>5}{lo:>13.1%} to {hi:>6.1%}{flag:>34.0%}")

This is a failure notice in the sense of the writing chapter: facts first, what we know and do not, what we recommend, and what we need from the reader.

Subject: LineLink: what we found in four weeks, and what we recommend for next quarter

Where we are. We reproduced the lab's headline result: the model matched 99.0% of lines on the lab's own benchmark. On 300 of your invoice lines it matched 63.0% (interval 57.4% to 68.3%). The accuracy figures come from 300 lines from three months of invoices and may be different for suppliers not in our sample. 11Limitations and the three-minute defence

List what your evidence cannot show. Then say your recommendation aloud in three minutes and answer the toughest question a reviewer will ask.

Compare with a worked answer

The limitations section

  • Stand-ins, not the repository. The numbers here come from two small matchers on a generated dataset. They illustrate how a model tuned on tidy text behaves on messy text and how to analyse that, not how any real research system performs. In a real submission the first table would come from running the actual repository.
  • Synthetic data. The abbreviations and noise in the generated lines are a guess at real suppliers. Real invoices have scans, multiple languages, merged lines, partial deliveries and price changes, all outside this evaluation.
  • Costs rest on assumptions. The 45 seconds, 15 seconds, $40 an hour and $18 per silent error are placeholders. The conclusion that automation loses to clerks depends on the $18; below a certain cost per error, it flips (the "Try it yourself" in stage 5 finds where).
  • Small samples. 300 lines per evaluation, 80 for the learned table. Intervals are reported; rankings between systems that differ by less than the interval are not claimed.
  • Time-sensitive. Invoice formats and suppliers change. The gold set must be refreshed each quarter and the audit sampler is what notices the drift.
  • Licence status is unresolved. The legal position is not something an engineer can settle; the submission flags it and says who must.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The customer asked for next quarter's matching on a paper that says ninety-nine per cent. I started with two cheap checks: could we legally use it, and does the number hold on their data. The weights are non-commercial, so we cannot deploy them without permission or retraining, and the result reproduced on the lab's benchmark but fell to sixty-three per cent on the customer's invoices, below a word-matching baseline I wrote in an hour and which reached eighty-five per cent with a table learned from eighty labelled lines. Reading the failures told me why: the model was tuned on tidy text and declined too often, while the baseline's dangerous errors were look-alike products. Then I priced it. With a rough eighteen dollars for a silent error, automatic matching cost more than the clerks at every threshold, so I recommend a suggestion screen: top three with reasons, no default, and a weekly audit. That is about forty per cent of today's cost on stated assumptions, but it only works if clerks do not rubber-stamp, so the pilot measures that. The plan has four gates with named stop conditions, and I wrote the customer a letter that says what did not carry over, what we measured, what we did not, and what we need from them."

Notice the shape: legal and reproduction checks, then the customer's data, then a baseline, then failure reading, then money, then a smaller first release with gates, then an honest letter.

The toughest question: "You had a model with a ninety-nine per cent paper. Why did you ship the weaker approach?" Because the paper's number was for different data, because we cannot legally deploy those weights, and because accuracy was never the measure that mattered: cost per line including undetected errors is, and the simple system with a human decision wins on that. When the lab's model is retrained on the customer's data and cleared by the licence, the same sealed set tells us whether it should replace the baseline.

Key takeaways

  • Check the licence and reproduce the result before you design anything; either can end the project in a week.
  • A paper's number is for the paper's data. Evaluate on the customer's own lines, with intervals and a sealed set.
  • A baseline written in an hour, with a small learned table, can beat the research model on the customer's data. That is a finding, not an embarrassment.
  • Read the failures: the two systems failed in opposite ways, and each needed a different fix.
  • The threshold is a business decision. Price silent errors; with plausible costs, a suggestion screen beats both full automation and no tool.
  • Gates, a smaller first release and a plain letter to the customer are part of the engineering.
