Take-Home: Clinical Note Summaries

A hospital medicine department wants a one-paragraph summary of a patient's earlier notes at the start of each shift. The risk is not the sentence that is wrong, which a reader sees, but the sentence that is missing, which nobody sees. You have one working day to scope it, measure omissions as carefully as errors, and defend a recommendation to people who can say no.

How a take-home studio works

This page simulates a take-home exercise of the kind described in Take-Homes, Portfolios & Interviews: a vague brief, sample material, a clock and a reviewer who is judging your judgement. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer. The worked answers are the key artefacts of one excellent submission: the requirements page, baselines, a fact-level gold set with severities, an evaluation with intervals, an error-analysis memo, a limitations section and the oral defence. Every script was run and its output pasted. The patients, notes and department are invented and synthetic, and the systems under test are deterministic stand-ins, so the numbers illustrate a method and are not benchmarks. This page is not medical or legal advice.

📐 The brief

The Chief Clinical Information Officer of Harbour Health (fictional) writes: "Our hospitalists spend a large part of their handover reading the previous admission's notes. We would like a short summary of a patient's earlier notes, ready when the clinician opens the chart. We are nervous: a smooth summary that drops an allergy is worse than none. Show us how you would know it is safe enough to try."

  • Time box: 4 to 6 hours of work, then a three-minute oral defence with a clinician and an engineer. Choose, and say what you left out.
  • Deliverables: (1) a one-page requirements restatement with assumptions and the questions you would ask; (2) a baseline before any framework; (3) a small gold set with its labelling procedure; (4) an evaluation with confidence intervals; (5) a quality, cost and latency table; (6) an error-analysis memo naming the first failure in sampled traces; (7) a limitations section; (8) a three-minute oral defence.
  • Sample material you are given: 40 synthetic admission notes (the pack states that no real patient data may be used or sent to any external service for this exercise), a draft clinical-safety questionnaire, and a note from the privacy officer saying that any production design must use only the minimum necessary data under an agreement with the model provider.
  • Constraints: the clinician reads, edits and signs the summary and remains responsible for it; the summary sits in the record; the tool must not recommend diagnoses or treatment; the hospital's governance group meets in six weeks and will decide whether a supervised pilot may start.
  • Builds on Healthcare & Life Sciences, Evaluation Plans Customers Trust, Capturing Expert Knowledge, human labels and statistics for evals.

1Restate the brief on one page

Write the one-page restatement a reviewer reads first: the problem, the users, what is in and out of scope, five assumptions and five questions for the clinicians. Which phrase in the brief carries the whole task?

Compare with a worked answer

The phrase is "a smooth summary that drops an allergy is worse than none". It tells you which error the customer fears (omission), which property they value over fluency (completeness of the safety-critical facts) and the comparison they will make (the tool against no tool, not against perfection). The healthcare chapter's rule applies: measure what the summary leaves out. Here is the page.

2Triage: data, device status and who is responsible

Before building: what does protected health data require of the design, could this be a medical device, and where does clinical responsibility sit? What must be true for the tool to stay on the lighter side of the line?

Compare with a worked answer

This stage uses the checks in the healthcare chapter and the feasibility triage, and a reviewer is looking for the habit, not for legal conclusions. State what you checked, what you assumed and who must confirm.

  • Data. In the United States, a vendor that handles protected health information for a hospital is a business associate and needs a business associate agreement; HIPAA's minimum-necessary standard means sending the fields the task needs, not a whole chart. In the EU, health data is a special category under GDPR Article 9. The design therefore sends only the notes for one admission, strips direct identifiers before they leave the hospital's boundary where the task allows, and logs what was sent. For the take-home the data is synthetic, and the submission says in its first paragraph that no real data was used.
  • Device status. In the US, software meant to diagnose, treat, mitigate or prevent disease is a medical device whatever it is built from, and some clinical decision support software falls outside the definition only if it meets four statutory criteria, including that the clinician can independently review the basis for its recommendation (the FDA's guidance was revised in January 2026; read the current text). A summary that only condenses notes for a clinician to read is generally administrative or documentation support. It would move toward device territory if it began to rank patients by risk, flag deterioration, suggest a diagnosis or time-critical action, or if the user could not practically check it. The memo lists those as features not to add without a regulatory review.
  • Clinical safety governance. Hospitals have clinical safety officers and risk processes; in England, the NHS standards DCB0129 (for manufacturers) and DCB0160 (for deploying organisations) set out clinical risk management for health IT, and similar structures exist elsewhere. The submission delivers a hazard log (stage 11) in the form such a process expects and does not pretend to complete one.
  • Responsibility. The clinician signs. That is workable only if they can check the draft in less time than writing it (stage 10), which is a measurable claim, and if the interface does not hide what was left out.

The conclusion for the reviewer: proceed as a documentation-support tool with a clinician in the loop, minimum-necessary data and a staged, silent-first pilot. Two things would stop the project: a vendor with no agreement covering the endpoint to be used, and a clinical team that cannot agree what counts as a critical fact.

3The data pack: read before you build

Read three notes. What structure do they share? What do you expect a summariser to find hard? List five checks you would run across all 40.

Compare with a worked answer

Real take-home packs for health data use synthetic notes, as here. Each note has five sections (history, medications, examination, results, assessment and plan) of mixed content: facts that matter for safety, facts that matter for context, and sentences that matter little (social chat, ward observations). The generator (notes.py, seeded so that the data is identical on every run) writes notes of about 100 words with 5 to 8 facts. Real notes are much longer; the proportion of important content falls as the note grows, so a tool that copes with these will have a harder job on real ones. Here is one note exactly as the pack shows it, with the facts the gold set records for it (stage 5).

(The generator reuses filler sentences between sections, as real notes repeat text. "Wife telephoned" in a woman's note is an artefact of the generator; in a real record such a slip would raise the question of copy-forward text, which this pack cannot represent.)

  • The facts that matter are in the middle. The allergy sits at the end of the medications section, the anticoagulant in a list, the hold instruction in the plan. A summariser that favours the opening of a note or the plan section will miss different things. The baselines in stage 4 test exactly this.
  • Safety facts have no common vocabulary. An allergy may be written "allergic to", "reaction to", "not tolerated", "intolerance on file" or "anaphylaxis to". A hold instruction may be "hold", "withhold", "omit" or "do not give". Any summariser built on phrasing it has seen will fail on phrasing it has not (stage 8 shows this happening).
  • Negation and numbers matter. "No chest pain" and "chest pain" differ by one word; "5 mg" and "50 mg" by one digit. These are the failure types an abstractive model makes (stage 9).

My five checks across the 40 notes: the word count and the share of words that are filler; where in each note the allergy and anticoagulant appear; how many notes contain a hold or stop instruction; the spread of phrasing for the same fact; and whether any note contradicts itself. I also split the notes into a development half I may look at and a sealed half I may not, before reading anything else (stage 7).

4Baselines before any model

Build two baselines in under an hour: the cheapest summaries you can imagine. What will each get wrong, and what do the baselines tell the department before any model is involved?

Compare with a worked answer

The first baseline takes the opening 50 words of the note (what a preview pane would show). The second copies the Assessment and plan section, because clinicians say "the plan is the summary". Neither involves a model. They are the control: a candidate that is not clearly better has no reason to exist. The scoring uses the gold set of stage 5; the code appears in stage 7 and the results in the table there, but the headline is worth stating now.

  • The lead baseline loses a critical fact in 40 of 40 notes. The opening of a note is history and context; allergies, medications and the plan come later.
  • Copying the plan loses a critical fact in 34 of 40. Plans contain the diagnosis, the new drug, the hold instruction and the follow-up, but not the allergy or what the patient already takes. It looks like a summary, it reads like a clinician's own, and it omits exactly the facts that make the plan safe.

These are the numbers to show the CCIO on day one, because they answer an unasked question: "why not just show the plan?" The answer is measured, not argued.

5The gold set: facts, severities and two clinicians

Design the gold set. What is the unit you label, how do you define severity so clinicians can apply it consistently, who labels, and how do you check they agree?

Compare with a worked answer

The unit is not the summary but the fact: an atom from the source note that a clinician has judged must survive. A summary is scored by which facts it keeps, which it drops and what it adds. This follows the method in capturing expert knowledge (ask clinicians to think aloud on real examples, then turn their judgements into a rubric) and the labelling discipline in human labels.

  1. Elicit the severities with the clinicians first, on three notes, before labelling anything: "if this fact were missing from a summary a colleague relied on, what could happen?" Their answers define three levels. Critical: a patient could be harmed by the omission (a drug allergy, an anticoagulant, an instruction to hold or stop a drug, a new drug and its dose). Major: the clinician would have to go back to the record to proceed (the working diagnosis, an abnormal result that drove the plan, a follow-up test). Minor: useful context (a past history item).
  2. Extract the facts per note (here the generator knows them; in a real pack a clinician marks them), each with a keyword rule that decides whether a summary has kept it, and spot-check the rules by reading 20 summaries. A keyword rule is a pragmatic first scorer; it is checked against clinicians, and later replaced or supplemented by a judge model that has been calibrated on clinician labels (LLM-as-judge).
  3. Two clinicians label severity independently for a sample of facts, I compute agreement, and we talk through every disagreement and rewrite the definitions, not just the labels. The script below does the arithmetic on 30 facts with six deliberate disagreements, which are invented but typical: whether a past history is context or relevant risk, whether an abnormal result is critical, whether a diagnosis alone is critical.
  4. Weights. For the headline I report the share of notes with any critical omission, because a single critical omission is the unit of harm. For a secondary figure I use a weighted loss with illustrative weights (critical 10, major 3, minor 1) which the customer must replace with their own. Both appear in every table; neither is averaged into "accuracy".
"""Two clinicians assign a severity to 30 candidate facts. Raw agreement flatters; kappa and weighted kappa do not."""
from collections import Counter
from notes import NOTES
LEVELS = ["minor", "major", "critical"]
facts = [(n["id"] + " " + name, sev) for n in NOTES[:6] for name, sev, _ in n["facts"]][:30]
A = [sev for _, sev in facts]
B = list(A)
def first(prefix, skip=0):
    return [i for i, (name, _) in enumerate(facts) if name.split(" ", 1)[1].startswith(prefix)][skip]
flips = {first("history"): "major", first("history", 2): "major", first("result"): "critical",
         first("diagnosis"): "critical", first("new drug"): "major", first("follow-up"): "minor"}   # B's different judgement calls
for i, sev in flips.items(): B[i] = sev
n = len(A)
po = sum(a == b for a, b in zip(A, B)) / n
ca, cb = Counter(A), Counter(B)
pe = sum(ca[k] * cb[k] for k in LEVELS) / n ** 2
kappa = (po - pe) / (1 - pe)
# linear-weighted kappa: a minor/critical clash counts as worse than major/critical
w = lambda a, b: abs(LEVELS.index(a) - LEVELS.index(b)) / 2
do = sum(w(a, b) for a, b in zip(A, B)) / n
de = sum(w(a, b) * ca[a] * cb[b] for a in LEVELS for b in LEVELS) / n ** 2
print(f"{n} facts: raw agreement {po:.0%}, kappa {kappa:.2f}, weighted kappa {1 - do / de:.2f}")
for i in flips: print(f"  disagreement: {facts[i][0]:48} A={A[i]:8} B={B[i]}")

  disagreement: N000 history: atrial fibrillation                A=minor    B=major
  disagreement: N002 history: osteoarthritis                     A=minor    B=major
  disagreement: N000 result: CRP                                 A=major    B=critical
  disagreement: N000 diagnosis: acute kidney injury              A=major    B=critical

A raw agreement of 80% sounds acceptable, and a kappa of 0.66 (0.72 weighted, since a minor/major clash is less serious than minor/critical) says it is acceptable but not strong. The six disagreements are not noise to average away: they are the places where the definitions are vague. The adjudication produces rules such as "a result is critical only if the plan changes because of it and the change is a drug action" and "a diagnosis is major unless the summary would otherwise mislead the reader about the reason for admission". Those rules go in the labelling guide, with the date, and the agreement is re-measured on the next batch.

Three decisions deserve comment. The unit is the note. Facts within a note are correlated (a sloppy summary drops several), so counting facts as independent trials would give intervals that are too narrow. The bootstrap in the script resamples notes. The bar is stated as an upper bound, because with a safety property you want to know how bad it could plausibly be, not how good the point estimate looks. The pass rule has three outcomes: pass, fail, and inconclusive.

The arithmetic that makes "inconclusive" real is again the rule of three: zero failures in n notes bounds the rate at about 3 / n (exactly, 7.2% for n = 40). Reviewer behaviour needs its own sample: to estimate how often a clinician detects a deliberately omitted fact to within ten points, about 93 notes. The next script prints both.

The sensible plan is to say so, to grow the sealed set to at least 150 notes before a supervised pilot, and to run the pilot silent first.

7Build the candidate, with a sealed set

Design the pipeline so that patient data goes only where it must, a clinician always reviews, and the omissions you fear are visible to them. Then build a first candidate summariser, and say how you will keep the test honest while you tune it.

Compare with a worked answer

The candidate in the take-home is a deterministic extractive summariser: it scores each sentence for cues (allergy words, doses, hold and start verbs, anticoagulant names, abnormal results, follow-up) and keeps the highest-scoring sentences that fit a 50-word budget. It is a stand-in for a model, chosen because it is reproducible, and because its failures are legible. (A model-based summariser slots into the same harness; see the stage 10 table for what changes.) The scorer, the three systems and the budget are in systems.py.

"""Three summarisers and the scorer. Deterministic stand-ins: the method is what matters, not these rules."""
import re
BUDGET = 50   # words in a summary

def sents(text):
    return [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.])\s+|\n", text) if s.strip()]

def lead(note):                     # baseline 1: the first sentences, cut at the word budget
    return " ".join(" ".join(note["text"].replace("\n", " ").split()[:BUDGET]).split())

def plan_copy(note):                # baseline 2: copy the Assessment and plan section, cut at the budget
    sec = note["text"].split("Assessment and plan:")[1]
    return " ".join(sec.split()[:BUDGET])

CUES = [(r"allerg|reaction|not tolerated|avoid latex|hives|wheeze", 4), (r"\b\d+(\.\d+)? ?(mg|micrograms)\b", 3),
        (r"\b(hold|start|stop|started)\b", 4), (r"warfarin|apixaban|rivaroxaban", 3), (r"creatinine|crp", 2),
        (r"repeat|recheck|echo|x-ray|imaging|arrange", 2), (r"impression", 3), (r"past history", 1)]
HEADERS = r"^(History|Medications|Examination|Results|Assessment and plan): "
def salience(note, fix_headers=False, cues=None):   # candidate: keep the highest-scoring sentences that fit the budget
    ss = sents(note["text"])
    scored = []
    for i, s in enumerate(ss):
        body = re.sub(HEADERS, "", s) if fix_headers else s.split(": ", 1)[-1]   # v1 cut at ANY colon
        sc = sum(w for pat, w in (cues or CUES) if re.search(pat, body, re.I))
        scored.append((sc / (len(body.split()) ** 0.5), i, body))
    chosen, used = [], 0
    for sc, i, body in sorted(scored, reverse=True):
        n = len(body.split())
        if sc > 0 and used + n <= BUDGET:
            chosen.append((i, body)); used += n
    return " ".join(b for _, b in sorted(chosen))

CUES_V3 = [(r"allerg|reaction|not tolerated|avoid|hives|wheeze|anaphyl|intoleran", 4), (r"\b\d+(\.\d+)? ?(mg|micrograms)\b", 3),
           (r"\b(hold|withhold|omit|start|started|stop|commence)\b", 4), (r"warfarin|apixaban|rivaroxaban", 3), (r"creatinine|crp", 2),
           (r"repeat|recheck|echo|x-ray|imaging|arrange", 2), (r"impression", 3), (r"past history", 1)]
WEIGHT = {"critical": 10, "major": 3, "minor": 1}
def kept(summary, keys):
    low = summary.lower()
    return all(any(alt in low for alt in k.lower().split("|")) for k in keys)

def score(note, summary):
    lost = [(n, sev) for n, sev, keys in note["facts"] if not kept(summary, keys)]
    total = sum(WEIGHT[sev] for _, sev, _ in note["facts"])
    return {"any_critical_omission": any(sev == "critical" for _, sev in lost),
            "n_critical_lost": sum(sev == "critical" for _, sev in lost),
            "weighted_loss": sum(WEIGHT[sev] for _, sev in lost) / total,
            "lost": lost, "words": len(summary.split())}

The evaluation script runs the baselines and the candidate on the 40 development notes, then, once, on the 40 sealed notes. The sealed notes use phrasings the rules were not written against, as real notes would. Bootstrap intervals resample notes, not facts. For the rate of notes with a critical omission, the table gives an exact upper bound rather than a bootstrap, because a bootstrap of "0 failures" collapses to a zero-width interval, which is false comfort.

"""Score summarisers on 40 development notes, then once on 40 sealed notes. Unit of analysis = the note."""
import random
from notes import NOTES, SEALED
from systems import lead, plan_copy, salience, score

SYSTEMS = {"lead (first 50 words)": lead, "copy plan section": plan_copy, "salience extractive v1": salience,
           "salience extractive v2": lambda n: salience(n, fix_headers=True)}

def cp_upper(k, n, alpha=0.05):
    """Exact (Clopper-Pearson) one-sided upper bound on a failure rate, by bisection."""
    from math import comb
    lo, hi = k / n, 1.0
    for _ in range(60):
        p = (lo + hi) / 2
        if sum(comb(n, i) * p ** i * (1 - p) ** (n - i) for i in range(k + 1)) > alpha: lo = p
        else: hi = p
    return hi

def boot(vals, fn, reps=4000, seed=11):
    rng = random.Random(seed); m = len(vals)
    xs = sorted(fn([vals[rng.randrange(m)] for _ in range(m)]) for _ in range(reps))
    return xs[int(.025 * reps)], xs[int(.975 * reps)]

def table(title, notes, names):
    print(title)
    print(f"{'system':24} {'notes with a critical omission':>31}  {'upper 95% bound':>15}   {'weighted loss (95% interval)':>28}")
    for name in names:
        rows = [score(n, SYSTEMS[name](n)) for n in notes]
        k = sum(r["any_critical_omission"] for r in rows)
        wl = sum(r["weighted_loss"] for r in rows) / len(rows)
        lo, hi = boot(rows, lambda s: sum(r["weighted_loss"] for r in s) / len(s))
        print(f"{name:24} {k:3d}/{len(rows)} = {k/len(rows):5.1%}  {cp_upper(k, len(rows)):14.1%}   {wl:8.1%} ({lo:4.0%} to {hi:4.0%})")

table("DEVELOPMENT SET (rules were written looking at these)", NOTES, list(SYSTEMS))

  • The structural fix is to stop scoring sentences for cues and to treat the safety-critical sections as mandatory. 9The other failure: unsupported claims, and how a clinician actually reviews

A model summary can add or alter content. List the kinds of unsupported claim, build a checker for the ones you can detect cheaply, and say what you would ask a clinician to do about the ones you cannot. How would you measure whether reviewers notice omissions?

Compare with a worked answer

Published evaluation work separates hallucinations (content not in the source) from omissions (source content dropped), and classifies each by potential harm. The framework by Asgari and colleagues (npj Digital Medicine, 2025) annotated 12,999 sentences across 18 configurations of note generation and reported, as quoted in the healthcare chapter, a hallucination rate of 1.47% and an omission rate of 3.45%. Take two things from that: omissions were more common than hallucinations, and a small rate multiplies across thousands of notes. The extractive stand-in above cannot hallucinate; a model can, so the harness needs a check for the kinds that matter. Below are five hand-written summaries of one note, one faithful and four with typical slips (written by hand for this exercise; they are not model outputs), run through a simple checker.

"""The other failure: unsupported claims in an abstractive summary. Four hand-written summaries of one note
(typical model slips, written by hand for this exercise, not produced by a model) against a simple checker."""
import re
NOTE = ("68-year-old man admitted with worse breathlessness. No chest pain. Takes apixaban 5 mg twice daily and metformin 500 mg twice daily. "
        "Allergic to penicillin (rash). Left knee replacement two years ago. Creatinine 182 micromol/L (baseline 90). "
        "Start doxycycline 100 mg twice daily. Hold apixaban until renal function reviewed.")
DRUGS = {"apixaban", "metformin", "doxycycline", "amoxicillin", "warfarin", "furosemide", "aspirin", "ramipril"}
SUMMARIES = {
  "faithful":        "68-year-old man with breathlessness, no chest pain. On apixaban and metformin; penicillin allergy. Creatinine 182. Start doxycycline 100 mg twice daily; hold apixaban until renal review.",
  "dose changed":    "68-year-old man with breathlessness. Allergic to penicillin. Start doxycycline 1000 mg twice daily; hold apixaban.",
  "negation flipped": "68-year-old man with breathlessness and chest pain. Allergic to penicillin. Start doxycycline; hold apixaban.",
  "invented drug":   "68-year-old man with breathlessness. Allergic to penicillin. Start doxycycline 100 mg twice daily and aspirin; hold apixaban.",
  "laterality":      "68-year-old man with breathlessness, right knee replacement. Allergic to penicillin. Start doxycycline; hold apixaban.",
}
def nums(t): return set(re.findall(r"\d+(?:\.\d+)?", t))
def flags(summary):
    out = []
    for n in sorted(nums(summary) - nums(NOTE)): out.append(f"number not in source: {n}")
    for d in sorted(w for w in DRUGS if w in summary.lower() and w not in NOTE.lower()): out.append(f"drug not in source: {d}")
    for term in ("chest pain",):
        if re.search(rf"\bno {term}", NOTE.lower()) and term in summary.lower() and not re.search(rf"\bno {term}", summary.lower()):
            out.append(f"negation lost: source says 'no {term}'")
    for side, other in (("left", "right"), ("right", "left")):
        if other in summary.lower() and other not in NOTE.lower() and side in NOTE.lower(): out.append(f"laterality: source says {side}")
    return out
for name, s in SUMMARIES.items():
    f = flags(s)
    print(f"{name:17} -> {'; '.join(f) if f else 'no flags'}")

dose changed      -> number not in source: 1000
negation flipped  -> negation lost: source says 'no chest pain'

  • The cheap checks catch the loud errors: a number that is not in the source (a tenfold dose), a drug that is not in the source, a negation dropped, and a side swapped. They cost nothing to run on every summary.
  • They do not catch the quiet ones: "stable" for "no acute changes", a plan sentence that fuses two patients' details, a causal claim ("because of the infection") the note never made. For those the harness needs a calibrated judge for entailment (fact-checking and citation verification) and a clinician-labelled sample to measure the judge against.
  • Quoting is safer than paraphrasing. If each sentence in the summary links to the source sentence it came from, the reviewer can check by clicking, and a sentence with no link is flagged. This is the idea of grounded answers and citations applied to a clinical note.

How a clinician reviews, and how you would know. The reviewing clinician is badly placed to see an omission: you cannot see what is not there. So the interface shows, beside the draft, the checker's list of critical facts found in the source with a tick or a gap, which turns an invisible omission into a visible one. To measure whether it works, plan a seeded-omission test: take 100 notes with accurate drafts, remove one critical fact from half of them without telling the reviewers, and measure the share of seeded drafts in which the reviewer restores or flags the fact, with and without the checker's list. The sample sizes are in the stage 6 script. Do this before the pilot, because a reviewer who detects 40% of omissions has turned the summary into a hazard that a signature makes the clinician's problem. This is automation bias (accepting what the machine offers) and the human-in-the-loop effects described in the public-sector chapter (measure the review, do not assume it).

Limitations.

  • Synthetic, short, regular notes. They are about a tenth the length of real ones and their phrasing was written by me. Every number here describes the method, not the department.
  • 40 notes per set. A bound of 7% at best. The sealed set must grow to at least 150 notes of real, de-identified or consented text, labelled by clinicians, before any claim.
  • The scorer is keyword rules. It can say a fact is present when only its name is (a mention of "warfarin" in a sentence that says warfarin was stopped looks like the same fact as "on warfarin"). Clinicians must read a sample of scorer decisions and the scorer's own error rate belongs in the report.
  • No measurement of human behaviour, which is where the risk lives: how often reviewers notice omissions, whether review becomes a click-through, whether workload falls.
  • The stand-in is not a language model. A model changes the failure types (stage 9), and I have measured the harness, not the model.
  • One admission only. Multi-admission summaries raise contradiction and staleness; they are out of scope and flagged.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The brief's key phrase is that a smooth summary that drops an allergy is worse than none, so I measured omissions first and by severity. With two clinicians I defined critical, major and minor facts, and agreement was moderate, kappa 0.66, which led to written rules. I'd recommend a silent trial, then a supervised pilot with a seeded-omission test and stop rules. Notice the shape: fear turned into critical omissions → baselines that show why not the plan → fact-level gold set with severity → exact bounds → sealed and fresh sets → structure, not phrase lists → time as a conditional → stop rules.

Why is "hallucinates less than 2%" not enough? Three reasons. It is a rate of one failure type, and omissions are separate and often more frequent (the cited study found omissions about twice as common as hallucinations). It is measured on someone else's data and prompts, which says little about this department's notes (the lesson of reading leaderboards critically). And it is an average over harms: what the department needs is the rate of the critical kinds, with an interval, on its own notes. What would advise against a pilot? No agreement on critical facts, no business associate agreement or equivalent for the endpoint, no named clinical lead, reviewers who miss more than half of seeded omissions, or a governance group unwilling to accept stop rules.

Key takeaways

  • In clinical summaries the dangerous error is the one nobody sees: measure omissions by severity, and report critical omissions on their own.
  • Label facts, not summaries, with clinician-defined severities; measure agreement, resolve disagreements by rewriting the definitions.
  • Baselines such as the first 50 words and the plan section answer "why not just show the plan?" with numbers.
  • Resample the note, not the fact, and use an exact bound when failures are rare: zero in 40 is a bound of about 7%.
  • A candidate tuned on one phrasing fails on another; keep a sealed set, open it once, and build a fresh one after tuning on it.
  • A model-free bug (a colon that ate a clause) was the first failure in every development trace: read traces before blaming the model.
  • Make omissions visible to the reviewer and test the reviewer with seeded omissions; a signature does not make a skipped check safe.
  • Time saved is conditional on verification time; show the break-even and measure it in a pilot.
