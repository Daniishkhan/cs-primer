Take-Home: An Analyst's Research Assistant

An equity research team wants to ask questions of annual reports and get numbers that tie back to the page they came from. In finance a wrong number is worse than no number, so the work is mostly about what the system must refuse to say, how it shows its evidence, and how you prove both with a small, honest test.

How a design studio works

This is a take-home simulation, the format applied AI teams use to hire (see take-homes, portfolios and interviews): a short brief, sample documents, a clock, and a reviewer who reads your pack and then questions you for three minutes. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answers show the key artefacts of an excellent submission, not the only acceptable one.

The sample documents are pages of a fictional annual report printed in the scripts, so every number can be checked by hand. The model's role is played by recorded plans: fixed outputs written to resemble the failure patterns real models show on financial tables, so the scripts give the same numbers on every run. A real submission would call a real model and log its replies in the same shape. The method is the lesson, not the percentages.

📐 The brief

A message from the head of research at Calder Asset Management (fictional): "Our analysts read a lot of annual reports. Most questions are simple: what was revenue, what was net debt, how did margin change. They still spend hours finding the page and checking the units. Could an assistant answer in seconds? The one rule is that every number has to point to the page it came from, because we will not publish a number we cannot source."

  • Users: twelve research analysts. They are experts who will check what they are told, but only if it is quick to check.
  • Data: annual reports as PDFs, 150 to 300 pages each, born-digital, English, with tables. You are given 14 pages of one report (the 2025 report of the fictional Marlowe Instruments plc) and one page of the previous year's report.
  • Stakes: numbers feed published research and client portfolios. A wrong figure with a confident citation is the worst outcome.
  • Time box: 4 to 6 hours of your own time (plan for 5), then a three-minute question session.
  • Deliverables: (1) a one-page restatement of the requirements with assumptions and questions; (2) a baseline built before any framework; (3) a small gold set with its labelling procedure; (4) an evaluation with confidence intervals; (5) a quality, cost and latency table; (6) an error-analysis memo naming the first failure in sampled traces; (7) a limitations section; (8) notes for a three-minute oral defence.
  • Builds on prototype the riskiest assumption, evaluation plans customers trust and finance. The retrieval side comes from PDFs and layout, tables, forms and key-value extraction and grounded answers and citations; the process is in evaluating agents. The closest real-world story is case 2: a research assistant for financial advisers.

1Restate the brief

What do you think "every number has to point to the page" requires? What do you assume, what would you ask, and what is the most dangerous word in the brief?

Compare with a worked answer

The dangerous word is "simple". "What was net debt?" sounds like a lookup, and it is a lookup in one report and a four-line computation in another; "operating profit for 2024" has two correct answers depending on whether the report restated it. Questions that look simple to an analyst contain definitions, units, periods and versions that the analyst resolves without noticing. A system must resolve the same four things, visibly (discovery interviews: ask what they did the last time).

Restatement, as submitted (page 1 of the pack)

Goal. Answer factual and arithmetic questions about one annual report in seconds, with the figure, its unit and period, and the page it was read from, so an analyst can confirm it in under ten seconds. Success (proposed, to confirm): at least 95% of answers numerically correct on analysts' own questions, with a lower interval bound above 90%; zero answers that cite a page that does not contain the figure; and the system declines when the report does not say.

What it is. A lookup-and-arithmetic assistant over a report the analyst selects. It is not a forecast, a recommendation or a summary of management's tone.

Assumptions. (a) One report at a time, born-digital PDF. (b) Units, periods and restatements are as the report states them. (c) A figure that the report itself defines (net debt, free cash flow) is answered with the report's definition. (d) Tables matter more than prose. (e) Wrong-but-cited is worse than a refusal.

Questions I would ask. What are the twenty questions an analyst asked last week, in their own words? Which measures does your team define differently from the company? When two reports disagree about a year, which wins? Is this ever used for work that reaches clients or the regulator, and who signs off on a number before publication? Are there reports in other languages or scanned?

Out of scope. Cross-company comparison, forecasts, charts, narrative analysis, multi-report questions beyond "as originally reported".

3Is a language model the right tool, and for which part?

Split the task into steps. For each one, say whether code or a model should do it and why. Is there a cheaper source of the same numbers?

Compare with a worked answer

Apply the questions from Is AI the right tool? per step, not per product. FinanceBench (Islam et al., 2023: 10,231 questions on public company filings) is the cautionary data point: its authors evaluated 16 model configurations on a sample of 150 cases and report that GPT-4-Turbo used with a retrieval system "incorrectly answered or refused to answer 81% of questions". That is a 2023 result about specific systems, not a verdict on the approach, and it says nothing about this design. It does say that reading financial filings is hard enough that "ask the model" is not a plan.

The cheaper source. Listed companies in many jurisdictions file machine-readable financial data alongside the PDF (in the US, XBRL tagged data from the SEC's EDGAR system). If the customer's questions are about tagged numbers, a structured lookup beats any reading of PDFs and the right submission says so. The assistant earns its place on what is not tagged: segment notes, narrative disclosures, the page itself as evidence, and reports with no data feed. Ask the customer which they need before assuming PDFs.

The error tolerance is near zero for numbers and generous for latency (seconds are fine). That shifts the whole design toward verification and away from clever generation, a theme of the finance chapter: "every number has an address".

4The gold set and its labelling procedure

Write twenty questions the way analysts ask them. What fields does each gold item need, how do you decide what evidence counts, and how do you stop the questions from all being the easy kind?

Compare with a worked answer

The gold set is 21 questions. They come from the analysts' own phrasing (the customer supplied last week's questions), stratified by kind so the hard kinds are visible rather than averaged away: plain lookups, unit traps, computations, multi-line definitions, company-defined measures, restatements and questions the report cannot answer. Each item records:

  1. The question, verbatim.
  2. The gold value and unit, to the precision the report prints. For unanswerable questions the gold answer is "abstain".
  3. The acceptable evidence: one or more alternative sets of pages. Net debt is acceptable from page 77 (stated) or from page 42 (the balance sheet lines); the original 2024 operating profit from the 2024 report (page 38) or from note 2 in the 2025 report (page 71). Writing the alternatives down before running anything prevents arguing afterwards about whether a citation "counts".
  4. Who labelled it: an analyst wrote the answer from the PDF, a second person checked it against the page without seeing the first answer, and any difference was settled by reading the page together (human labels). For numbers, agreement should be near total; every disagreement found is a trap that went undetected.

The set deliberately has 7 plain lookups, which a search box should get right, and 14 questions built to need more than a lookup, because a gold set made only of easy items proves nothing. Two further caveats belong in the pack. Questions are not independent: Q16, Q17 and Q18 all turn on the same restatement, so they behave as one cluster, and a sample of 21 contains fewer independent tests than 21 (statistics for evals). And one report is one sample: every trap here may have a cousin in another company's layout that this set cannot see.

5A baseline before any framework

Build the simplest search-and-extract system you can in twenty minutes, with no model and no framework. What does it get right, and what does that tell you about where the model must help?

Compare with a worked answer

The baseline is classical retrieval (BM25 over pages, inverted indexes and BM25) followed by a heuristic: take the table row on the top page whose label shares most words with the question, and return its first number as printed. It never computes, never scales units, never abstains.

"""Baseline before any framework: BM25 over pages, then the best-matching table row's first number."""
import math, re
from collections import Counter
from common import pct
from s2_pages import PAGES
from s2_gold import GOLD
from s2_parse import parse_number
from s2_run import answer_ok, cite_ok

STOP = set("what was the in of a an is for to did by how much many at end and from which as shown report annual group".split())
tok = lambda s: [w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9£%]+", s.lower()) if w not in STOP]
docs = {k: tok(v) for k, v in PAGES.items()}
N, avgdl = len(docs), sum(map(len, docs.values())) / len(docs)
df = Counter(w for d in docs.values() for w in set(d))
def bm25(q, d, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    tf = Counter(d); s = 0.0
    for w in set(tok(q)):
        if w in tf:
            idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[w] + .5) / (df[w] + .5))
            s += idf * tf[w] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[w] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(d) / avgdl))
    return s

def baseline(q):
    ranked = sorted(docs, key=lambda k: -bm25(q, docs[k]))
    page = ranked[0]
    best, best_overlap = None, 0
    for ln in PAGES[page].split("\n"):
        cells = [c.strip() for c in ln.split("|")]
        if len(cells) > 1 and parse_number(cells[1]) is not None:
            ov = len(set(tok(cells[0])) & set(tok(q)))
            if ov > best_overlap:
                best, best_overlap = parse_number(cells[1]), ov     # raw number: no unit scaling
    if best is None:
        m = re.search(r"\d[\d,.]*", PAGES[page]); best = parse_number(m.group()) if m else None
    return ranked, best

def main():
    hit1 = hit3 = ans = cit = 0
    for qid, q, kind, gold, unit, alts in GOLD:
        ranked, val = baseline(q)
        wanted = {p for a in alts for p in a}
        hit1 += ranked[0] in wanted if wanted else 0
        hit3 += bool(set(ranked[:3]) & wanted)
        a = answer_ok(gold, val, unit); ans += a
        cit += a and cite_ok(alts, {ranked[0]})
        if qid in (7, 9, 16, 19):
            print(f"Q{qid:<2} {kind:14s} top page {ranked[0]}  answer {val}  (gold {gold})")
    n_with_evidence = sum(1 for g in GOLD if g[5] != [set()])
    print(f"\nretrieval: right page first for {hit1}/{n_with_evidence} answerable questions, in the top 3 for {hit3}/{n_with_evidence}")
    print("answer correct:          ", pct(ans, len(GOLD)))
    print("correct AND page-correct:", pct(cit, len(GOLD)))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

The parser is the part that makes units and signs a solved problem rather than a hope. It is plain code with tests (tables, forms and key-value extraction explains why real tables are harder than this):

"""Turn page text into rows with provenance: value, unit scale, page and document."""
import re
from s2_pages import PAGES

def parse_number(cell):
    s = cell.strip().replace(",", "")
    neg = s.startswith("(") and s.endswith(")")
    s = s.strip("()")
    try:
        v = float(s)
    except ValueError:
        return None
    return -v if neg else v

def scale_of(unit_line):
    u = unit_line.lower()
    if "thousand" in u: return 0.001, "£m"      # store everything in millions
    if "£ million" in u: return 1.0, "£m"
    if "pence" in u: return 1.0, "pence"
    if "number of people" in u: return 1.0, "people"
    return 1.0, "?"

def parse_page(doc, page):
    lines = PAGES[(doc, page)].split("\n")
    header, scale, unit, rows = None, 1.0, "?", {}
    for ln in lines:
        if "|" not in ln:
            continue
        cells = [c.strip() for c in ln.split("|")]
        if cells[0].startswith(("Unit:", "All figures")):
            header = [h.split(" (")[0] for h in cells[1:]]          # '2024 (restated)' -> '2024'
            scale, unit = scale_of(cells[0])
            continue
        nums = [parse_number(c) for c in cells[1:]]
        if header and all(n is not None for n in nums):
            rows[cells[0].lower()] = {h: n * scale for h, n in zip(header, nums)}
    return {"unit": unit, "rows": rows}

Then the executor and verifier run every plan, with arithmetic evaluated through a small safe evaluator (no eval of model text, injection and untrusted input):

"""Executor and verifier: the model proposes cells and arithmetic; code reads, computes and checks."""
import ast, operator, re
from s2_parse import parse_page
from s2_gold import GOLD
from s2_plans import PLANS

OPS = {ast.Add: operator.add, ast.Sub: operator.sub, ast.Mult: operator.mul, ast.Div: operator.truediv,
       ast.USub: operator.neg}
def safe_eval(expr, env):
    def ev(n):
        if isinstance(n, ast.Constant): return n.value
        if isinstance(n, ast.Name): return env[n.id]
        if isinstance(n, ast.BinOp): return OPS[type(n.op)](ev(n.left), ev(n.right))
        if isinstance(n, ast.UnaryOp): return OPS[type(n.op)](ev(n.operand))
        raise ValueError("arithmetic only")
    return ev(ast.parse(expr, mode="eval").body)

def run(plan):
    """Returns (answer or None, cited pages, status)."""
    if "abstain" in plan:
        return None, set(), "abstained (model)"
    if "text_answer" in plan:                       # narrative fact; verified by finding the figure in the page text
        from s2_pages import PAGES
        doc, pg = plan["cite"][0]
        ok = f"{plan['text_answer']:.0f}%" in PAGES[(doc, pg)]
        return (plan["text_answer"], set(plan["cite"]), "ok") if ok else (None, set(), "abstained (verifier: figure not on page)")
    env, cited = {}, set()
    for name, r in plan["refs"].items():
        try:
            page = parse_page(r["doc"], r["page"])
            env[name] = round(page["rows"][r["label"].lower()][r["col"]], 6)
        except KeyError:
            return None, set(), f"abstained (verifier: '{r['label']}' not found on {r['doc']} p.{r['page']})"
        cited.add((r["doc"], r["page"]))
    return round(safe_eval(plan["expr"], env), 6), cited, "ok"

def answer_ok(gold, got, unit):
    if gold is None: return got is None
    if got is None: return False
    return abs(got - gold) < (0.5 if unit == "people" else 0.05 + 1e-9)

def cite_ok(alts, cited):
    return any(a <= cited for a in alts) if alts != [set()] else not cited

Notice the verifier's two jobs. If the plan names a row label that does not exist on the cited page (Q19, "Order backlog"), the system abstains; the model could not have forced an answer even if it wanted to. And the output always carries the pages it read, so the citation is a by-product of the computation, not a separate claim a model might invent.

8Verify before you answer

What facts does a report state twice? How could a system use those as tests that run on every answer, not only on the gold set?

Compare with a worked answer

Financial statements are redundant by design: totals equal sums, segments add up to the group, and a company-defined measure is also computable from the statements. Each redundancy is a free reconciliation check: compute the same fact two ways and compare. They catch the unit trap (thousands against millions), the transcription error and the wrong definition without any labelled data.

"""Reconciliation checks: facts the report states twice must agree, or the answer is held back."""
from s2_parse import parse_page
from s2_run import run
from s2_plans import PLANS

def cell(doc, page, label, col):
    return round(parse_page(doc, page)["rows"][label.lower()][col], 6)

checks = []
seg = sum(cell("AR2025", 58, s, "2025") for s in ("Instruments", "Services", "Software"))

Common misconception

"If the number appears on the cited page, the answer is verified." The number can appear on the right page and still be the wrong number: the prior-year column, the restated figure, a different line with the same value, or the thousands version. Presence on a page is a necessary check and a weak one. Reconciliation (two routes to the same fact) is much stronger, and the labelled gold set is the final test.

What I did. Read all five failing traces. What I found. Four distinct causes. Q16 and Q18 are one failure counted twice: both took the pre-restatement 2024 figure, so the real count is four root causes, not five, and correcting for the cluster makes 21 questions weaker evidence than they look.

11Limitations, regulation and the recommendation

What does your evidence not show? What rules apply when these numbers reach published research or clients? Write the recommendation as a decision with a gate.

Compare with a worked answer
  1. Twenty-one questions, one fictional report, fifteen pages. The intervals are wide and the questions cluster. Nothing here estimates accuracy on real 250-page reports with scanned pages, odd layouts or footnotes.
  2. Recorded plans. The system's numbers are an illustration of the method. A real pilot re-runs the evaluation on real model output.
  3. Plain-text fixtures. Table extraction from real PDFs is the largest risk not tested. The pipeline's quality is capped by the parser, and the parser needs its own evaluation on real pages (tables, forms and key-value extraction).
  4. Definitions and versions are curated by hand. They need an owner on the research team, or they drift.

Rules around the output. This is an internal research tool, not advice to clients, but its numbers reach research that is. A bank's model risk guidance (in the US, the April 2026 replacement for SR 11-7, finance) is not written for this use and some firms treat it as out of scope, but the same practices apply as good engineering: an inventory entry, a named owner, independent validation of the evaluation and a log of what was answered and shown. FINRA Regulatory Notice 24-09 (June 2024) says existing rules apply to generative AI, so supervision and record-keeping expectations for communications do not disappear because a model drafted a sentence. Confirm what applies to your customer; the firm's compliance team decides, not the engineer. Data handling for the reports themselves is easier than for most cases here (annual reports are public), but the questions an analyst asks reveal their interests and are confidential; check logging and vendor retention (privacy and data handling).

Recommendation, as submitted

Pilot with two analysts for four weeks, then decide. On a fictional 21-question set the design is clearly better than search (81% against 38%, paired p = 0.012) but not near the 95% the research team requires, and it makes four named kinds of mistake. Proceed if (1) the four fixes above are built, (2) a gold set of at least 150 real questions from last quarter is labelled by two analysts, (3) the gate is at least 95% answers correct with a lower bound above 90%, zero unreconciled published figures and every refusal tested, and (4) every answer shows value, unit, period and page, with a one-click link to the page. Until the gate is met the assistant is a faster way to find the page, which the analyst confirms, and not a source of numbers.

"The ask sounded simple: answer questions with the page they came from. The hard part is that a number has a unit, a period, a version and sometimes a company-specific definition. Grading rubric

Tick what your own submission (before reading the answers) covered. The first items are what a solid submission has; the later ones separate an excellent one.

  • Defined a correct answer as value, unit, period and page of the right document version, and named the traps (units, restatements, rounded narrative, defined measures, signs).
  • Decided what code does and what the model does: arithmetic and unit handling in code, language understanding in the model.
  • Considered a cheaper source (tagged financial data) and said when it would replace the PDF pipeline.
  • Built a baseline before any framework and showed what it gets right and wrong.
  • Wrote a gold set with a labelling procedure: stratified by question kind, double-checked, with acceptable evidence alternatives and unanswerable items.
  • Scored answer, citation and abstention separately, and showed that a citation can be present on a wrong answer.
  • Reported confidence intervals, a paired comparison with the baseline, and noticed that clustered questions overstate the sample.
  • Used reconciliation checks (sums, stated against computed) as runtime verification, not only as test data.
  • Made the system decline when the label is not on the page, and tested that on questions the report cannot answer.
  • Gave a cost and latency table, including the long-context design, with assumptions labelled.
  • Read every failing trace, named the first wrong step, merged failures that share a root cause and ordered the fixes.
  • Stated limitations: tiny set, fictional pages, recorded plans, no real PDF parsing, curated definitions.
  • Named the governance angle (ownership, logging, record-keeping, confidential questions) without pretending to give legal advice.
  • Ended with a gated recommendation and a plain statement of what the tool is until the gate is met.

Key takeaways

  • A number is a value, a unit, a period and a page. Define "correct" before building, and list the traps in the documents.
  • Let the model choose cells and arithmetic; let code read, compute and check. The model never writes a number.
  • A citation shows where the system looked, not that it read correctly. Score answer and citation separately.
  • Reports are redundant by design. Reconcile computed figures against stated ones at runtime; prefer the company's own figure.
  • With 21 questions, an 81% score has an interval from 60% to 92%, and questions that share a root cause count once. Claim the difference from search, not reliability.
  • The biggest silent error was a restated figure: the right page of the wrong document version.
