Take-Home: Rolling Out a Coding Assistant

A 300-engineer company wants AI coding tools in everyone's hands by the end of the year. The chief technology officer asks you to plan the rollout and to tell the company, honestly, whether it worked. The hard part is not choosing a tool. It is measuring an effect that developers feel strongly and that the best controlled studies cannot agree on.

How a take-home studio works

This page simulates a four to six hour take-home exercise of the kind described in Take-Homes, Portfolios & Interviews. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer and compare. The worked answer shows the artefact an excellent submission would contain, with numbers from scripts that were run, not guessed. It is one strong answer, not the only right one. The company, its teams and its data are invented for the exercise; the studies quoted in the answers are real and dated. Every price, rate and effect size inside a script is an assumption that you should replace with quotes and measurements from your own customer.

📐 The brief

An email arrives from the chief technology officer of Harbourline, a 300-engineer logistics software company:

"We have been told our competitors are shipping 30% faster with AI coding assistants. The board wants them rolled out to all engineers by the end of the year. I want to do it properly: pick a tool, roll it out safely, and know in six months whether it paid for itself. Our security lead is nervous about source code leaving the building. Our engineering managers are split: some are enthusiastic, some think it will fill the codebase with rubbish. I do not want a slide deck. I want a plan and a way to find out the truth."

  • Harbourline has 24 product teams of 7 to 20 engineers, one monorepo-style platform and a few dozen smaller services, a deployment pipeline with automated tests, and sixteen weeks of pull-request history (described below).
  • Constraints: source code is the company's main asset; two customer-facing services handle payment data and are under audit; the budget owner will ask for a return-on-investment case in the second quarter.
  • Builds on From Proof of Concept to Production, Cost Models & Return on Investment, Evaluation Plans Customers Trust, Security Reviews for AI Systems, Writing for Customers, Working with AI Coding Tools and Statistics for Evals. Related practice: Lab 2: A Cost & Latency Model, Lab 4: A Take-Home, End to End and Case 5: Rolling Out AI in a Law Firm.
  • Time box: 4 to 6 hours, then a three-minute oral defence. Suggested split: 30 minutes restating the brief, 90 minutes on design and power, 90 minutes on guardrails, security and cost, 60 minutes on analysis and the error memo, 60 minutes writing the report, 15 minutes preparing the defence.

The sample inputs

Four inputs come with the brief. The first is the email above. The second is the pull-request history, described here and summarised by a script in stage 3.

Input 2: the pull-request history (synthetic)

A CSV with one row per merged pull request over sixteen weeks: pr_id, team, week, cycle_hours, reverted_14d. cycle_hours is the time from opening the pull request to merging it. reverted_14d is 1 if the change was reverted or needed a hotfix within fourteen days. The five opening rows of the real file are printed in the output of stage 3. Input 3: the vendor and security sheet

Two candidate tools, anonymised. Tool B: self-hosted open-weight model in Harbourline's cloud account, editor plug-in only, no per-seat fee but a GPU cluster and one platform engineer. Input 4: a survey excerpt

The engineering managers ran an informal poll of 60 volunteers who tried the tool for a month. Of them, 48 said it made them "much faster" and 3 said it slowed them down. The poll did not ask about the kind of work, the repository, or what the volunteers did with the time. Treat it as a sign of enthusiasm, not as a measurement, and be ready to say why.

State of play, October 2026

The evidence on coding assistants is mixed and moves quickly. A randomised field study at Microsoft, Accenture and one more large company covered 4,867 developers using GitHub Copilot and reported a 26.08% increase in completed tasks, with a standard error of 10.3 percentage points and results that differed between companies (Cui et al., "The Effects of Generative AI on High-Skilled Work", published in Management Science, 2025; the working paper appeared in 2024). A Google randomised trial with 96 engineers found a task finished about 21% faster, with, in the authors' words, a large confidence interval (Paradis et al., 2024). The METR randomised trial of 16 experienced open-source developers on 246 of their own tasks, using tools from early 2025, found that allowing AI increased completion time by 19%, while the developers had predicted a 24% reduction beforehand and still believed after the study that AI had made them 20% faster (METR, July 2025). The 2024 DORA report, a survey of more than 39,000 professionals, estimated that each 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with a 1.5% drop in delivery throughput and a 7.2% drop in delivery stability, while individual measures such as perceived code quality rose. These are different designs, tools, tasks and years; none settles what will happen at Harbourline. I fetched the arXiv pages for the Google and METR studies and a search summary of the Cui et al. and DORA results; I did not read the full texts of the last two, and I have not checked for later studies published since. Tools have changed a great deal since 2025, so treat every number here as a reason to measure, not as a forecast.

Restated decision. By the second quarter the CTO must decide whether to expand, hold or stop spending on coding assistants, and must be able to defend that decision to a budget owner who will ask "how do you know?". My job is to make that decision possible, not to make it for her.

Assumptions I am making. (1) "Faster" means shorter time from starting work to having it safely in production, not more lines of code. (2) The two payment services are out of scope for the first waves, pending the security lead's approval. (3) Seat and usage prices are placeholders until written quotes arrive. (4) Engineers are not being evaluated individually by the measurements; I will say so publicly, because individual tracking changes behaviour and destroys trust. (5) A rollout that reaches everyone by December and a study that tells the truth can be the same plan, if the order in which teams get access is randomised.

Five questions I would ask.

  1. What would change your decision? Is there a result at which you would stop the programme, and one at which you would expand it? (This produces the decision rule in stage 9.)
  2. Does the "30% faster" figure come from a source we can read, or is it conversation? Who on the board holds it?
  3. Which repositories may never be read by an external service, and who decides? (The answer shapes the whole security design.)
  4. What happens to time saved? Will teams ship more, or will the plan quietly reduce headcount? The answer changes how honest the survey responses will be.
  5. Who reviews code today, how long does review take, and is review already the bottleneck? (If it is, faster writing makes the queue longer, not the delivery quicker.)

The fifth question matters most and is the one candidates skip. If review is the constraint, a tool that makes writing faster moves the delay without removing it.

Common misconception

"Developers know how much faster they are, so ask them." The METR study is the cautionary example: developers expected a 24% reduction in time, measured time went up by 19%, and after the study they still believed they had been 20% faster. Self-report is valuable for finding friction and for learning what people enjoy. It is a poor instrument for the size of a time effect, and it is worst when the answer will decide whether people keep a tool they like.

The metric I refuse to use: lines of code, or suggestions accepted, as evidence of value. A tool that writes 40% of the lines has not made delivery 40% faster; it may have made the codebase larger. Acceptance rate is useful to the platform team for judging whether the tool is configured sensibly, and that is all.

Per-engineer rankings. I also refuse to publish individual measurements. The unit of analysis is the team. This protects people, and it protects the data: the moment an engineer expects to be ranked by tool usage, usage counts stop meaning anything.

3Baseline before any tool

Before anyone switches the tool on, what do the sixteen weeks of history tell you? Compute the baseline, find the noise, and say what size of change you could hope to see.

4Design the rollout as an experiment

Everyone gets the tool by year end, but you want a trustworthy answer. Who gets it first? Compare "let volunteers go first" with a randomised, staged design, and say how many teams you need.

Compare with a worked answer

The company wants to give the tool to everyone, so a classic trial with a permanent control group is unwelcome. A stepped-wedge design keeps both goals: every team eventually gets the tool, but the order is random, so that at any moment teams with the tool can be compared with teams still waiting. The waves are chosen by a random draw, not by managers' preference.

Why not let enthusiasts go first? Because the enthusiastic teams are not typical. They may be the ones already improving, the ones with more time, or the ones with simpler code. The next script shows what happens. In the simulation the tool truly cuts cycle time by 8%; volunteers are the teams already speeding up on their own.

"""Stepped-wedge design for 24 teams: power, and what opt-in does to the estimate. Seeded; every input is an assumption."""
import numpy as np
T, W = 24, 20                      # teams, weeks
SD_TEAM, SD_NOISE, SD_WEEK, SD_DRIFT = 0.32, 0.12, 0.05, 0.04   # log-hours: team level, team-week noise, shared week shock, team drift

def panel(rng, effect, treated_from, drift_bias=None):
    team = rng.normal(0, SD_TEAM, T)[:, None]
    week = rng.normal(0, SD_WEEK, W)[None, :]
    drift = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, SD_DRIFT/2, (T, W)), axis=1)      # slow team-specific wander
    if drift_bias is not None: drift = drift + drift_bias[:, None]*np.arange(W)[None, :]
    treated = (np.arange(W)[None, :] >= treated_from[:, None]).astype(float)
    y = team + week + drift + rng.normal(0, SD_NOISE, (T, W)) + effect*treated
    return y, treated

def twfe(y, d):
    """Two-way fixed effects estimate of the treatment coefficient, with a team-clustered standard error."""
    ti = np.repeat(np.arange(T), W); wi = np.tile(np.arange(W), T)
    X = np.column_stack([d.ravel(), np.eye(T)[ti], np.eye(W)[wi][:, 1:]])
    yy = y.ravel()
    beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, yy, rcond=None)
    res = yy - X @ beta
    XtXi = np.linalg.pinv(X.T @ X)
    meat = sum(np.outer(X[ti==g].T @ res[ti==g], X[ti==g].T @ res[ti==g]) for g in range(T))
    V = XtXi @ meat @ XtXi * (T/(T-1))
    return beta[0], np.sqrt(V[0, 0])

def wedge(rng):
    waves = rng.permutation(T) % 4                 # random assignment of teams to 4 waves
    return 4 + 4*waves                             # access starts in week 5, 9, 13 or 17

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
    print("Power of a 20-week stepped wedge (24 teams, 4 waves), 1500 simulated studies per row")
    print(f"{'true change in cycle time':>26} {'power':>7}   {'mean estimate':>14}")
    for eff in (0.0, -0.05, -0.10, -0.15, -0.20):
        hits, est = 0, []
        for _ in range(1500):
            y, d = panel(rng, eff, wedge(rng))
            b, se = twfe(y, d)
            hits += abs(b/se) > 2.07; est.append(b)    # t critical value, 23 df, two-sided 5%
        print(f"{100*(np.exp(eff)-1):>25.1f}% {hits/1500:>7.0%}   {100*(np.exp(np.mean(est))-1):>13.1f}%")
    print("(row 1 is the false-positive rate; it should sit near 5%)")

    print("\nWhat opt-in does. True effect -8%. Volunteers are the 8 teams already improving fastest.")
    TRUE = np.log(0.92)
    naive, cmp_, wedged = [], [], []
    for _ in range(1000):
        bias = rng.normal(0, 0.006, T)                 # per-week drift in log-hours: some teams were already speeding up
        vol = np.argsort(bias)[:8]                     # keen teams are the ones already getting faster
        start = np.full(T, 99); start[vol] = 10        # opt-in: they start at week 11
        y, d = panel(rng, TRUE, start, bias)
        pre, post = y[:, :10].mean(1), y[:, 10:].mean(1)
        m = np.zeros(T, bool); m[vol] = True
        naive.append((post[m]-pre[m]).mean())          # volunteers, before versus after
        cmp_.append(post[m].mean() - post[~m].mean())  # volunteers versus everyone else, after
        y2, d2 = panel(rng, TRUE, wedge(rng), bias)
        wedged.append(twfe(y2, d2)[0])
    f = lambda v: 100*(np.exp(np.mean(v))-1)
    print(f"true effect                              : {100*(np.exp(TRUE)-1):6.1f}%")
    print(f"volunteers, before versus after          : {f(naive):6.1f}%")
    print(f"volunteers versus everyone else, after   : {f(cmp_):6.1f}%")
    print(f"randomised stepped wedge (TWFE estimate) : {f(wedged):6.1f}%")

  • Volunteers compared with themselves before and after show a 13.5% fall; volunteers compared with everyone else show 19.8%. The truth is 8%. Both numbers are inflated by selection: the keen teams were already improving. Neither looks wrong on a slide, which is why this error survives.
  • The randomised wedge recovers the true 8% (average over 1,000 simulated studies). Randomisation does not make one study exact; it makes the errors cancel on average and lets you compute an honest interval.
  • Power. With 24 teams and 20 weeks, a true change of about 5% is detected in 58% of simulated studies and a change of about 10% in 99%. The false-positive rate when there is no effect is 6%, close to the nominal 5%. A real effect of 5% would therefore often be missed; report a "no clear effect" result as "not distinguishable from zero, and not distinguishable from 5% either".
  • The simulation is optimistic. It assumes the effect is the same from day one and for every team. In practice the benefit arrives with a learning curve and uneven use. If engineers use the tool only half the time, the effect on a team is half as large, and the power in the table moves down the page. Plan for this by treating the primary analysis as intention to treat: compare teams by when access was granted, whether or not each engineer used it. That answers the question the CTO asks (what does giving people this tool do?) and cannot be rescued by cherry-picking the users.

Three practical details. (1) Randomise teams, not engineers, because engineers on a team share a review queue and a codebase, so they affect each other. (2) Stratify the draw so that every wave contains both fast and slow teams and no wave is made of the largest teams. (3) The payment services are excluded from waves A and B on the security lead's request; if they join later, their data are analysed separately and not mixed in as if they had been randomised with the others.

Go deeper optional

The estimator in the script is a two-way fixed-effects regression: each team gets its own baseline level, each week gets its own shock, and the treatment coefficient is what remains. With staggered adoption and effects that change over time, plain two-way fixed effects can mix comparisons in awkward ways, and recent econometrics work (for example by Callaway and Sant'Anna, and by Goodman-Bacon) describes better estimators. For a pilot with four waves and a moderate effect the plain version is a defensible first analysis, provided you also plot each wave's own before-and-after change and check that they tell a similar story. The standard error is clustered by team, because pull requests from one team are not independent observations.

5Guardrails: can you even see harm?

The worry from half the managers is that quality will fall. How many pull requests would you need to see a rise in the 14-day revert rate, and what does that tell you about the guardrail you can promise?

Compare with a worked answer

Rare events need many observations. The revert rate starts near 5.8%. The script below applies the standard two-proportion sample-size formula, then multiplies by a design effect to allow for the fact that pull requests from one team resemble each other (assumed intraclass correlation 0.03, which you should estimate from the baseline data in a real engagement).

"""Guardrail metric: how many PRs to see a rise in 'reverted or hotfixed within 14 days'? Assumptions marked."""
import math
p0 = 0.058                       # baseline rate from the 16-week history
PRS_PER_WEEK = 300*4.5           # assumption: 4.5 PRs per engineer-week
def n_per_arm(p1, p2, z_a=1.96, z_b=0.84):
    pbar = (p1+p2)/2
    return ((z_a*math.sqrt(2*pbar*(1-pbar)) + z_b*math.sqrt(p1*(1-p1)+p2*(1-p2)))**2) / (p1-p2)**2
ICC, M = 0.03, 55                # assumed intraclass correlation among PRs from one team; ~55 PRs per team-week
deff = 1 + (M-1)*ICC
print(f"baseline {p0:.1%}; design effect {deff:.1f} (ICC {ICC}, {M} PRs per team-week)")
print(f"{'rise':>8} {'target rate':>12} {'PRs per arm (simple)':>22} {'with clustering':>16} {'weeks if half the PRs are AI-assisted':>40}")
for rise in (0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03):
    n = n_per_arm(p0, p0+rise); nc = n*deff
    weeks = nc / (PRS_PER_WEEK/2)
    print(f"{rise*100:>6.1f}pt {p0+rise:>12.1%} {n:>22,.0f} {nc:>16,.0f} {weeks:>40.1f}")

  • To see a one-point rise (5.8% to 6.8%) you need about 24,000 pull requests in each arm after allowing for clustering. With roughly 1,350 merged pull requests a week and half of them assisted, that is 36 weeks. A one-point rise cannot be ruled in or out during a twenty-week study.
  • A two-point rise needs about 6,500 per arm: 10 weeks. So the study can promise to catch a large degradation, not a small one.
  • That limit is a finding. Tell the CTO plainly: "In the pilot window we can detect a harmful change of two points or more, not a change of half a point. We will keep monitoring after the pilot and widen the interval as data arrive."

Because the revert rate is slow and noisy, add faster leading indicators that are visible within days: review time per pull request, the share of pull requests that fail continuous integration on first run, and the share of changes touching more than a set number of files. None of them proves quality; they show where to look. This is the same logic as picking early signals in production gates: if the true outcome is slow, choose a proxy you have checked against it, and say which one is which.

For the primary analysis, specify one guardrail rule in advance: "If the 95% interval for the change in 14-day reverts has a lower end above +1.5 points, we stop and investigate." Writing the rule before looking is what keeps it honest (stage 9).

7Read the work: a gold set and an error-analysis memo

Numbers about cycle time will not tell you what the tool gets wrong. Describe how you would sample AI-assisted pull requests, what your labelling rule is, and what the first failure in each sampled pull request looks like in the worked sample.

Compare with a worked answer

This is the "read your data" step from error analysis, applied to code review. The procedure goes in the submission because a reviewer cannot trust a count without it.

  1. Sample. After wave A has been live for three weeks, draw 40 merged pull requests at random from those labelled as AI-assisted. Stratify: at least 5 from a shared library and 5 from a service with high traffic.
  2. Two readers. A senior engineer who did not write the code reads each one, then a second reader reads a random 10 as a check. They agree on the rule below before starting.
  3. Labelling rule. A pull request has "a problem" if a careful reviewer would have asked for a change before merging, or if a defect or deviation from convention was found later in the 14-day window. For each problem, record the first thing that went wrong, in one category, plus a free-text note. Everything downstream of the first failure is ignored (the method used in analysis memos).
  4. Report counts with intervals, not adjectives, and quote one real example per category.

The 40-pull-request sample supplied with the brief is synthetic, so that the answer key is reproducible; the categories are typical of what teams report. The script counts the first failures and puts a 95% Wilson interval on each share.

"""Error analysis of 40 sampled AI-assisted PRs (the sample supplied with the brief; synthetic). Label = FIRST thing that went wrong."""

from collections import Counter

    p = k/n; d = 1 + z*z/n; c = (p + z*z/(2*n))/d
    h = z*math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n))/d
    return c-h, c+h

9Analyse honestly, and write the decision rule first

Twenty weeks of data have arrived. Write the decision rule you would have fixed in advance, run the analysis with intervals, and draft the limitations section.

Compare with a worked answer

The rule is written before the data, in the plan the CTO signs in week 0. Otherwise the temptation to choose a threshold that fits the result is hard to resist. The script below simulates one complete study (true effects: cycle time down 8%, reverts up 0.8 points), estimates both with a two-way fixed-effects fit, and gets intervals by resampling whole teams 600 times, because teams are the unit that was randomised.

"""One simulated stepped-wedge study (true effects: cycle time -8%, reverts +0.8 points), analysed with a team-level bootstrap,
then a decision rule that was written down BEFORE looking."""
import numpy as np
from s7_sim import panel, twfe, wedge, T, W
rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
start = wedge(rng)
y, d = panel(rng, np.log(0.92), start)
# guardrail: revert rate per team-week from ~55 PRs; treatment adds 0.8 points
prs = rng.poisson(55, (T, W))
rate = 0.058 + 0.008*d + rng.normal(0, 0.01, (T, W)).clip(-0.03, 0.03)
reverts = rng.binomial(prs, rate.clip(0.001, 0.5))

def est(idx):
    ys, ds, rs, ps = y[idx], d[idx], reverts[idx], prs[idx]
    # re-use the TWFE fit on the resampled teams (teams duplicated in the bootstrap sample get the same effect weights)
    ti = np.repeat(np.arange(len(idx)), W); wi = np.tile(np.arange(W), len(idx))
    X = np.column_stack([ds.ravel(), np.eye(len(idx))[ti], np.eye(W)[wi][:, 1:]])
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(X, ys.ravel(), rcond=None)[0][0]
    g = rs[ds==1].sum()/ps[ds==1].sum() - rs[ds==0].sum()/ps[ds==0].sum()
    return b, g
b0, g0 = est(np.arange(T))
boots = np.array([est(rng.integers(0, T, T)) for _ in range(600)])
lo_b, hi_b = np.percentile(boots[:, 0], [2.5, 97.5]); lo_g, hi_g = np.percentile(boots[:, 1], [2.5, 97.5])
pct = lambda v: 100*(np.exp(v)-1)
print(f"cycle time:   {pct(b0):+.1f}%   95% interval {pct(lo_b):+.1f}% to {pct(hi_b):+.1f}%   (24 teams, 600 team-level bootstrap draws)")
print(f"revert rate:  {100*g0:+.2f} points   95% interval {100*lo_g:+.2f} to {100*hi_g:+.2f}")
print("\nDecision rule, fixed before the pilot:")
print("  EXPAND   if the upper end of the cycle-time interval is below -3% AND the revert interval's upper end is below +2.0 points")
print("  STOP     if the cycle-time interval is wholly above -1% OR the revert interval's lower end is above +1.5 points")
print("  EXTEND   otherwise (collect more weeks, do not guess)")
if hi_b < np.log(0.97) and hi_g < 0.02: v = "EXPAND"
elif lo_b > np.log(0.99) or lo_g > 0.015: v = "STOP"
else: v = "EXTEND"
print("verdict:", v)

revert rate:  +0.77 points   95% interval +0.06 to +1.46

  • The estimate for cycle time is a fall of 7.8%, with an interval from 12.0% down to 3.2%. The upper end just clears the 3% threshold in the rule. Report it as "likely a real reduction of between 3% and 12%" and not as "8% faster".
  • Reverts rose by 0.77 points (interval +0.06 to +1.46). That is below the stop line, but its lower end is above zero. A careful report says that the data are compatible with a small increase in reverts, and that the pilot could not exclude one.
  • The verdict is EXPAND, with conditions: expand to waves C and D, repeat the error-analysis sample, and keep the guardrail running. The rule has a middle outcome, "extend", because the honest response to an ambiguous result is more data, not a guess.

The numbers come from a simulation in which the true effects are known, so they show how the analysis behaves, not what Harbourline will find. In your submission use whatever the analysis gives and report it the same way, including a result that contradicts the CTO's hopes (presenting a failure honestly).

The limitations section

  • One tool, one period. The study measures this tool as configured in these months. Capabilities and pricing change quickly, so the result has a shelf life of a few months.
  • Cycle time is a proxy. It does not capture the value of what was built, nor delivery outside pull requests (design, incident work, meetings).
  • Short-lived harm is hard to see. The revert guardrail catches fast failures; maintainability costs appear over months and are not measured here.
  • Co-occurring changes. Randomisation protects against most of these, but a company-wide change in the middle of the study (a new build system) would affect the weeks, not the teams, and the model removes week effects. A change that hits some teams harder is a threat.
  • Spillovers. Teams share libraries and reviewers, so control teams may benefit indirectly or lose reviewer time. Both would bias the estimate towards zero.
  • Not generalisable to other kinds of engineering. Evidence from experienced engineers on mature codebases (the METR setting) points in a different direction from evidence on newer engineers or tasks with less context (the Microsoft and Accenture studies), and Harbourline has a mix.

10People: adoption, trust and what you do with the time

Half the managers are enthusiastic and half are sceptical. Write the adoption plan on one page, say what training matters, and say how you would keep the measurement from changing the behaviour it measures.

Compare with a worked answer

An assistant helps only if people use it well, and tools like this fail in familiar ways: a good first week, then abandonment; or uncritical acceptance, then a bad release. The change-management section applies directly, and the law-firm case shows the same dynamics where every answer must be verified.

Before wave A

A two-hour session per team, run by a champion from that team: what the tool is good at (boilerplate, tests in an established style, unfamiliar APIs), where it fails (the stage-7 categories, with real examples), and how to ask for help. Publish the rules: authors own every line; no restricted repositories; label assisted pull requests.

During the wedge

A shared channel for tips and failures. A fortnightly 15-minute check-in with each team lead: what is blocking, what surprised you. Keep a visible log of changes made because of what people said, so the feedback feels worth giving.

After the pilot

Publish the full result, including the limits. Decide what the time saved is for, in writing, with the engineering directors. Expand by wave, and let sceptical teams see the numbers from their peers.

Calibrated trust. Training should aim at the reviewer's habit as much as the author's. A reviewer who sees a polished, plausible change tends to approve it. The checklist item from stage 7 ("does the test fail when the code is wrong?") is a small change of habit that pays for itself.

Do not turn measurement into surveillance. Say in writing: measurements are at team level; no individual usage is shared with managers; telemetry is not used in performance reviews. Without this promise, people will game the numbers or stop using the tool when they are watched.

Juniors and seniors. The Microsoft and Accenture trials found larger gains for less experienced developers; the METR trial found a slowdown for experienced ones on their own mature repositories. Do not assume one effect size. Where numbers allow, report subgroups as exploratory and say that they were not planned (an unplanned subgroup is a hypothesis).

What if time is saved? If the company wants "30% faster", it should say what shipping faster means: more features, fewer incidents, shorter lead time, or lower cost. Without an answer, saved minutes disappear into meetings and the programme is accused of failing even if the numbers are good.

Likely hostile questions.

  • "Forty-eight of sixty volunteers said they were much faster. Why do we need a study?" Because volunteers differ from the rest and people overestimate their speed; the strongest controlled study found the two went opposite ways. The survey is a reason to try the tool, not evidence of size.
  • "The wedge delays the late waves by months. Is that fair?" Four weeks between waves; the last wave starts in week 17 of 20. The delay is the price of an answer, and it is far shorter than the cost of a wrong decision on a $340,000 programme. If the tool shows harm early, later waves are protected; if it shows large benefit early, we shorten the gaps.
  • "What if the result is no effect?" Then we say "not distinguishable from zero, and not distinguishable from a modest gain either", look at the error-analysis findings for what to fix, and extend before deciding.

Rubric

Tick what your own submission (before reading the answers) covered. The first items are what a solid submission has; the later ones separate a strong one.

  • Restated the brief as a decision to be made, listed assumptions, and asked whether review is already the bottleneck.
  • Defined outcome, guardrail, experience and cost metrics, named how each misleads, and refused lines of code or acceptance rate as evidence of value.
  • Built a baseline from history before any tool, noticed skew and between-team differences, and used log or median cycle time at team level.
  • Used randomisation of teams in a staged rollout, and showed (or explained) why volunteer-first inflates the estimate.
  • Did a power or sample-size calculation and stated what size of effect the study can and cannot detect.
  • Treated the revert guardrail as noisy and slow, added leading indicators, and fixed a stopping rule in advance.
  • Analysed with intervals clustered by team and reported the result as a range, not a single number.
  • Drew the data flow and gave concrete controls: gateway, allowlist, secret scanning, sandboxed agents, a package allowlist, a kill switch.
  • Chose a tool with conditions that would change the choice and did not claim vendor statements as verified.
  • Built a gold set and error-analysis memo with a labelling rule, two readers, counts with intervals, and a first-failure category ranking.
  • Costed the programme including administration and onboarding, found break-even in hours, and included the review tax.
  • Marked every price and rate as an assumption and said which would be replaced by quotes or measurements.
  • Wrote a limitations section (shelf life, proxy metrics, spillovers, generalisation) and did not overclaim from other organisations' studies.
  • Planned people and trust: team-level measurement, no individual rankings, training that targets reviewers, and a statement of what the saved time is for.

Key takeaways

  • Developers' feeling of speed is not a measurement. Randomise the order in which teams get the tool and measure outcomes at team level.
  • Volunteer-first rollouts overstate the effect: in the simulation they showed 13% to 20% when the truth was 8%.
  • Know what your study can detect before you start; a pilot that cannot see a small harm should say so.
  • Break-even is a few minutes a week, but the extra review time can use it all. Measure review load.
  • Read the work. The commonest first failure in the worked sample was tests that restate the code, which no metric shows.
  • Write the decision rule before the data, report intervals, and state the limits.
