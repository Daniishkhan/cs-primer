Take-Home: Citizen Services Q&A

A city council wants a help service that answers residents' questions from its own published guidance. It must be accurate where the law and money bite, usable by people with poor literacy or a screen reader, and accountable: every answer traceable, every failure routed to a person. You have one working day to show you can scope it, prove it and defend it.

How a take-home studio works

This page simulates a take-home exercise of the kind described in Take-Homes, Portfolios & Interviews. You get a vague brief, a pile of sample material and a clock, and a reviewer will judge your judgement as much as your code. At each stage there is a Your turn question. Write your answer in the notes box first (it saves in this browser), then open the worked answer. The worked answers show the key artefacts of one excellent submission: the requirements page, the baseline, the gold set, the evaluation with intervals, the error-analysis memo, the limitations and the oral defence. Every script on this page was run and its output pasted; the council, its pages and its figures are invented, and the system under test is a small deterministic pipeline, so the numbers illustrate the method and are not benchmarks.

📐 The brief

An email arrives from the Head of Digital at Eastbridge City Council (fictional): "Our contact centre takes about 18,000 contacts a month and a lot of them ask the same things: bins, parking permits, council tax, Blue Badges. We have 340 pages of guidance on the website. Can you build us something that answers residents' questions from those pages? We do not want a repeat of what happened to other councils. We would like to see what you would do in a day, and how you would know it works."

  • Time box: 4 to 6 hours of work, then a three-minute oral defence with two reviewers. Do not build everything; choose, and say what you left out.
  • Deliverables: (1) a one-page requirements restatement with assumptions and the questions you would ask; (2) a baseline before any framework; (3) a small gold set with its labelling procedure; (4) an evaluation with confidence intervals; (5) a quality, cost and latency table; (6) an error-analysis memo naming the first failure in sampled traces; (7) a limitations section; (8) a three-minute oral defence.
  • Sample material you are given: a ZIP of 340 guidance pages (HTML, with an owner and a last-reviewed date in each page's footer), 1,200 anonymised questions from the contact centre's search box and web chat, the current contact-centre cost per contact, and a one-page equalities statement from the council.
  • Constraints: the content is public, but questions can contain personal details; the council's accessibility policy requires WCAG 2.2 level AA; residents include people with low literacy and people writing in languages other than English; the council's legal team has said the service "must not give advice, only information".
  • Builds on Public Sector & Government, Evaluation Plans Customers Trust, a city's chatbot gives illegal advice, grounding and abstention and grounded answers and citations.

Five questions for the client. (1) Who owns each page, and who can fix a page the assistant exposes as wrong? Notice what the page does not do: it does not name a model, a framework or a vector database. Those come after the evidence says what is needed. A reviewer reading this page learns that you can hear a fear and turn it into a scope.

2Is this the right tool, and what must never go to a model?

Triage the 1,200 sample questions into kinds. Which kinds should the assistant answer, which should it hand to a person immediately, and which should it decline? Name the three most dangerous failures and what law or policy each touches.

Compare with a worked answer

Use the feasibility checklist from Is AI the Right Tool?: error tolerance, data availability, risk. The risk is not spread evenly across questions, so the triage is by what a wrong answer does to the resident. After reading a random 100 questions, an excellent submission sorts them into five kinds. The shares are from my reading of 100 questions, with the caveat that 100 is small (an interval for a share of 50% at n = 100 runs from about 40% to 60%).

KindExampleShare (n = 100)Treatment

The three most dangerous failures, which become the critical errors of the evaluation:

  • A wrong official fact about money or rights, usually from a stale source: telling a resident a permit costs £60 when it costs £75, or that a bill is due on the wrong date. In law, a public body that publishes wrong guidance can be held to it or face complaints; the airline case in the retrieval course, the chatbot's promise, shows a tribunal treating a chatbot's words as the company's own.
  • An answer to a personal-case question from general text. A resident told "council tax support is usually approved within a month" will read that as a decision about their claim.
  • Failing a person in crisis, by returning a polite sentence about rent deposits to someone who will be homeless tonight. The failure is not accuracy but routing, and it is the one with the worst consequence.

Two legal anchors belong in the memo, stated with care because I am not a lawyer: in the EU, the AI Act's transparency duty (Article 50) means people must be told they are talking to an AI system unless that is obvious, and the 2026 "Digital Omnibus" (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026) moved some high-risk dates but did not remove that duty, so check the current text; in the UK, central government departments are expected to publish a record of algorithmic tools under the Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard, which a council would be wise to follow. The public-sector chapter has the dated detail. Neither law tells you what the assistant must answer; both tell you what must be visible about it.

The conclusion for the reviewer: an assistant is suitable for the first kind and the stale-source kind, once the stale pages are fixed, and must be built so that the other three kinds cannot reach the answering path. That is an architectural constraint, not a prompt instruction: a router in front of retrieval.

My checks on the pages: (1) duplicate or near-duplicate pages; (2) review dates older than twelve months; (3) pages with a price, date or deadline, because these are the facts that cannot be wrong; (4) pages that say what they cannot do (these become refusal rules); (5) reading grade (stage 9). My checks on the questions: (1) the share that mention a personal detail such as an address or reference number; (2) the share that are not questions at all ("parking"); (3) typos and text-speak ("wasnt"); (4) other languages; (5) the share that describe distress. A quick pass finds the following, all of which shape the design.

  • Short, ungrammatical questions are the norm. "my bin wasnt emptied what do I do" is typical. An evaluation made of tidy questions would flatter the system.
  • Some questions contain personal data. The log of questions is itself personal data, so the take-home pack arrived anonymised, and the pipeline must redact before it logs (see Lab 3, a PII redaction and audit pipeline).
  • Distress is in the data. A handful of the 1,200 questions are people in trouble. They are rare and decisive, so they get their own slice in the gold set.
  • The pages are not a knowledge base; they are a record of what the council once decided to publish. The system can only be as current as its content owners make it, which is why the design includes an alert to the page owner whenever the assistant is asked something the pages cannot answer.

4A baseline before any framework

Build the dumbest thing that could possibly work, in under an hour and about 20 lines, and score it on your first 33 labelled questions. What does it tell you that no later number will?

Compare with a worked answer

The baseline is what the council could build with a search box: index every sentence, rank by keyword weight (TF-IDF, see inverted indexes and BM25), and return the best-matching sentence for every question. No routing, no refusal, no model. It is the control: anything fancier must beat it on the same questions. The shared pieces live in engine.py (shown in stage 7) and data.py (the 14 pages and the 33 gold questions).

  • It answers 12 of 19 plain questions correctly, which is better than the reviewer may expect. A baseline that strong tells the council that the content is searchable and that much of the value is in good pages, not in a model.
  • It fails all three stale-trap questions, every time using the 2024 page, because the old page is a perfect lexical match. This failure is not a model failure; a larger model fed both pages would still have to choose.
  • It answers every personal-case, out-of-scope and urgent question, which is the real finding. A search box has no concept of "I should not answer this". The gap between answering 12 of the 19 plain questions and answering all 11 of the questions it should not touch is what the rest of the work is for.

Keep the baseline. It stays in the evaluation as the row every other row is compared against, and it is the answer to "what would a cheaper solution do?" in the defence.

5The gold set and how you labelled it

Design a gold set you can build in two hours and defend. How many questions, drawn how, labelled by whom, with what categories, and how do you check that two labellers agree?

Compare with a worked answer

The procedure follows Evaluation Plans Customers Trust and, for the labelling, the guidance in human labels. The point of the exercise is not size (two hours gives you a few hundred items at most) but that every item has a reason to be there and a label you can defend.

  1. Sample. Draw 150 questions at random from the 1,200 for the ordinary mix. Add 30 on purpose: ten that mention a price, date or deadline (the critical-fact slice), ten that describe distress or a personal case, and ten that no page can answer. Record that these were oversampled, because the headline numbers must be reported separately for the random half and the deliberate half.
  2. Label the kind (answerable, stale-trap, personal case, out of scope, urgent) and the supporting page and fact for answerable items: the exact phrase that a correct answer must contain, such as "every two weeks" or "75 pounds". This is a deliberately narrow definition of correct. It is checkable by a script, and a reviewer can see exactly what it rewards.
  3. Two labellers, independent first. Label the first 50 items twice, once myself and once by a colleague from the council who did not see my labels. Compute agreement with Cohen's kappa, discuss every disagreement, and update the guidelines, not just the labels. A kappa below about 0.6 means the categories are ambiguous and the metrics built on them cannot be trusted.
  4. Freeze and split. Keep a sealed half that is not looked at during development; tune on the other half. Version the file (gold-v1.jsonl) and write down the date and the page snapshot it was labelled against, because the pages change.

The take-home pack here uses 33 items, so the whole set is visible. The 19 plain questions, three stale-trap questions, four personal-case, four out-of-scope and three urgent questions mirror the proportions above after oversampling the rare kinds. Below is the labelling-agreement check, run on invented second-labeller disagreements. The disagreements are plausible ones: one labeller thinks quoting a whole page is not "an answer", another thinks a wrong answer to an out-of-scope question is wrong but not critical.

"""Do two reviewers label the same way? Cohen's kappa on 33 graded answers (labels are an invented illustration)."""
from collections import Counter
from data import PAGES
from engine import Index

B[8] = "wrong"        # B says the permit page dump is not an answer to "what do I need"
B[2] = "wrong"        # B says quoting a whole page for a bin question is not an answer
B[28] = "wrong"      # B grades the pension answer as wrong but not critical (severity disagreement)

A kappa of 0.67 is acceptable but not comfortable, and the three disagreements are informative. Items 3 and 9 show that the guideline never said whether a whole-page quote counts as an answer; the guideline now says it does if the page is short and the fact is in it, with the style judged separately. Item 29 shows that "critical" needs a definition: an answer to a question the service should not have answered is always critical, whatever the content. Both rulings go into the labelling guide, the kappa is recomputed on the next batch, and the changes are logged. That sequence (disagree, rule, write down, re-measure) is what the reviewer wants to see.

Size matters more than it looks. A gold set of 33 items cannot certify a low critical-error rate. Stage 6 shows the arithmetic. Say so in the memo rather than hiding it.

Three decisions here are the ones a reviewer looks for. First, critical errors have their own bar and are not averaged into accuracy: a system that is 95% correct and answers one crisis question with a rent-deposit sentence has failed. Second, over-refusal is a metric, not a footnote, because it is the cost of the safety rules and the council should see it. Third, the pass rule has three outcomes: pass, fail, and inconclusive (the interval spans the bar), so that a small test set cannot be read as a green light.

The arithmetic that makes "inconclusive" a real outcome is the rule of three: if you observe zero failures in n independent trials, the one-sided 95% upper bound on the failure rate is about 3 / n. The script below (the end of s4_v2.py, shown in stage 8) prints the sizes. To show a critical-error rate below 2% with zero observed failures you need about 150 items that could trigger a critical error; to show below 1% you need about 300. Our 33 items give a bound of 9.1%. In the real take-home the gold set of 180 supports the 2% claim only if nothing fails, and the report should say so.

One more commitment belongs here: the metrics are fixed before the run. Writing "we will change the bar if the result is close" is the most common way for a customer to stop trusting a number.

7Build version 1 and measure it

Design the smallest architecture that stops the three dangerous failure kinds from reaching the answering path. Where does each safeguard live, and which ones can a prompt not provide?

The pipeline is small enough to read in full. This is the code behind every number on this page.

"""A deliberately small citizen-services Q&A pipeline: route, retrieve, quote, abstain."""
import math, re
from collections import Counter
from data import PAGES

STOP = set("a an the of to is are in on for and or do does i my me we you your it can be how what when much many by at with if from have has that this will there not no".split())
def toks(s):
    return [w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9']+", s.lower()) if w not in STOP]

def sentences(text):
    return [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.;])\s+", text) if s.strip()]

class Index:
    def __init__(self, pages):
        self.units = []                              # (page_id, sentence, tokens, reviewed)
        for pid, title, reviewed, text in pages:
            for s in sentences(text):
                self.units.append((pid, s, toks(title + " " + s), reviewed))
        df = Counter(t for u in self.units for t in set(u[2]))
        n = len(self.units)
        self.idf = {t: math.log((n + 1) / (c + 0.5)) for t, c in df.items()}
    def search(self, q, k=3):
        qt = set(toks(q))
        scored = []
        for pid, s, t, rev in self.units:
            tf = Counter(t)
            sc = sum(self.idf.get(w, 0) * (1 + math.log(tf[w])) for w in qt if w in tf)
            sc /= math.sqrt(len(t) + 3)
            scored.append((sc, pid, s, rev))
        return sorted(scored, reverse=True)[:k]

PERSONAL = re.compile(r"\b(my (application|claim|permit|badge|refund|payment)|has my|was my|why was my|status of my|i paid)\b", re.I)
URGENT = re.compile(r"\b(evict|nowhere to sleep|changed the locks|cant cope|can't cope|homeless|no food)\b", re.I)

URGENT2 = re.compile(r"\b(evict\w*|nowhere to sleep|changed the locks|cant cope|can't cope|homeless\w*|no food)\b", re.I)
PAGE_TEXT = {p[0]: p[3] for p in PAGES}

def answer(index, q, tau=0.9, drop_superseded=True, v2=False):
    """Returns (route, text, source_page, sources_considered)."""
    if (URGENT2 if v2 else URGENT).search(q):
        return "urgent", "If you may lose your home, call the housing advice line today. A person will help you.", "housing-help", []
    if PERSONAL.search(q):
        return "handoff", "I cannot see your account. Sign in to your account or call us and an officer will check.", None, []
    hits = index.search(q)
    if drop_superseded:
        hits = [h for h in hits if not h[1].endswith("-old")]
    score, pid, sent, rev = hits[0]
    if score < tau:
        return "decline", "I cannot answer that from the council's guidance. You can ask a person on 0300 000 0000.", None, hits
    if v2:                                           # quote the whole short page, not one sentence
        sent = PAGE_TEXT[pid]
    return "answer", sent, pid, hits

Four design choices to defend. The router uses plain rules, not a model, because the cost of missing an urgent question is high, the patterns are few and a rule can be tested and read by the council's legal team; a model-based classifier can sit behind it later and is judged against the rules. The index excludes superseded pages (in a real system, by a metadata field the content team controls, not by a name suffix). The answer is a quotation, not a paraphrase, so a wrong statement can only be a wrong choice of quotation. A model that rewrites in plain language is a later addition and each addition is measured against this version. Below a score threshold it declines, and the threshold is a dial, so the next script sweeps it and prints the cost of each setting.

The evaluation script grades every output on the gold set into four classes: correct, wrong (a miss that is not dangerous), critical (one of the three kinds in stage 2) and over-refused.

"""Evaluate the pipeline on the gold set: outcome classes, a threshold sweep, and honest intervals."""
import math
from data import PAGES, GOLD
from engine import Index, answer

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return max(0, c - h), min(1, c + h)

def grade(index, tau, drop=True, v2=False):
    out = []
    for gid, q, kind, page, fact in GOLD:
        route, text, src, hits = answer(index, q, tau, drop, v2)
        if kind in ("ans", "stale"):
            if route == "answer":
                cls = "correct" if (src == page and fact in text) else ("critical" if kind == "stale" and src != page else "wrong")
            else:
                cls = "over-refused"
        elif kind == "urgent":
            cls = "correct" if route == "urgent" else "critical"
        elif kind == "personal":
            cls = "correct" if route == "handoff" else "critical"
        else:  # oos
            cls = "correct" if route == "decline" else "critical"
        out.append((gid, q, kind, route, src, text, cls))
    return out

def main():
    index = Index(PAGES)
    print("tau   correct  wrong  critical  over-refused   critical rate (95% Wilson)")
    for tau in (0.0, 0.5, 0.9, 1.3, 1.7, 2.2):
        r = grade(index, tau)
        c = {k: sum(x[6] == k for x in r) for k in ("correct", "wrong", "critical", "over-refused")}
        lo, hi = wilson(c["critical"], len(r))
        print(f"{tau:3.1f}   {c['correct']:5}   {c['wrong']:5}  {c['critical']:7}  {c['over-refused']:11}    {c['critical']/len(r):5.1%} ({lo:4.1%} to {hi:4.1%})")

    print("\nWith the superseded 2024 page left in the index (tau 0.9):")
    r = grade(index, 0.9, drop=False)

          sum(1 for x in r if x[2] == "stale" and x[4] == "permit-old"))
    print("\nFailures at tau 0.9 (the memo reads these):")
    for gid, q, kind, route, src, text, cls in grade(index, 0.9):
        if cls != "correct":
            print(f"  {gid} [{kind}] {cls}: {q!r}\n      route={route} source={src}\n      said: {text[:110]}")

11Limitations, rollout and monitoring

Write the limitations section as if you were the customer's risk officer reading it. Then plan a staged rollout and the first monitoring dashboard.

Compare with a worked answer

Limitations (the part reviewers read twice; writing for customers explains why plain statements earn trust).

  • The evidence is thin. 33 items cannot certify a critical-error rate below about 9%; the 180-item set planned for the real engagement supports a 2% claim only with zero failures. Until then the service must run with a human route staffed.
  • The corpus is a 14-page slice. Behaviour on 340 pages, with near-duplicates and long pages, is unmeasured. Whole-page quoting works because these pages are short; it will not work for long ones.
  • The router is rules. It will miss distress phrased in ways I did not list, in other languages, or through misspelling. Recall on urgent items is 3 of 3 in the set after the fix; that says little about recall in the wild. A weekly review of a random sample of declined and answered questions is the real control.
  • Quoting a page is only as good as the page. The service surfaces stale content faster than before. Every page needs an owner and a review date enforced by the content system.
  • The labelling is from two people, kappa 0.67 on a first batch, and the "correct" definition is narrow: it checks a phrase, not whether the resident understood.
  • Not measured at all: real residents, other languages beyond a first count, assistive-technology users, adversarial users (the injection test is minimal because this version never follows instructions in text), and any long-run drift.

Rollout follows the gates in from proof of concept to production, in four stages, each with an exit criterion. (1) Shadow: run on live questions without showing answers, and have an officer grade 200 for critical errors. (2) Internal: give it to contact-centre staff as a suggestion tool and measure how often they use it unedited. (3) Limited public: one topic (bins and permits, the low-risk pages) for 10% of web visitors with the randomised comparison above. (4) Widen, topic by topic, only when the previous stage's critical-error count was zero and the interval is below the bar. The security review (security reviews for AI systems) starts in week one, not after stage 3.

Monitoring (reliability vocabulary from SLOs and error budgets, applied to answers): a weekly sample of 50 answered and 50 declined questions read by an officer; the decline rate and the hand-off rate by day; seven-day recontact; the oldest review date among pages cited in the last week; and any answer where the cited page changed after the answer was given. An alert goes to the page owner when a question cannot be answered, because a content gap found by a resident is a bug in the guidance, not in the assistant.

How a senior engineer would summarise this

"The brief says the council does not want a repeat of other councils, so I turned that into three critical errors: a wrong money or rights fact, an answer to a personal-case question, and failing someone in crisis. Notice the shape: fear turned into critical errors → baseline → structure that makes the errors unreachable → first failure per trace → honest interval → money as resolution → what I did not measure.

Why not put all 340 pages in a prompt? It may work for a demo, and the experiment is cheap, so a good answer says "I would run it as another row in the table". Three reasons it is not the starting design: the router and stale-page problems are the same either way (the model still has to pick between a 2024 and a 2026 price); a long prompt makes every answer cost the whole corpus in input tokens (see long context versus retrieval), and evidence for models using the middle of long inputs is mixed (lost in the middle); and the audit trail is weaker, because it is harder to say which page an answer rested on. What would stop a launch? One unexplained critical error in the shadow stage, a router recall below 100% on a distress test set, no named page owners, or a council unwilling to staff the human route.

Grading rubric

Tick what your own submission covered before reading the worked answers. The first items are what a solid submission has; the later ones separate a strong submission from an excellent one.

  • Restated the brief in one page with assumptions and questions, and turned "do not repeat what happened elsewhere" into measurable requirements.
  • Identified the rung of automation (inform, not decide) and kept personal-case and decision questions out of the answering path.
  • Defined critical errors separately from accuracy, and gave them their own bar.
  • Built a baseline before any framework and reported what it already achieves and what it cannot do.
  • Built a gold set from the customer's questions, oversampling the rare dangerous kinds and reporting them as slices.
  • Wrote a labelling procedure, labelled twice, measured agreement and recorded the rulings that resolved disagreements.
  • Reported confidence intervals, used a paired comparison between versions and showed the sample size needed for the claim made.
  • Handled the stale or superseded source in the architecture (index metadata, dates), not in the prompt.
  • Wrote an error-analysis memo naming the first failure in each sampled trace, grouped and ranked, and fixed the largest group.
  • Treated over-refusal as a measured cost and showed the safety versus coverage trade-off as a dial the customer chooses.
  • Gave accessibility and accountability concrete checks and artefacts (reading level, screen-reader test, trace record, transparency record), and said which were not done.
  • Distinguished answered, resolved and deflected in the savings case, gave a low, base and high scenario and a randomised pilot design.
  • Wrote a limitations section a risk officer would respect, with a staged rollout and exit criteria.
  • Delivered a three-minute defence that leads with the decision, uses numbers correctly and says what would stop a launch.

Key takeaways

  • Turn the customer's fear into a short list of critical errors with their own bar; do not average them into accuracy.
  • Safeguards for urgent, personal and out-of-scope questions are architecture (a router before search), not prompt wording.
  • A keyword baseline often does half the job and shows where the real risk is: the questions it must refuse.
  • Superseded and stale pages are a content-ownership problem that surfaces as a model problem; fix it in the index.
  • A small test set cannot certify a low error rate: with zero failures in n items the bound is about 3 / n. Say "inconclusive" when it is.
  • Read failing traces and name the first failure; here most were not about the model at all, and one was a regex.
  • Accessibility and accountability are checks and artefacts, plus an honest list of what was not tested.
  • Savings depend on resolution, not deflection, so the first pilot number is seven-day recontact against a control.
