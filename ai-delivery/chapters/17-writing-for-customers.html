Writing for Customers

The people paying for an AI system rarely read the code or the traces. They read what you write: the weekly status report, the memo that explains a number, and the message you send when something has gone wrong. This chapter teaches how to put the answer first, how to make a number mean what it says, and how to tell a customer about a failure so that they trust you more afterwards, not less.

Why this matters

A delivery engagement succeeds or fails in documents as much as in code. The requirements document set the bar, the evaluation plan measured against it, and the production gates decided whether to launch. Each of those only works if the customer's sponsor, owner and risk officer can read the evidence and act on it. Teams with excellent engineering still lose engagements because a status report hid a problem until it was large, because "accuracy improved" claimed more than the data supported, or because a failure arrived as a surprise instead of a message. After this chapter you can write a status report with a rating rule and a decision request, an analysis memo whose claims match its evidence, and a failure notice that follows a structure the reader can rely on. These are also the writing samples that hiring teams ask for (see the next chapter), because they show judgement in a way code alone does not.

Start from the reader's next action

Most weak customer writing has the same cause: the author wrote down what they did, in the order they did it, because that is how it was remembered. The reader did not ask for a diary. They want to know what is true now, whether to worry, and whether they must do anything. Begin every document by deciding who will read it and what you want them to do afterwards.

For an AI delivery engagement there are usually five kinds of reader, and one document often reaches several.

ReaderWhat they holdWhat they need from youTypical document
SponsorThe budget and the outcomeIs it on track, what will it cost, what decision is needed from meStatus report, decision memo

The structure that serves all of them is the same one journalists and military staff use, usually called BLUF, "bottom line up front": the conclusion and the request come first, the evidence second, the method last. Barbara Minto's The Pyramid Principle (1987) describes it as a pyramid with the answer at the apex and supporting groups beneath. A busy sponsor who reads only the first paragraph should still know the situation and the decision required. A sceptical reviewer who reads to the end should find everything needed to check the claim.

A one-sentence test keeps you honest. Before writing, finish the sentence "After reading this, [named person] will [do or decide]...". If you cannot, the document has no purpose yet. If the sentence is "know what we did", the purpose is weak and a shorter message will do.

The status report

A status report is a short, regular document that tells the customer where the engagement stands against the plan. Its value is not information; the customer could ask for that. Its value is that it is predictable. A sponsor who gets a one-page report on the same day each week stops wondering, and notices immediately when something changes. Hamel Husain, writing about how to work with stakeholders on AI products, recommends regular short updates and credits Eugene Yan's "fifteen-five": a weekly update that takes fifteen minutes to write and five to read. The discipline is in the second number. Respect the reader's five minutes.

Anatomy of one page

Three of these need more explanation than the diagram gives.

The rating needs a rule. A red, amber, green rating (RAG) means nothing until its meaning is written down and agreed. A workable rule for a delivery engagement is: green means every committed outcome is on its date and no gate is at risk; amber means at least one outcome or gate is at risk, you have a mitigation, and no decision is needed from the customer yet; red means a date will be missed or a gate will fail unless the customer decides or provides something. With a rule, a change from green to amber is a fact the reader can verify, not a mood. Without one you get what project managers call a watermelon report: green on the outside, red on the inside, because nobody wants to be the first to change the colour. A watermelon report is worse than a late one, because the surprise arrives with the bill.

Report outcomes, not activity. "Worked on retrieval" is activity. "Retrieval returns the right page in the top five for 171 of 200 gold questions (85.5%), up from 158 (79.0%) after adding the reranker" is an outcome. The reader cannot act on activity, and a long list of it hides the absence of results. If a week produced no outcome, say so and say why.

The decision box is the reason the report exists. If you need nothing from the reader, a message is enough. When you do, write the decision as a question with an owner and a date, and say what happens if it is not made: "Please confirm by Friday 16 October whether reviewers may label 40 table-heavy contracts. Without this, the table-clause fix cannot be tested before the 21 October gate." Vague requests ("we'd appreciate guidance on data") get vague, late replies.

Showing progress when the headline number is not there yet

In the early weeks of an AI engagement the headline number (say, "acceptable answers") may be low, and a flat low number for a month frightens sponsors. Husain suggests a capability funnel: break the task into stages from "the system returns an answer" to "a reviewer accepts it without edits" and report the fraction that passes each stage. Progress then shows up in the early stages first, and the point where cases drop out shows where the work is. The chart below uses illustrative numbers for a document-question system on a gold set of 200 cases.

Define each stage precisely, and make the stages nested so that the bars can only shrink. Keep the stages the same from week to week; if you add one, say so. A funnel also makes it hard to hide behind a single number, because anyone can see which stage a fix improved.

A mechanical check before you send

Many weaknesses in a status report can be found by a short program. The one below checks a draft for vague quantifiers, a rating without a stated criterion, percentages without a denominator, no request for the reader and no risk. It is crude (it flags words, not meaning), but it catches the habits that make reports weak, and running it on your own drafts is a quick way to notice them. The draft is typical of a first attempt.

# A tiny "report linter": mechanical checks a reviewer would make of a weekly status report.
import re
DRAFT = """Status: Green.
We made great progress this week and accuracy improved significantly to 91%.
The team is confident the system will be ready soon. Some issues remain.
Next week we will continue working on improvements."""

CHECKS = [
 ("vague quantifier", r"\b(significantly|great progress|some issues|soon|improvements|a lot)\b"),
 ("rating with no criterion", r"^Status: (Green|Amber|Red)\.?$"),
]
def lint(text):
    problems = []
    for name, pat in CHECKS:
        for m in re.finditer(pat, text, flags=re.I | re.M):
            problems.append(f"{name}: '{m.group(0)}'")
    for m in re.finditer(r"(\d+(?:\.\d+)?)%", text):
        window = text[max(0, m.start() - 60): m.end() + 60]
        if not re.search(r"\bn ?= ?\d+|\d+ (cases|documents|items|of \d+)|\d+/\d+", window):
            problems.append(f"number without denominator: '{m.group(0)}'")
    if not re.search(r"\b(decision|we need you to|please confirm|by \d{1,2} [A-Z][a-z]+)\b", text, flags=re.I):
        problems.append("no decision or request for the reader, and no date")
    if not re.search(r"\b(risk|blocked|did not|failed|missed)\b", text, flags=re.I):
        problems.append("no risk, miss or blocker stated")
    return problems

for p in lint(DRAFT):
    print("-", p)

- vague quantifier: 'significantly'
- vague quantifier: 'soon'
- vague quantifier: 'Some issues'
- vague quantifier: 'improvements'
- rating with no criterion: 'Status: Green.'
- number without denominator: '91%'
- no decision or request for the reader, and no date
- no risk, miss or blocker stated

Each line of the output is a sentence the sponsor cannot act on. "Great progress" and "significantly" assert an amount without giving it. "91%" has no denominator: is it 91 of 100 or 10 of 11? "Status: Green" has no rule behind it. There is no request, and no risk is named, so the reader cannot tell whether the author is looking. The repaired version would read, in part: "Amber. Quality gate at risk: reviewer-accepted answers are 131 of 200 (65.5%; 95% interval 58.7 to 71.7%) against a signed bar of 80%. Largest loss is figure errors in table-heavy documents (25 cases). Decision needed from the operations lead by Friday 16 October: allow reviewers to label 40 more table documents." Every phrase can be checked.

Common misconception

"Reporting problems early makes the customer lose confidence." The evidence from engineering practice points the other way. Google's SRE book on postmortem culture makes the argument for blameless, prompt reporting inside a team, and the same logic holds between supplier and customer: a problem reported while it is small gives the customer time to choose, and a problem discovered by the customer is read as concealment whatever your intent was. What damages confidence is the surprise, the vagueness and the sense that the supplier is managing perceptions instead of telling the truth.

The analysis memo

A status report says where you are. An analysis memo answers one question in depth: why accuracy dropped on contracts with tables, whether the new retrieval model is better than the old one, whether the system is safe to extend to a second department. It is the document that carries your judgement, and it is the form most often requested as a writing sample, so it repays careful structure. The shape below fits most memos, and it follows the pyramid above.

  1. Question and answer. One sentence each. "Question: is the new reranker better than the current one on our 200 gold questions? Answer: it is higher by 4 points but we cannot yet distinguish that from noise."
  2. Recommendation. What you propose, with the option you did not choose and why. If the memo informs a decision, say who decides and by when.
  3. Evidence. The numbers, with counts, denominators and intervals; two or three concrete examples of the system succeeding and failing, copied from real traces.
  4. Method. What you measured, on which cases, scored by whom, with what settings. Enough for someone else to repeat it; the analysis chapters error analysis and evaluation statistics give the technique.
  5. Limits. What the evidence does not cover: cases you did not sample, a metric that is only a proxy, a reviewer who was not blind to the system. This section is a strength. A reader who sees honest limits trusts the rest.
  6. Next steps. What would change your answer, and the cheapest test that would show it.

Making a number mean what it says

The most common error in an AI memo is a claim stronger than the data. On a gold set of 80 cases, a system that scores 62 against a baseline's 59 has not "improved". The difference of three cases is well inside what a different sample of 80 would change. A report that says otherwise will be overturned by the next evaluation, and the customer will remember. Three habits fix this.

First, give the count and the denominator, then the percentage: "62 of 80 (77.5%)". Second, give an interval: for a proportion, the Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927) behaves well at small sample sizes and near 0% or 100%, where the simple textbook interval breaks down. Third, compare on the same cases and give an interval for the difference, not just two intervals side by side, because two overlapping intervals do not by themselves settle whether the systems differ. The script computes all three for an invented comparison (a baseline and a new system scored on the same 80 cases, with a paired bootstrap for the difference).

# What can a status report claim? Baseline vs new system on the same gold set.
import math, random
def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z*z/n
    c = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

def paired_bootstrap(a, b, reps=5000, seed=3):
    rng = random.Random(seed); n = len(a); diffs = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        idx = [rng.randrange(n) for _ in range(n)]
        diffs.append(sum(b[i] - a[i] for i in idx) / n)
    diffs.sort()
    return diffs[int(.025*reps)], diffs[int(.975*reps)]

rng = random.Random(11)
n = 80
baseline = [1 if rng.random() < 0.70 else 0 for _ in range(n)]
# new system fixes some baseline failures and breaks a few passes
system = [1 if (b == 0 and rng.random() < 0.35) or (b == 1 and rng.random() < 0.93) else 0 for b in baseline]
for name, r in (("baseline", baseline), ("new system", system)):
    lo, hi = wilson(sum(r), n)
    print(f"{name:11s} {sum(r)}/{n} = {sum(r)/n:.1%}   95% interval {lo:.1%} to {hi:.1%}")
lo, hi = paired_bootstrap(baseline, system)
print(f"paired difference {(sum(system)-sum(baseline))/n:+.1%}   95% interval {lo:+.1%} to {hi:+.1%}")
if lo > 0:
    print("The interval excludes zero: 'improved' is supportable.")
else:
    print("The interval includes zero: write 'higher on this set, not yet distinguishable from noise'.")
baseline    59/80 = 73.8%   95% interval 63.2% to 82.1%
new system  62/80 = 77.5%   95% interval 67.2% to 85.3%
paired difference +3.8%   95% interval -6.2% to +13.8%
The interval includes zero: write 'higher on this set, not yet distinguishable from noise'.

The system scored 62 of 80 against the baseline's 59 of 80, a 3.8-point gap. The 95% interval for the difference runs from -6.2 to +13.8 points, so the data are compatible with the new system being a little worse, the same or considerably better. The honest sentence is the one the script prints. Notice that this is not a failure to report: it is a precise statement that tells the reader what would resolve the question. More cases would narrow the interval (roughly, four times the cases halve its width), and the memo should say what that would cost. Try the same arithmetic interactively.

The zero-failures row uses the rule of three: with no failures in n independent trials, the 95% upper bound on the true failure rate is about 3/n, which for 60 trials is 5%. The security review chapter uses the same rule for red-team results.

Analogy: a weather forecast

"Seventy per cent chance of rain" is useful because it carries its own uncertainty and can be checked later: of all the days that were forecast at 70%, about seven in ten were wet. A forecaster who said "it will rain" and was wrong half the time would be ignored. A memo that states a number with its interval is making a forecast about what a larger test would show, and invites the same check.

Where it breaks down: a forecaster's probability is calibrated against thousands of past days. Your interval describes only sampling noise from one gold set; it says nothing about whether the gold set resembles next month's traffic. The limits section must cover that.

Presenting a failure honestly

Every real engagement has a failure: a metric falls after a model change, a pilot misses its date, a customer finds an answer that is wrong and embarrassing. How you handle it often matters more for the relationship than the failure itself. There are cases on the site where the handling made the difference. In the MyCity case, a government chatbot gave advice that contradicted the law, and the response to the criticism became part of the story. In the Air Canada chatbot case, a company argued that its chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own statements, and a tribunal did not accept that. In the Klarna case, a company that had made strong public claims about automation later brought human agents back. None of these is a model of how to write a notice, but each shows that the customer, the public and the court will hold the supplier responsible for what the system said, and that defensiveness cost more than candour.

The structure of a failure notice

Under pressure, people write failure notices badly: too long, too defensive, or so soothing that nothing is said. A fixed structure removes the decisions and makes it easy to be complete. Step through the example below, an illustrative scenario for a contract-review assistant in a pilot. The numbers are invented for the example.

Step through

A failure notice in six parts

  1. The subject line carries the news. The fact and the action already taken, in one line. A reader who opens nothing else knows the situation. Avoid "Update" or "Quick note".
  2. State what happened as measurements. Counts, the comparison and the fact that the cases are the same. No adjectives, no apology yet.
  3. Say who was affected, and be exact about "no one". If nothing reached customers, say so and say why that is true (the mode of operation), not merely that it was lucky. If something did, say what and to whom.
  4. Separate what you know from what you do not. Include the evidence for what you know, and name the open questions. This is the part that earns trust, because it shows you are not guessing in either direction.
  5. Describe the containment and the fix with dates. Containment first (what stops further harm today), then the fix and its date, and the test that will stop a repeat.
  6. Make a clear request, and promise the next message. The decision or help you need, with a date, and when the customer will hear from you again. Keep that promise even when there is no news.

Four habits make the notice work. Say it early. Send it when you know the facts and the containment, not when you know the cause; the cause can follow. Do not blame the model. "The model hallucinated" describes a mechanism, not a responsibility. The customer hired you for the system, including its model, its checks and its monitoring, so describe what your system did and what you will change. Do not over-apologise or reassure. One plain acknowledgement is enough; promises you cannot keep ("this will never happen again") are the next failure. Keep the promised cadence. A short "no change, next update Tuesday" message is worth more than silence.

After the incident is contained, write the longer review: timeline, contributing causes, what worked, what you will change, with owners and dates. The format in postmortems and the AI incidents chapter fit well. Share it with the customer. A supplier that publishes its own account of a failure is demonstrating exactly the behaviour a customer wants in a long-term partner.

Recommendations the reader can accept

Many documents end with a recommendation: continue to production, extend scope, stop. A decision memo makes that choice easy to take, and hard to take blindly. Give two or three real options, including "do nothing" or "stop", and state for each the cost, the main risk and the evidence behind it. State your recommendation and the single fact that would change it.

Style that respects the reader

The details of prose matter, because every unclear sentence costs the reader time and every vague one hides a decision. The following habits cover most of what matters. The UK government's guidance on writing for GOV.UK and the US plain language guidelines (both in the reading list) say the same things at more length.

  • One idea per paragraph, the point in the first sentence. A reader skimming first sentences should still follow the argument.
  • Define terms the first time, in the customer's words. If the customer says "referral" and you say "escalation", use theirs. Avoid saying "hallucination" to a non-technical reader without explaining: "the system stated a figure that is not in the document" is clearer and more accurate.
  • Numbers with units, denominators and dates. "Latency fell" becomes "p95 latency fell from 4.1 s to 2.6 s on the 3,000 requests of 1 to 7 October".
  • Show examples. Two real inputs and outputs, one good, one bad, convey more than a paragraph of description. Redact personal data first (see the PII pipeline lab).
  • Tables for comparison, prose for argument. A table of options is easier to scan than three paragraphs. A chain of reasoning needs sentences.
  • One chart, one message. Title the chart with its conclusion ("Table documents account for most misses"), label the axes, start bar charts at zero and mark uncertainty. Never plot a three-point difference so that it looks like a cliff.
  • Plain, short sentences, without hype. Words such as "revolutionary", "seamless" and "state of the art" make a careful reader suspect the evidence is thin.
  • Have someone outside the project read it. If they cannot say what you want from them, rewrite the top.

A final practice concerns the numbers themselves. Generate every number in a report from the evaluation run, not from memory. Have the harness write a small file of results (counts, intervals, the run's commit and date) and paste from it, or better, template the report from it. A figure retyped by hand is a figure that will be wrong in one week out of fifty. If you use a language model to draft prose, treat its output as you would any junior's draft: you remain responsible for every claim, and any number it did not copy from your file should be deleted. For notes on writing for engineers rather than customers, see Writing for Engineers.

Systems connection

Customer reports borrow from operations practice. The rating rule mirrors error-budget policy: a stated threshold decides what happens next, so nobody argues about colour. The failure notice follows the structure of incident communication: say what is known, mitigate before diagnosing, and keep a cadence. The longer review is a blameless postmortem. Writing is part of the system you ship, and it needs the same version control, review and testing as the code.

Try it yourself

Take the last status update you wrote (for work, a course project, or a lab) and spend thirty minutes on it.

  1. Save it to a text file and adapt lint_report.py to your own habits: add the vague words you tend to use. How many hits?
  2. Rewrite it into the six boxes of the anatomy figure. Which box was hardest to fill, and what does that say about the project?
  3. For one number in it, work out the denominator and the 95% Wilson interval with the formula from claims.py. Does the sentence still say what you meant?
  4. Give it to a friend who does not know the project. Ask them: what do you think I want you to do? Rewrite until they are right.

Key takeaways

  • Write for a named reader and their next action. Put the answer and the ask first, then the evidence, then the method and limits.
  • A status report is short, regular and predictable. The rating follows a written rule, progress is stated as outcomes, risks are never empty, and the decision request has an owner and a date.
  • A capability funnel shows progress and shows where cases drop out, even when the headline number is still low.
  • A claim must match its evidence: give counts, denominators and intervals, compare on the same cases, and use the weaker verb when the interval includes zero.
  • A failure notice has six parts: the news, the measurements, the impact, what is known and unknown, containment and fix with dates, and the request with the next update. Send it early, and do not blame the model.
  • Give decision-makers real options and a recommendation, and record what they decided. Generate every number from the run, not from memory.

Check your understanding

A weekly status report begins with three paragraphs of activity and ends with "Status: Green". The retrieval metric has been flat for a month and a security review is two weeks late. What is the most serious flaw?

  1. It is too long.
  2. It does not mention the model by name.
  3. The rating has no written rule, so green hides a late gate and a flat metric (a watermelon report), and the reader cannot see what to decide.
  4. It should use red for everything.
Length is a style problem; the structural flaw is a rating that does not follow a rule and so conceals problems. With a rule (amber when a gate is at risk, red when the customer must decide) the late review would have forced a change of colour and a decision request.

A new system passes 62 of 80 gold cases against the baseline's 59 of 80, and the 95% interval for the difference is -6 to +14 points. Which sentence belongs in the report?

  1. "The new system scored higher on this set (77.5% against 73.8%), but the difference is within noise at 80 cases; we recommend labelling more cases before deciding."
  2. "The new system improved accuracy by nearly four points."
  3. "The two systems are identical."
  4. "The new system is worse."
The interval includes both improvement and harm, so neither "improved" nor "worse" is supported, and "identical" claims more than the data can show. The first option states the observed values, the uncertainty and the next step.

Which is the best first sentence of a failure notice sent the day after a metric fell?

  1. "We apologise for any inconvenience and are looking into an issue."
  2. "The model hallucinated on some documents, which is a known limitation of LLMs."
  3. "After a thorough investigation we have identified the root cause."
  4. "High-risk clause recall fell from 94% to 76% on this week's check, so we have paused automatic flagging for table documents; no output reached your customers."
The best opening gives the fact, the measurement and the containment, and says who was affected. The first is vague, the second blames the mechanism instead of owning the system, and the third claims a finished investigation that the facts of the day do not support.

In a failure notice, why should you separate "what we know" from "what we do not know"?

  1. To make the notice longer and more thorough-looking.
  2. Because it shows the reader which statements are evidenced and which are open, so they do not treat a guess as a fact or assume you are hiding the rest.
  3. Because legal teams require it.
  4. To postpone naming a cause indefinitely.
The split exists for the reader's benefit: it lets them calibrate their trust and see what you will test next. It is not about length or legal form, and naming open questions should come with a date for answering them.

A capability funnel shows 98% of cases return an answer, 94% are in the right format, 82% have correct figures, and 66% are accepted by a reviewer. Where should the next two weeks of effort go first?

  1. To the first stage, because it is the first to fail.
  2. To the last stage, because it is the headline number.
  3. To the step between format and correct figures, because the largest drop shows where most cases are lost.
  4. To the format check, because it is the easiest to improve.
The funnel's purpose is to locate the largest loss. The drop from 94% to 82% is 12 points, the largest of the four steps (the others are 4, 7 and 9). Ease of fixing matters too, but the funnel says where the biggest pool of failures is, and the next step is to read those cases.

Why should report numbers be generated from the evaluation run rather than typed in from memory?

  1. Typed numbers are always rounded wrongly.
  2. Customers prefer automatically generated documents.
  3. It makes the report shorter.
  4. A retyped number can drift from the run that produced it, and a templated number can be traced to a commit and date when someone checks.
The aim is traceability and avoiding silent transcription errors. Rounding, customer taste and length are not the reason. If a figure in a memo cannot be traced to a run, the reader has to take it on trust.
