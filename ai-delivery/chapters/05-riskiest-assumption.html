Prototype the Riskiest Assumption

A project is a stack of bets, and the bet most likely to be wrong is rarely the one the team enjoys testing. This chapter shows how to list the bets, pick the one that could sink the project, and build the smallest thing that gives it a fair chance to fail, in days and not months, with the pass and stop rules written down before the first run.

Why this matters

After discovery, requirements and triage you have a promising task and a customer who wants to see something. The natural next move is to build the impressive part: a polished demo of the model doing its trick. That demo proves the model can do the trick on inputs you chose, and tells the customer nothing about whether the project will work. Applied AI projects usually die of a different cause: nobody was allowed to send the data to the model, the scans were unreadable, the experts disagreed about the right answer, or the users never opened the tool. After this chapter you will be able to write a project's assumptions as testable statements, rank them by how much a test would teach you per day, design a prototype whose only job is to answer one question, fix the pass, sharpen and stop rules before running it, size the test so a pass means something, and report a negative result as a success of the method.

A project is a stack of bets

Suppose a fictional mid-sized insurer, Northgate Mutual, asks you to build an assistant for its property-damage claims team. An adjuster opens a claim (an email, a form, some photographs) and the assistant should write a short summary and say whether the water damage described appears to be covered by the customer's policy. The customer's sponsor has seen impressive demonstrations and wants it live next quarter.

Count what must be true for that project to succeed. Adjusters must trust a pre-filled summary enough to use it. A model must be able to apply the policy wording to a messy description and reach the same decision as a senior adjuster. The scanned forms must be readable. Claim text must be allowed to reach whatever model you use. The claims system must offer some way to write the result back. The cost per claim must fit the budget. Each of these is an assumption: a belief the plan depends on that nobody has checked. Eric Ries called the most dangerous ones leap-of-faith assumptions in The Lean Startup (2011): the beliefs that, if false, make the whole plan pointless, however well the rest is built.

Assumptions come in a few kinds, and it helps to name them because teams habitually test only one kind.

KindThe questionNorthgate example
DesirabilityWill people use it, and does it change what they do?Adjusters read and rely on the pre-filled summary
CapabilityCan a model do the core task well enough?It reaches the senior adjuster's cover decision on at least 85% of cases

Engineers are drawn to capability, because it is the interesting one and the one they control. The chapter on feasibility showed that the other kinds often decide the outcome before a model is involved. The discipline in this chapter is to treat all six as bets and spend your first days where the evidence is thinnest and the loss is greatest.

Finding the riskiest one

Two things make an assumption risky: how likely it is to be false, and how much you lose if you discover that late. A third thing makes it urgent to test: how cheaply you can find out. Multiply and divide accordingly. For each assumption write an honest probability that it is false (your gut estimate, ideally shared with the customer's experts), the weeks of work that would be wasted if it fell over at the end, and the days a small honest test would take. Then rank by expected weeks saved per day of testing.

# Rank the assumptions behind a project by how much a cheap test would teach us.
# p_false : our honest chance the assumption is wrong (0 to 1)
# weeks   : weeks of work wasted if we only discover it is wrong at the end
# days    : days a small, honest test would take
assumptions = [
    ("A1 Adjusters will use a pre-filled summary",   0.30, 6, 4),
    ("A2 A model can judge cover from the clause",   0.45, 8, 3),
    ("A3 Scanned claim forms can be read reliably",  0.40, 5, 2),
    ("A4 Claim text may be sent to a hosted model",  0.35, 8, 1),
    ("A5 The claims system has a usable interface",  0.20, 4, 2),
    ("A6 Cost per claim stays under 0.40 EUR",       0.15, 3, 1),
]

def value_per_day(p_false, weeks, days):
    # expected weeks saved by knowing now, per day spent finding out
    return p_false * weeks / days

ranked = sorted(assumptions, key=lambda a: -value_per_day(a[1], a[2], a[3]))
print(f"{'assumption':46s} {'p(false)':>8s} {'weeks':>6s} {'days':>5s} {'value/day':>10s}")
for name, p, w, d in ranked:
    print(f"{name:46s} {p:8.2f} {w:6d} {d:5d} {value_per_day(p, w, d):10.2f}")

The numbers are guesses, and that is fine: their job is to force a conversation and to make the ranking visible. Notice what came out on top. The riskiest technical assumption is A2, whether a model can judge cover. But the best first test is A4, the permission question, because it can be answered in one day by asking the customer's security team a direct question, and it would invalidate eight weeks of work if the answer were no. (It is also the kind of question the data protection chapter and restricted environments prepare you for.) Running the model experiment first would have been the more enjoyable choice and the less rational one.

Common misconception

"A convincing demo shows the idea is feasible." A demo shows that some inputs work. It is built to impress: the inputs are chosen by the builder, the failures are not shown, nobody measured how often it works, and the permission, adoption and integration bets are not tested at all. A prototype is different in purpose. It is built to be told "no", and its inputs are chosen by someone who would like it to fail.

Turn the assumption into something that can lose

Karl Popper's old point about science applies directly: a claim is only informative if some observation could contradict it. "The model can handle our claims" cannot lose; there is always another claim to try. A falsifiable assumption names the evidence, the amount and the consequence. Write it in four parts and agree it with the customer before the experiment:

  1. The belief. "A model, given a claim description and the policy clause, reaches the same cover decision as the senior adjuster."
  2. The test. "Thirty recent water-damage claims, chosen by the head of claims, decided blind by the senior adjuster first. One prompt, one run, no tuning on these thirty."
  3. The rule, in three zones. "At least 85% agreement and at most one wrongly approved claim: continue to a bigger test. Interval top below 70%: stop. Anything between: sharpen once, then rerun on new claims."
  4. The consequence. "If we stop, we write the memo and the sponsor decides whether to try an assistive version that only summarises."

Writing the rule first is the point. In clinical research, trials are registered with their analysis plan before the data are unblinded, because a hypothesis chosen after seeing the results will always find support. The same trap awaits you: after a disappointing 78%, it is tempting to discover that 78% was always fine, or that the five "wrong" claims were really labelling errors. Sometimes labels are wrong (the expert knowledge chapter explains why), but the time to decide how you will handle that is before you see which way it cuts. A rule signed on day one turns the end-of-week conversation from "how do we feel about this?" into "what does the rule say?".

Note the third zone. Real results often land between the lines, and a plan that only knows "pass" and "fail" invents a verdict in the grey area. Decide in advance what a middling result means and how many times you are allowed to try again. One honest iteration on new data is reasonable. A fifth round of prompt adjustments against the same thirty claims is overfitting to the test, and the score it produces describes your tuning effort, not the model.

The ladder of prototypes

"Prototype" covers a range from a conversation with a chatbot to a live pilot. Choose the lowest rung that can answer your question, because every rung up costs more days and is harder to throw away.

Hand test and notebook: the capability bet

The hand test from the feasibility chapter is the cheapest rung: paste twenty real, permitted inputs into a capable model by hand and read the results with an expert. It tells you whether the task is possible at all and it surfaces the first surprises. The notebook prototype turns it into an experiment: the same cases in a file, one fixed prompt, a script that calls the model and scores the output against labels the expert wrote first. This is where capability assumptions are tested, and it is a fraction of a day of work once the cases exist. Use the plainest approach: a direct prompt, one model call, no framework, no retrieval stack unless retrieval is itself what you are testing. Anthropic's essay on building effective agents (2024) gives the same advice for the same reason: start with the simplest thing that could work and add structure only when measurement shows it is needed. A baseline you can explain in one sentence is also what you will compare every later complication against, which the next chapter relies on.

Here is the experiment for assumption A2, with a stand-in for the model so the output is reproducible. The stand-in is a small set of keyword rules in the RuleModel of the course's shared llm.py, chosen to behave like a hasty prompt that keys on surface words. With a real model you would replace one line, model = get_model(...), and the rest would not change. The twenty cases are short, invented descriptions with the senior adjuster's decision beside them.

# Test assumption A2 against a rule written BEFORE the run.

from collections import Counter

 ("Frozen pipe split in the unoccupied holiday home", "refer"),
 ("Dishwasher overflowed during the night, water in the hall", "covered"),
 ("Water seeping up through the floor after heavy rain", "not_covered"),
 ("Radiator valve burst and flooded the living room", "covered"),
 ("Gradual damp from a leaking window seal over two winters", "not_covered"),
 ("Tenant reports burst pipe; the flat was vacant for 70 days", "refer"),
 ("Hail broke the skylight and rain damaged the carpet", "covered"),
 ("Flood from the burst water main in the street entered the garage", "refer"),
 ("Overflowing toilet after the blocked pipe, bathroom flooded", "covered"),
 ("Garden flooded after a cloudburst, water reached the patio doors", "not_covered"),
 ("Leaking roof flashing, stains growing over a long time", "not_covered"),
 ("Hot-water tank split and flooded the utility room", "covered"),
 ("Burst pipe in an empty rental between tenants", "refer"),
]

RULES = [
  (r"vacant|empty|unoccupied", "refer"),
  (r"burst|split|overflow", "covered"),
  (r"storm|hail", "covered"),
  (r"flood|river|sewer|seep", "not_covered"),
  (r"leak|gradual|slow|damp", "not_covered"),
]
model = RuleModel(RULES, default="refer")

def decide(text):
    return text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": text}])).strip()

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

results = [(t, g, decide(t)) for t, g in CASES]
k = sum(g == p for _, g, p in results); n = len(results)
lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
print(f"agreement with adjuster: {k}/{n} = {k/n:.0%}   95% interval {lo:.0%} to {hi:.0%}")

# Rule written on day 1, before any run:
#   STOP     if the interval's top is below 70%   (this cannot reach the bar)
#   CONTINUE if agreement is at least 85% AND at most one false "covered"
#            (saying "covered" when the adjuster would not is the costly error)
#   SHARPEN  otherwise: read the failures, change one thing, rerun on NEW cases
false_cover = sum(g != "covered" and p == "covered" for _, g, p in results)
print("false 'covered' decisions:", false_cover)
verdict = "STOP" if hi < 0.70 else "CONTINUE" if k / n >= 0.85 and false_cover <= 1 else "SHARPEN"
print("pre-agreed verdict:", verdict)

print("\nfirst failures (read these before anything else):")
for t, g, p in results:
    if g != p:
        print(f"  gold={g:11s} model={p:11s} | {t}")
print("\nerror pattern:", dict(Counter((g, p) for _, g, p in results if g != p)))

Three things in that output are worth slowing down for. First, the headline, 85%, exactly meets the bar written on day one, yet the verdict is sharpen, not continue, because the rule also looked at the direction of the errors. All three mistakes are false approvals: the stand-in saw "overflow" or "burst" and said covered when the adjuster would not. An average hides that asymmetry; a rule that names the costly error does not. Second, the interval, 64% to 95%, is wide. Twenty cases cannot distinguish a system that is truly 70% right from one that is truly 95% right, and the next section shows how wide that blind spot is. Third, the failures are not random: they share a cause (surface words beating context). That is what you carry into the next iteration, not the score. The habit of reading the first failures before touching the prompt is the subject of error analysis in the evals course.

How many cases can a small test carry?

A small test can be very good at one job and poor at another. Think of the test as a gate that a system passes if at least 80% of the cases come out right. What is the chance that systems of different true quality get through? That is binomial arithmetic, a few lines of standard-library Python.

# How likely is a small test to show "pass"? Binomial arithmetic, standard library only.
from math import comb, ceil

def p_pass(n, true_acc, bar):
    need = ceil(bar * n - 1e-9)                      # observed correct needed to pass
    return sum(comb(n, k) * true_acc**k * (1 - true_acc)**(n - k) for k in range(need, n + 1))

BAR = 0.80
print(f"test passes when observed accuracy >= {BAR:.0%}")
print(f"{'cases':>6s} | " + " ".join(f"true={t:.0%}" for t in (0.60, 0.70, 0.80, 0.90)))
for n in (10, 20, 30, 50, 100):
    print(f"{n:6d} | " + " ".join(f"{p_pass(n, t, BAR):8.0%}" for t in (0.60, 0.70, 0.80, 0.90)))

Read down the 60% column: a system that is truly 60% right passes a 20-case test only 5% of the time. A small test is therefore an excellent killer. If the system fails, you have strong evidence it is not good enough, and you learnt it in an afternoon. Read the 70% column: a system that is truly 70% right still passes a 20-case test 24% of the time, roughly one run in four. And the 80% column shows the bar's own boundary: a system exactly as good as the bar passes only about 60% of the time, because noise pushes it below the line as often as above. So a small test is a poor confirmer. A pass at 20 cases means "this survived a first attempt to kill it and is worth a larger, sealed test", never "this works". The next chapter is about that larger test. Analogy: a smoke alarm test, not a fire inspection

Pressing the test button on a smoke alarm in ten seconds tells you whether the battery and siren work. If it stays silent, you have learnt something important immediately, and nobody would argue with the result. If it sounds, you have not learnt that the alarm will detect a real fire in the loft at night; that needs a different, slower and more expensive check. A smoke-test prototype is the button: quick, decisive when it fails, and silent about everything it did not exercise.

Where it breaks down: the alarm test is deterministic, so one press is the whole answer. A model's output varies by input, so a pass at 20 cases is a sample from a wide distribution, and the sample can flatter the system by luck about one time in four, as the table above shows.

Bets that need people, not prompts

Capability bets are the easy ones to test, because the model is the only moving part. Desirability and workflow bets involve people, and the two cheapest techniques both put a human where the software will eventually be.

In a Wizard of Oz prototype, users interact with what looks like a finished system while a person behind the scenes produces the outputs. The name and the method come from human-computer interaction research in the early 1980s; John F. Kelley's 1984 paper on iterative design for natural-language office applications is the usual citation, where a hidden experimenter played the part of the not-yet-built language interpreter. For an AI product, the wizard might be you, drafting each summary by hand with a model's help and sending it to adjusters through a plain page, to find out whether adjusters read them, edit them, or ignore them. The point is to learn about behaviour before investing in automation.

A concierge prototype is the open version of the same idea: the customer knows a person is doing the work by hand, and you learn which parts of the job matter by doing them. If you send the three most senior adjusters a daily digest that you assemble yourself, the questions they ask and the corrections they make become your requirements, and the digest costs a day, not a quarter. Both techniques can be done with real data only if the customer's permissions allow a person on your side to see it; if not, run them with a customer employee as the wizard. Be honest with participants about whether a human is behind the curtain whenever personal data or a consequential decision is involved.

Step through

A five-day riskiest-assumption sprint

  1. Choose, and sign the rule. Take the top assumption from the ranking. Write the belief, the test, the three-zone rule and the consequence, and have the sponsor and the customer's expert agree them. Nothing after this point may move the bar.
  2. Collect real cases. Twenty to thirty inputs drawn from the customer's recent work, including some untidy ones. The expert records the right answer before any model has seen the inputs, so the labels cannot be anchored by the model's output.
  3. Build the plainest prototype. A direct prompt with the policy clause, one model, fixed settings. Check it works on three spare cases that are not in the test set, then freeze it. A prompt tuned against the test cases would make the result meaningless.
  4. Run once and read the failures. Score every case, apply the rule exactly as written, and sit with the expert over each wrong answer. Group the failures by cause. The causes, not the percentage, decide what happens next.
  5. Decide and write it down. A one-page memo: what was tested, what the rule said, what the failures taught, and one recommendation: continue to a bigger sealed test, sharpen once with a named change, or stop. A stop with clear reasons is a delivered result.

After the run: three outcomes, none of them "build it"

The rule you signed has three zones, and each has a defined next move. The surprising one is the first. A pass does not mean "start building the product". It means this assumption survived and the next riskiest one is now the top of the list, or that this one deserves a larger, sealed test with a proper evaluation plan. A prototype is retired by being answered, and a project advances by clearing its assumptions one at a time, riskiest first, until the remaining ones are cheap enough to settle during delivery.

Reading the failures

When a prototype fails or sits in the grey zone, the failures tell you which layer to blame, and the layers have different remedies. Sort each wrong answer into one of four piles: the model (given the right information it still reached the wrong conclusion), the prompt or context (the information was missing, buried or worded ambiguously), the data (the input was unreadable, truncated or unrepresentative), and the test (the label was wrong, or the experts would disagree on that case). In the Northgate run, all three failures were model or prompt errors with a common cause. If instead most had landed in the data pile, a better model would have been the wrong investment, and if most had landed in the test pile you would have discovered, in a week, that your expert agreement problem from the previous chapters needs solving first. Each of these is cheap to find in a prototype and ruinous to find after launch.

How prototypes go wrong

The demo case
You pick the inputs. Fix: the customer picks them, from recent real work, and includes ones they find hard. Better still, they pick some they believe the system will fail.
The streetlight effect
You test the assumption you know how to test, usually capability, because it is the one in your toolkit. Fix: the ranking in this chapter, with the permission, data and adoption bets on the same list.
Tuning on the test
You adjust the prompt after each run until the score rises. Each adjustment is a small act of fitting to those cases, and the final score is inflated. Fix: freeze before running, and use new cases for any rerun (the contamination chapter shows how the same leak spoils public benchmarks).
Anchoring on the model
The expert is shown the model's answer and then asked whether it is right. People are agreeable, so the score looks better than the truth. Fix: label first, compare after.
The prototype becomes the product
A notebook that worked is handed to a hundred users because it exists. It has no logging, no error handling and no security review, and the first outage is also the first time anyone looks at it. Fix: say on day one that the code is disposable, and plan what survives (the cases, the rule, the learning) separately from what does not. See From Proof of Concept to Production.
Moving the goalposts
The result misses the bar, so the bar is re-described as "ambitious" or the labels are "discovered" to be wrong. Fix: the signed rule, and a clause in it describing exactly how label disputes will be handled.

State of play, October 2026: cheap code makes cheap demos

AI coding tools (see Working with AI Coding Tools) have cut the time to produce a working interface from days to hours, and that is a real gain for the Wizard of Oz and thin-slice rungs. It also makes the demo trap worse: a polished, clickable prototype is now easy to build and persuasive to a sponsor, whatever its accuracy. This is the author's judgement rather than a measured result, but it follows from the economics: when appearance is cheap, appearance becomes weaker evidence. Spend the time you save on the cases and the rule, not on a nicer front end. Tool names and capabilities change quickly; the structure of the test does not.

Reporting a negative result

The hardest conversation in this chapter takes place on day five when the rule says stop. The customer has an expectation, perhaps a budget line, and you are about to tell them the headline idea will not work as imagined. Three habits make this much easier. Agree in the first meeting that "stop" is a legitimate outcome of a prototype, as in a clinical trial, so it is not a surprise. Bring the evidence, not the opinion: the rule they signed, the cases, the failures, side by side. And always offer the next-best option, which is usually available because the failures tell you what does work: the model could not decide cover, but it summarised claims well, so ship the assistive version; the scans were unreadable, but the born-digital forms were fine, so start with those. The chapter on writing for customers covers how to put this on one page. A project that stops in week one with a clear memo has delivered something real; one that stops in month five without one has not.

Systems connection

Engineers know this idea as a spike (Kent Beck's term from extreme programming: a short, throwaway piece of work to answer a technical question) and as a tracer bullet (Hunt and Thomas, The Pragmatic Programmer: a thin, working path through every layer to find out whether the architecture holds). Both are riskiest-first. Threat modelling ranks risks by likelihood and impact in the same way, and limiting what a premature commitment can cost is what blast radius and staged rollouts do for production. The habit of fixing a decision rule before looking, and the statistics behind a sample's blind spot, are developed in Statistics for Evals. For choosing the first thin slice of an ML system see Designing with ML & LLMs.

Key takeaways

  • A project is a stack of assumptions of six kinds: desirability, capability, data, permission, integration and viability. Teams over-test capability and under-test the rest.
  • Rank assumptions by chance of being false times loss if discovered late, divided by days to test. The cheap permission question often beats the interesting model question.
  • Make the assumption falsifiable: the belief, the test, a three-zone rule (continue, sharpen, stop) and the consequence, signed before the run.
  • Pick the lowest rung of the ladder that answers the question: hand test, notebook, Wizard of Oz or concierge, thin slice, then a pilot after a gate.
  • A small test is a good killer and a poor confirmer: a 60%-accurate system rarely passes 20 cases, but a 70% one passes about one time in four. A pass means "worth a bigger sealed test", not "works".
  • A pass never means "build it". Failures are sorted into model, prompt, data and test causes, and read before the prompt is touched. Tuning on the test and anchoring experts on the model's answer inflate scores.
  • Report a clear "stop" as a result: the rule, the evidence, and the next-best option the failures revealed.

Check your understanding

A team has six assumptions to test. One is "the model can summarise our case notes well enough"; another is "legal will allow case notes to be sent to an external model". The second would take a one-day conversation and, if false, invalidates two months of work. What should the team do first?

  1. Build the summariser first, because the model is the technically hardest part and the one the customer wants to see.
  2. Ask legal now, because a cheap test of an assumption that is fairly likely to be false and very costly to discover late has the best value per day.
  3. Skip both until a full evaluation plan exists.
  4. Test them in parallel by building the whole product and seeing what fails.
Ranking by chance of being false times loss, divided by days to test, favours the cheap, high-stakes permission question. Building the summariser first is the streetlight effect: it tests what the team finds interesting. An evaluation plan is for a later, larger test, and building the whole product wastes exactly the effort the ranking is meant to protect.

Which of these is a falsifiable assumption, written so that a test could contradict it?

  1. "The model understands our contracts."
  2. "The assistant will make adjusters more productive."
  3. "The solution will be accurate enough for production."
  4. "On 30 contracts chosen by the head of legal, a single prompt flags at least 9 of the 10 clauses a lawyer marked as high risk."
Only the last names the evidence (30 contracts chosen by the customer), the amount (at least 9 of 10) and the method (a single prompt). The binomial table shows a true 70% system passes a 20-case, 80%-bar test about 24% of the time. After three rounds of prompt changes against the same 30 claims, accuracy rose from 70% to 90%. The team reports 90%. What is the problem?

  1. None: the score only went up, which is what a better prompt does.
  2. Three rounds are too few; they should have run ten.
  3. The model has simply become more accurate in general.
  4. The prompt was fitted to those 30 cases, so 90% overstates how it will do on new claims; the claim needs a fresh sealed set.
Each change was chosen after seeing failures on those very cases, which is overfitting to the test. The improvement may be genuine, but the number no longer measures it. More rounds make the fit tighter and the number less trustworthy. A fresh set of cases that the prompt has never seen is the way to find out.
