Legal & Compliance

Lawyers are paid to be right and to be trusted with secrets, which makes law one of the hardest places to deploy a system that sometimes invents things. This chapter covers contracts, document review and research, what privilege and confidentiality require of your architecture, and why every citation must be verified by something that is not the model.

Why this matters

Law is where the failure of language models became public. In 2023 a lawyer filed a brief citing cases that did not exist, because a chatbot had made them up, and the story travelled round the world. Lawyers are trained to be sceptical and are professionally liable for what they sign, which makes them demanding customers and, once convinced, good ones: the work is text, the volume is huge, and a large share of it is reading. Yet a legal system also depends on confidence, since clients tell their lawyers secrets because the law protects those conversations, and a careless architecture can put that protection at risk. After this chapter you will be able to describe the main legal workloads and what each needs, explain why a citation must be verified by something other than the model that produced it, estimate how many relevant documents a review left behind, and design a system that respects privilege and confidentiality. It builds on grounded answers and citations, fact-checking and citation verification and the healthcare chapter's habit of measuring what a summary leaves out.

Not legal advice

This chapter explains how an engineer should think about legal work and legal rules. It is not legal advice, rules differ by jurisdiction and by court, and any real deployment needs the customer's own lawyers, who will know their professional rules better than this chapter can. What is different about legal work

Four features of the profession set the design constraints.

The product is a statement a professional vouches for. A brief, an opinion letter or a contract mark-up goes out under a lawyer's name. Lawyers owe their clients and the court competence and, towards a court, candour: they must not knowingly present false material. Delegating drafting to a machine does not delegate that responsibility. In July 2024 the American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 on generative AI tools said this directly, applying the existing Model Rules on competence, confidentiality, client communication, candour to the tribunal, supervision of staff and tools, and reasonable fees. As with finance and healthcare, the rules already exist, and the new tool must fit inside them.

Confidentiality and privilege are structural. A lawyer must make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorised disclosure of client information. Attorney-client privilege is the rule that confidential communications made to obtain legal advice cannot be forced into evidence, and the related work-product protection shields materials prepared for litigation. Both can be lost if confidentiality is lost. A system that sends client documents to the wrong place does not merely leak data; it can damage the client's legal position.

The setting is adversarial. Another side's lawyers read your filings looking for errors. A false citation is found, and the exposure is public, quickly, and attached to a name. This changes the cost of a rare error: in many domains a 1% failure rate is tolerable, but in a court filing one fabricated case can end up in a sanctions order that every future client can read.

The users are sceptical experts paid by the hour. Many firms bill by time, so a tool that saves hours changes the economics, and adoption depends on the firm's pricing model as much as on the tool. Expert users also know when an answer is shallow; the standard they apply is a senior colleague's, not a search engine's. The law firm rollout case follows how a verification culture, not the model, decides whether a pilot succeeds.

Contracts: structure matters more than length

A commercial contract looks like text but behaves like code. Obligations depend on defined terms ("Fees" means amounts paid in the preceding twelve months, "Affiliate" means any entity under common control), clauses point at each other ("subject to clause 14.3"), schedules override the body, and an amendment can change a clause without replacing it. A reviewer who reads clause 9 ("liability shall not exceed the Cap") without finding the definition of "Cap" in the schedule has not reviewed clause 9.

This has three consequences for the design. Chunking by page or by fixed token window splits clauses from the definitions they depend on, so structure-aware chunking and retrieval of definitions alongside clauses (see chunking and contextual retrieval) matter more here than almost anywhere. The task should be decomposed into steps a lawyer can inspect: find the clause, extract its operative terms, compare with the playbook position, explain the difference, as in the lending workflow of the bank lending case. And the evaluation counts the right things. A lawyer reviewing a supplier contract is looking for a handful of risky clauses (uncapped liability, broad indemnity, auto-renewal, unusual termination rights, data-use rights) in a long document. Missing one is far worse than flagging a clause that turns out to be acceptable, so the headline metric is recall of risky clauses on the customer's contracts, with precision as the cost to the reviewer's time. Public data helps with calibration: the Contract Understanding Atticus Dataset (CUAD; Hendrycks et al., 2021) offers expert annotations of 41 clause categories in 510 contracts. It is a good place to prototype but not a substitute for the customer's own agreements, which have their own templates and quirks. Studio 1 asks you to scope this exact task.

Document review: how much did we leave behind?

In litigation, each side must disclose relevant documents to the other (called discovery in the United States and disclosure in England and Wales). A large case can involve millions of emails, and lawyers must find those that are responsive and withhold those that are privileged. Long before language models, the field developed technology-assisted review (TAR): a classifier learns from lawyers' labels and ranks the rest. Grossman and Cormack (2011) argued that such review can be more effective and more efficient than exhaustive manual review, and in 2012 a United States magistrate judge, Andrew Peck, in Da Silva Moore v. Publicis Groupe, approved the approach in a written opinion. Courts have not fixed a universal required recall; protocols are agreed case by case, and the point of the law was never "use of the tool is allowed" but "the process must be reasonable and documented".

The key quantity is recall: of all the relevant documents, what share did the review find? You never know the denominator, because you have not read everything. The solution is a statistical one. Take the pile the process discarded without human review (the null set), draw a random sample, have lawyers label it and count the relevant ones. The proportion found is the elusion rate, and multiplying it by the size of the null set estimates how many relevant documents were left behind.

# Document review: how many relevant documents did the AI-assisted review leave behind?
# Estimate recall from a random sample of the discarded pile ("elusion sample").
import math, random

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

random.seed(11)
N, RELEVANT = 100_000, 2_000
docs = [True] * RELEVANT + [False] * (N - RELEVANT)
# The classifier flags ~87% of relevant documents and ~3% of the rest for human review.
flagged = [(d and random.random() < 0.87) or (not d and random.random() < 0.03) for d in docs]
null_set = [d for d, f in zip(docs, flagged) if not f]       # never reviewed or produced
found = sum(1 for d, f in zip(docs, flagged) if d and f)      # relevant, confirmed by reviewers

n = 1_500
sample = random.sample(null_set, n)
k = sum(sample)                                               # relevant docs in the sample
lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
print(f"flagged {sum(flagged):,}; null set {len(null_set):,}; relevant found {found:,}")
print(f"sample of {n}: {k} relevant -> elusion {k/n:.2%} (95% CI {lo:.2%} to {hi:.2%})")
for label, e in (("estimate", k / n), ("best case", lo), ("worst case", hi)):
    missed = e * len(null_set)
    print(f"{label:<10} missed ~{missed:,.0f}  recall {found / (found + missed):.1%}")
print(f"true recall (hidden from the reviewers): {found / RELEVANT:.1%}")

print("\nsame elusion rate, bigger samples:")
for m in (1_500, 6_000, 24_000):
    kk = round(m * 2 / 1_500)
    a, b = wilson(kk, m)
    print(f"  n={m:>6}: {kk:>2} hits, elusion {kk/m:.2%} (95% CI {a:.2%} to {b:.2%}), "
          f"recall between {found/(found+b*len(null_set)):.1%} and {found/(found+a*len(null_set)):.1%}")
flagged 4,738; null set 95,262; relevant found 1,744
sample of 1500: 2 relevant -> elusion 0.13% (95% CI 0.04% to 0.48%)
estimate   missed ~127  recall 93.2%
best case  missed ~35  recall 98.0%
worst case missed ~462  recall 79.1%
true recall (hidden from the reviewers): 87.2%

same elusion rate, bigger samples:
  n=  1500:  2 hits, elusion 0.13% (95% CI 0.04% to 0.48%), recall between 79.1% and 98.0%
  n=  6000:  8 hits, elusion 0.13% (95% CI 0.07% to 0.26%), recall between 87.4% and 96.4%
  n= 24000: 32 hits, elusion 0.13% (95% CI 0.09% to 0.19%), recall between 90.7% and 95.1%

Read the output slowly. The sample of 1,500 found two relevant documents (0.13%), which gives a point estimate of 93.2% recall. The true recall, hidden from the reviewers in this simulation, was 87.2%, inside the interval but well below the point estimate. The 95% interval for recall ran from 79.1% to 98.0%, wide enough to be uncomfortable: whether the review is defensible depends on whether the worst end of that range is acceptable to the parties. The second table shows what buys a tighter answer: the same elusion rate on a sample sixteen times larger narrows the range to roughly 91% to 95%. Sample size is a budget decision with a statistical answer, and the interval used here is the Wilson interval from statistics for evals, chosen because rare events make the textbook formula misleading.

For language models, the same logic applies with a different classifier. Models that label every document for relevance or privilege can be evaluated this way against a lawyer-labelled sample, and cost and speed can be traded off as in cost, latency and quality. Privilege review needs special care because the error is asymmetric: producing a privileged document to the other side can waive the protection, and US courts can issue orders under Federal Rule of Evidence 502(d) that protect against waiver by inadvertent production, which is why lawyers ask for them before large reviews. A model that is 98% accurate at privilege and sends 2% of privileged documents to the other side is a risk that a procedure (second-pass review of high-risk categories such as anything involving counsel) must contain.

Research and drafting: every citation must be verified

Language models write citations that look right. The failure has a name, hallucination, and in law its cost became public in Mata v. Avianca. A personal-injury lawyer in New York used ChatGPT for research and filed a brief citing cases including Varghese v. China Southern Airlines, which did not exist. When the opposing side could not find the cases, the court ordered copies; the lawyers produced fabricated text. On 22 June 2023 Judge P. Kevin Castel of the Southern District of New York sanctioned the two lawyers and their firm 5,000 US dollars. The opinion's emphasis, as summarised by commentators, was less on the first error than on what followed: the lawyers did not withdraw the cases when challenged and offered shifting explanations.

The problem has not gone away. A database maintained by Damien Charlotin of decisions in which courts found or clearly implied that a party relied on hallucinated material listed roughly 1,600 cases worldwide by mid-June 2026 according to secondary reports, growing by several a day, with lawyers and self-represented litigants both represented. In the United Kingdom a Divisional Court judgment in June 2025 in Ayinde v London Borough of Haringey and a linked case (Dame Victoria Sharp presiding) warned the profession after fictitious authorities were put before courts in two matters, referred the lawyers to their regulators, and said that placing false material before a court as genuine could in extreme cases amount to contempt or worse. Many courts now require disclosure or certification of AI use; Judge Brantley Starr of the Northern District of Texas issued an early standing order of this kind in May 2023.

Specialist legal tools reduce but do not remove the risk. Magesh and colleagues at Stanford (Journal of Empirical Legal Studies, 2025; first released 2024) tested leading commercial legal research products that combine retrieval with generation and reported (in the 2024 preprint) that they hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time on their queries, with Lexis+ AI at the lower end and Westlaw AI-Assisted Research at the higher end, and in the published abstract that the rate exceeded 17%; the tests were run in 2024 and the products have changed since. Earlier work by Dahl and colleagues (2024), "Large Legal Fictions", found that general-purpose models of that period gave erroneous answers to a majority of verifiable legal questions, with reported rates between 69% and 88%. The reasons are those of why models hallucinate, plus one specific to law: a retrieved case that is real can still be misdescribed, because what a case stands for depends on context, subsequent history and the court's hierarchy.

Common misconception

"If the system only answers from our own document store, it cannot hallucinate." Retrieval narrows the problem and does not remove it. The model can still misquote a retrieved passage, attribute a holding to the wrong case, merge two cases, or invent a supporting citation to round out an argument. The Stanford study tested retrieval-based tools and found substantial rates. Grounding reduces the chance of fabrication and, more usefully, gives you something to check against; the check still has to happen.

A ladder of checks

Verification is a ladder, and each rung catches a different failure. The lower rungs can be automated cheaply; the top rung is a lawyer's job.

The script below implements rungs 1 to 3 against a toy index standing in for an authoritative source such as a court database or licensed citator. The stand-in model drafts four citations; one is real and correctly quoted, one has a misquotation, one is the invented case from Mata, and one attaches the wrong citation to a real case name.

# Verify every citation in a draft against a trusted index before a human signs it.
import re
from llm import RuleModel, text_of

# Stand-in for a licensed citator or court database: citation -> (case name, a verbatim passage).
INDEX = {
    "5 U.S. 137":   ("Marbury v. Madison",
                     "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."),
    "347 U.S. 483": ("Brown v. Board of Education",
                     "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."),
}

model = RuleModel([(r"draft", """\
Courts decide what the law is. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803) ("It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.").
Segregated schooling is unlawful. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954) ("Separate is never equal under any circumstances.").
Delay is excused for airlines. Varghese v. China Southern Airlines, 925 F.3d 1339 (11th Cir. 2019).
Judicial review is settled. Marbury v. Madison, 347 U.S. 483 (1803).""")])

CITE = re.compile(r"([A-Z]\w+ v\. [A-Z]\w+(?: (?:of )?[A-Z]\w+)*), (\d+ [A-Z][\w.]* \d+) \((?:[\w. ]+ )?(\d{4})\)(?: \(\"([^\"]+)\"\))?")

def check(draft):
    for name, cite, year, quote in CITE.findall(draft):
        if cite not in INDEX:
            yield name, cite, "NOT FOUND: do not file; may not exist"
        elif INDEX[cite][0] != name:
            yield name, cite, f"MISMATCH: that citation is {INDEX[cite][0]}"
        elif quote and quote.lower().rstrip(".") not in INDEX[cite][1].lower():
            yield name, cite, "QUOTE NOT IN OPINION: check the source text"
        else:
            yield name, cite, "verified (existence and quote); a lawyer still checks the proposition"

draft = text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": "Write the draft."}]))
for name, cite, verdict in check(draft):
    print(f"{name} | {cite}\n    {verdict}")
Marbury v. Madison | 5 U.S. 137
    verified (existence and quote); a lawyer still checks the proposition
Brown v. Board of Education | 347 U.S. 483
    QUOTE NOT IN OPINION: check the source text
Varghese v. China Southern Airlines | 925 F.3d 1339
    NOT FOUND: do not file; may not exist
Marbury v. Madison | 347 U.S. 483
    MISMATCH: that citation is Brown v. Board of Education

One of four passes, and the checker's final message is deliberately modest: "a lawyer still checks the proposition". Two design points matter beyond the code. First, the verifier must use a source independent of the model. Asking a model whether a citation is real is asking the same mind that invented it, and models readily confirm their own inventions. Second, an unverifiable citation must stop the document, not merely add a warning: the safe default for "not found" is "do not file", and a human override is logged. The general machinery for claim extraction and checking is in fact-checking and citation verification, and abstention (a system that says "I could not find authority for this") is the behaviour to reward in grounding, abstention and confidence.

Analogy: the junior associate's first draft

A senior lawyer does not sign a junior's memo without pulling the cases it relies on. The junior is fast, a little over-confident and sometimes wrong, and the review process is built for that. A model's draft fits the same process: useful, quick, and checked before it carries the firm's name.

Where it breaks down: a junior who is unsure of a case usually says so, and the senior learns each junior's habits over months. A model states a fabricated case in exactly the same tone as a real one, and its error patterns shift every time the model is updated, so the reviewer cannot rely on a learned sense of where to look.

Confidentiality and privilege in the architecture

Lawyers must make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorised disclosure of information relating to a client. Applying this to an AI system turns into architectural questions you can answer with standard security techniques.

  • Where does the text go? Which provider receives client documents, in which country, under which contract? Does the contract prohibit use for training, set retention to zero or a short period, name sub-processors, and give notice of breaches? See choosing models and privacy and data handling for AI.
  • Who can see what? Law firms use ethical walls (also called information barriers): rules that keep one team away from a matter because of a conflict of interest. A search index that mixes all matters would break them. Permissions must be enforced at retrieval, before generation, as in filters, metadata and permissions, and the isolation must be strong enough that one matter's text cannot appear in another's answer; see multi-tenancy and isolation.
  • What is logged, and where? Prompts and responses contain the same confidential material as the documents. Logs need the same access controls, retention limits and deletion on matter closure as the documents themselves.
  • Does the client know? Engagement letters and some clients' outside-counsel guidelines address AI use, and some require consent or prohibit it for particular matters. ABA Formal Opinion 512 discusses when informed consent is needed.

Privilege has a newer wrinkle. In February 2026 Judge Jed Rakoff of the Southern District of New York ruled, in United States v. Heppner, that conversations a criminal defendant had with a consumer AI chatbot while preparing to talk to his lawyers were neither covered by attorney-client privilege nor protected as work product. As reported in the legal press, the reasoning was that the chatbot is not a lawyer and that a service whose terms permit retention, training use and disclosure to third parties does not give a reasonable expectation of confidentiality. Commentators have warned that the logic could reach some other services. Treat this carefully and read the opinion: it involved a non-lawyer's own use of a consumer tool, not a law firm's use of an enterprise service under confidentiality terms. The practical lessons for a delivery engineer are the ones the architecture already encodes: keep client material inside services whose terms preserve confidentiality, make the safe path the easy one so staff are not tempted by free tools, and advise clients that pasting their legal questions into a consumer chatbot can create discoverable records.

Rolling out in a firm

The technology is the easier half. Firms that adopt well tend to do four things. They write the verification rule down: no authority is cited, no number is relied on and no clause is called "standard" unless a person has checked it against the source, and the tool's interface makes the source one click away. They start where the cost of error is low and checking is cheap: first drafts of summaries, issue lists and clause tables that a lawyer would otherwise assemble from scratch, before anything that goes to a court. They train and supervise: ABA Opinion 512 treats supervision of tools and of staff using them as part of the lawyer's duty. And they measure honestly, with time saved on matched tasks and error rates found by senior lawyers on a sample, not enthusiasm from a survey, in the way evaluation plans customers trust describes. The pricing question is real too: if the firm bills by the hour, a tool that cuts hours cuts revenue unless pricing changes, and the partners will notice before the engineers do. Raise it with the sponsor early, as part of the requirements.

Systems connection

Matter isolation is the silo-versus-pool decision of multi-tenancy, enforced in the retrieval path rather than in the application. Retention limits and deletion on matter closure are data-lifecycle problems from data protection and privacy. Handling millions of documents is a batch pipeline with retries and idempotence (logs and exactly-once semantics), and a verification step that must block output is a gate like those in deployment strategies. Untrusted documents in a review set can carry instructions aimed at the model, which is the prompt-injection risk of agent security: contracts and emails from the other side are adversarial input.

Key takeaways

  • Legal work is text a professional vouches for, in an adversarial setting, under duties of competence, candour and confidentiality. The tool fits inside those duties; it does not replace them.
  • Contracts behave like code: definitions, cross-references and schedules matter. Chunk by structure, decompose the review into inspectable steps and measure recall of risky clauses on the customer's own contracts.
  • Estimate what a document review left behind by sampling the discard pile (elusion). Report recall with an interval, and spend sample size to narrow it.
  • Fabricated citations are a known, recurring, sanctionable failure, including from specialist retrieval-based tools. Verify existence, identity and quotation automatically against an independent source, check good law with a citator, and leave the proposition to a lawyer.
  • An unverifiable citation blocks the document. A model cannot verify its own citations.
  • Architect for confidentiality: contract terms with no training and minimal retention, per-matter isolation with permissions before retrieval, controlled logs, and an approved path that is easier than a consumer tool.
  • Adoption is a verification culture, a pricing conversation and honest measurement, not only a model.

Check your understanding

A team proposes to verify AI-drafted citations by asking a second model call, "Is this a real case?", for each one. What is the main weakness?

  1. It is too expensive to be practical.
  2. The check relies on the same kind of system that invented the citation, and models readily confirm their own inventions; verification needs an independent authoritative source.
  3. Models cannot answer yes-or-no questions.
  4. Courts forbid using a model more than once per document.
A second model shares the first one's blind spots and will often agree that a plausible citation exists. Cost is secondary, and models can answer yes-or-no questions; the issue is reliability. No court rule limits the number of calls. The fix is lookup in a court database or citator.

A draft passes the existence, identity and quotation checks for all its citations. A colleague says it is now safe to file. What remains?

  1. Nothing: a real, correctly quoted case is a correct citation.
  2. Only a formatting check.
  3. Only a spelling check on the case names.
  4. Whether each case is still good law and actually supports the proposition it is cited for, which need a citator and a lawyer's reading.
The first three rungs stop fabrication but say nothing about whether a case was overruled or whether it means what the draft claims. Real cases misdescribed are a documented failure of retrieval-based tools. Formatting and spelling are not the risk.

A review flagged 1,744 relevant documents. A random sample of 1,500 from the 95,262-document discard pile contained 2 relevant ones. Which statement is best supported?

  1. The point estimate suggests a few hundred relevant documents were missed at most, but two hits give a wide interval, so the recall estimate has a wide range and a larger sample is needed to defend a specific figure.
  2. Recall is exactly 100% because the elusion rate is below 1%.
  3. Nothing can be said without reading all 95,262 documents.
  4. The review missed exactly 127 documents.
Elusion of 0.13% times 95,262 is about 127, but with only two hits the 95% interval runs from roughly 35 to 460 missed documents, so recall is only bounded loosely. Sampling is exactly the way to avoid reading everything, and a point estimate is not an exact count.

A firm's AI search index contains all of its matters in one vector store, and queries are filtered by matter after the top results are retrieved and before the answer is shown. What is the best criticism?

  1. Vector stores cannot hold legal documents.
  2. Filtering is unnecessary because the model will not reveal other matters.
  3. Post-filtering can leak by side-channel and also lets walled-off content influence the result; permissions should be enforced before or during retrieval, with matter-level isolation for conflicts.
  4. One index is always slower than several.
A model cannot be relied on to withhold text it was given, and content from a walled-off matter that reaches the context can shape the answer even if some of it is later hidden. Enforce access at retrieval, and isolate matters where ethical walls require it. Storage type and speed are not the issue.

An associate pastes a client's draft contract into a free consumer chatbot "to get a quick summary". Which concern is the most direct?

  1. The summary will be too short.
  2. Confidential client material has left the firm's controlled environment under terms that may allow retention and training use, which can breach confidentiality duties and put privilege at risk.
  3. Chatbots cannot read contracts.
  4. The contract will be changed by the chatbot.
The core risk is disclosure under terms the firm has not reviewed, with possible consequences for the client's privilege. Summary quality and changes to the document are secondary, and chatbots can read contracts. The remedy is an approved tool that is easier to use than the free one.

A contract-review assistant is evaluated on 40 supplier contracts. It flags 90% of the clauses lawyers marked risky and 15% of its flags are clauses lawyers judged acceptable. What should the report lead with?

  1. The 15% of unnecessary flags, because wasted time is the main cost.
  2. An average of both numbers as a single quality score.
  3. The number of contracts processed per hour.
  4. The recall of risky clauses with an interval and the list of missed clause types, then the precision as the reviewer-time cost; a missed risky clause is worse than a spurious flag.
In review, a missed risky clause can cost the client far more than an extra flag costs in minutes, so recall comes first, with the misses analysed by type. A blended score hides the asymmetry, and throughput says nothing about whether the review is reliable.
