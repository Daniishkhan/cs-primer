Restricted Environments

Some of the customers who most need AI help cannot send a byte to the internet. This chapter explains what on-premises and air-gapped deployment really change, how data-residency and sovereignty rules shape the design, how to build and update a system you cannot reach, and how to do good work when only three models are on the approved list.

Why this matters

Banks, hospitals, defence suppliers, ministries and courts hold the data that makes AI most valuable, and the rules that govern it are the strictest. The system you built in a cloud notebook with a hosted model will not be allowed in. The engineer who understands the constraints can still deliver, while the one who learns them in the third month of a pilot cannot. After this chapter you will be able to place a customer on the spectrum from vendor API to air gap, separate data residency from data sovereignty, design an import and export process for software and model weights, check that a model fits the hardware on the shelf, avoid the format and supply-chain traps of downloaded weights, and plan support when you cannot see the system you built.

Why a customer says "nothing leaves the building"

Restriction is rarely a whim. It comes from one of a handful of sources, and knowing which one applies tells you how negotiable it is.

  • Law and regulation. Data protection law can limit where personal data goes; financial and health regulators can require control over outsourced processing; public bodies may be bound by procurement and classification rules.
  • Classification. Government and defence information is graded by sensitivity, and the highest grades may not touch a network that connects to the outside world at all.
  • Contract. The customer's own clients may forbid sub-processors. A law firm that promised a client its documents would never leave the firm's systems cannot then send them to a vendor.
  • Risk appetite. Some organisations accept a vendor's zero-retention contract, others do not, and the position is often set by a security leader who has seen an incident.
  • Operational isolation. Industrial control networks and laboratory systems are kept apart from the internet so that a compromise elsewhere cannot reach them.

The customer's position sits somewhere on a spectrum. Choosing models described the three deployment shapes; restricted environments push further along the same line.

Two definitions pin the far end. On-premises means the system runs on hardware the customer owns or leases and controls, in their data centre or building, rather than on a provider's cloud. An air gap goes further: the network that holds the system has no connection to any outside network, so data crosses only by a deliberate, controlled physical or logical transfer. An air-gapped system is always on-premises; an on-premises system may be connected to the customer's wider network and the internet through firewalls.

Data residency, sovereignty and the law

Data residency means that data is stored and processed in a stated country or region. It is the requirement customers mention first and the easiest to satisfy: choose a cloud region, or a data centre. Data sovereignty asks a harder question: whose law can reach the data? A European customer's data may sit in a Frankfurt data centre run by a company headquartered elsewhere, whose home government may have legal powers over the company wherever its servers are. Residency says where the bytes sit; sovereignty says who can compel their disclosure.

State of play, October 2026

Facts that frame European conversations, as I understand them from the sources listed at the end; check current status with the customer's lawyers. The GDPR (Regulation 2016/679) restricts transfers of personal data outside the EU/EEA unless a lawful mechanism applies (Chapter V). In Schrems II (Court of Justice of the EU, case C-311/18, 16 July 2020) the Court invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield and required extra scrutiny of standard contractual clauses. The European Commission adopted a replacement adequacy decision, the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, in July 2023. On 3 September 2025 the EU General Court dismissed a challenge to it (Latombe v Commission, T-553/23), and the applicant lodged an appeal to the Court of Justice (C-703/25 P) in late October 2025. I could not confirm the appeal's outcome, so treat the framework as in force but contested. The US CLOUD Act (2018) allows US authorities to require US-based providers to disclose data in their possession, custody or control even when it is stored abroad, subject to legal process and challenge. For EU AI Act obligations and their dates, see the dated notes in the finance chapter; the 2026 omnibus amendment moved some dates. In the US federal market, cloud services used by agencies are authorised under FedRAMP, and the US defence department groups cloud workloads into impact levels of rising sensitivity. Rules change, and none of this is legal advice.

What matters to the engineer is the design consequence. When the answer to the third question matters, a "managed" model from a foreign provider may not be acceptable even inside the customer's region, and the system must run on open-weight models on the customer's own hardware or with a local operator. When only residency matters, a private endpoint in a named region with the right terms may be enough, and is much cheaper to run. Ask early, in requirements, which question the customer is asking, and write the answer into the hard-constraints list.

Data protection reaches into the design in other ways too: retention and deletion of prompts and logs, who can see them, and what is sent to a model at all. The data protection chapter and privacy and data handling for AI cover the mechanisms, and Lab 3 builds a redaction and audit pipeline.

Building for a place you cannot reach

On a connected laptop a project assembles itself: pip install fetches libraries, a model loader downloads weights, a tokeniser pulls a vocabulary file, a font loads from a CDN, and a telemetry call reports a version number. Inside an air gap, each of those silently fails or, worse, hangs until a timeout. The central discipline is to make the artefact self-contained and to treat the boundary as a deliberate, inspected crossing.

Close the dependency set

Build the application in the connected zone with every dependency pinned to an exact version and fetched into a local mirror: Python wheels, system packages, container base images (referenced by digest, a hash of the content, not by a mutable tag like "latest"), the tokeniser files, the model weights and any datasets for evaluation. Then test the bundle in a network-less container so surprises show up on your side, not at the customer's. A disciplined team also produces a software bill of materials (SBOM): a machine-readable list of every component in the bundle, with versions and licences, which the customer's security team will ask for (next chapter) and which lets them check a new vulnerability against what you installed. The wider supply-chain issues are in memory safety and the supply chain.

Be suspicious of anything that wants the network. Common phone-home behaviour in AI stacks includes model and tokeniser downloads, usage telemetry, update checks, licence servers, and web assets loaded by a documentation page. Many libraries have an offline switch (Hugging Face's libraries read the HF_HUB_OFFLINE environment variable, for example), but a switch for one library is not a guarantee about the whole process. The reliable test is to run the full application in a container with its network disabled and watch what fails.

Verify what crossed

The gate is only meaningful if the receiving side can check that what arrived is what left. The usual mechanism is a manifest: a file listing each file's cryptographic hash, itself signed and carried separately (for example, on different media or by a different person). The script below builds a manifest, simulates a transfer, then damages the copy in three ways to show what the verifier reports.

# Import gate: hash everything on the connected side, verify it on the isolated side.
import hashlib, json, os, shutil, tempfile

def sha256(path):
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    with open(path, "rb") as f:
        for chunk in iter(lambda: f.read(1 << 20), b""):
            h.update(chunk)
    return h.hexdigest()

def build(root):
    out = {}
    for d, _, files in os.walk(root):
        for name in sorted(files):
            p = os.path.join(d, name)
            out[os.path.relpath(p, root)] = sha256(p)
    return dict(sorted(out.items()))

def verify(root, manifest):
    seen = build(root)
    for rel, digest in manifest.items():
        if rel not in seen:       print(f"  MISSING     {rel}")
        elif seen[rel] != digest: print(f"  MISMATCH    {rel}")
        else:                     print(f"  ok          {rel}")
    for rel in seen:
        if rel not in manifest:   print(f"  UNEXPECTED  {rel}")

base = tempfile.mkdtemp()
src, dst = os.path.join(base, "connected"), os.path.join(base, "isolated")
os.makedirs(os.path.join(src, "model"))
files = {"model/weights.safetensors": b"\x01" * 4096, "model/config.json": b'{"layers": 32}',
         "model/tokenizer.json": b'{"vocab": []}', "requirements.lock": b"numpy==2.1.0\n"}
for rel, data in files.items():
    with open(os.path.join(src, rel), "wb") as f:
        f.write(data)
manifest = build(src)
print("manifest (signed and carried separately):")
print(json.dumps({k: v[:12] + "..." for k, v in manifest.items()}, indent=2))

shutil.copytree(src, dst)                                   # the "transfer"
print("\nverify after a clean transfer:")
verify(dst, manifest)

with open(os.path.join(dst, "model/weights.safetensors"), "r+b") as f:   # one flipped byte
    f.seek(100); f.write(b"\x02")
os.remove(os.path.join(dst, "model/tokenizer.json"))
with open(os.path.join(dst, "model/extra.py"), "w") as f:
    f.write("print('hello')\n")
print("\nverify after one flipped byte, one deleted file and one extra file:")
verify(dst, manifest)
shutil.rmtree(base)

The three failure kinds are different incidents: a mismatch means corruption or tampering, a missing file means an incomplete transfer, and an unexpected file means something was added that nobody reviewed. A hash only proves the bytes are the ones listed; it does not prove the listed bytes are safe, which is why the scan and review happen before the manifest is signed. The principle of signing and verifying artefacts is covered in applied cryptography and keys, secrets and PKI.

The weights themselves are code you did not write

Model weights are downloaded from outside, and a file format can be an attack. Older PyTorch checkpoints are often stored with Python's pickle, a serialisation format that can instruct the loader to call arbitrary functions when the file is opened. Loading an untrusted pickle is running untrusted code. The safetensors format was designed to avoid this: it stores a length, a JSON header describing each tensor, and raw numbers, and nothing in it can execute. The demonstration below uses a harmless print in place of anything malicious, and a small reimplementation of the same layout idea.

# Why weight FORMAT matters on a locked-down site: pickle runs code on load, safetensors is only data.
import json, pickle, struct

class Innocent:                      # what a booby-trapped "checkpoint" can contain
    def __reduce__(self):
        return (print, ("  >>> code ran just because the file was loaded",))

blob = pickle.dumps(Innocent())
print("loading a pickle checkpoint:")
pickle.loads(blob)                   # a real attacker would not call print

# The safetensors layout: 8-byte little-endian header length, a JSON header, raw bytes.
def write_st(tensors):
    header, data, off = {}, b"", 0
    for name, values in tensors.items():
        raw = struct.pack(f"<{len(values)}f", *values)
        header[name] = {"dtype": "F32", "shape": [len(values)], "data_offsets": [off, off + len(raw)]}
        data += raw; off += len(raw)
    h = json.dumps(header).encode()
    return struct.pack("<Q", len(h)) + h + data

def read_st(buf):
    (n,) = struct.unpack("<Q", buf[:8])
    header = json.loads(buf[8:8 + n])
    base = 8 + n
    return {k: list(struct.unpack(f"<{(v['data_offsets'][1]-v['data_offsets'][0])//4}f",
                                  buf[base + v['data_offsets'][0]: base + v['data_offsets'][1]]))
            for k, v in header.items()}

buf = write_st({"w": [0.5, -1.25, 2.0], "b": [0.125]})
print("\nreading a safetensors-style file:")
print(" ", read_st(buf))
print("  (parsing a length, JSON and raw numbers: nothing in the file can execute)")

The practical rules follow directly. Prefer the data-only format when you have the choice; if a model exists only as a pickle checkpoint, convert it on the connected side in a throwaway sandbox, then scan and hash the converted file; and record the origin and hash of every weight file in the SBOM. Weights are part of the supply chain, with the licence questions raised in choosing models.

Common misconception

"An air-gapped network is secure because nothing can get in." It is better contained, not automatically safe. Everything that matters still crosses the gate: your code, your dependencies, the weights, updates, and the people carrying removable media. Attackers who target isolated networks go through those crossings, and the best-known public example is the Stuxnet worm, discovered in 2010 and widely reported to have reached an isolated industrial network on removable drives. The air gap does not remove the need to defend; it concentrates it on the import gate and on insiders. Treat inbound transfers as the most security-critical process in the system.

Working with a limited menu of models

Large organisations often keep an approved model menu: a short list of models (and versions) that have passed legal, security and risk review for use on their data. Your preferred model is usually not on it, and adding one can take months. The menu may be as short as one or two open-weight models, and each may be a size or two behind the frontier. That sets the project's ceiling, so find out what it is on day one.

Does it fit the hardware on the shelf?

On-premises, you do not choose a GPU count to suit the model; you take the hardware that has been bought and ask what fits. Choosing models derived the memory formula (weights plus the KV cache of concurrent requests, plus overhead); see also the KV cache and quantisation. The script below turns it into a table for a three-model menu and three GPU configurations. The model shapes are illustrative assumptions (parameter counts and a typical grouped-query attention layout), not specifications of any real product.

# The site has these GPUs on the shelf and an approved menu of models. What fits?
# Architectures are ILLUSTRATIVE assumptions (a typical grouped-query attention shape), not real products.
GB = 1e9
MENU = {  # name: (parameters in billions, layers, kv heads, head dim)
    "menu-A (8B)":  (8,  32, 8, 128),
    "menu-B (32B)": (32, 64, 8, 128),
    "menu-C (70B)": (70, 80, 8, 128),
}
BYTES = {"bf16": 2.0, "int8": 1.0, "int4": 0.55}   # int4 includes scales and zero-points
HARDWARE = {"1 x 24 GB": 24, "2 x 48 GB": 96, "4 x 80 GB": 320}   # total GPU memory in GB
USERS, CTX = 16, 8000        # concurrent requests, tokens of context each
OVERHEAD = 0.12              # runtime, activations and fragmentation, as a share of the total

def need_gb(params_b, layers, kv_heads, head_dim, bytes_per_w):
    weights = params_b * 1e9 * bytes_per_w
    kv = 2 * layers * kv_heads * head_dim * 2 * USERS * CTX    # keys+values, 16-bit cache
    return (weights + kv) * (1 + OVERHEAD) / GB

print(f"{USERS} concurrent users x {CTX} tokens of context, 12% overhead\n")
print(f"{'model / precision':22s} {'need GB':>8s}   " + "  ".join(f"{h:>10s}" for h in HARDWARE))
for name, (p, L, kv, hd) in MENU.items():
    for prec, b in BYTES.items():
        n = need_gb(p, L, kv, hd, b)
        cells = "  ".join(f"{'fits' if n <= cap else '-':>10s}" for cap in HARDWARE.values())
        print(f"{name + ' ' + prec:22s} {n:8.1f}   {cells}")

Read it as a negotiation table. Quantising the 8B model to 4-bit squeezes it onto a single 24 GB card with 16 users at 8,000 tokens each, but the 32B model needs the 96 GB configuration even at 8 bits, and the 70B model only fits in 4-bit on the same card pair, with so little headroom that the 12% overhead assumption becomes the thing to test. It also does not say the fit is fast: spreading a model across several cards adds communication, and throughput is a separate measurement (serving engines and the economics of a token). The accuracy cost of 4-bit depends on the model and task, so every cell that says "fits" because of quantisation needs a re-run of the evaluation.

When the best available model is not very good

With a smaller or older model, the engineering shifts from "ask harder" to "ask less". Levers, roughly in the order to try them:

  1. Narrow the task. A weaker model can do a well-specified extraction or classification at high accuracy where it would fail at open-ended analysis. Re-read the feasibility triage with the smaller model in mind.
  2. Decompose. Split one hard question into several easy ones, each checkable (workflow patterns).
  3. Give it the facts. Retrieval over the customer's own documents lifts a small model more than it lifts a large one (RAG end to end).
  4. Constrain the output with schemas or grammars, so format errors vanish.
  5. Adapt it. Fine-tuning on the customer's data, on the customer's hardware, with LoRA or by distilling from a larger model used on permitted data, often closes much of the gap. The adapted model is a new model for approval purposes: ask whether the menu covers derivatives.
  6. Reduce what must be right. Put the human step where the model is weakest.

Whatever you choose, keep the application swappable: model calls go through one interface, prompts live in configuration with the model name beside them, and the evaluation kit runs against any entry on the menu. When a better model is eventually approved, you then migrate by running the kit, not by rewriting the system. This is the "second choice ready" idea from the previous chapters, applied to a menu that changes slowly.

Operating blind

The hardest part of a restricted deployment arrives after launch. You cannot open the logs, run a debugger or look at a failing example, because the example is the customer's data. Plan for it as a design requirement.

  • Build the observability inside. Tracing, metrics and dashboards run in the isolated zone, with the customer's staff as the audience (LLM observability). Without it nobody can tell whether the system is healthy.
  • Ship the evaluation kit and run it there. The gold set and scoring code must be inside the boundary, so that quality is measured on the real data and re-measured after each change. Only the aggregate result leaves, through export review.
  • Design the diagnostic bundle. A command that gathers versions, configuration, error counts, latency histograms and anonymised failure categories into one file a customer's engineer can inspect and then release. Having this ready turns a three-week email chain into one transfer.
  • Develop on a twin. Build with synthetic or sanitised data that has the same schema and awkward cases as the real thing, and keep a rehearsal environment on the connected side that mirrors the customer's software versions.
  • Treat expiries as outages. Certificates from an internal authority, licences from a licence server, and tokens all expire, and automatic renewal needs a network. Keep a calendar of every expiry date and a manual renewal procedure.
  • Fixed capacity means shedding load. You cannot add GPUs on a busy Monday. Decide in advance what is dropped first when demand exceeds capacity (overload and load shedding) and size with the queueing arithmetic in queueing theory for engineers.
  • Plan updates and rollback. A release is a bundle through the import gate with a manifest, a rehearsed install and a tested way back. Agree a cadence (monthly, quarterly) with the customer; "whenever we need a fix" turns into never. Disaster recovery means a copy of the bundle and the data inside the zone (backups and disaster recovery).

Analogy: a research station

An air-gapped deployment resembles a remote research station. Supplies arrive on scheduled flights, so everything needed for the season is packed in advance, labelled and checked against a manifest. Whatever breaks must be fixed with what is on site, by the people who are there, who need manuals and spares more than clever improvisation. News goes out as a short radio report, not by sending the samples back.

Where it breaks down: a station's remote location is a fact of geography and everyone accepts it. In a data centre the isolation is a policy, which people are tempted to relax: a "temporary" internet connection for a fix, a laptop plugged in to move a file. Each exception becomes a path that a security review would not have approved, so the discipline has to come from process.

Try it yourself

Take a small project of your own (a script that uses a Python library and loads a model or tokeniser) and make it run on a machine with no network. Build the dependency list with exact versions, copy everything into one folder, add a manifest like the one above, disable the network (a container with networking switched off works, or turn off Wi-Fi), and run it. Write down every single thing that tried to reach the network and what you did about each. Then edit fit.py for your own laptop's memory and see which sizes of model would be possible there.

Systems connection

The import gate is a trust boundary of the kind taught in threat modelling, and the network design behind it is network security and zero trust. Pinned, scanned and signed bundles are the supply-chain defences of memory safety and the supply chain, and running a model server in someone else's data centre is cloud and container security without the cloud. Fixed capacity and no elasticity make load testing and capacity planning a one-time sizing decision.

Key takeaways

  • Find out why the customer restricts (law, classification, contract, risk appetite, isolation) and where they sit between a vendor API and an air gap; it tells you what is negotiable.
  • Residency is where the bytes sit. Sovereignty is whose law can reach them. Ask which one the customer means and write it into the hard constraints.
  • Make the deployment self-contained: pinned dependencies, local mirrors, signed manifests, an SBOM, and a test run in a container with no network.
  • Two gates carry all traffic: an import gate with hash, scan and review, and an export gate that lets numbers, not records, out.
  • Weights are code you did not write. Prefer data-only formats, never load an untrusted pickle, and record the origin and hash of every file.
  • On a limited model menu, check the fit on the hardware you actually have, narrow and decompose the task, keep the application swappable, and plan operations you cannot watch: internal observability, an evaluation kit inside the zone, diagnostic bundles and tracked expiries.

Check your understanding

A European bank says "our data must stay in the EU". Your design uses a managed model endpoint in an EU region, run by a provider headquartered in another country, with the provider's staff able to access it for support. Which statement is most accurate?

  1. The requirement is fully met, since the endpoint is in the EU.
  2. The requirement is certainly violated, since the provider is foreign.
  3. Residency is probably met, but sovereignty and operational-control questions are open and depend on what the bank actually requires; ask which they mean.
  4. Residency does not apply to model endpoints.
The region answers where the bytes sit. Who can access them and which jurisdiction's law can reach the provider are separate questions, and whether they matter depends on the bank's real requirement and risk appetite. Declaring compliance or violation outright skips that clarification, and residency applies to any processing of personal data.

The manifest check on the isolated side reports "UNEXPECTED model/extra.py" and "MISMATCH weights.safetensors". What should happen?

  1. Stop the install, treat both as incidents, and obtain a fresh transfer; investigate how the extra file arrived.
  2. Delete the extra file and continue, since the weights probably only have a small error.
  3. Regenerate the manifest on the isolated side so the check passes.
  4. Ignore the mismatch if the model still loads.
A mismatch means the bytes differ from what was reviewed, and an unexpected file means something unreviewed entered. Either can be tampering or corruption. Regenerating the manifest locally defeats the check, and a model that loads proves nothing about whether its contents are the approved ones.

The only available checkpoint for an approved model is a pickle file from a public hub. What is the safest way to bring it into the isolated zone?

  1. Load it on the production server to check it works.
  2. Hash it and import it as is, since a hash proves it is safe.
  3. Ask the vendor to confirm by email that it is fine.
  4. Convert it to a data-only format in a throwaway sandbox on the connected side, scan it, hash the converted file, and import that through the gate.
Loading a pickle runs whatever code it contains, so doing it on the production server is the exposure itself. A hash shows the bytes did not change in transit, not that they are harmless. Converting in a sandbox contains the risk before the file crosses.

With the chapter's illustrative numbers, the customer has two 48 GB GPUs (96 GB). Which statement follows from the table for 16 users at 8,000 tokens each?

  1. The 70B model fits in 8-bit.
  2. The 32B model fits in 8-bit and the 70B model only in 4-bit, with little headroom, so the 4-bit quality needs re-measuring.
  3. Only the 8B model can ever be used.
  4. Quantisation never affects accuracy, so 4-bit is free.
The 70B model in 8-bit needs about 125 GB, over 96 GB, while the 4-bit version needs about 90 GB. Quantisation changes the numbers the model computes with, so accuracy must be re-checked on the customer's task. Saying only the 8B fits ignores the larger quantised models.

Three months after launch, the model server's TLS certificate from the customer's internal authority expires and the application stops working. What would most directly have prevented this?

  1. A larger GPU.
  2. A more accurate model.
  3. A tracked calendar of every expiry date with a manual renewal procedure, because automatic renewal assumes a network the zone does not have.
  4. Disabling certificate checks.
Expiry is a predictable failure that automation normally hides. In an isolated zone the renewal path must be designed and rehearsed. Turning off verification removes a security control to cure an operations problem, and capacity or accuracy are unrelated.

A support engineer asks the customer to email "a few failing examples" so they can debug. What is the better pattern in a restricted environment?

  1. A pre-built diagnostic bundle containing versions, configuration, counts and anonymised failure categories, inspected by the customer and released through export review.
  2. Remote screen-sharing into the production system.
  3. Copy the failing examples onto a personal laptop to analyse offline.
  4. Ask the customer to disable logging to simplify the problem.
The diagnostic bundle gives the engineer what is needed to reason about the fault, while the customer decides what leaves. Screen-sharing and personal copies create exactly the paths the isolation exists to prevent, and disabling logging makes the next incident harder to diagnose.
