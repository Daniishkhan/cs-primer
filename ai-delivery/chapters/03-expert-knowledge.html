Capturing Expert Knowledge

The most valuable thing a customer can give you is not data but judgement: how their best people tell a good case from a bad one. Experts rarely know how they do it, and two experts often disagree. This chapter covers shadowing, think-aloud sessions and worked examples, and how to turn what you gather into rubrics, labelled data and a measure of how far the experts agree.

Why this matters

In expert domains such as lending, law, medicine and claims, "correct" is defined by people. Without their judgement you have no gold set, no rubric for a judge model, no tests for the prompt and no way to tell the customer's reviewers anything they do not already know. With it, you have the specification and the evaluation at once. Two traps recur. Experts cannot articulate much of what they know, so asking them for rules produces textbook answers. And experts disagree more than anyone expects, so a single expert's labels are one opinion, not the truth. After this chapter you will be able to choose an elicitation method for a given kind of knowledge, write criteria with anchored levels, measure agreement honestly, and spend scarce expert hours where they count.

Why experts cannot simply tell you

The philosopher Michael Polanyi put it in a sentence in The Tacit Dimension (1966): "we can know more than we can tell". Tacit knowledge is skill and judgement a person uses without being able to state it fully: the experienced adjuster who senses a claim is off, the analyst who looks at a balance sheet and goes straight to receivables. Ask these people for their rules and they will give you the official procedure, which is partly true and leaves out most of what they actually do. This echoes Nisbett and Wilson's finding in chapter 1 that people's accounts of their own reasoning are often reconstructions.

There is a second, less comfortable fact. Even when experts can explain themselves, they often explain different things. Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, in Noise (2021), describe an audit at an insurance company in which underwriters priced identical policies: the median difference between two underwriters' quotes was 55 per cent, while the executives who were asked beforehand expected about 10 per cent. A similar audit of claims adjusters found a median difference of 43 per cent. The authors call this unwanted variability noise, and it comes from different people and from the same person on different days. For you, the consequence is practical: a rubric or a gold set built from one expert encodes that person's quirks as if they were the standard.

So elicitation has two jobs. One is to make the tacit explicit enough to test. The other is to find out how much the experts agree and to settle the differences that matter, in writing. The rest of the chapter takes them in order.

Methods: from what they say to what they change

Different methods reach different layers of knowledge. A sensible plan uses several, and moves down the list as the cost of an error rises.

Shadowing

Shadowing means observing someone do their real work, without directing it. Sit beside them for a few two-hour sessions, ideally on different days and on ordinary work, not a demonstration. Write down, in order: what they open first, what they skip, what they check twice, what they ask someone else, which tools and documents they use, and every moment they hesitate. Collect the artefacts they touch. Ask for examples where the usual rule did not fit, since exceptions show you where the judgement lives.

Practicalities decide whether you are allowed back. Agree in advance what you may see and record; when real customer or personal data is on screen, get permission, follow the customer's confidentiality rules, and never copy data out of their environment without approval (chapter 15 covers why). Be a quiet observer. And note your own presence changes behaviour: people tidy up, so several sessions beat one.

Think-aloud sessions

In a think-aloud session the expert performs a real task and says aloud whatever passes through their mind. The method comes from Ericsson and Simon's work on verbal reports (1980, "Verbal Reports as Data"; the book Protocol Analysis, 1984). Their argument was that speaking out the contents of working memory as you work reveals the sequence of thoughts without much changing the task, whereas asking people afterwards why they did something invites a tidy but invented story. The practical instructions:

  • Give a real case they have not seen, of a kind that is neither trivial nor rare.
  • Ask them to say what they are looking at, thinking and considering. Not to explain or justify. A prompt such as "keep talking" is enough when they fall silent.
  • Do not interrupt to ask why. Note moments to return to, and ask afterwards, case by case, using the recording.
  • Record audio (with consent) and the screen if you can. Transcribe within a day.
  • Run it with at least three experts on overlapping cases. The differences between them are data.

Think-aloud works best on tasks that are mostly verbal and sequential, such as reading a memo or triaging a claim. It is weaker when the skill is perceptual (reading an X-ray) or very fast, where the words lag behind the judgement; there, contrast pairs and edit capture do better.

Critical incidents and contrast pairs

The critical decision method (Klein, Calderwood and MacGregor, 1989) is an interview about one memorable hard case. You ask the expert to walk through a specific incident from its start, mark the decision points, and for each ask what they noticed, what they considered, and what a novice would have done differently. It is the same stance as a blameless postmortem, aimed at expertise instead of error. It is the best tool for the rare, high-stakes cases that ordinary sampling misses.

Contrast pairs exploit the fact that people find it far easier to say what differs between two cases than to state a rule. Show two near-identical cases with different outcomes ("this loan was approved, this one referred") and ask "what is the difference that matters?". The answers are sharp criteria and, as a bonus, each pair is a minimal test case for your system.

Edit capture

Once you have any draft output, from a prototype or even a template, ask the expert to correct it, and record each edit with a one-line reason. An edit is both a labelled preference (this version is better) and a hidden rule (this is why). Edit capture is also how many production systems later improve: reviewers correct outputs, and corrections become data (A6's improvement flywheel). It is easier on the expert than any interview, which is why it works.

Analogy: learning a recipe from a grandparent

Ask a grandmother how she makes bread and she says "flour, water, yeast, salt, knead until it's ready". Stand beside her and you notice she presses the dough with a thumb and adds a splash of water without measuring. Ask her to make a worse loaf deliberately, or to fix yours, and the rules come out: "too tight, it will tear", "wetter than that". The method that works is watching, then comparing and correcting, not asking for the recipe.

Where it breaks down: a recipe has one right answer you can taste. In expert judgement two skilled people can both be right and still differ, and there may be no oracle. The extra work here is agreeing which differences are acceptable.

From a transcript to a criterion

Raw material is not yet a specification. Turning transcripts into something testable is analytical work, and doing it in small visible steps stops you importing your own assumptions. The standard sequence is: mark each cue the expert noticed; note the judgement they made; write each as a single observable check; give the check levels with an anchor example for each; test with a second expert; revise.

Step through

One sentence of think-aloud becomes a rubric criterion

  1. The remark. A credit analyst, thinking aloud over a borrower's accounts, says: "Revenue's up 12 per cent. But receivables are up 40 per cent. That's the thing." (Illustrative transcript.) Nothing here is a rule yet.
  2. The cue. What did the expert notice? Receivables growing much faster than revenue. Experts often skip to the cue without saying why it matters; mark it anyway.
  3. The follow-up check. They then looked at the ageing of receivables: the share older than 90 days was 22 per cent, against 9 per cent a year earlier. The action after the cue is as important as the cue.
  4. The inference. "So the profit is not turning into cash." This is the reasoning that links cue and action, and the part that was never in the written procedure.
  5. The criterion. Write it as something a reviewer, human or model, can verify in a draft: "Compares growth in receivables with growth in revenue; if receivables grow much faster, cites ageing and says what that means for cash conversion."
  6. The revision. A second analyst objects: receivable growth is fine if payment terms were lengthened for one large customer. The criterion gains an exception ("unless terms or customer mix explain it"). That one objection prevented a rule that would have punished correct work.

Writing a rubric that people can apply

A rubric is a list of criteria, each with levels and anchors, that lets different people (or a model) score the same output the same way. Good ones share a few features.

  • Observable. "Cites the page for every ratio" can be checked; "shows good judgement" cannot.
  • One idea per criterion. "Accurate and well written" hides two scores.
  • Few levels, with an anchor example at each. Three or four levels, each with a short example output. Without anchors, a 3 out of 5 means different things to different people.
  • Binary where you can. Pass/fail with a written reason is easier to agree on and to automate than a five-point scale. Hamel Husain recommends this in practice, with periodic checks of automated judges against human judgement.
  • Hard failures kept apart. Some behaviours should fail an output whatever else it does well: recommending an approval where the system may only advise, or inventing a citation. Mark these as must-nots, not as heavy weights.
  • Weights agreed by the experts, not by you, and written down.

Here is a small rubric captured in code, with three drafts of a credit-memo section scored against it. The rubric is illustrative; the point is that it is data, so it can be versioned, reviewed by the experts, and used the same way by a human grader, a script or a judge model.

# A rubric captured from think-aloud sessions with senior credit analysts.
RUBRIC = [
    # id, what a reviewer checks, weight, hard_fail
    ("R1", "States the borrower's repayment source in one sentence", 3, False),
    ("R2", "Cites the figure and the page for every ratio quoted",    3, False),
    ("R3", "Flags any covenant breach in the last 4 quarters",        2, False),
    ("R4", "Does not recommend approval or decline",                  0, True),
]
def score(judgements):
    """judgements: dict id -> bool. Returns (percentage, hard_failed)."""
    got = sum(w for i, _, w, h in RUBRIC if judgements[i] and not h)
    total = sum(w for _, _, w, h in RUBRIC if not h)
    failed = [i for i, _, _, h in RUBRIC if h and not judgements[i]]
    return round(100 * got / total), failed

drafts = {
    "draft A": {"R1": True,  "R2": True,  "R3": False, "R4": True},
    "draft B": {"R1": True,  "R2": True,  "R3": True,  "R4": False},
    "draft C": {"R1": False, "R2": False, "R3": True,  "R4": True},
}
for name, j in drafts.items():
    pct, failed = score(j)
    print(f"{name}: {pct:3d}%  hard fails: {failed or 'none'}")
draft A:  75%  hard fails: none
draft B: 100%  hard fails: ['R4']
draft C:  25%  hard fails: none

Draft B scores a perfect 100 per cent on the weighted criteria but breaks the hard rule (it recommends a decision), so it fails. Draft A is clean but misses a covenant breach. Draft C is correct on the covenant but misses the two core criteria. A single weighted score would have ranked B first; separating the must-nots is what stops that. When you give the rubric to a judge model, score each criterion separately and ask for the quoted evidence, as A6's LLM-as-Judge chapter explains, then compare the judge's labels to the experts' before trusting it.

Worked examples as records

Alongside the rubric, store each case the expert worked on as a structured record: the input, the decision, the cues, the reasoning, a candidate rule, any exceptions voiced, and how confident the expert was. These records become few-shot examples in a prompt, test cases, and the seed of the gold set. The script below checks a record has the fields you need before it is accepted, so a gold set does not quietly fill with half-filled entries.

Common misconception

"If I ask the experts to write the rules down, I will have the specification." What you get is the official procedure, usually shorter and cleaner than practice, and missing the exceptions that make the work hard. Rules written in an armchair also tend to be tested on nothing. The reliable path is to observe, capture real cases, extract rules from them, and then test those rules on new cases with a second expert. Treat any written rule as a hypothesis until it has survived a case it was not drawn from.

Measuring how far experts agree

Before you treat any expert labels as a gold set, have at least two people label the same sample independently and measure agreement. The raw proportion of items on which they agree is misleading, because two people guessing will often agree by chance, especially when one label is common. Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) corrects for this:

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)

Here po is the observed agreement and pe is the agreement expected if each person labelled at random with their own label frequencies. Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for no better than chance, and can be negative. Landis and Koch (1977) proposed verbal bands (0.61 to 0.80 "substantial", above 0.80 "almost perfect"), which are widely quoted but arbitrary; what level you need depends on the stakes. Here is a case where the headline agreement misleads.

from collections import Counter
def kappa(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    po = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    pe = sum(ca[k] * cb[k] for k in set(a) | set(b)) / n ** 2
    return po, pe, (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

# 40 claims, each labelled "pay" or "refer" by two adjusters. 36 of 40 are 'pay'.
A = ["pay"] * 34 + ["refer"] * 2 + ["pay"] * 2 + ["refer"] * 2
B = ["pay"] * 34 + ["pay"] * 2 + ["refer"] * 2 + ["refer"] * 2
po, pe, k = kappa(A, B)
print(f"skewed set:   agreement {po:.2f}, expected by chance {pe:.2f}, kappa {k:.2f}")

# A balanced set with the same 90% raw agreement
A2 = ["pay"] * 18 + ["refer"] * 2 + ["pay"] * 2 + ["refer"] * 18
B2 = ["pay"] * 18 + ["pay"] * 2 + ["refer"] * 2 + ["refer"] * 18
po, pe, k = kappa(A2, B2)
print(f"balanced set: agreement {po:.2f}, expected by chance {pe:.2f}, kappa {k:.2f}")

disagree = [i for i, (x, y) in enumerate(zip(A, B)) if x != y]
print("claims to take back to the experts:", disagree)
skewed set:   agreement 0.90, expected by chance 0.82, kappa 0.44
balanced set: agreement 0.90, expected by chance 0.50, kappa 0.80
claims to take back to the experts: [34, 35, 36, 37]

The last line of the output lists the four claims to take back to the experts. That is the main use of agreement statistics: not the single number but the list of disagreements. Each is a conversation to have, and each conversation produces a written ruling: "referral is mandatory when the claimant is also the repairer", or "both are acceptable; use pay". A ruling that two experts can both live with becomes a criterion or a documented tolerance.

What to notice. With both experts 95 per cent accurate and only 10 per cent of items "refer", the expected kappa is only around 0.6, and across 40-item sessions it can land anywhere from roughly 0.25 to 0.9: two accurate people can look poor or excellent depending on the draw. Raise the share of "refer" to 50 per cent and kappa rises with no change in skill. Raise the sample to 400 and the band narrows. The lessons are the ones in chapter 2: report an interval, and stratify your sample so that the rare, important category has enough examples to say anything about it (A6 covers this in Human Labels).

Calibration: where agreement is built

Low agreement at the first round is normal and not a failure; it is how you find the ambiguity in the criteria. The loop that fixes it is a calibration session.

Two disciplines make this work. Experts must label independently before they see each other's answers, or the first confident voice wins and you measure deference. And every ruling is written down with the case that prompted it, so the guideline grows from real disputes. The final guideline is part of the deliverable: new reviewers, and the customer's auditors, will need it.

Spending expert hours

Expert time is the scarcest input in this stage, usually measured in a few hours a week from people with full jobs. The right question is not "how many labels?" but "where does each hour buy the most information?". Three plans for a 300-item gold set, with illustrative timings of four minutes for a first label with a reason, and ten minutes to discuss and rule on each disagreement:

# What does senior-expert time cost for a 300-item gold set under three plans?
MIN_PER_ITEM_FIRST = 4      # a first label with a one-line reason
MIN_PER_DISPUTE = 10        # discussing a disagreement and writing the ruling
n_items, agreement = 300, 0.85
plan_a = n_items * MIN_PER_ITEM_FIRST / 60
plan_b_first = n_items * MIN_PER_ITEM_FIRST / 60 * 2          # two experts label everything
plan_b_disputes = n_items * (1 - agreement) * MIN_PER_DISPUTE / 60
print(f"one expert labels all {n_items}: {plan_a:.1f} h (no way to measure agreement)")
print(f"two experts label all {n_items}: {plan_b_first:.1f} h, plus {plan_b_disputes:.1f} h on disputes = {plan_b_first + plan_b_disputes:.1f} h")
# cheaper: second expert labels a random 60 for agreement, then disputes only on those
n2 = 60
cost = (n_items * MIN_PER_ITEM_FIRST + n2 * MIN_PER_ITEM_FIRST + n2 * (1 - agreement) * MIN_PER_DISPUTE) / 60
print(f"one labels all, second labels {n2}: {cost:.1f} h")
one expert labels all 300: 20.0 h (no way to measure agreement)
two experts label all 300: 40.0 h, plus 7.5 h on disputes = 47.5 h
one labels all, second labels 60: 25.5 h

One expert labelling everything is cheapest but gives no estimate of reliability. Having two experts label everything gives the strongest set at about 2.4 times the cost. The compromise labels everything once and has a second expert label a random 60 (stratified to include the rare class), spending the discussion time on those disputes: about 25 hours, with an agreement estimate and a list of rulings. Where an item set is large, add selection: spend expert time on items where a model is unsure or where two models disagree, since easy items add little. Whatever the plan, tell the customer the hours you need in the requirements (chapter 2) before the project starts, and ask the sponsor to protect that time.

State of play, October 2026

Language models can now draft candidate criteria from transcripts, cluster disagreements and suggest edge cases, which can cut the analysis time considerably. The risk is that the draft reads fluently whether or not it matches what the expert meant, so it can look finished when it is wrong. A workable pattern: let a model propose, but have the expert accept, edit or reject every criterion, and measure the model-assisted labels against independent expert labels as you would any judge. Tools and capabilities here change quickly; the discipline of putting the expert's judgement back in the loop does not.

Using what you have captured

  • As the evaluation. Rubrics and labelled cases are the gold set and metrics of chapter 6; they make the "success metric" of chapter 2 measurable.
  • As the prompt. Criteria become instructions; worked examples become few-shot demonstrations; exceptions become explicit conditions. See Prompting as Programming.
  • As the design. The order in which experts look at things (receivables before margin) suggests the order of steps in a workflow, and the cues suggest which tools or fields to expose; see Workflow Patterns.
  • As the feedback loop. Reviewers' corrections after launch are the same edit capture, now continuous. Plan where they will be recorded.

The case study An Expert Workflow for Bank Lending shows this chain end to end: decomposing expert reasoning into steps, and calibrating the feedback loop to the reviewers.

Working with experts, humanely

Experts are being asked to help build something that may change their jobs, and they know it. Be straightforward about what the system is for. Frame it, where it is true, as taking the repetitive part so that their judgement goes to the hard cases, and do not promise what you cannot control. Credit their contribution in the documentation. Keep individual attributions out of aggregate findings: an expert who sees "analyst B was the outlier" in a report will not help again. Protect confidential material that appears in cases, and obtain permission before using real records as examples. If the knowledge you capture is the firm's core know-how, agree who owns it and where it is stored, since a rubric and a set of worked rulings can be the most valuable artefact the project leaves behind.

Try it yourself

Choose a judgement you can test on a friend or colleague: ranking job applications, deciding whether an email is urgent, or grading a short essay. Give two people the same 20 items and ask them to label independently. Type the labels into kappa.py in place of the examples and compute raw agreement and kappa. List the disagreements, discuss each with both people, and write a one-line ruling per dispute. Then label a fresh set of 20 and see whether kappa went up.

Systems connection

Capturing tacit knowledge is what a good runbook does for operations, and the critical decision method is a close relative of the blameless postmortem: reconstruct a specific event step by step, and ask what the person noticed and why, not who was at fault. Agreement statistics are the same instrument as the inter-rater checks in Human Labels, and the permission and consent questions connect to data protection.

Key takeaways

  • Experts know more than they can tell. Asking for rules yields the official procedure; observation and real cases reveal the practice.
  • Experts disagree with each other and with themselves (noise). A single expert's labels are one opinion.
  • Climb the methods as stakes rise: interview, shadowing, think-aloud, critical incidents, contrast pairs, edit capture. Think-aloud asks for thoughts, not explanations.
  • Turn transcripts into observable criteria with few levels and anchor examples. Keep hard failures separate from weighted scores, and test each criterion on a second expert.
  • Measure agreement with kappa on independent labels, report it with its uncertainty, and use the list of disagreements to write rulings.
  • Calibrate in a loop, test on fresh cases, and spend scarce expert hours on disputes, rare classes and uncertain items.
  • Treat experts as partners: be honest about the purpose, credit them, and protect confidentiality.

Check your understanding

You ask a senior underwriter to list the rules she uses. The rules she gives are tidy and complete, but when you watch her work she makes decisions the rules do not cover. What is the best explanation and response?

  1. She is hiding her real method; ask a more junior colleague instead.
  2. The rules are correct, and the extra decisions are mistakes to ignore.
  3. Much of her skill is tacit; capture real cases through shadowing, think-aloud and contrast pairs, then write rules and test them on new cases.
  4. Record her reasoning afterwards by asking why she chose each way.
Stated rules usually describe the official procedure, and expertise includes unspoken judgement (Polanyi). Retrospective "why" questions invite constructed explanations (Nisbett and Wilson, Ericsson and Simon), so they are the weakest remedy, though tempting. A junior colleague would know less, not more, and the unlisted decisions are the point of the exercise.

Two adjusters label 40 claims and agree on 36, but 36 of the 40 are "pay" and kappa is 0.44. How should you read this?

  1. Raw agreement is inflated by chance on the common label; the rare "refer" cases are where they disagree, so review those.
  2. Kappa is wrong; 90 per cent agreement means reliable labels.
  3. The adjusters are unskilled and should be replaced.
  4. Add more "pay" examples to raise kappa.
When one label dominates, two people agree often by chance, so the corrected statistic is much lower. The disagreement sits in the rare class, which is usually the important one. Adding more "pay" cases would raise agreement without fixing the issue. Nothing about the data shows the adjusters are unskilled; it shows the guideline for "refer" is unclear.

A draft credit-memo section scores 100% on all weighted rubric criteria but recommends approving the loan, which the system must never do. What should the rubric's result be?

  1. Pass, because the weighted score is perfect.
  2. Pass with a deduction of a few points.
  3. Fail only if a human also objects.
  4. Fail, because a hard-fail criterion is separate from the weighted score and overrides it.
Must-not behaviours should not be traded off against quality elsewhere, which is why the rubric keeps them apart from the weights. A deduction would let a harmful output rank well. The rubric is meant to be applied the same way by a script or a judge model, without an extra human objection.

During a think-aloud session the expert falls silent for twenty seconds while studying a table. What should you do?

  1. Ask "why did you stop?" and have her explain her reasoning.
  2. Prompt with something neutral like "keep talking" or "what are you looking at?", and save the "why" for afterwards, using the recording.
  3. Fill the silence by suggesting what she might be thinking.
  4. End the session, since silence means the method is not working.
The method asks for a running report of thoughts, not explanations, which would change the thinking. A neutral prompt keeps the flow. Suggesting thoughts contaminates the data with your own assumptions. Pauses are normal and informative.

After a calibration session where two experts discussed 12 disputes and updated the guideline, their kappa on those same 12 items rises to 0.9. Why is this not good evidence the guideline works?

  1. Kappa cannot be computed on only 12 items.
  2. Kappa is always higher after a discussion.
  3. They have now seen those cases and the rulings, so agreement on them reflects memory; the guideline must be tested on a fresh batch.
  4. The experts should have been allowed to see each other's labels in advance.
Agreement on the cases that shaped the guideline is circular. A fresh, unseen batch shows whether the written guideline lets two people reach the same label. Kappa can be computed on small samples (with wide uncertainty), and seeing each other's labels beforehand would defeat the point of independent labelling.

You have 25 hours of expert time to build a 300-item gold set for a task with a rare but important class. Which plan is most sensible?

  1. One expert labels everything and nobody checks, to save time.
  2. Two experts label all 300 independently, regardless of the budget.
  3. Pick only the easiest 100 items, since they are quickest.
  4. One expert labels all; a second labels a random sample that includes enough of the rare class; discuss only the disagreements and write rulings.
The last plan gives an agreement estimate and a list of rulings within budget (about 25 hours in the chapter's numbers). Single-expert labels cannot show whether the set is reliable. Labelling everything twice takes about 47 hours. Easy items teach little and leave the rare class untested.
