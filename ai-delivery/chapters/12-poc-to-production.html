From Proof of Concept to Production

The demo worked, the customer was pleased, and then nothing shipped. This chapter explains why so many AI pilots stall, what a production gate actually checks, how a system is wired into the places where work happens, who looks after it on a Tuesday night, and how to get people to use it. It answers one question: what separates a convincing proof from a service someone relies on?

Why this matters

The earlier chapters of this course get you to a result a customer believes: a problem worth solving, a requirements document both sides signed, a prototype of the riskiest assumption and an evaluation plan they trust. The proof is usually the smaller half of the job. Surveys and analyst forecasts keep reporting that a large share of generative AI pilots never reach daily use, and the reasons are rarely the model. They are an unowned system, a missing integration, a security review nobody started, a cost nobody priced, and users who were never asked to change how they work. After this chapter you will be able to explain what a pilot skips, define and enforce production gates with evidence, plan a staged rollout that earns autonomy slice by slice, design the integration so that failure is safe, write down who supports the system and how it is kept healthy, and plan the human side of the change.

Why pilots stall

A proof of concept (PoC) answers the question "can this work at all?". A pilot is a larger trial with real users on real work for a limited time, which answers "does it work here?". Production means that people rely on the system for their normal work, it keeps running when you are not watching, and someone is accountable for it. The distance between the last two is where projects die.

State of play, October 2026

Two widely quoted numbers frame the problem; treat both with care. In July 2024 the analyst firm Gartner forecast that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after the proof-of-concept stage by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value. In June 2025 it forecast that over 40% of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing cost, unclear value and weak risk controls. In August 2025 coverage of an MIT Project NANDA report, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, popularised the claim that about 95% of organisations saw no measurable profit-and-loss return from their generative AI initiatives. I could not fetch the Gartner releases or the MIT report itself (the sources here are press coverage of them), and the 95% figure rests on the report's own definitions of "pilot" and "measurable", so quote it as "reported", not as a law of nature. Forecasts are not measurements. What matters for your work is the pattern all of them name: failures are mostly about value, risk and cost, not about model quality.

The pattern has an uncomfortable cause. A pilot is designed to succeed. It uses a hand-picked set of documents, a friendly group of users, a developer standing nearby to restart things, and credentials on someone's laptop. Each of those is a sensible way to learn fast, and each hides a production requirement. Compare what the pilot proved with what production needs.

Google researchers made the same point about machine learning systems in general. In "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" (Sculley et al., 2015) they showed, with a now famous figure, that the model code is a small box surrounded by much larger boxes for data collection, configuration, serving infrastructure and monitoring. Modern LLM systems have a different shape (the model is often rented, not trained) but the proportions are similar: the prompt and the model call are a small part of what you ship.

Four specific causes of stalling come up again and again, and each can be seen coming.

  1. No agreed definition of "good enough". The pilot ended with a feeling ("it's impressive"), not a number both sides signed. Without a bar, any remaining defect can postpone launch indefinitely. This is the work of requirements under ambiguity and evaluation plans, and it is cheapest to do before the pilot.
  2. No owner on the customer's side. The sponsor funded an experiment; nobody has the budget line, the headcount or the mandate to run a service. When your engagement ends, so does the project.
  3. Late-arriving gatekeepers. Security, legal, privacy, procurement and the risk function each hold a veto and each moves in weeks or months. A project that meets them for the first time after the pilot has lost a quarter. The security review chapter shows how to start that conversation on day one.
  4. The economics were never tested. A pilot on 200 documents costs pennies; the same design on 2 million may not be affordable, or may save less than the review it requires. Cost models and return on investment covers the arithmetic.

Common misconception

"If the pilot's accuracy was good, production is an engineering task we can schedule afterwards." Accuracy on a curated sample is one input to a launch decision, and often not the binding one. The binding constraint is more often the security sign-off, the integration with a system nobody has an API for, or the fact that the approval step costs more than the work it replaces. Plan production from the first week: put the gates, the owner and the unit cost on the same page as the accuracy bar.

Production gates: evidence, not enthusiasm

A production gate is a named condition that must be met, with evidence someone other than the author can inspect, before a system moves to the next stage of exposure. The word "gate" is deliberate: it is a decision point, with a person who may say no, not a document to be filled in. Software teams have always had release criteria; what changes for AI systems is that the output is not deterministic, so several gates are statistical, and that some of the risks (a model that follows instructions hidden in a customer's email) have no counterpart in ordinary software. Six gates cover most engagements. Each has an owner and artefacts, and each links to a chapter that teaches the underlying skill.

Two design rules make gates work. First, every gate demands an artefact. "The team is confident" is not evidence; a link to the sealed-set run, with the commit it ran on, is. Second, not every gate is equally hard. For an internal drafting aid that a person always reads, a missing runbook is a condition to fix within a month. For a system that sends money or advice to customers, it blocks launch. Decide which gates are hard before you start, and write that down with the customer. Gates are earned in stages, not once

A gate is not a single event on launch day. A system passes a lighter version of each gate to reach a limited audience, and the full version to widen it. This is the same idea as the canary and staged rollouts of ordinary software, with one addition specific to AI: the system's autonomy grows in steps, because the cost of an error depends on whether a person sees the output before it acts.

Step through

The autonomy ladder: each rung is earned with evidence

  1. Shadow. The model processes real inputs and its answers are recorded but never shown or acted on. Humans keep doing the work. You learn how it behaves on the real distribution, which is rarely the one in your gold set, at no risk to anyone.
  2. Assist. Users see the draft and decide. Risk is bounded because a person approves every output, but watch for people approving without reading. Track how often they edit or reject the draft: that is your first honest quality signal from production.
  3. Partial automation. Only the slices of work whose shadow and assist numbers clear the bar go straight through; everything else stays in assist. The next section shows this decision on real counts. Sample a fraction of the automated cases for audit.
  4. Wider automation. More slices are admitted as evidence accumulates. Humans handle exceptions and audits. Some slices never get here by policy, however good the numbers look.
  5. Rungs are reversible. An alarm, an incident or a model change sends the affected slice back down. A ladder you cannot descend is a cliff, so build the switch (and rehearse it) before you climb.

Deciding which slices to automate

"Automate the task" is rarely the right unit. A support queue contains address changes, which are routine, and bereavement notices, which are not. The model's quality differs by slice, and so does the cost of an error. The script below takes counts from a two-week shadow run on an insurer's servicing queue (the counts are illustrative assumptions, not a real deployment) and applies a rule: a slice is automated only if the lower end of a 95% Wilson interval for agreement with the human decision reaches 95%, the slice has at least 100 cases, and policy does not forbid it. The interval is the one from statistics for evals; using the lower end means a slice has to prove itself, not just look good.

# Shadow mode: the model decides silently beside the humans for two weeks.
# Which slices of the work have earned automation? (Counts are illustrative assumptions.)
import math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

# slice -> (cases seen in shadow, cases where model == human final decision)
SHADOW = {
    "address change":       (412, 405),
    "payment date change":  (268, 259),
    "dispute: amount":      (143, 126),
    "complaint: tone":      ( 96,  79),
    "bereavement notice":   ( 38,  37),
}
BAR = 0.95          # lower end of the 95% interval must reach this to automate
MIN_N = 100         # and the slice needs at least this many cases
ALWAYS_HUMAN = {"bereavement notice"}   # policy: never automated, whatever the numbers say

print(f"{'slice':22s} {'n':>4s} {'agree':>6s} {'low':>6s} {'high':>6s}  decision")
auto = total = 0
for name, (n, k) in SHADOW.items():
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    if name in ALWAYS_HUMAN:
        d = "human only (policy)"
    elif n < MIN_N:
        d = "keep watching (too few cases)"
    elif lo >= BAR:
        d = "AUTOMATE"; auto += n
    else:
        d = "assist only (draft, human sends)"
    total += n
    print(f"{name:22s} {n:4d} {k/n:6.1%} {lo:6.1%} {hi:6.1%}  {d}")
print(f"\nshare of volume that would be automated: {auto/total:.0%}")

Read the output as a negotiation aid. Address changes clear the bar with room to spare (96.5% at the lower end). Payment date changes look good at 96.6% agreement, but 268 cases leave the lower end at 93.7%, so they stay in assist until more evidence arrives. Disputes and complaints are far below, and the complaint slice has too few cases to say anything. The bereavement notices agree 97.4% of the time, yet the policy line keeps them with humans: some decisions are not delegated whatever the statistics say, and the customer should be the one to name them. The headline is also more useful than "94% accurate": 43% of volume can be automated now, and here is the evidence for the rest.

A natural next question is how long shadow mode must run. That depends on how good you expect the slice to be, because proving a high rate takes surprisingly many cases.

# How many shadow-mode cases before a slice can clear a 95% lower bound?
# Smallest n such that, at the expected agreement rate, the Wilson lower end reaches the bar.
import math

def wilson_low(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h

BAR = 0.95
print("expected agreement -> cases needed for lower end >= 95%")
for rate in (0.96, 0.97, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995):
    n = 20
    while wilson_low(round(rate * n), n) < BAR:
        n += 1
        if n > 5000: break
    print(f"  {rate:5.1%}  ->  {n if n <= 5000 else 'more than 5,000'}")
expected agreement -> cases needed for lower end >= 95%
  96.0%  ->  1736
  97.0%  ->  414
  98.0%  ->  173
  99.0%  ->  110
  99.5%  ->  73

A slice that truly agrees 96% of the time needs about 1,700 cases before its lower end reaches 95%, while one at 99% needs about 110. At 99.5% the lower end clears the bar after 73 cases with no disagreement at all. The practical lessons: a slice that is only slightly better than the bar will take a long time to prove (so either lower the bar with the customer's agreement, or accept assist mode), and a rare slice may never accumulate enough cases, which is another reason to keep it with humans.

Integration: where the system meets the work

A model that answers questions in a notebook is not yet part of anyone's job. People work in a case-management tool, a CRM, a ticket queue, an email client or a spreadsheet, and the system has to appear there, with the right permissions, and leave its results where the next step of the process expects them. This is usually the largest block of unglamorous work in an engagement, and the one most often left out of the estimate.

Five integration concerns decide whether the system survives contact with the real organisation.

  • Identity and permissions. The system must act with the user's rights, not its own. If a retrieval index contains documents from several departments, a user must only receive answers drawn from what they could open themselves; filters, metadata and permissions covers the mechanism, and authorisation the principles. A pilot that ran as one service account usually breaks this.
  • The write path must be safe to repeat. Networks fail between "sent" and "acknowledged", and workers retry. If the model's result creates a ticket, updates a record or sends an email, a retry must not do it twice. The standard fix is an idempotency key: a unique identifier for the intended action, stored by the receiving system so that a repeated request is recognised and ignored. See failure, timeouts and retries and logs and exactly-once.
  • Provider failure is a normal event. A model provider will time out, rate-limit you, or retire a version. The model gateway (a thin internal service or library through which all model calls pass) is where timeouts, retry budgets, spend caps and a fallback model live, and where you can swap providers without touching application code. Choosing models explains why you keep a second choice ready.
  • Systems without APIs. Many customers hold crucial data in an old application with no interface. Options, in order of preference: ask for a database view or an export job; use a supported integration; use robotic process automation; and last, drive the screen with a computer-use agent, which is slow, brittle and needs the monitoring of an unreliable component. Cost the brittle options honestly.
  • Where the human sits. The review step should happen inside the tool people already use, with the evidence beside the draft (the source passage, the figures it used), and one-click accept, edit or reject. A review screen in a separate application that nobody opens is the quiet way that automation fails. Grounded citations are what make a fast review possible.

Analogy: a new hire, not a new gadget

A pilot is like a trial day for a candidate: a manager watches, picks the tasks and takes the result away. Production is the first month of employment. The new hire needs a login, the right access (and no more), a desk in the place where the work happens, someone to ask when stuck, a manager who reviews early work and gives more responsibility as it proves reliable, and colleagues who have been told what to expect. The autonomy ladder is probation, and the production gates are onboarding.

Where it breaks down: a person learns from correction and carries yesterday's lessons into today. A deployed model does not improve unless you retrain, re-prompt or change the system, and a provider's update can alter its behaviour overnight with nobody telling you. The "employee" also works at a thousand times the speed, so a mistake is repeated at scale before anyone notices.

Staying healthy: support, monitoring and drift

A deployed AI system degrades even when nobody touches it. Inputs change (new product lines, a new form template), the provider updates the model, a connected system changes its schema, and users find new ways to use it. Looking after it means detecting these changes quickly, deciding what to do, and doing it without breaking something else. The chapters on observability and the improvement flywheel teach the machinery; here is what a delivery engineer must put in place before leaving.

Three streams of signal

  • Automated checks on every call: format validity, refusal rate, latency, cost per task, citation present, guardrail trips. Cheap, immediate, and good for catching outages and gross changes.
  • Sampled human review: a reviewer re-checks a random sample of outputs each week against the same rubric used in evaluation. This is the only signal for subtle quality loss. It needs a named reviewer and protected time, or it quietly stops.
  • User feedback and overrides: how often users edit, reject or ignore the output, and what they say. A rising override rate is often the first visible symptom, and the edits are labelled data for the next round of improvement.

The sample size matters. With a weekly sample of 80 answers a team can see a fall from 95% to about 86%, but only with an interval that accounts for the sampling noise, or it will drown in false alarms. The simulation below uses a random sample (seeded, so it is repeatable) in which the provider updates the model at the start of week 7. The alarm rule is: raise it when the upper end of the 95% interval falls below a floor of 90%, which means the data say quality is below the floor, not merely that this week was unlucky.

# Weekly quality monitoring: a reviewer re-checks a random sample of the model's answers.
# In week 7 the provider silently updates the model and true accuracy drops.
import random, math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

random.seed(12)
SAMPLE = 80          # answers re-checked per week
FLOOR = 0.90         # alarm if the UPPER end of the interval falls below this
for week in range(1, 13):
    true_acc = 0.95 if week < 7 else 0.86
    k = sum(random.random() < true_acc for _ in range(SAMPLE))
    lo, hi = wilson(k, SAMPLE)
    flag = "ALARM: quality below floor" if hi < FLOOR else ""
    print(f"week {week:2d}  {k:2d}/{SAMPLE}  {k/SAMPLE:5.1%}  interval {lo:5.1%} to {hi:5.1%}  {flag}")

Two lessons come out of the figure. First, a sample of 80 does catch a 9-point fall, within one week here, but a sample of 20 would not. Size the sample to the change you must detect, and use the arithmetic from statistics for evals. Second, the alarm stopped firing while the system was still worse than before. A rule that fires only on a significant breach is an alarm, not a status. Pair it with a standing comparison against the launch baseline ("the lower end is still below the launch number, so the incident stays open until fixed"), and make sure a human has to close the incident, not the alarm's silence.

What the support model must say

Write the following down, with names, before handover. It reads as dull paperwork; it is what makes the system survive staff turnover.

QuestionExample answer
Who owns the system on the customer's side?A named business owner for outcomes, a named technical owner for the service.
Who is paged, and when?Technical owner's team, for outage, a cost spike, or a safety alarm; business hours otherwise.
What are the top failure modes and the first response to each?A runbook page each: provider down (switch to fallback), quality alarm (freeze prompt changes, roll back), cost spike (apply cap).
How is a change released?Prompt, model and retrieval changes go through the regression suite and a staged rollout, like code.
Who decides when a provider retires a model?The technical owner, using the migration plan: re-run the eval, compare, re-approve.
Who reviews the sample each week, and what do they do with findings?A named reviewer; findings go to a triaged backlog with a monthly review.
What does the customer get when the contract ends?Code, prompts, eval sets, runbooks and access, so they can run it without you.

Treat prompts and model versions as configuration under version control, as in configuration and infrastructure as code, with a regression run attached to each change. The common failure is a well-meaning edit to a prompt in an admin screen, made to fix one complaint, that breaks three cases nobody remembered. And plan the AI incident process before you need it: how to pause automation for one slice, who tells affected users, and how the postmortem feeds the eval set.

Change management: the part with the people in it

Change management is the work of helping the people affected by a new system adopt it and keep their trust calibrated. For AI it is not decoration. A system that is technically live and unused earns nothing, and one that is over-trusted is dangerous. Treat adoption as a product requirement with its own metrics.

Understand what the change means for each group

Different people see the same system differently, and you meet all of them.

The people doing the work

They ask: will this make my job easier or watch me? will I be blamed if it is wrong? are my skills being replaced? Their expertise is the quality bar, and their goodwill decides whether the override button is used honestly.

Their managers

They own the targets. If a team is measured on tickets closed per hour, a tool that adds a review step looks like a penalty. Change the measures, or the tool will be quietly avoided.

Risk, legal and the works council

They hold vetoes and want to be consulted early, not informed late. In some countries employee representatives must be consulted before workplace monitoring tools are introduced; ask the customer's HR early which rules apply.

Trust has to be calibrated in both directions

Parasuraman and Riley's "Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, and Abuse" (Human Factors, 1997) named two ways people get automation wrong: misuse, over-relying on it (often called automation bias), and disuse, ignoring it when it would help. Both are live risks with a fluent language model. Reviewers who see a polished draft tend to approve it, and reviewers burnt by one confident mistake may never trust it again. What you can do:

  • Show evidence, not just conclusions. Put the source passage next to the claim. A reviewer can check a cited sentence in seconds; they cannot check a bare assertion.
  • Be honest about what it is bad at. A one-page "what this tool does well, what it gets wrong, what to always check" sheet, written from your error analysis, calibrates trust better than a training deck on its strengths.
  • Seed known errors. In assist mode, periodically inject drafts with a deliberate flaw and measure whether reviewers catch it. A catch rate that falls over time is automation bias made visible. Tell users this happens; do not trap them.
  • Make dissent cheap. One click to flag a bad output, a visible response ("fixed in this week's update"), and a person who reads the flags.

An adoption plan that fits on a page

  1. Find a champion in each team: a respected practitioner (not the most enthusiastic manager) who tries it first, gives frank feedback and helps colleagues. Give them time on the project, not an extra duty.
  2. Train on the customer's real cases, in the tool they will use, for 45 minutes, including how to disagree with the system.
  3. Roll out in waves by team, each with a feedback week. Fix the top three complaints between waves and say so.
  4. Measure adoption with behaviour: weekly active users out of eligible, share of eligible tasks that went through the tool, override rate, time to complete against the baseline from the evaluation plan. Do not rely on a satisfaction survey alone.
  5. Align the incentives. If the metric rewards raw volume, reviewers will rubber-stamp; if it rewards quality caught, they will review.
  6. Plan the work that disappears. If the system removes tasks, the customer must tell people honestly what happens to the roles. Hidden headcount plans leak, and distrust follows the project into every later rollout.

Two cases in this course show the human side at different scales. The law-firm rollout is a study in verification rules and adoption when every output must be checked by a professional, and Klarna's AI assistant shows what happens when automation claims meet customers and the organisation later changes its mind about the balance with humans.

Handing over, and deciding what comes next

The last gate is yours. A delivery engineer's work is finished when the customer can run, change and fix the system without them, and when both sides know what the next investment would buy.

  • Handover pack: architecture diagram; the evidence for each gate; code, prompts and configuration in the customer's repository; the evaluation set with the labelling procedure; runbooks; dashboards with sample queries; a cost model with its assumptions; a known-limitations page; and a list of decisions made and why.
  • A period of shadowed operation. Their team runs it for two to four weeks while you watch, answer questions and fix gaps. The first incident they handle themselves is the real acceptance test.
  • A written review at 30, 90 and 180 days comparing the launch targets with measured results, including the cost and adoption numbers. Customers who see an honest review are the ones who fund the next phase; those who hear nothing assume the worst.

Systems connection

Almost everything here is ordinary operations applied to a component that happens to be statistical. Gates mirror release criteria and the deployment strategies of staged rollout, rollback and canary analysis. Safe write-back is the idempotency of retries and the design of ML-backed services. Monitoring and the on-call model come from SLOs and error budgets and postmortems, and the permission model from secure design review. What is new is that the "service level" includes a quality number you must estimate from samples, with all the statistics that implies.

Key takeaways

  • Pilots are built to succeed; each shortcut that made them fast hides a production requirement. Reported stall rates are forecasts and survey claims, but they point to value, risk and cost more than model quality.
  • A production gate is a decision with evidence an outsider can open. Agree up front which gates are hard for the stakes involved.
  • Earn autonomy in steps (shadow, assist, partial, wider), slice by slice, using the lower end of an interval, not the point estimate. Build the way back down before climbing.
  • Integration is most of the work: act with the user's permissions, make the write path idempotent, put the human review inside their tool, and keep provider failure behind a gateway.
  • Systems drift. Combine automated checks, a sampled human review sized to the change you must detect, and override tracking. Do not let a quiet alarm mean a healthy system.
  • Adoption is a product requirement: calibrate trust, align incentives, name champions, and measure behaviour. Hand over so the customer can run it without you.

  4. How many clerks can attend the training session?
A curated sample is the pilot's biggest source of optimism: real input includes the strange cases the sample left out. Measuring on the real distribution (shadow mode) tells you what accuracy to plan around. Raising a number that may already be inflated, or choosing the newest model, does not address that; training matters, but cannot compensate for an unmeasured quality gap.

In shadow mode, a slice of 268 cases agrees with humans 96.6% of the time (lower end of the 95% interval 93.7%). The bar is a lower end of 95%. What is the right decision?

  1. Automate: 96.6% is above 95%.
  2. Reject the slice permanently.
  3. Automate but sample 1% of cases.
  4. Keep it in assist mode and keep collecting evidence; the data do not yet show the slice clears the bar, though it may.
The rule uses the lower end, which is below the bar, so the slice has not earned automation. It is not hopeless either, because the upper end is well above 95%: more cases would narrow the interval. Using the point estimate is the temptation the rule exists to prevent, and sampling afterwards does not substitute for earning the rung first.

Your system writes an approved reply into the customer's case tool. A network timeout makes the worker retry the write, and the customer finds two identical replies on some cases. What would have prevented this?

  1. An idempotency key stored by the case tool, so a repeated request for the same action is recognised and ignored.
  2. A larger model that produces fewer duplicates.
  3. Removing retries so a timeout always fails the case.
  4. Asking users to delete duplicates manually.
The duplicates come from the write path, not the model: a timeout does not tell the sender whether the request arrived, so a retry can repeat the action. An idempotency key makes the repeat harmless. Removing retries trades duplicates for lost replies, and manual clean-up moves the cost to users.

After a provider silently updates a model, your weekly sample (80 answers) shows alarms in weeks 7 to 9, then no alarms in weeks 10 to 12 although accuracy is still about nine points below launch. What does this show?

  1. The problem fixed itself, so no action is needed.
  2. The sample is too large; use 20 answers instead.
  3. An alarm that fires only on a significant breach says little about whether the system is back to normal; compare against the launch baseline and require a person to close the incident.
  4. Alarms should be removed because they are noisy.
With 80 answers the interval is wide, so a milder but real shortfall can sit above the alarm floor. A standing comparison with the baseline keeps the incident open. A smaller sample would be noisier and miss more, and deleting alarms removes the only automatic signal.

An insurer's claims system is a medium-stakes tool. All Quality and Security evidence is in place, the runbooks are not written, and the champion programme has not started. Can the board report "ready with conditions"? Which statement about its logic is correct?

  1. Yes, because Quality and Security are the only hard gates at any stakes.
  2. No: at medium stakes Operations and Safety are also hard gates, so missing runbooks block launch; only Reliability and Adoption would be conditions.
  3. Yes, because runbooks can always be written after launch.
  4. No, because adoption is always a hard gate.
At medium stakes the chapter's board makes Quality, Security, Safety and Operations hard. Missing runbooks therefore block launch (as would missing safety evidence). Adoption is only a hard gate at high stakes. "Runbooks can come later" is exactly the habit that turns the first incident into a crisis.

Reviewers in assist mode approve 99% of drafts, and a seeded test shows they catch only 3 of 10 deliberately flawed drafts. What is the most likely explanation and the best response?

  1. The drafts are excellent, so reduce review.
  2. The reviewers are careless, so replace them.
  3. The test is unfair, so stop running it.
  4. Automation bias: fluent drafts are being approved without checking. Show evidence beside each claim, brief reviewers on known weak spots, and treat the catch rate as a metric.
A 99% approval rate combined with a low catch rate is the signature of over-trust, which Parasuraman and Riley called misuse. The cure is to make verification easy and visible, not to blame people or remove the check. A high approval rate alone cannot show the drafts are good, because approval is what over-trust looks like.
