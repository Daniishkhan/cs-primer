Where Next

You have been through nine courses and a set of studios. The field will keep changing under what you learned. This closing chapter separates what lasts from what expires, offers a routine for keeping up that fits into a working week, and lays out next steps: getting a first role, going deep on one area, and learning from real customers.

Why this matters

Course material ages at different speeds. A model name or a framework API can be stale in months, while a good evaluation habit or a clear failure notice lasts a career. People who burn out in this field usually try to follow everything. People who last follow a few sources on a schedule, keep their own tests, and let the durable skills compound. After this chapter you can sort what you read into durable and volatile, set a reading budget, and choose a next step that fits your situation.

What you now have

The path was built in layers, and the layers explain what to maintain. The foundations (mathematics and code for machine learning, classical machine learning, deep learning) change slowly. The build courses (training and serving language models, agents and protocols, retrieval and document AI) change faster, in their tools more than their ideas. The safety and evaluation course (A6) is about methods that survive model generations: error analysis, statistics, guardrails, observability. The search course (A7) is the most research-facing. This capstone ties them to the work of a customer engagement. Under all of it sit the first eight courses on networks, systems, data, architecture, distributed systems, reliability, security and design, which is why you could write an SLO and a threat model for an AI service in chapter 16.

Keeping up without drowning

The number of papers, releases and commentary posts per week is larger than anyone can read, so the aim is not to read everything but to choose a small set of inputs and a routine, a reading budget fixed in advance, with the original paper, changelog or data (the primary source) preferred to summaries. Three ideas make this manageable.

Sort by half-life. The half-life of a piece of knowledge is how long it stays true. Ask of anything you read: will this still be true in two years? A new model's benchmark ranking will not be. A technique that changes how you structure an evaluation, or a failure mode you had not considered, might be. Spend more time on the second kind. Throughout this course, volatile facts sit in dated "State of play" boxes for exactly this reason; the durable explanation is in the main text. Use the same split in your notes.

Use your own tests as the sensor. A regression suite is a saved set of cases rerun after every change. The best way to know what a new model or tool changes for you is to run your regression suite and evaluation set against it. If you kept a gold set from your projects (see the previous chapter), a new release costs an afternoon to assess against your own tasks, which tells you more than a leaderboard. This is why the improvement flywheel matters for your career as well as your product.

Read with three questions. For a paper, post or release note: what exactly is claimed, and on what evidence? Would it change something I build or measure? What would I test first if it did? If the answer to the second is no, stop reading. The three-pass method in Reading Papers Efficiently is the long form.

State of play, October 2026

Sources worth considering, chosen by the author and not ranked: provider changelogs and deprecation notices for any API you depend on; the proceedings of the main machine learning, language and retrieval conferences (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL, SIGIR) and the arXiv listings behind them; practitioner blogs that show data and failures, such as Hamel Husain's and Eugene Yan's; and your own colleagues' post-incident reviews. Aggregators and newsletters help discovery but repeat unverified claims, including model names and scores, so go to the primary source before you rely on a figure. This list will age; the habit of preferring primary sources will not.

Common misconception

"To stay employable I need to try every new framework and model the week it appears." Most releases do not change the work of delivering a reliable system, and trying all of them leaves no time to get good at anything. A better test is whether a release moves a number on your own evaluation set or removes a real constraint (cost, latency, a data-residency requirement). If it does, adopt it deliberately, with a regression run. If not, note it and move on.

Next steps

Where you go depends on where you are. Three common situations, each with a concrete first move.

You want a first role

Turn the studios into portfolio material: pick two of the take-home studios (for example the analyst assistant and productising research), complete them under the clock, and publish the repositories and memos following the previous chapter. Add one customer-style write-up. Then apply, and treat each rejection as feedback: ask for it.

You want depth in one area

Choose the part of the path that held your attention: retrieval and document AI, evaluation and safety, agents, training and serving, or search and self-improvement. Reproduce one paper in it, write up what held, and contribute a fix or a test to an open-source project in the area. Depth is shown by a result others can check.

You are working with customers

Keep a deployment log of decisions and surprises, run a retrospective at the end of each engagement, and hand over every system with a runbook and an evaluation harness. Reread proof of concept to production and security reviews before your next kick-off, and write your own failure notice template before you need it.

In all three, the same loop applies: build something small, measure it honestly, write down what you found, and show it to someone who will disagree. The course labs and studios are a place to practise that loop safely. After that, a real project with a real reader does the teaching.

Systems connection

The track-1 courses each end with their own "where next" chapter, which point at the next layer down: reliability, security and design. If your work leans towards operating AI systems, those are the natural complements to this one. If it leans towards the science, go back to the deep learning and training courses with a specific question in mind.

Try it yourself

Write your own plan in thirty minutes. List the two sources you will follow weekly; the one area you will deepen this year; the one thing you will let lapse; and the project you will finish and publish within three months, with a first deliverable and a date. Put the date in a calendar and send the plan to someone who will ask you about it.

Key takeaways

  • Sort what you learn by half-life: spend most effort on methods and habits, and treat model names, scores and tool versions as volatile.
  • Keep your own gold set and regression suite; they tell you what a new release changes for you.
  • Use a small, regular reading budget, a learning log and a periodic cull of sources, with primary sources first.
  • Choose a next step that fits: portfolio and applications, depth in one area, or reflective practice on real engagements.
  • Whatever you do, repeat the loop: build small, measure honestly, write plainly, share with a sceptic.

Further reading

  • Hamel Husain, "A Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products" (2025). A compact statement of the measurement-first habits that outlast any particular tool.
  • Eugene Yan's writing on applied machine learning and LLM systems. Practitioner essays that show data, trade-offs and failures; a good model of the "show your evidence" style.
  • Simon Willison's weblog. Frequent, hands-on notes on new models and tools, often with runnable examples; a useful discovery source if you check claims against primary sources.
  • arXiv. The primary source for most machine learning papers; use the listings for the categories you follow rather than aggregated summaries.
  • Chip Huyen, AI Engineering (O'Reilly, 2025). A book-length reference that organises the field around evaluation, data and deployment decisions.

Check your understanding

A new model tops a popular leaderboard. What is the most useful first response for an applied engineer?

  1. Switch all projects to it immediately.
  2. Ignore it, since leaderboards are meaningless.
  3. Run the existing gold set and regression suite against it, and compare quality, cost and latency on your own tasks.
  4. Wait for a newsletter to say whether it is good.
Your own evaluation set is the sensor for your tasks. Switching blindly risks regressions, ignoring forfeits real gains, and secondary summaries repeat the leaderboard claim that you are trying to check.

Which item is most likely to remain useful in two years?

  1. A way of structuring error analysis so that failures are counted by first cause.
  2. The ranking of models on a given date.
  3. The exact parameters of a particular framework's current API.
  4. The price per million tokens of a specific model.
Methods outlast artefacts. Rankings, API details and prices are volatile facts, which is why this course dates them. The error-analysis method applies across model generations.

You read a paper whose method, if it works, would not change anything you build or measure. According to the three questions, what do you do?

  1. Read it in full anyway to be safe.
  2. Reproduce it this week.
  3. Add it to the portfolio.
  4. Note it in the log in one line and stop reading.
The second question is a filter. If the answer is no, deep reading is a poor use of a limited budget, though a line in the learning log keeps the option open if your work changes.

What is the main value of sharing finished work with someone who will disagree?

  1. It guarantees approval.
  2. It exposes weak claims and supplies the next question to investigate.
  3. It replaces the need for evaluation.
  4. It builds a larger audience, which is the main goal.
Disagreement tests your claims and points to the next experiment. It does not guarantee approval and cannot stand in for measurement; audience size is a side effect.
