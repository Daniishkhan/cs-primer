Healthcare & Life Sciences

In healthcare an error can hurt a person who never saw the software, and the data is protected by law from the first keystroke. This chapter explains how clinical documentation tools, decision support and device regulation differ, what protected health data demands of an AI system, and how to measure safety as carefully as accuracy.

Why this matters

Healthcare is the domain where the people harmed by a mistake are not the people using the software, and where the people who could catch the mistake are the busiest in the building. It is also where AI has some of its clearest benefits: clinicians spend hours a day writing notes, and patients wait for answers that a well-built system could prepare. A project here fails in a few characteristic ways: the data rules are misread and the pilot is stopped by the privacy office, an accurate-looking summary quietly drops an allergy, or a feature turns out to be a regulated medical device after the demo. After this chapter you will be able to separate documentation, decision support and device functions, explain what protected health information demands of the architecture, design an evaluation that measures omissions and harm and not only accuracy, and describe the regulatory path a clinical AI function takes. It builds on capturing expert knowledge (clinicians define what "harm" means), evaluation plans and the finance chapter's treatment of model risk and evidence packs.

Not medical or legal advice

This chapter teaches how an engineer should think about healthcare rules. It is not legal advice, rules differ by country and state, and every deployment needs the customer's privacy officer, clinical safety lead and counsel. Dates and statuses below are as of October 2026 and some are moving.

What is different about healthcare

Four properties shape the engineering.

Harm is physical and specific. A wrong number in a report costs money; a wrong dose, a missed allergy or a delayed diagnosis can injure or kill. Clinical teams therefore think in terms of patient safety: preventing harm from care itself, including from tools. Hospitals run incident reporting, review committees and clinical safety officers, and a new AI tool enters that machinery. Your reliability vocabulary of SLOs and error budgets translates only partly: for most software a 1% error rate is a budget, but for a medication list it depends entirely on which 1%.

The patient is a data subject who never agreed to your project. Health data is among the most sensitive personal data. In the United States it is protected by HIPAA; in the European Union health data is a special category under GDPR Article 9; most countries have their own rules. A leak is not only a fine, it is a breach of the trust that makes patients tell clinicians the truth.

The users are experts with legal liability. A clinician who signs a note owns it. They will not forgive a tool that adds work, and a tool that makes them feel responsible for errors they cannot see will be switched off. The best clinical AI tools fit the existing responsibility: the clinician reviews and signs, as they would for a human scribe's draft.

Evidence culture is strong. Medicine has a tradition of trials, validation on local populations and peer review, and a hospital's clinical leaders will ask for evidence in those terms. "It worked in the demo" is received the way a pharmaceutical company would receive "it worked on three people". Studios like clinical note summaries practise exactly this standard.

As in finance, the line is drawn by consequence, but here the line also has a legal edge: a software function whose intended use is to diagnose, treat, mitigate or prevent disease is a medical device whatever technology it uses. Intended use is established by what the maker claims, how the product is labelled and marketed, and what it is designed to do, not by what you privately intend. The same underlying model can be an administrative tool in one configuration and a device in another, which is why you read the feature list with a regulatory eye before you build.

Protected health data

HIPAA in the shape engineers need

The US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996) and its rules protect protected health information (PHI): individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity (a health plan, a clearinghouse or a provider that transmits claims electronically) or by its business associate, a vendor that handles PHI on its behalf. That last term is you, or your company, when you build for a hospital. Four pieces matter.

  • The Privacy Rule limits use and disclosure of PHI and sets the minimum necessary standard: use only the PHI needed for the purpose. Sending a whole chart to a model that needs three fields is a design flaw, not just a bad habit.
  • The Security Rule requires administrative, physical and technical safeguards for electronic PHI: risk analysis, access control, audit controls, integrity protection, transmission security.
  • The Breach Notification Rule requires notice to affected individuals without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery of a breach of unsecured PHI, with further duties to regulators and sometimes media.
  • A business associate agreement (BAA) is the contract by which a vendor promises to safeguard PHI and to be bound by HIPAA's duties. A covered entity may not give PHI to a vendor, including a model provider, without one.

De-identification is harder than removing names

Data that has been properly de-identified is no longer PHI and can be used for development and evaluation under looser rules. HIPAA offers two routes: Safe Harbor, which removes 18 listed kinds of identifier (names, geographic units smaller than a state with limited exceptions for the first three digits of a postcode, all elements of dates except the year, phone numbers, record numbers, ages over 89 and so on) and requires that the holder has no actual knowledge the rest could identify someone; and Expert Determination, in which a qualified statistician certifies that the risk of re-identification is very small. Notes are the hard case, because identifiers hide in free text. The script below applies typical rule-based scrubbing to a made-up note.

# Rule-based scrubbing handles the structured identifiers and misses the free-text ones.
import re
NOTE = ("Seen 03/14/2025. Mrs Aoife O'Neill, 91 years old, MRN 00482913, phone 555-0142. "
        "Lives in Tralee. Her daughter, who works as the town's only vet, collected her. "
        "Discharged on 03/16/2025 with apixaban.")

RULES = [(r"\b\d{2}/\d{2}/(\d{4})\b", r"[DATE-\1]"),     # Safe Harbor keeps the year only
         (r"\bMRN \d+\b", "[MRN]"),
         (r"\b\d{3}-\d{4}\b", "[PHONE]"),
         (r"\b(\d{2,3}) years old\b", lambda m: "[AGE 90+]" if int(m.group(1)) > 89 else m.group(0))]

out = NOTE
for pattern, repl in RULES:
    out = re.sub(pattern, repl, out)
print(out)
leaks = ["O'Neill", "Tralee", "only vet"]
print("still present:", [l for l in leaks if l in out])
Seen [DATE-2025]. Mrs Aoife O'Neill, [AGE 90+], [MRN], phone [PHONE]. Lives in Tralee. Her daughter, who works as the town's only vet, collected her. Discharged on [DATE-2025] with apixaban.
still present: ["O'Neill", 'Tralee', 'only vet']

The rules did their job on the structured identifiers: dates reduced to years, the age of 91 generalised to "90+", the record number and phone number gone. They missed the surname, the town and a description ("the town's only vet") that identifies the family to anyone who lives nearby. Real pipelines add named-entity models and dictionaries (the PII redaction lab, Lab 3, builds one), and they still measure leakage on a hand-labelled sample and often prefer Expert Determination or a controlled-access environment for the highest-risk data. Treat de-identified data as lower risk, not as no risk, and see data protection and privacy and privacy and data handling for AI for the general techniques.

Common misconception

"Our model provider signed a BAA, so the system is HIPAA compliant." A BAA is one required contract, not a certificate. Compliance is a property of how the whole system is used: whether the minimum necessary data is sent, whether access is controlled and logged, whether the risk analysis exists, whether staff use the approved route, whether a retention setting is actually on. A signed BAA with a provider also usually covers specific products and configurations, so check that the endpoint you call is the one the agreement names. The opposite error is also common: assuming HIPAA applies to every health app. It covers covered entities and their business associates; many consumer products fall under other laws (in the US, the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule and state privacy laws).

Patient safety: measure what the summary leaves out

Clinical documentation is the most common first project. An ambient scribe listens to the consultation (with consent), transcribes it and drafts the note for the clinician to edit. Others summarise a long chart before a visit or draft a discharge letter. The value is real: a randomised trial at UCLA reported in NEJM AI in November 2025 compared two ambient-scribe products with usual care among 238 outpatient physicians and found modest reductions in note-writing time that differed between products, alongside some improvement in workload and burnout measures. Read the paper for the figures; the lesson for a delivery engineer is that the benefit is modest and measurable, not a transformation to be promised.

The danger lies in two failure types that the reviewing clinician is badly placed to catch. A hallucination is content that was not in the source: a medication the patient never took. An omission is source content that was dropped: an allergy, a changed dose, a worrying remark. A reader checking a draft for wrong statements will notice the first more often than the second, because you cannot see what is missing. A study by Asgari and colleagues (npj Digital Medicine, 2025) built a clinician-annotated framework for exactly this and, on 12,999 annotated sentences across 18 experimental configurations of note generation, reported a hallucination rate of 1.47% and an omission rate of 3.45%, classifying each by potential harm. Two points follow: omissions were more frequent than hallucinations, and a rate that sounds small multiplies across thousands of notes a day.

So the evaluation scores both, and weights them by severity as the clinicians define it, using the elicitation methods of capturing expert knowledge. The script below scores a stand-in model's summary of a short note against a fact list with severities.

# Score a draft summary against the source note: omissions (weighted by severity) and unsupported claims.
import re
from llm import RuleModel, text_of

NOTE = ("68-year-old man, chest pain for two days. Allergic to penicillin (rash). "
        "Takes warfarin 5 mg daily and metformin 500 mg twice daily. "
        "BP 142/88, heart rate 96. ECG shows no acute changes. Plan: troponin at 3 hours, "
        "cardiology review, hold warfarin until INR checked.")

# Facts a clinician says must survive summarisation, with how bad it is to lose them.
FACTS = [("chest pain",         ["chest pain"],               "major"),
         ("penicillin allergy", ["penicillin"],               "critical"),
         ("warfarin",           ["warfarin"],                 "critical"),
         ("metformin",          ["metformin"],                "minor"),
         ("ECG result",         ["ecg"],                      "major"),
         ("troponin plan",      ["troponin"],                 "major"),
         ("hold warfarin",      ["hold warfarin"],            "critical")]

model = RuleModel([(r"summar", "68-year-old man with two days of chest pain, on warfarin and metformin. "
                               "ECG normal, no acute changes. Troponin at 3 hours and cardiology review. "
                               "Started on aspirin 300 mg.")])

STOP = {"with","of","on","and","at","the","a","an","in","to","for","no"}
def words(s): return set(re.findall(r"[a-z0-9/]+", s.lower())) - STOP

def score(summary):
    low = summary.lower()
    omitted = [(n, sev) for n, keys, sev in FACTS if not all(k in low for k in keys)]
    note_words = words(NOTE)
    unsupported = [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=\.)\s+", summary)
                   if len(words(s) - note_words) >= 2 or
                   any(t.isdigit() and t not in note_words for t in words(s))]
    return omitted, unsupported

summary = text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": "Summarise this note."}]))
omitted, unsupported = score(summary)
print("summary:", summary)
print(f"omissions: {len(omitted)} of {len(FACTS)} facts")
for name, sev in omitted: print(f"  [{sev}] {name}")
print("unsupported sentences:", unsupported)
summary: 68-year-old man with two days of chest pain, on warfarin and metformin. ECG normal, no acute changes. Troponin at 3 hours and cardiology review. Started on aspirin 300 mg.
omissions: 2 of 7 facts
  [critical] penicillin allergy
  [critical] hold warfarin
unsupported sentences: ['Started on aspirin 300 mg.']

This summary would pass a naive review: it reads smoothly and most of it is right. It omits the penicillin allergy and the instruction to hold warfarin, both critical, and it adds a drug start (aspirin) that nobody ordered. It also says the ECG was "normal" where the note says "no acute changes", a subtle overstatement this crude word-overlap checker cannot see. A production evaluation uses a clinician-labelled gold set, an LLM or human judge calibrated against clinicians (see LLM-as-judge and human labels), and reports critical omissions and critical inventions as separate headline numbers with intervals, not an average quality score. The acceptance criterion is stated per severity: perhaps no tolerance on a sampled set for critical errors that reach the signed note, which in practice becomes a design requirement for what the reviewer sees (highlighting medications, allergies and plan items drawn from the source, with links back to the transcript).

Alerts, base rates and automation bias

Decision support has a different problem. A risk model that raises an alert has a sensitivity (the share of true cases it flags) and a specificity (the share of non-cases it leaves alone), but the burden on staff depends on how rare the condition is. The best-known example is the Epic Sepsis Model, a proprietary early-warning score used in many US hospitals. Wong and colleagues (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021) evaluated it externally on 38,455 hospitalisations at one academic centre, 2,552 (about 7%) of which involved sepsis. They reported an area under the curve of 0.63, and at the vendor's suggested alert threshold a sensitivity of 33%, specificity of 83% and positive predictive value of 12%, with alerts on 18% of all hospitalisations. The vendor disputed aspects of the analysis and argued thresholds should be tuned locally. Whatever the final verdict on that model, the arithmetic is general, and the script reproduces the paper's reported figures from its inputs.

# Why a model with decent-looking sensitivity and specificity floods staff with false alerts.
# Figures from Wong et al., JAMA Intern Med 2021 (Epic Sepsis Model, external validation).
N, sepsis = 38_455, 2_552
sens, spec = 0.33, 0.83

tp = sens * sepsis
fn = sepsis - tp
fp = (1 - spec) * (N - sepsis)
alerts = tp + fp
print(f"prevalence         {sepsis / N:.1%}")
print(f"true alerts        {tp:,.0f}")
print(f"false alerts       {fp:,.0f}")
print(f"missed sepsis      {fn:,.0f}  ({fn / sepsis:.0%})")
print(f"PPV                {tp / alerts:.1%}   (paper reports 12%)")
print(f"false alerts per true alert: {fp / tp:.1f}")
prevalence         6.6%
true alerts        842
false alerts       6,104
missed sepsis      1,710  (67%)
PPV                12.1%   (paper reports 12%)
false alerts per true alert: 7.2

For every true alert there are about seven false ones, and two-thirds of the cases are missed anyway. Staff learn quickly that most alerts are noise, and the system degrades into one that is ignored, which is called alert fatigue. There is a mirror problem, automation bias: the tendency to follow a machine's suggestion even when one's own judgement would have been better, which makes a mostly right assistant most dangerous exactly when it is wrong. Both are human-factors facts, not model defects, and both are why the evaluation must include the workflow and the people (a silent trial that records what the model would have said, followed by a supervised pilot) and not only the model's offline scores. See imbalance, thresholds and calibration for the statistics of rare events.

Analogy: a smoke alarm next to the toaster

An alarm that sounds every morning when the toast browns teaches the household to take out the battery. The alarm is "sensitive", and the fire it was installed for gets no warning. A clinical alert model is the same: sensitivity bought with a flood of false alarms is not safety, because the people who must respond adapt to the noise.

Where it breaks down: a smoke alarm's false alarm is obvious at once, and a household can move it. In a hospital the missed case is invisible when it happens, and many different people see the alerts, so nobody notices the pattern unless someone is measuring it.

When software becomes a medical device

In the United States the Food and Drug Administration regulates software with a medical purpose as a device. A software as a medical device (SaMD) function is software that performs such a purpose without being part of a hardware device. Class and risk decide the route: most AI functions reach the market through the 510(k) pathway (showing substantial equivalence to an existing device), with a smaller number through De Novo (for novel, lower-risk devices) and PMA (the strictest). The FDA publishes a list of AI-enabled devices it has authorised; reported counts passed 1,500 in early 2026, with radiology accounting for roughly three-quarters of them, so imaging dominates what has been cleared, and general-purpose language-model assistants for clinicians remain largely outside the list.

For decision support the 21st Century Cures Act (2016) carved out some software from the device definition. Software is outside it when, broadly, four conditions hold: it does not analyse medical images, signals or patterns from a device; it displays or analyses medical information; it supports a healthcare professional by providing recommendations; and the professional can independently review the basis for the recommendation, so that they are not relying primarily on it. The FDA's guidance on clinical decision support (CDS) software explains how it applies these criteria. The agency issued a revised version in January 2026 that replaced the 2022 text; law-firm commentary reports it showing more flexibility for tools that give a single clinically appropriate recommendation, and no change in treating image-analysis software as a device. For an engineer, the practical test is design for the fourth criterion: show the sources and reasoning, so that a clinician can check them, and do not present a number or a command in a way that discourages checking.

If a function is a device, the maker must hold a quality management system and design controls, manage risk to the standard ISO 14971, and develop software under a lifecycle such as IEC 62304. Models that learn or are updated create a particular difficulty, because each change could need a new submission. The FDA's final guidance of December 2024 on predetermined change control plans (PCCPs) lets a manufacturer agree in advance what kinds of model changes (and with what data management, retraining and performance-evaluation protocols) may be made without a fresh submission. In the European Union, medical device software is regulated under the Medical Device Regulation (EU 2017/745), and an AI system that is itself a product, or a safety component of one, requiring third-party conformity assessment is classed as high-risk under the AI Act's Annex I, with application dates moved by the 2026 Digital Omnibus (see the note below). The UK has its own regulator, the MHRA, with a software and AI as a medical device programme.

You rarely need to build a device to be useful. A common strategy is to deliver documentation and operational tools first, measure them well, and treat device-class features as a separate programme with regulatory expertise on the team.

Life sciences: validated systems and regulatory writing

Pharmaceutical, biotechnology and medical-device companies add another layer. Work that supports a regulated product runs under good practice quality rules (GxP: good manufacturing, laboratory, clinical and pharmacovigilance practice), and computerised systems used in it must be validated to show they do what they claim. In the United States, 21 CFR Part 11 sets the conditions for trustworthy electronic records and electronic signatures: access control, audit trails, and records that cannot be silently altered. A literature-search assistant for safety scientists, a tool that drafts sections of clinical study reports, a pharmacovigilance system that extracts adverse events from free-text reports: each is a candidate, each has a human who signs, and each needs a validation story that is built into the plan from day one. The audit-trail and change-control habits of the finance chapter carry over directly.

When AI generates evidence for a regulatory decision about a drug, the FDA's January 2025 draft guidance on the use of AI to support regulatory decision-making for drugs and biological products proposes a seven-step, risk-based credibility framework. It starts by defining the question the model answers and its context of use (the precise role and scope of the model's output in the decision), assesses model risk from how much the model influences the decision and how serious a wrong decision would be, and then plans, executes and documents credibility activities, concluding whether the model is adequate for that context. Reported status in 2026 is still draft, so check the FDA's page. The habit it teaches is general: **state the context of use precisely, because evidence of adequacy is always for a particular use**.

Governance inside a health system

Hospitals are building their own oversight for AI. In September 2025 the Joint Commission, which accredits US hospitals, and the Coalition for Health AI (CHAI) issued joint voluntary guidance on responsible AI use. As summarised by trade press, it sets out seven elements: governance, patient privacy, data security, ongoing quality monitoring, voluntary reporting of AI safety events, risk and bias assessment, and education and training, and it is expected to inform later accreditation expectations. In US federal health IT rules, the Office of the National Coordinator's HTI-1 final rule created transparency requirements for certified health IT that supplies predictive decision support, including disclosure of the data and logic behind a model, effective from 2025. At state level, laws are appearing about disclosure to patients: California's AB 3030, effective January 2025, for example, requires a notice when generative AI is used to produce patient clinical communications that a clinician has not reviewed. These are examples to check for the customer's jurisdiction, not a complete list.

For the delivery engineer these become ordinary deliverables: an entry in the hospital's AI inventory, a named clinical owner, a way for staff to report an AI-related safety event, a monitoring plan with the review of sampled cases, and a plan for what happens when the model version changes. Pair this with the incident practices in AI incident response and the governance frameworks in governance frameworks and regulation.

Systems connection

The boundary diagram is a trust boundary of the kind drawn in threat modelling, and the audit log is the detection evidence of detection and security incidents. Minimum necessary is least privilege (security design principles) applied to data. Isolating one hospital's data from another's is the problem of multi-tenancy and isolation. Alert fatigue is the monitoring lesson of alerting and on-call: symptom-based, actionable alerts, and a cost to every page. Note and discharge-summary generation from long records leans on long context versus retrieval and grounded citations so that a clinician can click through to the source sentence.

Key takeaways

  • Healthcare AI is sorted by consequence and by intended use: administration, documentation, patient-facing communication, then decision support. Intended use, not technology, decides whether software is a medical device.
  • HIPAA's practical demands on you: a BAA with every vendor that touches PHI, minimum necessary data, security safeguards with audit logs, and breach duties. A BAA is necessary, not sufficient. Outside HIPAA, other laws (GDPR Article 9, state laws, the FTC) apply.
  • De-identification is a process with failure modes. Rules remove structured identifiers and miss free-text ones; measure leakage and treat de-identified data as lower, not zero, risk.
  • Score omissions and inventions separately, weighted by clinician-defined severity, with intervals. A smooth summary that drops an allergy passes a review for wrong statements.
  • Alerts live or die by base rates and human factors. Compute positive predictive value at the real prevalence, run a silent trial, and design against alert fatigue and automation bias.
  • A decision-support feature may meet the CDS exclusion if the clinician can independently review the basis; image and signal analysis is regulated. Device functions bring quality systems, risk management and change-control plans, and need regulatory expertise early.
  • Life sciences adds validated systems, Part 11 audit trails and a precise context of use for any AI-generated evidence.

Check your understanding

A hospital pilots a summariser. Reviewers read 200 summaries and find no false statements, and the team declares it safe. What is the most important gap in this evaluation?

  1. Two hundred summaries is too few for any conclusion.
  2. The summaries were not checked for fluency.
  3. Omissions were not measured: facts dropped from the source, weighted by severity, are invisible to a reviewer looking for wrong statements.
  4. The reviewers should have been engineers, not clinicians.
Checking for wrong statements finds hallucinations but cannot see what is missing, and omitted allergies or plan items are among the most harmful errors. The sample size matters too, but it is the metric, not the count, that leaves the biggest hole. Fluency is already high, and clinicians are the right reviewers.

A vendor offers a hosted model with a signed business associate agreement. A developer wants to send whole patient charts to it "because we have a BAA". What is the best response?

  1. The BAA permits sending PHI to the vendor, but minimum necessary still applies: send only the fields the task needs, check the agreement covers this endpoint and configuration, and log what is sent.
  2. The BAA makes the whole system compliant, so send the charts.
  3. PHI can never be sent to a third party, even with a BAA.
  4. The BAA only matters if the vendor is based in the United States.
A BAA is the enabling contract, but the Privacy Rule's minimum necessary standard and the Security Rule's safeguards still bind the design. It does not make a system compliant by itself, PHI can go to a vendor under one, and the duty follows the covered entity's data, not the vendor's location.

An early-warning model has 85% sensitivity and 90% specificity. The condition affects 2% of patients. Roughly what share of alerts are true?

  1. About 85%.
  2. About 50%.
  3. About 90%.
  4. About 15%.
Per 1,000 patients: 20 have the condition and 17 are flagged correctly; of the 980 who do not, 10% (98) are flagged wrongly. True alerts are 17 of 115, about 15%. Quoting sensitivity or specificity as "accuracy" ignores prevalence, which is why positive predictive value must be computed at the real base rate.

A team plans a tool that reads a radiology image and suggests a diagnosis to the radiologist, who can see the image. Under the CDS criteria described in the chapter, how should the team plan?

  1. It is outside device regulation because a human reads the result.
  2. Assume it is a regulated device: analysing medical images falls outside the exclusion, so involve regulatory affairs early and plan for premarket review and a quality system.
  3. It is a device only if it uses deep learning.
  4. It is exempt because it only makes recommendations.
The exclusion does not cover software that acquires, processes or analyses medical images, signals or patterns, even if a clinician reviews the output. Technique is irrelevant to the classification, and "recommendation only" applies solely to software that meets all the criteria.

A de-identification pipeline removes names, phone numbers, record numbers and exact dates from discharge notes, and the team calls the output "anonymous". What should you advise?

  1. Agree: those are the main identifiers.
  2. Disagree: de-identified data is never useful.
  3. Measure leakage on a hand-labelled sample, expect residual identifiers in free text (places, rare descriptions, relatives), and treat the data as lower risk rather than risk-free, considering Expert Determination or controlled access for sensitive sets.
  4. Replace the rules with a language model and assume it catches everything.
Free text hides identifiers that rules do not capture, and Safe Harbor also requires there be no actual knowledge that the rest could identify someone. De-identified data is useful, so refusing it is wrong, and a model-based detector also needs measurement rather than trust.

A customer says its clinicians are "overwhelmed by alerts" from a new risk model and wants to lower the threshold so fewer fire. What is the key question before agreeing?

  1. Which clinicians complained loudest?
  2. Whether the model can be retrained on public data.
  3. Whether the alerts are formatted clearly.
  4. What the sensitivity and positive predictive value are at the new threshold on local data, and what missed cases would cost, since fewer alerts means more misses unless the model is better.
The threshold trades missed cases against false alarms, so the decision needs both numbers on the local population and an agreement with clinical leadership on the acceptable trade. Complaints are a signal, not an analysis, and retraining or formatting do not answer the trade-off.
