Public Sector & Government

A government cannot choose its customers, cannot walk away from the difficult cases and must be able to justify every decision to a person who is entitled to challenge it. This chapter covers citizen services, analysts and caseworkers, procurement, and the transparency and accountability that make a public-sector AI system defensible.

Why this matters

Governments hold the data, the caseloads and the legal authority that make AI assistance valuable, and also the power to do real harm when it goes wrong: a benefit wrongly stopped, a visa wrongly refused, a citizen told by an official chatbot that something illegal is allowed. They also cannot choose their customers, cannot turn away hard cases, and answer to courts, auditors, legislators and the press. A project here fails in characteristic ways: the system is quietly turned from decision support into decision making, procurement rules make a good prototype impossible to buy, a transparency duty is discovered after launch, or an official answer is wrong and nobody can show why. After this chapter you will be able to separate information, support and decisions, design for accessibility and accountability from day one, explain how public bodies buy AI and what the buying rules require of you, and say what a transparency record should contain. It builds on feasibility triage, choosing models and the finance, healthcare and legal chapters, whose themes (who decided, on what basis, can you show it) return here in public-law form.

Not legal advice

Public-sector rules differ by country, level of government and agency, and change with each administration. This chapter teaches the shape of the problem. It is not legal advice, and every deployment needs the agency's counsel, privacy and records officers. Statements about 2025 and 2026 policy are as of October 2026 and drawn partly from secondary summaries.

What is different about government

The public cannot go elsewhere. A bank can decline a customer; a tax authority or a benefits office serves everyone, including people with poor literacy, no broadband, a disability or a language the designers did not expect. A system that works for 95% of users and fails the other 5% is a failure of the service, and its victims are often those with the least ability to complain. Accessibility, plain language and a human alternative are requirements, not polish.

Decisions need legal authority and due process. A public body may only do what the law allows, and a decision that affects a person's rights, such as granting or stopping a benefit, usually requires notice, reasons and a way to challenge it. A system cannot supply authority it does not have. Due process is the group of procedural protections (notice, an opportunity to respond, a reasoned decision, review by someone independent) that make an exercise of state power legitimate.

Everything can become public. Freedom-of-information laws (in the United States the Freedom of Information Act, and state equivalents) let citizens request records, and records laws can treat prompts, outputs and logs as official records to be retained. Audits, inquiries and journalists examine government systems with a diligence few private systems face. Design as if your logs will be read aloud.

The buying rules are a design constraint. Public money is spent through rules on competition, fairness and value, which shape what can be bought, from whom and how fast. They also ensure that the winning prototype may not be the thing that is eventually purchased. We return to this below.

Trust is the asset. A private company can survive one bad week. A public service that is seen to be unfair loses legitimacy that is slow to rebuild. The cases below, which involve real harm to many thousands of people, are why public-sector AI work is cautious and why the caution is justified.

Citizen services: accurate, accessible and honest about being a machine

An assistant that answers questions about benefits, permits or taxes is the most visible public-sector use. Its answers carry the authority of the government, and people act on them. In New York City, a business-advice chatbot launched in 2023 was reported in 2024 to have told business owners they could do things the law forbids; the MyCity case follows what went wrong and what the city did. The lessons apply broadly.

  • Answer only from current, official sources, and show them. The retrieval corpus is the published guidance, with dates and owners, refreshed when the law changes, and each answer links to the page it rests on (see grounded answers and citations). Out-of-scope or low-confidence questions get a refusal and a route to a person, which grounding, abstention and confidence shows how to evaluate.
  • Say it is a machine and say what it cannot do. The EU AI Act's transparency obligation (Article 50) requires that people interacting with an AI system are told so, unless obvious. Beyond the law, a visible note that answers are general information and not a decision, with a link to the authoritative page and to a human, manages both expectation and liability.
  • Write for the reader. Public information should be in plain language. A government's own guidance is often written for lawyers, and a model that repeats it faithfully repeats the problem. Reading-grade measures are crude, but they are cheap and catch the worst gaps. The script below uses the Flesch-Kincaid grade formula on two versions of the same answer.
# Plain-language check for citizen-facing answers: Flesch-Kincaid grade level (standard library only).
import re

def syllables(word):
    w = re.sub(r"e$", "", word.lower())          # silent final e
    groups = re.findall(r"[aeiouy]+", w)
    return max(1, len(groups))

def grade(text):
    sentences = [s for s in re.split(r"[.!?]+\s", text.strip()) if s]
    words = re.findall(r"[A-Za-z']+", text)
    syl = sum(syllables(w) for w in words)
    return 0.39 * len(words) / len(sentences) + 11.8 * syl / len(words) - 15.59, len(words)

               "within the statutory period following notification of the determination.",
 "rewritten":  "If we turned down your claim, you can ask us to look at it again. Write to us within 28 days "

}
for label, text in ANSWERS.items():
    g, n = grade(text)
    print(f"{label:<11} {n:>3} words  grade level {g:4.1f}")

The drafted version reads at a postgraduate level (grade 23.7); the rewrite reads at about a second-grade level by this crude measure, which is probably too low in the other direction, because very short sentences push the formula down. Use the number as a screen, with a target range (often about grade 6 to 9 for public information) and a person testing the real text with real users. Beyond reading level, the service must work for people using screen readers and keyboards (in the United States Section 508 applies to federal technology, and WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, is the common standard across many jurisdictions), in the languages the population speaks, and on a phone with a poor connection. An assistant that works only for confident English readers has excluded the people most likely to need help. Studio 4, citizen services Q&A, puts these requirements into one brief.

Analysts and caseworkers: decision support that stays support

Analysts use models to digest volume: thousands of public consultation responses, long reports, case law and statutes. The UK government's "Humphrey" suite of AI tools announced in 2025 includes one for this purpose (Consult, for analysing and summarising consultation responses), and the UK Government Digital Service published an AI Playbook in February 2025 with ten principles for civil servants using AI. The risk is subtle: a summary that gives equal weight to a thousand identical campaign emails and one detailed submission misrepresents the public, and a summary that smooths over minority views removes the information consultation exists to gather. Evaluate summaries against a hand-coded sample of responses, check that themes are traceable to the responses behind them, and disclose AI use in the published analysis.

Caseworkers decide things about individuals: whether a claimant is eligible, whether a visa is granted, whether a tax return is audited. Here the central design question is the one in the figure above, and two large public failures show what happens when automation moves to the right of it.

Robodebt (Australia, 2015 to 2019)

A debt-recovery scheme compared welfare recipients' reported fortnightly income with annual tax-office data, averaged the annual figure across the year, and raised debts automatically, placing the burden on recipients to prove they did not owe. Courts found averaging alone could not lawfully establish a debt, the government settled a class action, and a Royal Commission reported in July 2023 that the scheme was unlawful and harmful. Press accounts put refunds and wiped debts at well over A$1.7 billion. No machine learning was needed: the failure was automating a legally unsound inference at scale, without review.

Dutch childcare benefits (2010s to 2021)

The tax authority used a risk-classification model, with other practices, to flag childcare-benefit claims as potentially fraudulent. Tens of thousands of families (estimates vary by source) were wrongly accused and ordered to repay large sums; the data protection authority fined the tax authority in 2021 for unlawful, discriminatory processing of nationality data, and the Dutch cabinet resigned in January 2021. An inquiry found rigid rule application, no proportionality or hardship routes and officials who did not provide information to Parliament. The Netherlands later introduced a fundamental-rights impact assessment for government algorithms.

Both are cases where the system's output was treated as a finding, the affected person had little chance to contest it, and the harm fell on people with the least power. They are accounts from public inquiries and court findings, and the figures above are drawn from secondary summaries; read the primary reports (listed below) before citing numbers. What to take away for design: show the officer the evidence and the rule behind a suggestion; require the officer to record their own reasoning for an adverse outcome; give the affected person notice of the basis and a real route to challenge; and never treat a statistical flag as proof.

Common misconception

"A human reviews every decision, so a person is accountable." A human in the loop only helps if they have the time, information and authority to disagree. If reviewers see hundreds of cases a day and approve 99% in seconds, the review is formal and the system decides. Measure the review: acceptance rate, time per case, how often reviewers change an outcome, and whether they differ from one another. The script below shows the signal in a synthetic log of three caseworkers.

# Is the human review real? Look at what reviewers actually do with the assistant's suggestions.
# Synthetic log: 3 caseworkers, 400 suggestions each. Seeded, so the output is reproducible.
import random, statistics

random.seed(5)
def reviewer(name, accept_rate, mean_secs):
    rows = []
    for _ in range(400):
        accepted = random.random() < accept_rate
        secs = max(2, random.gauss(mean_secs, mean_secs * 0.3))
        rows.append((accepted, secs))
    return name, rows

for name, rows in (reviewer("A", 0.82, 95), reviewer("B", 0.99, 7), reviewer("C", 0.74, 120)):
    acc = sum(a for a, _ in rows) / len(rows)
    med = statistics.median(s for _, s in rows)
    flag = "  <- check: review looks like a click-through" if acc > 0.97 and med < 15 else ""
    print(f"reviewer {name}: accepts {acc:.0%}, median {med:.0f} s per case{flag}")
reviewer A: accepts 83%, median 97 s per case
reviewer B: accepts 100%, median 7 s per case  <- check: review looks like a click-through
reviewer C: accepts 73%, median 119 s per case

Reviewer B accepts nearly everything in a median of seven seconds, which is not reading. That does not prove misconduct (the cases may be easy, the interface may pre-fill, or B may handle only simple files), but it is the kind of evidence an auditor would ask for, and a well-run service looks at it before the auditor does. The data are synthetic and seeded for illustration. The remedy is design: show the evidence not just the conclusion, randomly route some cases to second review, rotate reviewers, and set realistic caseloads. In the language of the next section, it makes the human oversight measurable.

Transparency and accountability

Public bodies are increasingly required to say what automated tools they use. The UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard (ATRS) asks departments to publish a structured record of each algorithmic tool: its purpose, how it is used in a decision, the data and model, human oversight, risks and testing. Government sources describe it as mandatory for central government departments and for arm's-length bodies that deal with the public, under a scope policy published in December 2024, with a public repository of records. The EU's AI Act adds, for high-risk AI deployed by public bodies (and by private bodies providing public services), a fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27): a documented analysis, before first use, of the processes involved, the people likely to be affected, the specific risks of harm, the human oversight in place and what happens if risks materialise, with notice to the market surveillance authority. In the United States, OMB memorandum M-25-21 (April 2025) sets government-wide policy on federal agencies' use of AI, requires agencies to appoint chief AI officers, and applies minimum risk-management practices to high-impact AI, defined by whether the output serves as a principal basis for decisions with legal, material, binding or significant effect on rights or safety. As summarised by law firms, the practices include pre-deployment testing, an AI impact assessment, ongoing monitoring, training and human oversight, a route for remedy or appeal, and consultation with the public and end users. Compliance dates reported in secondary sources conflict, so read the memo and the agency's plan.

For an engineer, these frameworks are checklists of things to have ready. The script below treats a transparency record as data, and refuses to call it publishable until every required section has content. The sections here are an illustration modelled on the kinds of fields transparency standards ask for, not the schema of any particular one.

Two sections are empty, so the record is not publishable, and they are precisely the two that teams find hardest: the risks and testing (which needs the evaluation to exist and be honest) and the appeal route (which needs a process the agency has not yet designed). Writing the record early is therefore a useful forcing function; a team that cannot fill it in has found a gap in the project. Procurement: how government buys, and what it asks of you

Public bodies buy under rules meant to ensure fair competition and value for public money. Whatever the jurisdiction, a typical path is: define a requirement, test the market, issue a solicitation, evaluate bids against published criteria, award a contract, and then secure the permissions needed to operate the system in the agency's environment. The consequences for delivery work are practical.

  • Evaluation criteria are published and scored. A bid is judged on the stated criteria, so evidence on the buyer's cases and a clear cost model (see cost models and ROI) often decide outcomes. Be careful to follow the contact rules during a live solicitation.
  • Contract terms matter as much as the model. In April 2025 the US Office of Management and Budget issued M-25-22 on acquiring AI, applying to solicitations issued from October 2025 as summarised by law firms. It directs agencies to consider terms on data and model portability, knowledge transfer and transparency of licensing and pricing to avoid vendor lock-in, to retain rights to what is produced under contract, and to prohibit use of non-public agency data to train publicly or commercially available models without consent; for high-impact AI it calls for pre-award testing and ongoing monitoring against contractual performance metrics. Read the memo itself, but the habit is general: negotiate who owns the data, prompts, fine-tuned weights and evaluation sets, and what happens at exit.
  • Authority to operate comes after the contract. In the US federal government, cloud services need FedRAMP authorisation and the system needs an authority to operate under the NIST risk management framework; defence uses graded impact levels for data sensitivity. Equivalents exist elsewhere. Systems that handle classified or sensitive data often must run in restricted environments, the subject of the restricted environments chapter, and your list of usable models is whatever is approved, which is why choosing models starts with hard constraints. The security package draws on security reviews for AI systems.

State of play, October 2026

Pricing: in August 2025 the US General Services Administration announced OneGov agreements with AI vendors offering enterprise products to federal agencies for a nominal dollar per agency or user for a year; later terms reported by trade press differ and expire, so check current schedules and what happens after the introductory period. US policy: OMB M-25-21 and M-25-22 (April 2025) are the main federal AI use and acquisition memos; compliance deadlines are reported inconsistently, and presidential priorities have shifted policy before, so confirm with the agency's chief AI officer. UK: the ATRS is mandatory for central government by a scope policy published December 2024; sources disagree whether the mandate started in 2024 or 2025; the AI Playbook dates from February 2025. EU: prohibited practices (including social scoring by public authorities) apply since 2 February 2025; the 2026 Digital Omnibus on AI, reported as published in July 2026, moves Annex III high-risk obligations, including access to essential public services and benefits, to 2 December 2027, so confirm in the Official Journal. All of this is volatile; verify before advising.

Rolling out in a public body

Teams that succeed in government usually sequence the work. They begin with staff-facing tools, where the user is an employee who can be trained, the content is internal and mistakes are caught before they reach a citizen. They move to citizen-facing information only after a measured pilot with real users, including people who use assistive technology and people who do not speak the main language well. They keep decisions about individuals for last, if at all, and then with the human, notice and appeal chain above built and tested first. They publish what they can, since in this domain openness is part of the product, and they plan for failure: an agreed way to take a service down, correct an answer already given and tell the people who relied on it. The incident practices of AI incident response and the communication skills of writing for customers apply directly, and the cautionary experience of MyCity is a reminder that an honest correction earns more trust than a defence.

Analogy: the counter clerk with the rulebook

At a good public counter, the clerk answers from the rulebook, can show you the page, says "I'll ask my supervisor" when unsure, and writes down what was decided and why. If you disagree, there is a form for that. An AI service for the public should behave the same way: answers from the official text, shows the source, hands over to a person, and leaves a record.

Where it breaks down: a clerk knows when a question is outside their remit and feels the awkwardness of guessing. A language model produces a confident answer to anything unless it has been built, and tested, to refuse. The refusal behaviour is engineered, not inherited from the metaphor.

Systems connection

The chain of accountability is an end-to-end trace, in the sense of distributed tracing, from a decision back to the rule, version and evidence that produced it; keeping it is the discipline of logs and structured events. Records laws turn your logs into retained, producible artefacts, which raises the stakes of data protection and privacy. Authority to operate is a formal security review of the kind in secure design reviews. Protecting the document store from users who should not see a record is a problem of authorisation, and the availability of a public service during an election or a disaster is a question for overload and load shedding.

Key takeaways

  • Government cannot choose its users, must act within legal authority and due process, and faces public scrutiny. Accessibility, plain language and a human alternative are requirements.
  • Place each system on the ladder from inform, support, propose to act. Watch for drift to the right; a review that approves everything in seconds is not oversight.
  • Citizen-facing assistants answer from current official sources, cite them, say they are machines, refuse out-of-scope questions and hand over to people.
  • Robodebt and the Dutch childcare benefits scandal show the cost of treating automated output as a finding without evidence, notice and appeal. A statistical flag is not proof.
  • Transparency duties (ATRS in the UK, fundamental rights impact assessments in the EU, M-25-21 minimum practices in the US) are checklists: purpose, data, oversight, risks and testing, appeal route, owner. Draft the record early.
  • Procurement shapes delivery: prepare evidence before a solicitation opens, negotiate data, portability and exit terms, and plan for security authorisation and a limited menu of approved models.
  • Sequence rollout from staff tools to citizen information to individual decisions, publish what you can and plan how to correct a wrong answer.

Check your understanding

A benefits agency pilots a tool that drafts decision letters for officers to approve. After three months, officers approve 99.5% of drafts in a median of nine seconds. What is the best reading and response?

  1. The "human in the loop" may be a formality: check whether officers see the evidence, whether the cases are easy, and how often they change outcomes, and redesign the review (show the basis, sample cases for second review, adjust caseloads).
  2. The tool is excellent, so reduce review to save time.
  3. The officers are negligent and should be disciplined.
  4. The result proves the model is unbiased.
A very high acceptance rate with very short review times is a warning sign, not proof of quality or of misconduct; the cause may be easy cases or a poor interface. Remove review and you have moved to automatic action. Blame ignores design, and agreement does not show absence of bias.

A city wants an assistant to answer questions about licensing rules. Which design best fits the lessons of the MyCity case?

  1. Let the model answer from its general knowledge to be as helpful as possible.
  2. Answer from general knowledge and add a disclaimer at the bottom.
  3. Answer only from the current official guidance, cite the page and date, refuse out-of-scope questions, state that it is an automated tool and offer a route to a person.
  4. Disable the assistant whenever the law changes and hope staff notice.
Official answers carry the authority of government, so they must rest on current official text and be traceable, with graceful refusal. General knowledge is the source of illegal or outdated advice, and a footer disclaimer does not stop people acting on the answer. Manual disabling without a refresh process is fragile.

What is the central lesson shared by Robodebt and the Dutch childcare benefits scandal?

  1. Machine learning models cannot be used by governments.
  2. Fraud detection is illegal.
  3. Large language models are too inaccurate for public services.
  4. Automated output was treated as a finding about individuals without adequate evidence, human reasoning, notice and a real route to challenge, and the harm fell on people least able to contest it.
Robodebt used simple averaging rather than machine learning, which shows the failure is about process, not technique. Governments may detect fraud and use models; the failures were in how flags became accusations. Neither case involved large language models.

You have won a public tender with a strong prototype, and the contract is being drafted. Which term most directly protects the agency from vendor lock-in?

  1. A clause requiring the vendor to use the newest model version at all times.
  2. Provisions on data and model portability, ownership of data, prompts and evaluation sets, rights to what is produced under the contract and an exit plan.
  3. A longer contract term at a fixed price.
  4. A confidentiality clause covering the vendor's pricing.
Lock-in is about how hard it is to leave, which portability, ownership and exit terms address, consistent with the direction of the US acquisition memo. Forcing the newest version undermines validated behaviour, a longer term deepens dependence and secret pricing reduces transparency.

A citizen-facing answer scores grade 22 on a reading-level formula. The team proposes lowering it to grade 4 by cutting every sentence to six words. What is the best advice?

  1. Use the formula as a screen, aim for a sensible range, and test the real text with real users, since very short sentences can lower the score without improving understanding.
  2. Accept grade 4 as the target: lower is always better.
  3. Ignore reading level because content matters more.
  4. Use only the formula and skip user testing.
Readability formulas are crude: they reward short sentences and short words whether or not the message is clear. They catch gross problems cheaply, but comprehension is established by testing with people, including those using assistive technology.

An EU city plans to use AI to help decide eligibility for housing benefits. Which obligation is most directly relevant to the public body as a deployer?

  1. None, because benefits are national matters.
  2. Only the duty to publish the source code.
  3. A fundamental rights impact assessment before first use of a high-risk system, covering the affected people, risks, human oversight and remedies, in addition to data protection duties.
  4. Only the duty to tell the vendor about incidents.
AI used to evaluate eligibility for essential public benefits is listed as high-risk, and public bodies deploying high-risk systems must carry out the Article 27 assessment before first use. Dates for application have been moved by the 2026 omnibus, so check them. The AI Act does not require publishing source code.
