Finance

Finance is the domain where an AI system's errors have a price in money, law and licences, and where somebody must be able to explain every output afterwards. This chapter covers what lending, research, compliance and wealth management each allow and forbid, how bank model risk management works (and what changed in April 2026), and how to build an assistant that can survive a validator, an examiner and an auditor.

Why this matters

Banks, insurers, asset managers and brokers were using models, and arguing about them, long before language models arrived. That history is an advantage if you know it: finance already has vocabulary, review processes and examiners for exactly the question a customer's risk team will ask you ("how do you know it works, and what happens when it does not?"). It is a trap if you do not, because a prototype that impressed the business can be stopped by a model validation team in week one. After this chapter you will be able to sort finance use cases by how much regulation they attract, explain model risk management and where generative AI sits in it as of October 2026, design the evidence a lending, research, compliance or wealth assistant needs, and say plainly which parts of a task must never be left to a model. It builds on feasibility triage, evaluation plans customers trust and choosing models.

What makes finance different

Almost every industry cares about accuracy. Finance is different in three ways that change how you build.

First, the output often is the product. A number in a research note, a credit decision, a suitability recommendation or a suspicious-activity report is a statement that a regulated firm stands behind. If it is wrong, the cost is not a bad user experience but a loss, a fine, a lawsuit or a withdrawn licence.

Second, the rules attach to the decision, not to the technology. Lending law does not care whether a human or a gradient-boosted tree turned an applicant down; it requires a lawful, explainable reason. Securities law does not care whether a chatbot or an adviser made a misleading statement to a client. The regulator's position, repeated in notices such as FINRA's Regulatory Notice 24-09 (June 2024) on generative AI, is that existing rules already apply to new tools. You will rarely find a rule that says "AI is forbidden"; you will find dozens that say "you must be able to show X", and AI makes X harder.

Third, afterwards matters as much as now. Finance runs on records. Supervisors, internal audit and sometimes courts will ask, months later, what the system saw, what it said, who approved it and which version was running. A system that cannot answer that is not deployable, however good its answers are.

Customers in this domain tend to ask three questions in some form, and a good delivery answers them before they are asked: Who decided? (a named person or a named, validated model, never "the AI"), On what basis? (the inputs and the rule or evidence, reproducible), and Can you show me? (logs and test results that outlive the project team).

Model risk management: the framework you will be audited against

A model, in the language of bank supervisors, is a quantitative method that turns input data into estimates used for decisions: a credit scorecard, a value-at-risk calculation, a pricing curve. Model risk is the harm that follows when a model is wrong or misused. Model risk management (MRM) is the set of practices that limit it, and in the United States it was codified for banks by the Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's matching bulletin 2011-12, both issued in April 2011. Its ideas spread well beyond banks: insurers, asset managers and regulators abroad borrowed them, and a customer's validation team will use its vocabulary whether or not the law requires it.

The 2011 guidance had three pillars, and they remain the right mental model:

  1. Development, implementation and use. The developer documents the purpose, the theory, the data, the limitations and the tests. Use outside the intended purpose is itself a risk.
  2. Validation. A validator, someone with the skill but not the incentive of the developer, tests conceptual soundness, compares outcomes with reality and reviews ongoing monitoring. The goal is effective challenge: critical analysis by informed people who can identify limitations and force changes.
  3. Governance. A model inventory lists every model with its owner, purpose, status and risk; policies say who may approve what; senior management and the board receive reports.

Tiering: how much validation a model deserves

Validating every spreadsheet as hard as a capital model would stop all work, so firms tier their models: low-risk tools get registration and monitoring, high-risk ones get full independent validation. Tiering usually combines how consequential the output is (money, customers, regulatory filings), how much autonomy the model has, and how reversible an error is. The script below is an illustrative scheme of the kind a first-pass triage might use, not any regulator's formula; real firms set their own and reviewers override the score.

# A first-pass risk tier for a model inventory. Illustrative scheme, not any regulator's formula.
USE_CASES = [
    # name, decides_alone, customer_facing, money_at_stake (USD per decision), reversible
    ("Meeting-notes summariser",       False, False,       0, True),
    ("Research Q&A for advisers",     False, False,    1_000, True),
    ("Client-facing portfolio chatbot", False, True,   10_000, False),
    ("Credit pre-screening",          True,  True,    25_000, False),
    ("AML alert triage",              False, False,  100_000, False),
]

def tier(decides_alone, customer_facing, money, reversible):
    score = 2 * decides_alone + 2 * customer_facing + (money >= 1_000) + (money >= 25_000) + (not reversible)
    return ("Tier 3 (full validation)" if score >= 5 else
            "Tier 2 (targeted validation)" if score >= 2 else "Tier 1 (register and monitor)"), score

for name, *attrs in USE_CASES:
    label, score = tier(*attrs)
    print(f"{name:<34} score {score}  {label}")
Meeting-notes summariser           score 0  Tier 1 (register and monitor)
Research Q&A for advisers          score 1  Tier 1 (register and monitor)
Client-facing portfolio chatbot    score 4  Tier 2 (targeted validation)
Credit pre-screening               score 7  Tier 3 (full validation)
AML alert triage                   score 3  Tier 2 (targeted validation)

Notice what the crude score gets right and wrong. Credit pre-screening lands in the top tier because it decides alone, about a customer, irreversibly. The adviser research assistant lands in the lowest tier, which a cautious validator would argue with: if advisers act on its answers, it influences advice. That argument is the point. The score starts a conversation, and you should walk into it with your own proposal rather than wait for one. When you can say "we propose tier 2 because advisers act on this, here is the evidence plan", the validation team treats you as a colleague.

State of play, October 2026: SR 11-7 has been replaced

On 17 April 2026 the Federal Reserve (as SR 26-2), the OCC and the FDIC jointly issued revised model risk management guidance that replaces the 2011 SR 11-7 and OCC Bulletin 2011-12. The Federal Reserve's own letter states that it supersedes SR 11-7 and SR 21-8, and says the guidance is expected to be most relevant to banking organisations with over 30 billion US dollars in total assets. Law-firm summaries of the text (for example Orrick, April 2026) report further changes: a narrower definition of a model (a "complex quantitative method"), a stronger risk-based approach so that low-risk tools get lighter treatment, less prescriptive validation and effective-challenge language, and a statement that the guidance is not enforceable in itself. They also report that generative and agentic AI models are excluded from its scope, with banks told to rely on their broader risk-management and governance practices, and that the agencies plan to seek public input on AI use in banking.

Read the guidance itself before advising a bank; this is a summary of summaries, and details will be tested in practice. The durable lessons do not depend on it: independent challenge, an inventory, monitoring against outcomes and records of change. Many firms will keep applying their existing MRM process to language-model systems because it is the review machinery they have. Outside the United States, the EU's AI Act, the Prudential Regulation Authority's model risk principles in the UK and local supervisors' expectations continue to apply on their own terms.

Common misconception

"Generative AI sits outside the model risk guidance, so we do not need validation." Being outside one document's scope removes one duty, not the others. Consumer-protection law, securities law, anti-money-laundering rules, the firm's own risk framework and the customer's internal audit all still apply, and the exclusion pushes the controls into the firm's general governance rather than abolishing them. In practice you will be asked for the same things (purpose, limits, test results, monitoring, change control), under a different name.

Lending: decisions that need a reason

Consumer lending is the most regulated place to use AI. In the United States, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974) and its Regulation B forbid discrimination on protected grounds and require a creditor that turns an applicant down, or offers worse terms, to give a statement of specific reasons for the action (12 CFR 1002.9). The Fair Credit Reporting Act adds notice duties when a credit report was used. The reasons must be the real ones: those that actually drove the decision, in terms the applicant can understand.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau said in 2022 and 2023 (Circulars 2022-03 and 2023-03) that a creditor cannot excuse missing or vague reasons by saying its algorithm is too complex, and that the checklist reasons on the sample forms may not be used if they do not match the real principal reasons. The Bureau withdrew those circulars with dozens of other guidance documents in May 2025. A withdrawn circular is not a repealed rule: the Regulation B requirement stands, and the withdrawal changes how the Bureau will interpret and enforce it, not what the text says. In the European Union, the AI Act classes AI systems used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons (and for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance) as high-risk in Annex III, which brings duties on data governance, logging, human oversight, accuracy and documentation. As of October 2026, the Digital Omnibus agreed in 2026 moves the date from which stand-alone high-risk obligations apply to 2 December 2027 (see the note below).

The engineering consequence is simple to state and often missed: the reasons must be derived from the decision system, not written by a language model afterwards. A model asked "why was this applicant declined?" will produce a fluent, plausible, partly invented story. The script below shows the alternative for a transparent scorecard: the reasons are the features that cost the applicant the most points, read straight from the arithmetic.

# Adverse-action reasons must come from the scoring model's real drivers, not from a narrative.
# An illustrative linear scorecard: points per feature, relative to a reference applicant.
WEIGHTS = {  # points per unit; positive helps the applicant
    "utilisation_pct": -0.60, "months_since_delinquency": 0.35,
    "income_to_debt": 12.0, "recent_inquiries": -4.0, "account_age_years": 2.5,
}
REFERENCE = {"utilisation_pct": 30, "months_since_delinquency": 36,
             "income_to_debt": 2.5, "recent_inquiries": 1, "account_age_years": 8}
REASONS = {
    "utilisation_pct": "Proportion of revolving credit in use is too high",
    "months_since_delinquency": "Delinquency on an account is too recent",
    "income_to_debt": "Income is too low relative to debt obligations",
    "recent_inquiries": "Too many recent credit enquiries",
    "account_age_years": "Length of credit history is too short",
}
CUTOFF = -10  # score relative to reference; below this the application is declined

def explain(applicant, top=4):
    contrib = {k: WEIGHTS[k] * (applicant[k] - REFERENCE[k]) for k in WEIGHTS}
    total = sum(contrib.values())
    worst = sorted((c, k) for k, c in contrib.items() if c < 0)[:top]
    return total, [(k, round(c, 1), REASONS[k]) for c, k in worst]

applicant = {"utilisation_pct": 82, "months_since_delinquency": 5,
             "income_to_debt": 1.6, "recent_inquiries": 4, "account_age_years": 3}
total, why = explain(applicant)
print(f"score vs reference: {total:.1f}  ->", "DECLINE" if total < CUTOFF else "APPROVE")
for k, c, text in why:
    print(f"  {c:>6}  {k:<26} {text}")
score vs reference: -77.3  -> DECLINE
   -31.2  utilisation_pct            Proportion of revolving credit in use is too high
   -12.5  account_age_years          Length of credit history is too short
   -12.0  recent_inquiries           Too many recent credit enquiries
   -10.8  months_since_delinquency   Delinquency on an account is too recent

For a boosted tree or a neural network, the same idea needs attribution methods and careful validation of them (see interpreting models), and lenders often choose simpler, monotonic models for the decision itself precisely so the reasons are trustworthy. Where do language models fit in lending? In the work around the decision: extracting figures from payslips and statements, assembling a credit file, drafting the memo an underwriter reads, checking a file against policy, answering a relationship manager's questions about product rules. The bank lending workflow case shows this in practice: the assistant is built by decomposing an expert's reasoning into steps whose outputs a reviewer can check, and the decision stays with the human. The extraction steps are exactly what tables and forms and OCR prepare you for, and the evaluation uses the customer's own files, as in evaluation plans customers trust.

Fair lending adds a measurement duty. If an assistant changes who gets approved, or how long it takes, for different groups, someone must measure that. Typical checks compare approval rates and processing times across groups, and a legal team decides what counts as a problem; as an engineer you make the measurement possible by keeping the data (protected attributes are often held separately and used only for testing) and by documenting what the assistant touches.

Research and analysis: every number has an address

Sell-side and buy-side analysts, credit analysts and strategy teams spend their days reading filings, transcripts and broker notes and building tables from them. A research assistant looks like the easiest finance use case: the user is an expert, a human reads the output, and nothing goes to a customer directly. It is easy to start and hard to finish well, because the failure that matters is quiet. A table with one transposed digit or a margin computed from the wrong year looks the same as a correct one.

The control is mechanical. Every figure in a generated answer must tie out: match a value in the cited source, with the page or table it came from. The script below runs a tie-out on a stand-in model's summary of a filing extract.

# Number tie-out: every figure in a generated summary must match the source table.
import re
from llm import RuleModel, text_of

SOURCE = {  # FY2025 filing extract, USD millions unless stated
    "revenue": 4812.0, "operating income": 903.5, "net income": 688.2,
    "net interest margin %": 2.91, "cet1 ratio %": 13.4,
}

model = RuleModel([
    (r"summar", "Revenue was $4,812 million and operating income $903.5 million. "
                "Net income reached $688.2 million, a 14% rise. The CET1 ratio was 13.9%."),
])

def numbers(text):
    """Pull out every number with an optional trailing % sign."""
    return [(m.group(0), float(m.group(1).replace(",", "")), m.group(2) == "%")
            for m in re.finditer(r"(?<![A-Za-z\d.])(\d[\d,]*\.?\d*)(%?)", text)]

def tie_out(summary, source, tol=0.005):
    vals = list(source.values())
    report = []
    for raw, x, pct in numbers(summary):
        hit = any(abs(x - v) <= tol * max(abs(v), 1) for v in vals)
        report.append((raw.rstrip("."), "ties to source" if hit else "NOT IN SOURCE"))
    return report

reply = text_of(model.complete([{"role": "user", "content": "Summarise the filing."}]))
print(reply, "\n")
for raw, verdict in tie_out(reply, SOURCE):
    print(f"{raw:>8}  {verdict}")
Revenue was $4,812 million and operating income $903.5 million. Net income reached $688.2 million, a 14% rise. The CET1 ratio was 13.9%. 

   4,812  ties to source
   903.5  ties to source
   688.2  ties to source
     14%  NOT IN SOURCE
   13.9%  NOT IN SOURCE

Three numbers tie, two do not, and the two are instructive. "14%" is a derived figure: net income growth needs last year's value, which is not in the source extract, so the model either used outside knowledge or guessed. "13.9%" is a changed figure: the source says 13.4%. A real checker also catches the harder cases this crude one misses: a number that matches a different metric by coincidence, a correct number with the wrong unit or period, and a derived figure that is right but needs showing. Production versions require each claim to carry a citation to a source passage and verify the passage supports it, as in grounded answers and citations and fact-checking and citation verification; arithmetic (growth rates, ratios, sums) is best done by code that the model calls rather than by the model, as in tool calling.

The Morgan Stanley research assistant case is the model for how a large firm rolls this out: an evaluation framework built with domain experts gates each release, and the assistant answers from the firm's own library rather than from the open web. Studio 2, an analyst's research assistant, asks you to build the tie-out discipline yourself.

Two further rules apply when research leaves the building. Communications with clients and the public are regulated (in the United States FINRA Rule 2210 for broker-dealers and the SEC's Marketing Rule for advisers), so a draft that an assistant writes still needs the same review and approval before it is sent. And claims about the system itself are covered too: in March 2024 the SEC settled charges against two investment advisers, Delphia and Global Predictions, for making false or misleading statements about their use of artificial intelligence, with penalties of 225,000 and 175,000 US dollars. "AI-powered" in your own marketing, or your customer's, is a factual claim that must be true.

Compliance: triage without silent failure

Compliance teams drown in alerts. Transaction-monitoring systems flag activity that might be money laundering; communications surveillance flags messages that might breach conduct rules; sanctions screening flags names. Most alerts are false positives, and each needs an analyst to look. A language model can read the alert, the account history and the policy, and write a summary with a recommended priority, which shortens the look. This is a good use, and the design question is what happens to the alerts the model ranks low.

If low-ranked alerts are closed automatically, you have built a decision system whose recall (the share of truly suspicious cases it surfaces) is now a regulatory exposure, and every missed case is a case the firm chose not to examine. If they stay in the queue with a lower priority, the worst outcome is a slower look. The safe pattern is to measure recall on a labelled sample before changing any threshold, as in imbalance, thresholds and calibration; to ship ranking first and automation, if ever, much later; and to sample the low-ranked alerts continuously, so you keep estimating what the ranking misses (the same technique appears in the legal chapter as the elusion sample).

Confidentiality is a second constraint. Under the Bank Secrecy Act, financial institutions are forbidden from disclosing to the person involved that a suspicious activity report has been filed, and the contents of such reports are tightly restricted. Any tool that touches that material must keep it inside the firm's controlled environment, with access limited to the people entitled to it. That usually points to the deployment shapes with the most containment in choosing models, and to the permission-aware retrieval of filters, metadata and permissions. Records matter as well: books-and-records rules (SEC Rule 17a-4 for broker-dealers is the best-known) mean that regulated communications, including those generated or analysed by AI, may need to be retained in a form that can be produced on request.

Wealth management: advice with a duty attached

A wealth manager owes clients duties: to recommend what is suitable, in the United States to act in the client's best interest under Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI, compliance date June 2020) for broker-dealers or the fiduciary duty for advisers, and in the European Union the suitability assessment under MiFID II. These are duties of the firm and the adviser. A system that drafts a recommendation does not discharge them; the adviser who sends it owns it.

That shapes the product. Good wealth tools prepare the adviser: summarise a meeting from a transcript, pull the client's holdings and stated goals into a briefing, draft a follow-up letter in the firm's house style, surface relevant house research. The firm then reviews the output as it would any communication. The red lines are clear. Do not let a chatbot give personalised investment recommendations to clients without the same supervision and records an adviser would face, do not let it state performance figures or forecasts that were not taken from approved sources, and do not let it contradict the client's recorded risk profile. Meeting notes are a classic quiet risk: a summary that drops the client's remark that they cannot afford a loss is an omission with a suitability consequence, which is why omissions need their own measurement (the same logic as in clinical documentation).

Analogy: maker and checker

Banks have long used a "four-eyes" principle: the person who prepares a payment is not the person who approves it. The preparer is fast and the checker is independent, and the process works because the checker can only be fooled by errors both would make. Model validation applies the same idea to code, and a human reviewing an assistant's draft applies it to documents.

Where it breaks down: a human checker is independent of the maker's mistakes, but a language model's mistakes are fluent and confident, which makes them unusually easy to wave through, and checkers who see a 99% agreement rate start to rubber-stamp. The check needs design: citations to open, spot-checks of the approved items, and a record of how often the reviewer changes anything.

Third parties, records and the evidence pack

Most finance AI systems depend on someone else's model or cloud. Regulators treat that as a risk to be managed, not a way to hand off responsibility. In the European Union the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), which has applied since 17 January 2025, imposes detailed requirements on financial entities' use of ICT third parties, including contract terms, a register of arrangements, exit plans and incident reporting. In the United States, the banking agencies' 2023 interagency guidance on third-party relationships sets out due diligence, contracting and ongoing monitoring across the life of a relationship. The consequence for your design is the one from choosing models: know the second-choice model, keep the provider behind a thin interface, and pin versions so a silent upgrade cannot change a validated behaviour.

Pull this together into an evidence pack: the dossier you hand the customer's risk, compliance and audit teams so they can approve and later re-examine the system. It is a deliverable, designed from the first week, and it draws on work you are doing anyway:

  • Purpose and limits: what the system is for, what it must not be used for, its tier and the human role.
  • Data: sources, permissions, what is sent to which model, retention. See privacy and data handling for AI.
  • Evaluation: the gold set and how it was built, results with intervals, critical-error rates, results by segment, and the sealed test. See evaluation plans customers trust.
  • Monitoring: what is logged and sampled in production, who reviews it, the thresholds that trigger rollback. See tracing and observability for LLM systems.
  • Change control: model and prompt versions, how a change is tested before release, and the previous-version fallback.
  • Audit trail: for each output, the inputs, retrieved sources, model and prompt version, reviewer and decision, stored so it can be produced and cannot be quietly edited. The logging ideas are in detection and security incidents.

Systems connection

Finance applies, at higher stakes, ideas you already know. The audit trail is a write-ahead log you do not get to lose (logs and exactly-once semantics); a decision that cannot be replayed from its inputs is a decision you cannot defend. Model validation is a deployment gate of the kind in deployment strategies. Confidentiality of reports is an access-control problem (authorisation) and a data-protection one (data protection and privacy), and vendor dependence is a supply-chain and blast-radius question (the supply chain). The governance vocabulary (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001) is in governance frameworks and regulation.

Key takeaways

  • In finance the rules attach to the decision, not the technology. Design so a named person or a validated deterministic process decides, and the model prepares.
  • Rank use cases by the effect of a mistake on third parties: research, compliance, wealth, lending. Evidence owed rises in that order.
  • Model risk management means development with documentation, independent validation, an inventory, tiering, monitoring and change control. In the US, SR 11-7 was replaced in April 2026 by SR 26-2, which reportedly excludes generative and agentic AI; the practices still apply through general governance.
  • Adverse-action reasons must come from the decision system, never from a language model's after-the-fact story.
  • Research outputs need figures that tie out to cited sources; arithmetic belongs in code. Marketing claims about AI must be true.
  • Compliance triage must not silently drop cases: measure recall, keep low-ranked items visible and sample what the ranking misses. Respect report confidentiality.
  • Third-party dependence is a regulated risk (DORA in the EU, interagency guidance in the US). Keep an evidence pack and an audit trail from the first week.

Check your understanding

A lender wants its assistant to tell declined applicants why they were declined. The team plans to have the language model read the application and write the explanation. What is the strongest objection?

  1. Language models cannot write in plain language.
  2. The stated reasons must be the real principal reasons for the decision, which the decision system knows and a language model can only guess at, so the reasons should come from the scorecard or attribution method.
  3. Regulation B forbids the use of any technology in adverse-action notices.
  4. Explanations are optional if the decision was made by software.
Regulation B requires specific reasons that reflect what actually drove the decision. A fluent after-the-fact narrative can be plausible and wrong, which is worse than a short true reason. The rule does not ban technology and does not excuse software decisions; a language model can still help rewrite true reasons into plain wording if the content is fixed.

A bank's validation lead notes that the April 2026 interagency guidance excludes generative AI from its scope and says "so we can skip validation for the research assistant." Which response is best supported?

  1. Agree: scope exclusion removes every governance duty.
  2. Disagree: the exclusion applies only to banks under 30 billion dollars.
  3. Agree, but only for tools that never touch customers.
  4. Treat it as a pointer to the firm's broader risk framework: other laws, internal audit and the customer's own policies still expect purpose, testing, monitoring and change control for the tool.
The summaries of the guidance say generative and agentic AI are left to firms' wider governance, not exempted from governance. Securities, consumer and conduct rules and audit expectations still apply. The 30 billion threshold concerns who the guidance is mainly relevant to, not the generative AI exclusion, and "never touches customers" does not remove the need to show the tool works.

An assistant summarises an annual report and writes "net income rose 14%". The extract you gave it contains only the current year. What does the tie-out find, and what is the right fix?

  1. The figure does not appear in the source, so it is unsupported; add the prior-year figure to the retrieved context and compute the growth with code, or have the answer say it cannot be computed.
  2. The figure ties out because 14% is a plausible growth rate.
  3. The figure is fine because the model is usually right about growth.
  4. Remove all percentages from summaries.
A derived number needs its inputs. Without last year's value the model used memory or invented it. Plausibility is not evidence, and banning percentages throws away useful output; the fix is to supply the inputs and make the arithmetic deterministic.

An anti-money-laundering team wants to close alerts the model scores below a threshold, to save analyst time. What should you do before agreeing?

  1. Agree, since the model's top-ranked alerts looked good in the demo.
  2. Refuse any use of AI in compliance.
  3. Measure recall on a labelled sample, including a random sample of the alerts below the threshold, and propose ranking first, with automatic closure only if the measured miss rate is acceptable to the firm and its regulators.
  4. Raise the threshold until no alerts are closed.
Closing alerts converts a ranking into a decision, so the missed-case rate becomes the number that matters, and it can only be known by sampling the discarded alerts. A good demo shows precision at the top, not recall below the line. A blanket refusal and a threshold that closes nothing both avoid the question rather than answering it.

A wealth firm's meeting-notes assistant scores 98% on "no incorrect statements" in review. A senior adviser is still uneasy. Which additional measurement addresses the likely problem?

  1. Measure fluency with a readability score.
  2. Measure omissions: whether important facts the client stated, such as a limit on acceptable losses, survive into the summary, weighted by their consequence for suitability.
  3. Measure the model's response time.
  4. Measure how many words the summary uses.
A summary can contain only true statements and still leave out the fact that changes the advice. Omissions are invisible to a check for wrong statements, so they need their own gold facts and severity weights. Readability, speed and length do not touch the suitability risk.

Your customer is an EU bank that uses a hosted language model through an API. Which is the most important design consequence of DORA's third-party provisions?

  1. Nothing, because the vendor is responsible for compliance.
  2. The model must be trained inside the EU.
  3. The bank must not use cloud services of any kind.
  4. The bank stays accountable for the arrangement, so you design for contract terms, an inventory entry, incident reporting, a tested exit route and a ready second choice behind a thin interface.
DORA keeps the financial entity responsible for ICT third-party risk and expects documented arrangements and exit plans. It does not require training in the EU or ban cloud use, and a vendor's own compliance does not transfer the bank's accountability.
