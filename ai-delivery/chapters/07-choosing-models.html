Choosing Models

Which model should this project use: a frontier model behind an API, an open-weight model on a managed endpoint, or the same open-weight model running inside the customer's own walls? This chapter gives a method that settles it by evidence and constraints: rule out what is not allowed, measure what remains on the customer's gold set, price it at real volume, and keep a second choice ready.

Why this matters

Model choice is the decision customers expect you to have an opinion on and the one most often settled by habit or fashion: the model the team used last time, the one at the top of a leaderboard, or whatever the customer's cloud offers. Habit is cheap, but a wrong choice shows up late: a security team that says no after the prototype is built, a bill that triples at real volume, a model retired by its provider in the middle of a regulated validation. After this chapter you will be able to separate hard constraints from soft preferences, describe the three deployment shapes and what each commits you to, run a fair bake-off on the customer's own cases, estimate the GPU memory a self-hosted model needs, find the volume at which paying per token stops being cheaper, and plan for the day the model you chose changes.

Start with what is not allowed

The order in which you consider things matters more than which model wins. Chip Huyen's AI Engineering (2025) frames model selection as two steps: first filter by hard attributes, which are properties you cannot change or trade away, such as the licence and where data may go; then compare what is left on soft attributes, which you can improve or trade against each other, such as accuracy, latency and cost. The filter comes first for a practical reason: a model that fails a hard attribute is out however well it scores, so measuring it wastes a week.

For applied work the hard attributes usually include:

  • Where data may go. Some customers allow a call to a vendor's API, some only to a vendor's service in a named region, some only to a service inside their own cloud account, and some to nothing outside a building. Related terms: data residency (data is stored and processed in a given country or region) and zero data retention (the provider contractually does not keep prompts and outputs beyond processing the request). Whether provider staff may read prompts, and whether inputs may train future models, belong on the same list.
  • The licence. Commercial use, limits on how many users you may serve, rules on what the outputs may be used for, an acceptable-use policy, and whether you may modify or redistribute. This is a lawyer's question, but you must raise it, because it can remove a model after the prototype is built.
  • The customer's approved list. Large organisations often maintain a short list of vendors and models that passed procurement and security review, so "the best model" may not be an option. Restricted environments returns to this.
  • Evidence the customer needs from the vendor. Audit reports, certifications, incident notification terms, a data processing agreement. The security review chapter lists the questions.
  • Technical must-haves. The modality (images, scanned pages, audio), the language, the context length the documents need, structured output or tool calling if the design needs them.
  • The hardware you actually have. In an air-gapped site, the fastest model that fits the GPUs on the shelf.

Get these from the customer in writing during requirements, and put them at the top of the evaluation plan. The first test of the riskiest assumption is often exactly this one: "claim text may be sent to a hosted model" was the best-value test in that chapter's ranking.

Three deployment shapes

It helps to separate who owns the weights (the learned numbers that make up the model) from who runs the server. Three shapes cover most projects.

A frontier API is a hosted model whose weights the vendor keeps closed; you send text (or images) and pay per token. You get the highest capability available at any moment, no hardware, usually the best tooling for tool calling and structured output, and a vendor who improves the model without being asked. You give up control of data location (up to what the contract promises), of when the model changes, and of price.

An open-weight model is one whose trained parameters are published for download, so anyone with the hardware and a licence can run it. It is not necessarily open source. The Open Source Initiative's Open Source AI Definition 1.0 (28 October 2024) asks for more than weights: enough information about the training data, and the code used to train and run the model, to let someone study and rebuild it. Many popular open-weight models fall short of that and carry custom licences. The distinction is not pedantry. A permissive licence such as Apache 2.0 or MIT lets you use the model commercially with few conditions; a custom "community" licence may add terms. Meta's Llama 3.1 licence, for example, requires a separate licence from Meta for products with more than 700 million monthly active users and incorporates an acceptable-use policy. OpenAI's gpt-oss models (August 2025) were released under Apache 2.0 with a usage policy. Read the text for each model you shortlist, every time, and have the customer's lawyers read it too.

Open-weight models can then be run two ways. On a managed endpoint, a cloud provider hosts the model for you, often inside your own cloud account and region, billed per token or per provisioned capacity. That solves most data-location questions without you running GPUs. Self-hosted means you run the model server on hardware you control: your cloud account's GPUs, a data centre, or an air-gapped network with no outside connection at all. It gives the strongest containment and a version that never changes until you change it, and it makes you responsible for capacity, patching, monitoring and the cost of idle hardware.

Common misconception

"Self-hosting is automatically more private, more secure and cheaper." It is more contained: prompts do not leave your network. But you now own everything a vendor used to: patching the serving software and the operating system, access control to the model server, logging that does not itself leak customer data, and the supply chain of the weights you downloaded. An unpatched, internet-reachable model server is a worse security position than a well-run vendor service. And it is cheaper only above a volume that you should compute (below), with a utilisation you should measure. Pick it when the constraints demand it or the arithmetic favours it, not because it feels safer.

Evidence: a bake-off on the customer's cases

After the hard constraints, quality is measured, not assumed. A bake-off is a head-to-head run of the surviving candidates on the same gold set, scored with the same metrics, under the evaluation plan from the last chapter. Some rules keep it fair.

  • Same cases, same scoring, same time budget for prompting. Give each candidate a comparable amount of prompt work, because a prompt written for one model often under-serves another. Freeze each candidate's final prompt before the sealed run.
  • Use the development half to tune and the sealed half to decide. A bake-off in which the winner was tuned hardest on the test cases measures effort, not models.
  • Report intervals. A 2-point gap between candidates on 250 cases is inside the noise; see the paired comparison in the previous chapter.
  • Measure the whole system, not only accuracy. Record latency percentiles (a model that answers in 2 seconds on average but 20 at the 99th percentile fails a user-facing task), cost per task from real token counts, refusal behaviour, how reliably it follows an output format, and whether it supports the features you need.
  • Read public leaderboards as a shortlist at most. They measure other tasks and sometimes leak into training sets; reading leaderboards critically explains why.

The script below runs the selection logic on five candidates. The accuracy, cost and latency figures are illustrative assumptions standing in for a real run on 250 gold cases, so the output shows the method, not a ranking of real products. The same five candidates are put through two scenarios: one where a vendor API with a zero-retention contract is allowed, and one where nothing may leave the customer's site.

# A model bake-off on the customer's gold set: hard constraints first, then evidence, then cost.
# Every candidate figure below is an ILLUSTRATIVE assumption standing in for a real run.
import math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

N = 250  # gold-set cases every candidate answered
CANDIDATES = [  # name, correct, cost per 1,000 tasks (USD), p95 latency (s), where it runs
    ("frontier-api",       238, 14.20, 5.1, "vendor cloud"),
    ("mid-tier-api",       231,  3.10, 2.8, "vendor cloud"),
    ("open-70B int8",      229,  3.60, 6.4, "customer site"),
    ("open-8B int4",       212,  0.45, 1.9, "customer site"),
    ("open-8B fine-tuned", 223,  0.45, 1.9, "customer site"),
]
BAR, MAX_P95 = 0.86, 8.0   # accept if the interval's lower end >= 86% and p95 <= 8 s

def run(scenario, allowed_locations):
    print(f"\nscenario: {scenario}")
    print(f"{'candidate':20s} {'acc':>6s} {'lower':>6s} {'p95 s':>6s} {'$/1k':>6s}  result")
    eligible = []
    for name, k, cost, p95, where in CANDIDATES:
        lo, _ = wilson(k, N)
        if where not in allowed_locations:
            why = "out: hard constraint (data location)"
        elif lo < BAR:
            why = "out: quality gate"
        elif p95 > MAX_P95:
            why = "out: latency gate"
        else:
            why = "eligible"; eligible.append((cost, name))
        print(f"{name:20s} {k/N:6.1%} {lo:6.1%} {p95:6.1f} {cost:6.2f}  {why}")
    print("cheapest eligible:", min(eligible)[1] if eligible else "none: change scope or constraints")

Three things are worth reading off. The constraint, not the leaderboard, decided scenario B: two strong candidates were never measured against the customer's cases because they were excluded first. The price of the constraint is visible, about 0.50 dollars per thousand tasks here, plus the operations burden, and the customer can decide whether it is worth paying. Second, the small, cheap models fail on lower bounds, not on point estimates. The fine-tuned 8B model has an observed accuracy of 89.2%, which looks close to the others, but its interval reaches down to 84.7%, below the 86% gate. With more cases, or more fine-tuning data, it might get through, and the report can say what would change the verdict. Third, the frontier model is the most accurate and the most expensive by far; whether its extra accuracy is worth more than four times the price of the mid-tier API is a business question the cost-weighted metric from the evaluation chapter lets the customer answer. A common production answer is to route: send easy cases to the cheap model and hard ones to the strong one (see cost, latency and quality trade-offs).

Will it fit? Memory arithmetic for self-hosting

If a self-hosted model is on the list, you need to know whether it fits the hardware before anyone orders it. GPU memory (the fast memory on the accelerator card) must hold two things. The first is the weights: the parameter count times the bytes per parameter. A 70-billion-parameter model in 16-bit numbers needs about 140 GB; quantisation, storing weights in 8 or 4 bits, shrinks that to roughly half or a quarter, usually at some cost in quality that you must re-measure on the gold set (quantisation). The second is the KV cache: the keys and values of every token of every request currently in flight, which the model keeps so it does not recompute attention (the KV cache and the cost of decoding). Its size per token is 2 × layers × KV heads × head dimension × bytes per number. It grows with the number of simultaneous users and with context length, and it is why long documents and many users cost memory even when the weights are small.

# Will it fit? GPU memory for weights plus the KV cache of concurrent requests.
# Model shapes are the published Llama 3.1 8B and 70B configurations (check config.json on the model page).
import math

MODELS = {  # parameters (billions), layers, KV heads, head dimension
    "8B-class":  (8.03, 32, 8, 128),
    "70B-class": (70.6, 80, 8, 128),
}
BYTES = {"fp16": 2.0, "int8": 1.0, "int4": 0.5}   # int4 ignores the small overhead of scales
GPU_GB, USABLE = 80, 0.90                          # assumed 80 GB cards, 10% kept for activations and runtime
USERS, CONTEXT = 24, 6000                          # assumed: 24 requests in flight, 6,000 tokens each

print(f"workload: {USERS} concurrent requests x {CONTEXT} tokens, KV cache in fp16, {GPU_GB} GB GPUs at {USABLE:.0%} usable\n")
print(f"{'model':10s} {'format':>8s} {'weights GB':>11s} {'KV KiB/tok':>11s} {'KV GB':>7s} {'total GB':>9s} {'GPUs':>5s}")
for name, (params_b, layers, kv_heads, head_dim) in MODELS.items():
    kv_per_token = 2 * layers * kv_heads * head_dim * 2           # K and V, 2 bytes each (fp16)
    kv_gb = kv_per_token * USERS * CONTEXT / 1e9
    for fmt, b in BYTES.items():
        w_gb = params_b * 1e9 * b / 1e9
        total = w_gb + kv_gb
        gpus = math.ceil(total / (GPU_GB * USABLE))
        print(f"{name:10s} {fmt:>8s} {w_gb:11.1f} {kv_per_token/1024:11.0f} {kv_gb:7.1f} {total:9.1f} {gpus:5d}")

Read this carefully before ordering anything. The 8B-class model fits on a single card in every format, with room to spare. The 70B-class model needs two or three cards, and tensor parallelism across cards (parallel training and serving splits the layers' arithmetic between GPUs) brings its own engineering. The KV cache for 24 users with 6,000-token contexts is 18.9 GB for the small model and 47.2 GB for the large one: once the weights are quantised, the cache can be the larger share. Quantisation shrinks the weights; it does not shrink the cache unless you also quantise that, which is a separate setting with its own quality test. And the table says nothing about speed: tokens per second depends on memory bandwidth, batching and the serving engine (serving engines, the economics of a token), and you should measure it on the real hardware with a load test (load testing and capacity planning). The shapes in the script are the published Llama 3.1 configurations (Dubey et al., 2024); check the config.json of whichever model you shortlist.

Price at real volume

The cost structures differ in kind. A per-token API is a variable cost: nothing at zero volume, linear in use, no idle capacity to pay for. A self-hosted model is mostly a fixed cost: the GPUs, a second set for redundancy if the service matters, and a slice of an engineer's time, whatever the volume. Variable beats fixed at low volume and loses at high volume, and the question is where the lines cross. The script uses assumed round numbers (all prices, throughputs and the staffing share are illustrative, not any vendor's list) for a workload of 3,000 input tokens and 400 output tokens per request.

# Pay per token, or pay for a GPU server? All prices and rates below are ILLUSTRATIVE ASSUMPTIONS.
IN_TOK, OUT_TOK = 3000, 400            # tokens per request (assumed workload)
API = {"mid-tier API": (1.00, 5.00),   # USD per million input / output tokens (assumed round numbers)
       "frontier API": (3.00, 15.00)}
GPU_HOUR, GPUS = 2.50, 2               # assumed on-demand price of one 80 GB GPU; two for redundancy
OPS_MONTH = 1500                       # assumed share of an engineer's time for patching, monitoring, upgrades
HOURS = 730
CAPACITY = 6 * 2 * HOURS * 3600 * 0.40 # 6 req/s per GPU, 2 GPUs, 40% average utilisation (assumed)

fixed = GPU_HOUR * GPUS * HOURS + OPS_MONTH
print(f"self-hosted fixed cost: {fixed:,.0f} USD a month; capacity about {CAPACITY/1e6:.1f}M requests a month")
print(f"{'requests/month':>15s} " + " ".join(f"{n:>13s}" for n in ("mid-tier API", "frontier API", "self-hosted")) + "   cheapest")
for volume in (50_000, 200_000, 1_000_000, 5_000_000, 12_000_000):
    costs = {}
    for name, (pin, pout) in API.items():
        costs[name] = volume * (IN_TOK * pin + OUT_TOK * pout) / 1e6
    costs["self-hosted"] = fixed if volume <= CAPACITY else float("inf")
    best = min(costs, key=costs.get)
    print(f"{volume:15,d} " + " ".join(f"{costs[n]:13,.0f}" for n in ("mid-tier API", "frontier API", "self-hosted")) + f"   {best}")
for name, (pin, pout) in API.items():
    per_req = (IN_TOK * pin + OUT_TOK * pout) / 1e6
    print(f"break-even vs {name}: {fixed / per_req:,.0f} requests a month")

The crossing is at roughly a million requests a month against the mid-tier API and about a third of that against the frontier API. Below it, self-hosting is a fixed cost chasing too little work. Above it the conclusion reverses, within the capacity limit, beyond which you add servers in steps. Three honest caveats keep this from being a verdict. The self-hosted model must first pass the quality gate, and the models that fit cheap hardware are often the ones that fail it. The utilisation figure (40%) hides a lot: traffic is bursty, so an idle night and a busy morning on the same fixed fleet is typical, and queueing makes latency climb well before capacity is reached (queueing theory). And the figure leaves out some real costs on each side: for self-hosting, the evaluation to re-qualify every model upgrade, the capital or reservation commitments, and the time to rebuild if the engineer who set it up leaves; for APIs, committed-spend discounts, batch and caching discounts, and the rate limits that may stop a launch. Cost models and return on investment builds the full model, and Lab 2 asks you to price a workload across all of these.

Analogy: taxi, leased van or your own lorry

If you move goods a few times a month, you hire a taxi or a courier: no capital, you pay per trip, and the driver and vehicle improve without you noticing. If you run a delivery route daily, a leased van costs less per delivery but you pay for it standing in the yard overnight. If you move heavy or confidential cargo every hour of every day, you buy a lorry and employ a mechanic: cheapest per trip at that volume, and entirely your responsibility when it breaks down. The right answer depends on volume, cargo and who is allowed to touch it.

Where it breaks down: a taxi does not change its engine overnight, while a hosted model can be updated or retired on the provider's schedule, and a lorry does not become obsolete every few months. Models do, so the cheapest option today may not be the cheapest option when you next review it.

The model will change: plan for it

Whatever you choose, it will not stay the same. Hosted models are retired on published schedules, and providers post deprecation timetables that give notice measured in months, not years. Anthropic stated in November 2025 that it would preserve the weights of publicly released models for at least the lifetime of the company, which is a commitment about keeping the weights, not a promise to keep serving them through the API. Even when the name stays, a hosted model may be updated behind it unless you use a pinned, dated version. Open-weight models are stable in a different way: the file you downloaded never changes, and a regulated customer that validated that file can keep running it, but you will be patching the software around it for years and eventually migrating when the community has moved on.

Four practices make a change a routine release and not an emergency.

  1. Pin versions. Call a dated model identifier, not an alias that moves, and record it in every trace. For open weights record the repository revision and a hash of the files.
  2. Keep the evaluation rerunnable. The gold set, the scorer and the frozen configuration from the evaluation plan are what turn "a new model is out" into a one-day test. Without them every change is a leap of faith.
  3. Keep the seam thin. One small interface in your code for "send messages, get a response" (see abstractions and interfaces) is enough; resist a framework that hides provider differences you will need to see. Provider formats for tools and structured output differ in ways that matter (function calling across providers).
  4. Hold a second choice. Know which candidate came second in the bake-off and how long a switch would take. An outage, a price change or a retirement notice then has an answer ready. Redundancy across providers has costs of its own, so decide how much of it the service's importance justifies (resilience patterns).

Open weights also carry a supply-chain duty. Treat a downloaded model like any third-party dependency: take it from a source you trust, check hashes, and prefer the safetensors file format, which stores only numbers, over formats built on Python pickling, which can execute code when loaded. The memory safety and supply chain chapter explains the general case, and the security review will ask where every model file came from.

Systems connection

Choosing a deployment shape is the build-versus-buy decision of any system, with the same hidden costs on each side. The memory arithmetic is the resource accounting of parameters, FLOPs and memory. A fixed fleet serving bursty traffic is a capacity problem (load testing and capacity planning), and the cost comparison echoes cost and efficiency. Where a model runs is a data-protection and network-boundary decision (data protection, cloud and container security), and keeping the provider behind a small interface applies evolving contracts and versioning to a dependency you do not control.

Key takeaways

  • Filter by hard attributes first (data location, licence, approved list, modality, hardware), then compare soft attributes (quality, latency, cost). Measuring a model the customer cannot use wastes time.
  • Three shapes: a frontier API, an open-weight model on a managed endpoint, and a self-hosted open-weight model. They trade capability and convenience against containment, control and operational burden. "Open-weight" is not "open source"; read each licence.
  • Run a fair bake-off on the customer's gold set with the same scoring, a comparable prompting budget, intervals, and system measures (latency percentiles, cost per task), and decide on the sealed half.
  • For self-hosting, memory is weights plus a KV cache that grows with concurrent users and context. Quantisation shrinks the weights, must be re-evaluated, and does not shrink the cache by itself.
  • APIs are a variable cost, self-hosting is mostly fixed. Compute the crossing from your own assumptions and check utilisation, quality and the hidden operations costs before moving.
  • Models change. Pin versions, keep the evaluation rerunnable, keep the provider behind a thin interface and hold a second choice. Treat downloaded weights as a supply-chain dependency.

Check your understanding

A hospital group says patient text may not leave its network. Your team's preferred model is a frontier API with a strong public ranking. What is the right first step?

  1. Run the bake-off including the API, since quality should decide.
  2. Ask the vendor to promise it will not look at the data, and proceed.
  3. Reduce the task until the hospital agrees.
  4. Treat the data-location rule as a hard constraint: remove the API from the candidates and bake off the models that can run inside the network.
Hard attributes filter before soft ones, and a model that cannot be used is not worth measuring. A vendor's promise does not change where the data goes, and shrinking the task to suit the model reverses the logic of the project. If the API is truly necessary, the route is a customer decision to relax the constraint, not a workaround.

A colleague says "this model is open source, so we can use it in any product". You find that the weights are downloadable under a custom community licence with a monthly-user threshold and an acceptable-use policy. What is the most accurate description?

  1. It is an open-weight model with licence terms that need reading against the product, and it may not meet the Open Source AI Definition.
  2. It is fully open source, since the weights are public.
  3. It is closed, since there are conditions.
  4. The licence does not apply to companies.
Open weights means the parameters are published; open source in the sense of the OSI definition asks for more (information on training data and code) and permissive terms. A custom licence is neither closed nor unconditional: it grants real rights with conditions you must check. Licences apply to companies, and are in fact written mainly for them.

You want to self-host a 70B-parameter model in 8-bit weights for 24 users with 6,000-token contexts, as in the chapter's calculation. A teammate proposes one 80 GB GPU "because the weights are only 70 GB". What is wrong?

  1. Nothing: 70 GB is under 80 GB.
  2. 8-bit weights cannot be used for serving.
  3. The KV cache (about 47 GB here) and runtime overhead must also fit, so the total is well over one card and at least two are needed.
  4. The KV cache is stored on the CPU, so it does not matter.
GPU memory holds weights plus the KV cache of every request in flight, plus headroom for activations and the runtime. With the cache included the total is about 118 GB. Eight-bit serving is common, and the cache normally lives in GPU memory, so ignoring it is the usual sizing mistake.

With assumed prices, self-hosting costs about 5,150 dollars a month and each request costs 0.005 dollars via a mid-tier API. At 600,000 requests a month, what is the best-supported recommendation?

  1. Self-host, because self-hosting is always cheaper.
  2. Use the API: 600,000 requests cost about 3,000 dollars, below the fixed cost, and the crossing is near a million requests a month, provided the API meets the quality and data constraints.
  3. Self-host, because fixed costs are more predictable than variable ones.
  4. The comparison cannot be made without knowing the model's name.
The arithmetic is simple: 600,000 × 0.005 is 3,000 dollars, under the 5,150 fixed cost. Predictability may matter to a customer, but it has a price here, and "always cheaper" ignores volume. The model's name is not needed for a cost crossing, though quality and constraints still gate the choice.

An open-weight 8B model scores 89% on 250 cases with an interval from about 85% to 92%; the quality gate is "interval lower end at least 86%". How should the result be reported?

  1. Pass, because 89% is above 86%.
  2. Fail permanently, because the interval touches 85%.
  3. Pass, because small models are always good enough.
  4. Not yet shown to meet the gate: the lower end is below 86%. State what would change that, such as more cases or targeted fine-tuning, and re-measure.
The rule applies to the lower end, which is below the bar, so the model is not yet shown to pass. It is not rejected either, because the upper end clears the bar. Calling it a pass uses the point estimate the rule excludes, and "always good enough" is an assumption, not evidence.

A year after launch, your provider announces the retirement of the model version you use in 90 days. Which earlier practice most directly makes this a routine change?

  1. Having chosen the most accurate model originally.
  2. Having used an alias that always points to the newest model.
  3. Having kept the gold set, scorer and frozen configuration so a replacement can be evaluated in a day, with a second choice already identified.
  4. Having self-hosted from the start.
A rerunnable evaluation and a known second choice turn retirement into a normal release. An alias that moves silently is what makes changes surprising, original accuracy says nothing about replacement, and self-hosting trades this risk for others (maintenance and migration) without removing the need to evaluate.
