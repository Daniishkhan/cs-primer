Take-Homes, Portfolios & Interviews

Applied AI teams hire for judgement they can see. This chapter explains what they test, how to scope a take-home so that the evidence is stronger than the demo, how to build a portfolio that proves you can ship and explain, and how to answer the interview questions that probe evaluation, debugging and customer work. It answers one question: how do you show, on paper and in conversation, that you decide well when the problem is vague and the data are messy?

Why this matters

The rest of the course taught you to find a problem, build a proof, respect a domain and deliver. Hiring is the same work compressed: a vague brief, a clock, a reviewer deciding whether to trust you with a customer. The skills tested are the ones you have been practising, but the format punishes common habits, such as spending the whole time on the model, hiding limitations, or being unable to explain your own code. After this chapter you can plan a take-home against a time budget, produce the artefacts a reviewer looks for, build two or three portfolio projects that each prove something specific, and prepare for the interview formats you are likely to meet. The path is general, not aimed at one employer, so the advice describes patterns that recur across companies and says where the evidence is thin.

What applied AI teams are actually testing

Hiring for an applied or forward-deployed AI role looks for five things, and a good process tests each in more than one place. A forward-deployed engineer (FDE) is an engineer embedded with customers to make a system work in their environment; Palantir's engineering blog, in "Dev versus Delta", describes the contrast as a software engineer building one capability for many customers and a forward-deployed engineer working with one customer across many capabilities. The title varies (applied AI engineer, solutions engineer, deployment engineer), and the screening looks similar.

Notice what is missing from the list: raw benchmark chasing, framework trivia and puzzle-solving speed. Hiring guides for evaluation-focused roles describe loops that are lighter on algorithm puzzles than traditional machine learning loops and heavier on data work (pandas, SQL), confidence intervals, harness design and discussing trade-offs. Treat that as a pattern, not a rule; individual companies differ.

The hiring loop, and where each skill is used

Scoping a take-home

A take-home is a timed exercise done on your own, usually 2 to 6 hours of intended effort, which a reviewer then discusses with you. The brief is almost always incomplete on purpose: a business problem, a pile of data, a deadline, and a request to "build something and tell us what you found". Your first task is scoping. You will not have time to do everything, and what you leave out is as informative as what you include.

The deliverables that carry the weight

A strong submission for an AI take-home contains the same artefacts a customer engagement would, in miniature. This is the list used throughout the take-home studios of this course, for example contract clause review and productising a research prototype.

  1. A one-page restatement of the requirements. What you believe the customer needs, the assumptions you made, and the questions you would ask if you could. Applied work begins here (see requirements under ambiguity).
  2. A baseline before any framework. The simplest thing that could work: keyword search, a rule, one model call with one prompt. If your system cannot beat it, that is the finding.
  3. A small gold set with its labelling procedure. Labelled cases you checked yourself, with the rule you used to label. Without it, no number in the submission means anything (evaluation plans).
  4. An evaluation with intervals. Counts, denominators and 95% intervals, comparing baseline and system on the same cases.
  5. A quality, cost and latency table. Money and time per task next to accuracy, because the right answer is rarely the most accurate one (cost and return).
  6. An error-analysis memo. Read a sample of failures, name the first thing that went wrong in each, count the categories, and say which one to fix first. The method is in error analysis.
  7. A limitations section. What the submission does not show. Reviewers trust a candidate who states them.
  8. A three-minute oral defence. Prepared notes for the conversation that follows (see below).

Common misconception

"The candidate with the highest accuracy, or the most sophisticated architecture, wins." Reviewers read dozens of submissions and can usually tell which ones were tuned until the number rose. Hamel Husain's advice for evaluation interviews, as summarised in hiring guides, is to prize judgement over hill-climbing: inspect the data for traps, set baselines, avoid generic or composite metrics, present quality against cost and latency, and propose next steps. A modest system with an honest evaluation, a named first failure and a clear recommendation beats an elaborate one with an unexplained score.

Spend the time on the evidence

The most common error is time allocation: candidates spend four of five hours building and ten minutes writing up. The script below turns a plan into numbers before you start. It checks the total against the budget, flags missing deliverables and reports how much of the time goes on building versus proving the build works.

# Check a take-home plan against a 5-hour budget before you start.
BUDGET_H = 5.0
PLAN = {                       # activity -> hours planned
    "read brief, restate, list assumptions": 0.5,
    "inspect data, build gold set (40 items)": 1.5,
    "baseline (no framework)": 0.5,
    "main system": 1.0,
    "eval with intervals, cost, latency": 0.5,
    "error analysis of 30 traces": 0.5,
    "memo, limitations, README": 0.5,
}
MUST = ["gold set", "baseline", "eval", "error analysis", "memo"]
total = sum(PLAN.values())
print(f"planned {total:.2f} h of {BUDGET_H:.1f} h")
for act, h in PLAN.items():
    print(f"  {h:>5.2f} h  {h/total:>4.0%}  {act}")
build = PLAN["main system"] / total
print(f"building the system: {build:.0%} of the time; everything that proves it works: {1-build:.0%}")
missing = [m for m in MUST if not any(m in a for a in PLAN)]
print("missing deliverables:", missing or "none")
print("OVER BUDGET" if total > BUDGET_H else "within budget, with %.2f h spare" % (BUDGET_H-total))

The plan spends 20% of the time on the system and 80% on everything that shows it works. That ratio surprises people, but it matches what is graded. A rough rule: in a five-hour take-home, the system should take no more than a quarter of the time. Drag the sliders below to feel the trade-offs.

How big should the gold set be?

You cannot label hundreds of items in five hours, and you do not need to. What you need is to know how much a given size can tell you. The script prints the 95% Wilson interval for a system that truly scores 80%, at several gold-set sizes, together with the labelling time under an assumption of two minutes per item plus ten per cent double-labelled to check agreement. The two-minutes figure is an assumption; time yourself on five items and replace it.

# How big a gold set can you afford in a 5-hour take-home?  Interval width vs n at 80% accuracy,
# and the time to label (assumption: 2 minutes per item, one labeller, plus 10% double-labelled).
import math
def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z*z/n
    c = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h
print(f"{'n':>4} {'interval at 80%':>18} {'width':>7} {'label time':>11}")
for n in (20, 40, 60, 100, 200, 400):
    lo, hi = wilson(round(.8*n), n)
    minutes = n*2 + 0.1*n*2
    print(f"{n:>4} {lo:>8.1%} to {hi:>6.1%} {100*(hi-lo):>6.1f}pt {minutes/60:>8.1f} h")

Forty items give an interval 24 points wide and cost about an hour and a half of labelling, which is roughly the whole budget for the data step. At 100 items the width falls to about 15 points, but labelling alone takes nearly four hours. The practical answer for a take-home is 30 to 60 carefully chosen items, stratified (drawn deliberately from each kind of case, including the awkward ones) and honest about the width: "with 40 items my interval is wide, so I report differences below about 10 points as unresolved". Saying so earns more credit than a larger, sloppier set. The statistics are the same as in evaluation statistics, and the labelling procedure follows human labels.

Ambiguity, traps and AI tools

When the brief is unclear, write the question down, choose the most reasonable default, say that you did, and continue. If the process allows questions, ask a few good ones early, and still state your default in case no answer comes. Do not stall, and do not silently pick an interpretation.

Inspect the data before building. Take-home datasets often contain deliberate traps: duplicate rows that would leak between a development and test split, labels that disagree, questions the documents cannot answer (which should get an abstention), personal data that should not go to a third-party API, and a distribution that differs from the one the brief describes. Spend the first half hour reading fifty rows by hand and write down what you see. A line in the memo such as "found 14 near-duplicate questions; deduplicated before splitting" is a strong signal.

Using AI tools. Read the rules; some processes allow assistants and some do not, and some ask you to disclose what you used. Where tools are allowed, the standard to meet is that you can explain every file and every number. Hiring guides report that interviewers probe exactly this: they ask where an AI assistant helped, where it was wrong and how you checked generated code. Use tools to go faster on boilerplate, never to decide what to measure, and run what you ship. Candidate write-ups that cannot be defended are worse than short ones that can. Some recruiting guides argue that take-homes are weaker evidence now that assistants can do much of the building, and recommend live work-sample exercises instead; if you are given a live exercise, the same advice applies, with the explanation done aloud.

The oral defence

Many processes follow the take-home with a short conversation. Prepare three minutes you can deliver without notes, in this order: the question you answered and your headline result with its interval; the one design decision you are most sure of, and why; the biggest weakness and what you would do next with another day. Then expect questions. The ones that recur: "why this baseline?", "how did you label, and how do you know the labels are right?", "what does this interval mean?", "what is the first failure you saw?", "what would you change for 100 times the traffic?" and "where did you use an assistant?". Two habits help. When you do not know something, say so and describe how you would find out. When the reviewer pushes on a choice, state the trade-off you made and what evidence would change your mind, rather than defending or collapsing. That is the diplomatic pushback hiring guides describe, and it is also the skill a customer needs from you.

Try it yourself

Run a three-hour mini take-home on any small dataset (a folder of fifty support emails, the questions from a public FAQ, a few contracts). Set a timer and follow this list.

  1. Fill in plan.py for three hours, and fix it until it fits.
  2. Write the one-page restatement first, with at least three assumptions.
  3. Label 30 items, timing yourself, and compute your own interval from the formula in goldsize.py.
  4. Build a baseline, then a system. Record accuracy, cost and time for both.
  5. Read ten failures, name the first error in each, and count.
  6. Record yourself giving the three-minute defence. Watch it once.

A portfolio that proves judgement

A portfolio is the set of projects and writing a reviewer can inspect without you in the room. It has one job: to make the claims on your CV checkable. A reviewer opens a repository for perhaps ten minutes. What they look for is not scale but the habits of an applied engineer: a stated problem, a baseline, an evaluation they can trust, an honest look at failures and a way to reproduce the result.

Three projects, each proving something

The advice below is the author's judgement, not a measured result: no source gives a verified number of projects that works best. The principle is that each project should demonstrate a different skill, rather than three variations on the same chatbot.

1. An agent or tool-using system, with traces

Shows you can build and debug a loop. The proof is not the demo but the failure analysis of recorded runs: the first wrong step in each, the fix, and a regression test. Builds on the A4 course, the tool loop.

2. A document or retrieval system with a domain benchmark

Shows you can handle messy sources and measure. The proof is a small gold set from a real domain, retrieval and answer metrics separately, and a regression run. Builds on end-to-end RAG.

3. A reproduction or a small training run

Shows research literacy: you took a paper's claim, tried to reproduce it, and reported honestly what held. A failed reproduction, well documented, is a strong piece. Builds on reading papers.

Add one customer-style write-up: a status report, an analysis memo or a deployment retrospective, written as in the previous chapter, for a real or realistic engagement. The FDE hiring signals that appear in surveys include "ships production code" and "can run a discovery interview without a sales script". A written discovery synthesis or deployment write-up evidences the second signal in a way code cannot, but remember the source caveats: it is a vendor survey.

The anatomy of one repository

The checklist is mechanical enough to automate. The script below audits a project folder for the artefacts above and for a few hygiene problems. It runs on two small example folders it builds itself: one that follows the anatomy and one that looks like a typical weekend demo, with an API key left in the source.

# Audit a project folder for the artefacts a reviewer looks for in the first ten minutes.
import os, re, tempfile, textwrap

README_SECTIONS = ["problem", "baseline", "evaluation", "results", "failure", "limitations", "cost", "reproduce"]

def audit(root):
    files = {os.path.relpath(os.path.join(d, f), root)
             for d, _, fs in os.walk(root) for f in fs}
    readme = open(os.path.join(root, "README.md")).read().lower() if "README.md" in files else ""
    report = []
    for sec in README_SECTIONS:
        report.append((f"README mentions {sec}", sec in readme))
    reqs = open(os.path.join(root, "requirements.txt")).read() if "requirements.txt" in files else ""
    lines = [l for l in reqs.splitlines() if l.strip()]
    report.append(("dependencies pinned with ==", bool(lines) and all("==" in l for l in lines)))
    report.append(("gold set file present", any(f.startswith("data/gold") for f in files)))
    report.append(("eval script present", any(re.match(r"eval.*\.py$", os.path.basename(f)) for f in files)))
    report.append(("results table saved", any(f.startswith("results/") for f in files)))
    report.append(("LICENSE present", "LICENSE" in files))
    secrets = any(re.search(r"(sk-[A-Za-z0-9]{10,}|api[_-]?key\s*=\s*['\"][^'\"]+)",
                  open(os.path.join(root, f), errors="ignore").read()) for f in files if f.endswith((".py", ".env", ".md")))
    report.append(("no key-like strings in code", not secrets))
    return report

def make(root, files):
    for name, text in files.items():
        path = os.path.join(root, name); os.makedirs(os.path.dirname(path) or root, exist_ok=True)
        open(path, "w").write(textwrap.dedent(text))

strong = {"README.md": "Problem\nBaseline\nEvaluation\nResults\nFailure analysis\nLimitations\nCost\nHow to reproduce\n",
          "requirements.txt": "numpy==2.1.0\n", "data/gold.jsonl": "{}\n", "eval_run.py": "print(1)\n",
          "results/table.md": "x\n", "LICENSE": "MIT\n"}
weak = {"README.md": "My cool RAG demo!\n", "requirements.txt": "numpy\nopenai\n",
        "app.py": "api_key = 'sk-abcdefghijklmnop'\n"}
for name, files in (("strong-project", strong), ("weak-project", weak)):
    with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as d:
        make(d, files)
        rep = audit(d)
        passed = sum(ok for _, ok in rep)
        print(f"{name}: {passed}/{len(rep)} checks")
        for label, ok in rep:
            if not ok: print("   missing:", label)

The point is not the script. It is that every missing line is something a reviewer notices in seconds. A key-like string in the source ends the review at once, because it shows how you would treat the customer's credentials. Check history too, not just the current files: a removed secret stays in the repository's past, so rotate it.

Systems connection

The portfolio habits are ordinary reliability and security hygiene applied to your own work. Pinned dependencies and a one-command run are reproducible configuration; a clean repository and no secrets are the basics of supply-chain security and secrets handling; an error-analysis note is a small postmortem. Interviews that ask you to discuss trade-offs in a design use the vocabulary of trade-offs and communication.

What to leave out, and what not to claim

Leave out projects you cannot explain end to end, tutorials repackaged without change, and any claim you did not measure. Do not put benchmark scores in the README without saying which cases and how many. Do not use a customer's or former employer's data, code or prompts, even if you could. Mark what is synthetic. If a project depended on an assistant for a large part of the code, say so and show how you verified it. Reviewers are looking for red flags that hiring guides repeat: surface-level demos, internals the candidate cannot explain, unverified model output and keyword-padded CVs.

Interview formats, and how to answer them

Interview conversations in this field are short and applied. Below are the formats that recur, with the approach that works for each. Practise aloud, because the common failure is to know the answer and not be able to structure it in eight minutes.

"Design an evaluation for a support chatbot"

This is the staple of evaluation-focused roles. A structure keeps you from rambling. Follow it in order and say which step you are on.

"A 95% pass rate, but users are complaining"

Another recurring prompt (reported in guides to evaluation interviews) asks you to explain an evaluation that looks excellent while the product is not. There are only a handful of causes, and a good answer works through them in order of how cheap each is to check.

  1. The set does not look like live traffic. Compare the distribution of the set to a sample of real requests. Common causes: the set was written by the team, or is old, or lacks the awkward cases users send.
  2. The aggregate hides a slice. A 95% overall can be 99% on easy, frequent cases and 60% on a rare, important slice. Break the result down by slice and by customer segment, and weigh by cost of error, not only by frequency.
  3. The grader is not measuring what users care about. A judge or metric may reward fluent, long answers. Check grader agreement with human labels on a sample, especially on the cases users complained about.
  4. The pass criterion is too loose. "Mentions the right policy" passes answers that state it wrongly. Re-grade a sample strictly.
  5. Leakage or contamination. Examples from the set appear in prompts, few-shot examples or training data. See contamination.
  6. Complaints are about something other than correctness. Latency, tone, refusal of reasonable requests, or the interface. Read twenty complaints first; the categories tell you whether the evaluation is even aimed at the right target.

The final recommendation in the answer is what makes it good: read the complaints, add them to the set as a new slice, and rerun. Interviewers want to hear that you start from the data, not from a theory.

And end with what you would measure first, because applied work is a sequence of tests of the riskiest assumption (see that chapter), and interviewers are checking that you think the same way.

Key takeaways

  • Applied AI hiring tests judgement under ambiguity, evidence before claims, data reading, production sense and honest communication. Evidence about hiring practice is mostly second-hand, so read the actual posting.
  • In a take-home, the system is the smaller part. Allocate most of the time to the restatement, gold set, baseline, evaluation with intervals, cost and latency, error analysis and limitations.
  • A small, stratified gold set with an honest interval beats a large, sloppy one. Time your own labelling and plan around it.
  • Inspect the data for traps first, state defaults for ambiguity, follow the rules on AI tools, and be able to explain every file and number.
  • A portfolio makes your claims checkable: three projects proving different skills plus one customer-style write-up, each repository passing a ten-minute inspection with no secrets.
  • Structure interview answers: clarify, assume with numbers, design, name failures, say what you would measure first. Say "I don't know, and here is how I would find out" when true.

Check your understanding

In a five-hour take-home, a candidate spends 3.5 hours building a multi-agent system and 20 minutes on a README. What is the most likely reviewer reaction?

  1. Impressed by the scope of the build.
  2. Unable to tell whether the system works, because there is no baseline, no trustworthy evaluation and no error analysis.
  3. Pleased that the candidate avoided over-writing.
  4. Indifferent, as long as the demo runs.
Reviewers grade the evidence that a system works, not its size. Without a baseline, labelled cases and failure analysis, there is nothing to trust. A working demo shows the build, not the judgement.

You have budget to label either 40 or 100 gold items, and labelling 100 would consume most of the take-home. Which choice is best?

  1. Label 100, because more data is always better.
  2. Label 10, because the interval does not matter.
  3. Skip the gold set and use an LLM judge.
  4. Label about 40 stratified items, report the wide interval honestly, and treat small differences as unresolved.
At 40 items the 95% interval is about 24 points wide, at 100 about 15; both are wide, and the extra hours would be taken from error analysis and the memo. A small, deliberately chosen set with honest uncertainty shows better judgement. Skipping labelled data leaves nothing to validate a judge against.

While inspecting a take-home dataset you find 14 near-duplicate questions. What should you do?

  1. Deduplicate before splitting into development and test sets, and note it in the memo.
  2. Ignore it, since the brief did not mention duplicates.
  3. Keep them all because they raise accuracy.
  4. Delete the duplicates only from the test set.
Near-duplicates across a split leak test answers into development and inflate the score. Deduplicating first and saying so shows you check the data. Removing them only from the test set leaves the leak in the other direction, and ignoring them is the trap the dataset was set to catch.

An interviewer asks why a 95% pass rate coincides with user complaints. Which first move is strongest?

  1. Argue that users are mistaken.
  2. Raise the threshold to 98%.
  3. Read a sample of complaints, categorise them, and compare with what the evaluation set covers and what the grader measures.
  4. Switch to a larger judge model.
The cheapest, most informative step is to look at the complaints; they show whether the set is unrepresentative, a slice is failing, the grader is mis-aimed or the issue is something else, like latency. A new threshold or judge changes the number, not the diagnosis.

A reviewer asks, "Where did you use an AI assistant, and how did you check what it wrote?" What is the best kind of answer?

  1. "I didn't use any," whether or not that is true.
  2. Specific examples where it helped with boilerplate, one where it was wrong, and how you tested the generated code and checked the numbers yourself.
  3. "It did most of the work, so I can't say."
  4. "Everything was checked by the assistant."
Interviewers are testing ownership and verification. Honesty with concrete detail passes. Denial that proves false and delegating the check to the same tool both fail, and "I can't say" shows you cannot defend the work.

Which portfolio project best demonstrates research literacy?

  1. A chatbot with a polished interface.
  2. A copy of a tutorial notebook with a new dataset name.
  3. A leaderboard screenshot with no code.
  4. An attempt to reproduce a paper's claim, with a write-up of what held, what did not and why.
Reproduction shows that you read a claim, tested it and reported honestly, including negative results. Polish, copied tutorials and unverifiable screenshots show none of that. A failed reproduction, carefully documented, is stronger evidence than a clean but unexamined success.
