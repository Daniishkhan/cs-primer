Turning Research into Products

A paper reports a result, and a repository reproduces it on the author's machine. A customer wants it running next quarter, on their data, with someone to call when it breaks. This chapter covers the distance between those two things: reproducing before promising, a contract for the interface, service-level objectives that include quality, documentation for four different readers, and a support path that feeds back into the evaluation set.

Why this matters

Applied AI work often starts from something that already exists: a research group's repository, an open-weight model, a technique from last month's paper. The proof-of-concept chapter explained why pilots stall on the way to production. This chapter is the same journey seen from the other end: the starting point is research code, which was built to establish a claim, not to serve a user. If you treat it as a product you inherit its silent assumptions. If you treat it as a specification for a product, you decide which assumptions to keep. After this chapter you can list what must be added before research code is something a customer can rely on, write down an interface and three kinds of objective for it, and plan the documentation and support that go with it. The take-home Productise a Research Prototype exercises all of it.

A result is not a product

A research artefact is the paper, repository, weights and benchmark numbers that together support a claim: "this method beats that method on this task". Its audience is other researchers, who will read the paper, run the script and judge the claim. A product is something a user relies on without reading the code, over time, on inputs the author never saw, with someone accountable when it fails. The two have different jobs, so they are built differently, and the gap between them is not a matter of polish.

Google researchers made a related point about machine learning systems in general. In "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" (Sculley et al., 2015) they showed that the model code is a small box surrounded by much larger ones: data collection, configuration, serving infrastructure, monitoring. A research repository contains the small box and a rough version of one or two of the others. Everything else is yours to build, and it is most of the work.

Analogy: a recipe and a restaurant

A published recipe tells a skilled cook how to make one excellent dish, once, in a well-equipped kitchen, with fresh ingredients and no time pressure. A restaurant has to make that dish two hundred times on a Friday night, with whatever the supplier delivered, by staff who were not the recipe's author, while meeting hygiene rules, quoting a price and handling the customer who is allergic to nuts. The recipe matters a great deal, because without it there is nothing to serve. But the recipe is not the restaurant.

Where it breaks down: a recipe is deterministic enough that a second cook can follow it and get nearly the same dish. A research method can fail to reproduce at all, or work only with the author's data and settings. That is why the first step below is checking that the recipe works in your kitchen.

Common misconception

"The paper reports 92% on the benchmark, so we can promise the customer about 92%." A benchmark number describes performance on the benchmark's inputs, scored by the benchmark's metric, using the authors' configuration. The customer's documents differ in length, language, formatting and difficulty, and their definition of "correct" may differ from the metric's. The benchmark number tells you which method is worth trying. The number you can promise comes from your evaluation plan, run on the customer's own cases.

Step zero: reproduce, then check the right to use it

Before designing anything, find out whether the research artefact does what it says, on your machine, with your data. Reproduction means re-running the authors' method and getting a result consistent with the paper. Transfer is the second, separate question: does it still work on a different distribution? Keep them apart. A reproduction failure points at the artefact (a missing seed, an undocumented preprocessing step, a bug, a result that does not hold). A transfer failure points at your problem, and may be the most useful thing you learn in the first week. The three-pass method and the checklist in Reading Papers Efficiently apply here, and "Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research" (Pineau et al., 2021) is a good checklist of what a reproducible release should include.

Budget a fixed, short time for this. A reasonable rule is two days to reproduce the headline number on the authors' own data. If you cannot, write down exactly where you got stuck, ask the authors, and decide with the customer whether the idea is still worth pursuing by another route. Failing to reproduce is a finding, not a failure of effort.

Then check what you are allowed to do. Research code, model weights and datasets usually carry three separate licences, and they often differ. Code may be permissive while the weights are restricted to non-commercial use, or the training data may carry terms that pass obligations to anyone who uses a model trained on it. Read all three before you build on them. The customer's legal and security teams will ask, and the security review will expect an inventory. The supply-chain risks (an unmaintained package, a dependency that pulls in something unexpected) are covered in memory safety and supply chain. The short version for research code: pin every dependency to an exact version, record hashes for downloaded weights, and scan what you install.

State of play, October 2026

Open-weight models and research code are released under a wide range of licences, from permissive licences to custom ones with usage restrictions, size thresholds or non-commercial clauses. These terms vary by release and change between versions of the same model family, so read the licence file shipped with the exact weights you download rather than relying on the family's reputation. If you serve a model through a hosted API instead, the provider's terms and deprecation schedule are what matter: providers retire model versions on published timetables, so record the exact version you tested against and watch for the retirement notice.

The interface is a contract

A research script is called by its author with arguments they remember. A product is called by other programs, and by people the author will never meet. The first design act is to write down the contract: what the caller sends, what they get back, what happens when things go wrong, and what will not change without notice. If you write nothing down, the contract is whatever the code happens to do, and the customer will build on that by accident.

A good contract for an AI service has five parts.

  1. Typed inputs with limits. Say what is accepted (a non-empty string up to 2,000 characters, a PDF under 20 MB) and reject everything else loudly and early. Model behaviour on huge or malformed inputs is the most common source of surprises.
  2. A response with a status, not just an answer. Return ok only when the answer passed your checks. Other statuses (rejected, timeout, unverified) tell the caller what to do next. A service that returns plausible text with no status makes every caller guess.
  3. A deadline and a fallback. Every call has a time limit. When the model is slow, return a cheaper, weaker answer clearly labelled as such, or an honest "no answer", rather than hanging. The failure, timeouts and retries chapter explains why a missing deadline turns one slow dependency into an outage.
  4. A verifier. A check that runs on the output before it leaves, for example "every number in the summary appears in the source", or "every citation matches a retrieved passage" (see grounded citations). The verifier is what lets you say ok and mean it.
  5. A version. Put a schema version in every response and a model-and-prompt version in the logs. When you change behaviour, the version tells the customer and tells you which change caused a regression.

The script below wraps a stand-in "research function" with that contract. The stand-in is slow on one request in four and invents a figure about one time in four. It is a deliberately unreliable model, not a real one, so that every branch of the wrapper runs.

# From research function to service: the wrapper adds a contract, a deadline,
# a verifier and an honest status. The "research model" is a stand-in.
import json, random, time

SCHEMA_VERSION = "2026-10-01"
MAX_CHARS = 2000

def research_summarise(text, seed):
    """Stand-in for the paper's code: slow sometimes, sometimes wrong."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    latency = rng.choice([0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 3.5])          # seconds; one request in four is slow
    first = text.split(".")[0].strip()
    summary = first if rng.random() > 0.25 else first + ". Revenue rose 40%."  # invented claim
    return summary, latency

def handle(request, deadline_s=1.0):
    rid = request.get("id", "?")
    text = request.get("text")
    if not isinstance(text, str) or not text.strip():
        return {"id": rid, "status": "rejected", "reason": "text must be a non-empty string"}
    if len(text) > MAX_CHARS:
        return {"id": rid, "status": "rejected", "reason": f"text over {MAX_CHARS} characters"}
    summary, latency = research_summarise(text, seed=hash_id(rid))
    if latency > deadline_s:
        return {"id": rid, "status": "timeout", "fallback": text.split(".")[0].strip(),
                "note": "extractive fallback: first sentence"}
    # verifier: every number in the summary must appear in the source
    nums = [w.strip(".") for w in summary.replace("%", "").split() if w.strip(".").isdigit()]
    bad = [n for n in nums if n not in text]
    if bad:
        return {"id": rid, "status": "unverified", "unsupported_numbers": bad, "summary": None}
    return {"id": rid, "status": "ok", "summary": summary, "schema": SCHEMA_VERSION}

def hash_id(s):
    return sum(ord(c) * (i + 1) for i, c in enumerate(str(s)))

DOC = "Q3 revenue was 12 million pounds. Costs were flat. The board met twice."
requests = [{"id": "r1", "text": DOC}, {"id": "r2", "text": DOC}, {"id": "r3", "text": ""},
            {"id": "r4", "text": DOC}, {"id": "r5", "text": "x" * 3000}, {"id": "r6", "text": DOC}]
for r in requests:
    print(json.dumps(handle(r)))
{"id": "r1", "status": "timeout", "fallback": "Q3 revenue was 12 million pounds", "note": "extractive fallback: first sentence"}
{"id": "r2", "status": "ok", "summary": "Q3 revenue was 12 million pounds", "schema": "2026-10-01"}
{"id": "r3", "status": "rejected", "reason": "text must be a non-empty string"}
{"id": "r4", "status": "unverified", "unsupported_numbers": ["40"], "summary": null}
{"id": "r5", "status": "rejected", "reason": "text over 2000 characters"}
{"id": "r6", "status": "unverified", "unsupported_numbers": ["40"], "summary": null}

Read the six lines of output as six different promises kept. Request r2 is the happy path. Request r1 hit the deadline and returned an extractive fallback (the first sentence of the source), which is weaker but cannot invent anything. Requests r4 and r6 are the important ones: the model produced a fluent summary containing "40", a figure that is not in the source, and the verifier withheld it instead of passing it on. Without the verifier, those two summaries would have reached the customer looking exactly like r2. Requests r3 and r5 were refused at the door, with a reason the caller can act on.

Systems connection

This is ordinary API design with one addition. The versioning, error contracts and idempotency questions are in APIs; deadlines and retries in failure, timeouts and retries; and the shape of ML-backed services in Designing with ML and LLMs. The addition is the verifier and the unverified status: a deterministic service is either up or down, while a model-backed one can be up and wrong. The status field is how you make "up and wrong" visible to the caller.

Service-level objectives for a system that can be wrong

An SLI (service-level indicator) is a measurement, such as the fraction of requests answered within 2.5 seconds. An SLO (service-level objective) is a target for that measurement over a period, such as "99% within 2.5 seconds over 30 days". The error budget is the allowed shortfall: 1% of requests may be slow, and once that is spent, reliability work takes priority over new features. The reliability course covers the mechanics in SLOs and error budgets, and the Google SRE book's chapter on service-level objectives is the original reference.

An AI service needs the three usual objectives and one more. The usual ones are latency (how long a request takes), availability (the fraction that return a usable response) and cost (spend per request, which the cost chapter treats). The addition is quality: the fraction of answers that are correct, grounded or accepted by a reviewer. Quality cannot be read off a dashboard of timings. It needs a sampled review (a person or a validated judge scoring a slice of live traffic every day) or a cheap proxy that is checked against sampled review, such as the citation check above. The LLM observability and improvement flywheel chapters show how to wire that up.

The script below simulates a month of traffic and checks it against three SLOs. All rates are illustrative assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic, not measurements of any real system.

# Does the service meet its SLOs? Simulated month of traffic (illustrative assumptions).
import random, math
rng = random.Random(7)
N = 20000
lat, ok, grounded = [], 0, 0
for _ in range(N):
    base = rng.lognormvariate(math.log(0.9), 0.45)          # seconds, median 0.9
    if rng.random() < 0.012: base += rng.uniform(4, 10)      # provider hiccup
    lat.append(base)
    ok += rng.random() > 0.004                                # 0.4% hard errors
    grounded += rng.random() > 0.031                          # 3.1% answers fail the citation check
lat.sort()
p = lambda q: lat[int(q * N) - 1]
under = sum(1 for x in lat if x <= 2.5) / N
print(f"requests            {N}")
print(f"latency p50 / p95 / p99   {p(.5):.2f}s / {p(.95):.2f}s / {p(.99):.2f}s")
print(f"within 2.5 s        {under:.3%}   (SLO: 99.0% -> {'MET' if under>=.99 else 'MISSED'})")
print(f"availability        {ok/N:.3%}   (SLO: 99.5% -> {'MET' if ok/N>=.995 else 'MISSED'})")
print(f"grounded answers    {grounded/N:.3%}   (SLO: 97.0% -> {'MET' if grounded/N>=.97 else 'MISSED'})")
budget = N * 0.01
bad = N - int(under * N)
print(f"latency error budget: {budget:.0f} slow requests allowed, {bad} used ({bad/budget:.0%})")
requests            20000
latency p50 / p95 / p99   0.91s / 2.00s / 5.91s
within 2.5 s        97.650%   (SLO: 99.0% -> MISSED)
availability        99.580%   (SLO: 99.5% -> MET)
grounded answers    97.005%   (SLO: 97.0% -> MET)
latency error budget: 200 slow requests allowed, 470 used (235%)

Three things to notice. First, the median and p95 look healthy (0.91 s and 2.00 s), yet the latency SLO is missed: the failure is in the tail, the 1.2% of requests that hit a provider hiccup, which is why objectives are written on a fraction of requests within a limit and not on an average (see tail latency). Second, the budget is 1% of 20,000, that is 200 slow requests, and 470 happened: the budget is 235% spent, so the right response is to fix the tail (a tighter deadline with a fallback, a second provider) before shipping features. Third, the grounded-answer SLO passes at 97.005% against a 97.0% target. That is a pass, but a fragile one: with a 20,000-request sample the measurement itself has a margin of error of roughly a quarter of a percentage point, so a pass this close should be reported as "at the threshold", not "met with room to spare". The same discipline from evaluation statistics applies to live metrics.

Choose targets by asking what the user's task can tolerate, not by copying a round number. A drafting aid that a person always reads can have a loose quality SLO and a tight latency one (nobody waits for a draft). A service that files an insurance claim automatically needs the reverse. Agree the numbers with the customer, write them into the contract, and report against them in every status report.

Try it yourself

Save slo.py and change one thing at a time, predicting the effect before you run it.

  1. Lower the hiccup probability from 0.012 to 0.004. Does the latency SLO pass? How much of the budget is left?
  2. Keep the hiccups but add a deadline: any request over 2.5 s is replaced by a fallback answer taking 0.3 s. What happens to the latency SLO, and what should you now measure so the fallback's quality is not hidden?
  3. Change the SLO for grounded answers to 98%. By how much is it missed, and what would you do about it?

Step 2 is the important one. A fallback fixes the latency number by moving the cost somewhere else, so every mitigation needs a new indicator that watches what it gave up.

Documentation for four readers

The README that comes with a research repository is written for a reader who wants to reproduce a result. A product has at least four kinds of reader, and one document cannot serve them all. The documentation framework known as Diátaxis (Daniele Procida) separates documentation into four kinds by what the reader is doing, and the split fits AI services well.

One document belongs to AI systems more than to ordinary software: the statement of limitations. "Model Cards for Model Reporting" (Mitchell et al., 2019) proposed a short standard document for a model that states its intended use, the data it was evaluated on, performance broken down by slice, and known limits. "Datasheets for Datasets" (Gebru et al., 2018) does the same for data. You do not need to adopt either format exactly, but a customer-facing system should come with one page that says what it was tested on, what it was not tested on, where it is weakest (by slice, with numbers and intervals) and what a user should never rely on it for. Writing that page often finds problems that testing missed, because it forces you to say the limits out loud. Treat the limitations section as a deliverable, not an apology.

Support: who answers, and where does the evidence go?

Support is the arrangement by which someone answers questions and repairs faults after launch. For a research prototype the arrangement is "ask the author". For a product you decide, in writing, who handles which kind of problem and how fast. The typical split is by who can fix it.

Kind of problemExampleFirst responderEvidence to collect
Usage"How do I send a PDF?"Customer's champion or help desk, from the how-to guidesWhich doc failed them
FaultTimeouts for twenty minutesOn-call owner, from the runbookRequest ids, status counts, dependency health

ChangeProvider retires the model version in 90 daysDelivery engineer with the customer's ownerRegression run on the replacement, signed off

The quality row is where AI support differs from ordinary software support. A reported wrong answer is not only a ticket to close; it is a free, labelled test case from real use. Add it to the regression set (with the customer's permission and privacy rules respected), and the same mistake will be caught automatically after any future change. Over a few months this turns support into the best source of evaluation data you have, and it is the loop described in the improvement flywheel. Keep the loop honest by recording the share of tickets that led to a new test, and by reviewing a sample of "no problem found" closures.

Plan the end of your involvement at the start. A handover that leaves the customer with a system nobody understands is the usual way productised research dies. Include a runbook for the five most likely failures, a recorded walkthrough, the evaluation harness with instructions to run it, and a named owner on their side who has run it once without you. After a bad incident, write a blameless review in the style of postmortems, and share it.

Deciding whether to productise at all

Not every promising paper should become a product. Four questions help you decide, and they are best asked of the customer, in writing, before you start building.

  1. Does a simpler method get most of the value? Compare the research method against a plain baseline on the customer's data. If a keyword search or a rule gets 90% of the benefit, the research method's extra maintenance cost may not pay. The feasibility triage and riskiest-assumption chapters give the habit.
  2. Who will maintain it? Research code often depends on one person's knowledge. If the customer cannot or will not maintain it, either you stay on a support contract or you choose something less clever.
  3. What changes under it? A method built on a particular model version, a particular library or a particular data feed inherits their change schedule. List them and the dates you know.
  4. What does it cost per task? A method that needs many model calls per answer (search, voting, self-critique) can be accurate and unaffordable. Compute unit cost early using the cost chapter method.

If the answers favour building, you now have a plan: reproduce, check the licences, write the contract, set four kinds of objective, write four kinds of documentation, and arrange support and handover. The studio Productise a Research Prototype asks you to do this against a clock, and search in products shows how one family of research methods meets these constraints in practice.

Key takeaways

  • A research artefact supports a claim; a product is relied on by strangers. The product needs a contract, objectives, documentation, an owner and a supply chain that the repository does not have.
  • Reproduce the headline result first (in a fixed, short time), and keep reproduction separate from transfer to the customer's data.
  • Check licences for code, weights and data separately, pin dependencies and record hashes.
  • A contract has typed inputs, a status on every response, a deadline with a labelled fallback, a verifier and a version. Never return plausible text with no status.
  • SLOs for AI services include quality, which needs sampled review, and are written on fractions of requests so that tails are visible.
  • Document for four readers (learners, workers, lookers-up, sceptics) and always ship a page of known limitations.
  • Treat every reported wrong answer as a labelled test case, and plan the handover on day one.

Check your understanding

A paper reports 92% on its benchmark. You run its repository on your laptop and get 85%. What is the most sensible next step?

  1. Tell the customer to expect 85%.
  2. Spend a bounded time finding the gap (seeds, preprocessing, versions), ask the authors, and treat it as a reproduction finding before testing on the customer's data.
  3. Assume the paper is wrong and drop the idea.
  4. Fine-tune until you reach 92%.
A gap on the authors' own benchmark is a reproduction question, and a short, bounded investigation usually explains it. Quoting 85% promises a number that says nothing about the customer's data; dropping the idea is premature; tuning to match the paper hides the gap instead of explaining it.

The research code is MIT-licensed, so a colleague says you can ship the model it downloads. What is the flaw?

  1. MIT licences prohibit commercial use.
  2. Licences do not matter for internal tools.
  3. The licence only applies in the author's country.
  4. Code, weights and training data are separate artefacts with possibly different licences, and the weights or data may restrict commercial use.
MIT is permissive, so the first option is wrong. The code licence covers the code only. Weights and data often carry their own terms, which is why all three are read separately and recorded in the inventory the customer's security team will ask for.

In the wrapper's output, requests r4 and r6 return unverified with no summary. Why is that better than returning the model's fluent summary?

  1. The summary contained a figure not present in the source, and an unlabelled invented number looks identical to a correct answer once it reaches the user.
  2. It makes the service faster.
  3. Fluent summaries are always wrong.
  4. It reduces the model's cost.
The verifier exists to stop fabricated specifics reaching the user. Withholding costs one answer; passing it on costs the customer's trust. Speed and cost are not affected, and fluency is not evidence of error, which is exactly why the check is needed.

A month of data shows p50 = 0.9 s and p95 = 2.0 s, but the SLO "99% within 2.5 s" is missed. Which explanation fits?

  1. The median is too high.
  2. The SLO must be wrong, because p95 is under 2.5 s.
  3. More than 1% of requests exceed 2.5 s, which lives beyond p95 in the tail.
  4. Percentiles cannot be compared with SLOs.
p95 under 2.5 s only says that at least 95% are fast enough. Missing a 99% objective means between 1% and 5% are slow, so the problem is in the far tail, for example provider hiccups. Check p99 and the tail, not the median.

You add a 2.5 s deadline with a cheap fallback and the latency SLO is now met. What should you add next?

  1. Nothing; the SLO is met.
  2. An indicator for the fallback rate and the fallback's quality, since the mitigation moved the cost into answer quality.
  3. A longer deadline.
  4. A rule to hide the status field.
A fallback improves latency by giving a weaker answer. If you do not measure how often it fires and how good it is, the dashboard looks green while users receive worse answers. Each mitigation needs an indicator for what it trades away.

A user reports a wrong date extracted from a contract. What is the best use of the report?

  1. Fix it by hand and close the ticket.
  2. Add a rule to the prompt for dates and move on.
  3. Record it as a one-off and wait for more reports.
  4. Fix the cause if one is found, and add the case (input, output, correct answer, version) to the regression set so that any later change is tested against it.
A real failure with a known correct answer is the most valuable test case there is. Fixing by hand or adding a prompt rule without a test means the mistake can silently return after the next model or prompt change.
