Security Reviews for AI Systems

Before a customer lets your system touch their data, their security team will interrogate it. This chapter explains who is asking and why, groups the questions they ask, shows how each AI-specific risk becomes a control with evidence, and builds the evidence pack that answers them in days instead of months.

Why this matters

The security review is the gate most likely to stop a good project, and the one engineers most often meet for the first time after the prototype is done. A review that starts late costs a quarter; one that starts in discovery runs in parallel with the build. The reviewers are not obstacles. Their job is to put a name against a risk the organisation is about to accept, and your job is to make that decision easy to take with true evidence. After this chapter you will be able to predict the seven families of questions, map each AI-specific risk to a control and a document, produce a data-flow inventory and a traceability matrix, report red-team results without overclaiming, answer a questionnaire honestly, and handle findings so they close instead of linger.

Who is asking, and what they are deciding

A security review (also called a vendor risk assessment or third-party risk review) is the customer's process for deciding whether a supplier's system may handle their data and connect to their environment. The people involved vary: a security architect who reads your design, a third-party-risk analyst who processes your questionnaire, a privacy or data-protection officer, sometimes a legal counsel and a business owner who must sign the risk acceptance. What they are deciding is not "is this perfect?", which nobody can prove, but "do we understand the risks, are the controls proportionate, and is somebody accountable for what remains?" That last part, the residual risk that remains after controls, must be accepted by a named person on the customer's side. Your evidence is what lets them do it.

The paperwork you will meet falls into a handful of types.

  • A security questionnaire. Often hundreds of questions, from a customer's own template or a shared standard. Two widely used standards are the SIG questionnaire from Shared Assessments and the CAIQ from the Cloud Security Alliance; both have added AI content (details change, so check the current versions).
  • An architecture review. A conversation (and diagrams) in which a security architect walks through data flows, trust boundaries, authentication and failure modes. It is the highest-value meeting. See secure design reviews for the other side of the table.
  • Assurance documents. Independent attestations about your organisation or your cloud provider's: a SOC 2 Type II report (an auditor's opinion on controls operating over a period), an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate, and increasingly an ISO/IEC 42001 certificate for an AI management system. Be exact about what you hold; do not imply a certification you lack.
  • Contract schedules. A data processing agreement (DPA), a list of sub-processors (every third party that touches the data, including the model provider), breach-notification terms and audit rights.
  • Testing. A penetration test (authorised attackers probe the running system) by you or by the customer, and for AI systems a red-team exercise aimed at the model-specific attacks (red teaming).
  • The customer's internal AI policy. Many organisations now have one, listing what is forbidden outright (for example, training on customer data, or personal data in free-text prompts). Ask for it in the first week.

The seven families of questions

Questionnaires look endless, but they cluster. If you can answer these seven families with evidence, you have answered 90% of what any particular form asks (a rule of thumb, not a measured figure). The AI-specific content sits mostly in the third and fourth families.

FamilyTypical questionsEvidence that answers them
1 DataWhat data do you receive? Where is it stored and processed? Who are the sub-processors? How long is it kept, and how is it deleted? Is any of it used to train a model?Data-flow inventory; DPA; sub-processor list; retention and deletion statement; the model provider's data terms
2 Access and identityHow do users authenticate? How is access to data limited per user and per tenant? Who on your staff can see customer data, and how is that access approved and logged?SSO and role design; permission tests; staff-access policy; audit-log sample (authorisation, OAuth, multi-tenancy)
3 Model and prompt risksHow do you defend against prompt injection and data leakage through the model? What can the model do on its own? How is output validated before it is used?Threat model; red-team report; tool-permission configuration; output-validation tests
4 Quality and safetyHow do you know it is accurate? How do you detect and handle wrong or harmful output? Is there human oversight?Evaluation report with intervals; guardrail tests; oversight design (evaluation plans)
5 Software supply chainWhat third-party code and models do you ship? How are vulnerabilities found and patched? Is there a software bill of materials?SBOM; dependency scanning output; patch policy; weight provenance (restricted environments)
6 Operations and resilienceHow is the system monitored? What is your incident response and notification time? What are your backup, recovery and change-management processes?Incident plan and contact tree; runbooks; backup tests; change log (production gates)
7 Governance and complianceWhich regulations apply, and how do you comply? Do you hold SOC 2, ISO 27001 or ISO 42001? Who is accountable for AI risk?Certificates (as held); compliance mapping; named owner; model card and risk register

Note what is not on the list: no one asks you to prove the model is incapable of ever misbehaving. They ask how you limit what happens when it does. That framing, assume the model can be fooled or wrong, and design so that this is survivable, is the heart of the AI-specific answers.

AI-specific risks, turned into controls

The injection, architectural defence and agent security chapters teach the attacks and defences in depth; do not re-teach them in a review. What the reviewer wants is a one-page mapping from each risk to a concrete control in your system. The OWASP list is a convenient spine because reviewers know it. Here is how the answers usually run.

RiskWhat the reviewer is really askingThe strongest kind of answer
Prompt injection (LLM01)What happens when hostile text in a document or email gives the model instructions?Structural limits, not a "we told the model not to": untrusted text cannot trigger consequential actions without approval; tools have narrow permissions; results tested with a red-team set
Sensitive information disclosure (LLM02)Can one user obtain another user's data through the model?Permission filters applied at retrieval, before the model sees text; tests that try cross-user reads (filters and permissions)
Supply chain (LLM03)Where did the model, the libraries and the plugins come from?Pinned versions, SBOM, hashed weights from a recorded origin, a review step for new tools (supply chain)
Data and model poisoning (LLM04)Can an attacker plant data that changes behaviour later?Who may write to the knowledge base or fine-tuning data; review and provenance of those writes
Improper output handling (LLM05)Is model output ever executed, rendered or used as a query unchecked?Output treated as untrusted input: schema validation, escaping, no direct execution (injection)
Excessive agency (LLM06)What can the system do, and who authorised it?An inventory of tools with permissions, least privilege, human approval for irreversible actions, spend and rate limits
System prompt leakage (LLM07)Is anything secret in the prompt?No credentials or sensitive logic in prompts; assume the prompt can be extracted
Vector and embedding weaknesses (LLM08)Can the index leak across tenants or be poisoned?Per-tenant indexes or enforced filters; write controls; deletion that reaches the index (approximate nearest-neighbour indexes)
Misinformation (LLM09)What is the harm if it is confidently wrong?Evaluation with intervals; citations; abstention; human oversight sized to the harm (grounding and abstention)
Unbounded consumption (LLM10)Can a user or a loop run up cost or take the service down?Token caps, step limits, rate limits per user, budget alerts (cost guardrails, abuse and denial of service)

Common misconception

"Our model provider is SOC 2 certified, so the security review is mostly done." The provider's report covers the provider's own service. It says nothing about how your application handles permissions, what it logs, which tools it exposes to the model, or what happens when it retrieves a hostile document. Responsibility is shared: the provider secures the model service, and you secure everything you built around it. Reviewers know this, and answering a question about your system with a link to someone else's certificate is one of the quickest ways to lose their confidence. Cite the provider's attestation for what it covers, and supply your own evidence for the rest.

The evidence pack

An evidence pack is a folder, prepared in advance and kept current, that holds the documents that answer the seven families. Teams that have one answer a questionnaire by pasting and linking, and meet an architect with the diagrams open. Teams that do not spend weeks assembling it under pressure, and give inconsistent answers. A good pack contains the following.

  1. A one-page system description: purpose, users, data classes handled, deployment shape, and what the system can and cannot do.
  2. Architecture and data-flow diagrams with trust boundaries marked, and a data-flow inventory listing each flow.
  3. A threat model (threat modelling) naming assets, attackers and the AI-specific attack paths.
  4. A traceability matrix from each risk to a control to a piece of evidence.
  5. Test evidence: evaluation report, red-team results, permission tests, load test.
  6. Operational documents: incident response plan with notification times, runbooks, backup and recovery tests, change process, a log sample showing what is recorded and what is redacted.
  7. Supply-chain records: SBOM, pinned model versions and hashes, sub-processor list.
  8. Contract and compliance items: DPA, data terms of every provider, certificates you hold (with scope and dates), and the mapping to regulations that apply.
  9. A model card and a residual-risk register: known limitations, the risks you did not eliminate, the owner of each, and the mitigation plan with dates.

The data-flow diagram and inventory

The most useful single artefact is a data-flow diagram in which every arrow is numbered and every arrow that crosses a boundary is obvious. The system below reads customer emails, retrieves from a knowledge base, asks a model to draft a reply and opens a ticket. Six flows, three boundaries.

The diagram becomes a checkable artefact when each flow has a row stating what data it carries, whether it leaves the boundary, where it lands, how long it is kept, whether a data processing agreement covers it and whether the recipient may train on it. The script applies the rules a reviewer would apply to each row.

# A data-flow inventory, checked against the rules a customer's security team applies.
ALLOWED_REGIONS = {"eu-west", "customer-dc"}
MAX_RETENTION_DAYS = 30
FLOWS = [  # name, from, to, data, leaves customer boundary, encrypted, region, retention days, DPA, trains on data
    ("F1 email in",         "mail gateway",  "workflow service", "customer email (personal data)", False, True,  "customer-dc", 0,  True,  False),
    ("F2 retrieval",        "workflow",      "vector index",     "knowledge-base passages",        False, True,  "customer-dc", 0,  True,  False),
    ("F3 model call",       "workflow",      "model provider",   "email + passages (personal data)", True, True,  "eu-west",     30, True,  False),
    ("F4 ticket write",     "workflow",      "ticket system",    "draft + customer id",            False, True,  "customer-dc", 0,  True,  False),
    ("F5 traces",           "workflow",      "observability SaaS","full prompts and replies",       True,  True,  "us-east",     90, False, False),
    ("F6 error reports",    "workflow",      "crash reporter",   "stack traces with inputs",       True,  False, "us-east",     365,False, True),
]
print(f"{'flow':18s} {'leaves':>6s}  findings")
total = 0
for name, src, dst, data, leaves, enc, region, days, dpa, trains in FLOWS:
    problems = []
    if not enc: problems.append("not encrypted in transit")
    if leaves:
        if "personal" in data or "prompts" in data or "inputs" in data:
            if not dpa: problems.append("personal data, no data processing agreement")
        if region not in ALLOWED_REGIONS: problems.append(f"region {region} not approved")
        if days > MAX_RETENTION_DAYS: problems.append(f"retention {days} d > {MAX_RETENTION_DAYS} d")
        if trains: problems.append("provider may train on the data")
    total += len(problems)
    print(f"{name:18s} {'yes' if leaves else 'no':>6s}  {'; '.join(problems) if problems else 'clean'}")
print(f"\n{total} findings across {len(FLOWS)} flows")

8 findings across 6 flows

The model call, F3, is clean because the team negotiated for it. The two findings are on the flows that nobody designed: the observability service receives full prompts (personal data) with a 90-day retention in a region not on the approved list and no agreement in place, and the crash reporter receives stack traces that embed the customer's inputs, unencrypted, retained for a year, with a clause that allows training. It is common to find that the riskiest data flow in an AI system is the logging, not the model. The fixes are mundane: redact before sending, shorten retention, choose an approved region, sign the agreement, or turn the tool off. Finding them yourself, before the reviewer does, changes the tone of the whole review. The privacy and data handling chapter explains redaction, and Lab 3 builds it.

The traceability matrix

A traceability matrix connects each risk to the control that addresses it and to the document that proves the control exists and works. Its virtue is that a gap becomes visible as an empty cell.

The same idea as code makes gaps countable. The script below holds the ten OWASP risks for the email assistant with the control and the evidence file for each, checks which evidence files exist in the pack, and lists gaps by severity.

Reporting test results without overclaiming

"We ran 60 injection attacks and none succeeded" is a statement about 60 attacks, not about all attacks. The honest summary gives the number of attempts, the number of successes and an upper bound on the true rate, as in statistics for evals. The script prints the 95% upper bound for several outcomes and the rule of three: with no successes in n tries, the true rate could still be as high as about 3/n.

# How to report "we tested and saw few or no successes". Wilson upper bound and the rule of three.
import math

def wilson_upper(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c + h

print(f"{'attacks':>8s} {'succeeded':>10s} {'observed':>9s} {'95% upper bound':>16s}")
for n, k in ((60, 0), (60, 2), (300, 0), (300, 6), (1200, 0), (1200, 24)):
    print(f"{n:8d} {k:10d} {k/n:9.1%} {wilson_upper(k, n):16.1%}")
print("\nrule of three: with 0 successes in n tries, the 95% upper bound is about 3/n")
for n in (60, 300, 1200):
    print(f"  n = {n:4d}: 3/n = {3/n:5.1%}")

Sixty clean attempts leave room for a rate of about 6%, which no one should call "secure". Twelve hundred leave room for 0.3%. Put differently, the report should say "0 of 1,200 attacks from our red-team set succeeded; the true rate is probably below 0.3% for attacks like these", and name the attack families covered, the ones not covered, and the date. Attackers adapt, so frame red-team results as a measurement of a set, repeated on every release, and keep the set growing from real incidents (red teaming).

Answering honestly, and handling what you find

The tone of your answers matters as much as their content. Reviewers read hundreds of vendor answers, and they quickly learn to distinguish a specific, evidenced reply from boilerplate.

  • Answer the question that was asked, in the first sentence: "Yes", "No", "Partially: X is done, Y is planned for the 14th of March". Then give the evidence and a link.
  • Never claim a certification, control or test you do not have. A false "yes" is discovered at the worst time (an incident, an audit) and ends the relationship. A truthful "no, and here is our compensating control" is routine.
  • Be precise about scope. "SOC 2 Type II" covers specific systems for a period; say which. "Encrypted" should say in transit, at rest, and with whose keys.
  • Mark shared responsibility. State what the model or cloud provider covers (with their attestation) and what you cover (with your evidence).
  • Keep an answer library with an owner, a date and a version for each answer, so that the third customer gets the same truth as the first, and a change in the system triggers an update.
  • Treat "not applicable" as a claim that needs a reason in a sentence.

Findings, severity and risk acceptance

The review will produce findings. That is a success of the process, not a failure of yours. Handle each in the same way: give it a severity agreed with the customer (high findings block launch; medium findings need a dated plan; low findings are tracked), a fix or compensating control, an owner and a date. For a risk that cannot be removed, record a risk acceptance: a written decision, signed by a named person with authority on the customer's side, saying that they understand the risk, the mitigations and what would trigger a review. Do not accept risk on the customer's behalf, and do not leave it unsaid. The residual-risk register is the document that carries these across the life of the system, and the incident and postmortem processes feed it.

Analogy: a building inspection

Before an occupancy certificate is issued, an inspector walks through a new building with a checklist and the architect's drawings. They look at the exits, the wiring, the fire doors and the load-bearing walls, and they want paperwork: the structural engineer's calculations and the supplier's test certificates. A prepared builder arrives with a folder and a list of the three things she knows are not finished, with dates. An unprepared one is surprised by every question.

Where it breaks down: a building does not change after the inspection, while an AI system changes whenever a prompt, a model version or a connected tool changes. The review is therefore not a gate passed once; the evidence pack has to be re-issued, or at least re-confirmed, with each significant release, and the customer should know the trigger conditions in advance.

Timing: start in discovery

The security review has a long lead time and most of its inputs exist early. The data classes, the hosting shape and the model provider are decided in discovery, and the data-flow sketch costs an afternoon. Starting then lets the customer's team ask for what they need while you can still change the design cheaply.

These are the blockers to look for in the first week, because each takes the longest to unblock: the model provider's data terms (training, retention, region, sub-processors), your own logging and observability tools (as the data-flow script showed), service accounts with broad access that the pilot used, secrets stored in prompts or notebooks, the customer's rules on personal data in prompts, and access for a penetration test inside their environment. In the New York City chatbot case the failure was not a hack but unchecked output, a reminder that reviewers worry about what the system says as well as what can be taken from it.

Systems connection

A security review of an AI system is a secure design review with a few extra rows. The assets, boundaries and attackers come from threat modelling; the access questions from authentication and authorisation; the input-handling questions from injection (with the model as one more untrusted interpreter); and the data questions from data protection and privacy. The notification and logging questions are detection and response. What is new is only where the trust boundary falls: the model reads text that an attacker can write.

Key takeaways

  • The security team is deciding who accepts which residual risk. Make that decision easy with true evidence, and start in discovery so the review runs beside the build.
  • Questions fall into seven families: data, access, model and prompt risks, quality and safety, supply chain, operations, governance. Prepare once, answer many times.
  • Turn each AI-specific risk into a structural control you can test (limits on what the system can do), then into an evidence document. The OWASP LLM Top 10 is a handy spine.
  • The riskiest data flow is often logging or error reporting, not the model. Number every arrow in the data-flow diagram, inventory it, and check it against the rules.
  • Report tests with counts and an upper bound, not "no attack succeeded". Zero in 60 attempts still allows a rate near 6%.
  • Answer yes, no or partially first, never overclaim, mark shared responsibility, and handle findings with severity, owner, date and a signed risk acceptance.

Check your understanding

A questionnaire asks, "Do you hold SOC 2 Type II certification?" Your company has not been audited, but your cloud provider has. What is the best answer?

  1. "Yes", and attach the provider's report.
  2. "Yes, through our cloud provider", without further detail.
  3. "No. Our hosting provider holds a SOC 2 Type II report, attached for the infrastructure layer; for our application we provide these other controls and evidence, and our audit is planned for [date]."
  4. Leave it blank.
The truthful answer states the fact, gives the provider's attestation for what it covers, and supplies your own evidence for the rest. A "yes" based on the provider's report misstates your scope, and a blank invites the assumption that you hide something.

In the chapter's data-flow inventory, F5 (full prompts to an observability service) is flagged and F3 (the model call) is clean. What is the likeliest reason?

  1. The model call was deliberately negotiated (agreement, region, retention) while the monitoring tool was added without a data review.
  2. Monitoring tools are inherently insecure.
  3. Model providers never retain data.
  4. The inventory is wrong, because prompts are not personal data.
Teams think about the model call because it is the headline risk, and install logging and error tools by default. Those tools receive the same prompts. Monitoring tools are fine if the data terms, region and retention are agreed, and prompts containing customer emails are personal data.

A red-team run of 60 injection attempts has no successes. A colleague writes "the system is immune to prompt injection". What is the most accurate rewording?

  1. "The system is secure against all injection."
  2. "The success rate is exactly 0%."
  3. "We tested too few attacks to say anything."
  4. "0 of 60 attacks from our test set succeeded, which allows a true rate up to about 5 to 6% for attacks like these; structural limits on tools also bound the damage."
The observed 0 of 60 is a fact about the sample. A 95% upper bound is near 6%, so the data cannot rule out a rate that high. The data do say something useful, so "nothing" is too strong, and "immune" or "exactly 0%" claims more than the evidence supports.

A customer's architect asks how you prevent a hostile PDF from making your assistant send data to an attacker. Which answer is strongest?

  1. "The system prompt tells the model never to follow instructions inside documents."
  2. "Document text cannot trigger outbound actions: the assistant has no tool that can send data externally, the ticket tool is create-only and approved by a person, and our red-team set of 300 hostile documents is rerun on every release."
  3. "We use a leading model that is trained to resist injection."
  4. "Attacks like that are unlikely."
A structural control, which removes the capability the attacker needs, holds even when the model is fooled, and it can be tested. Instructions to the model and model training reduce risk but can be bypassed, and "unlikely" is not an argument.

When is the best time to send the customer's security team your data-flow sketch and ask for their questionnaire and AI policy?

  1. After the pilot succeeds, so they see a finished system.
  2. Just before launch, to avoid delays from changing requirements.
  3. In discovery, and update it as the design firms up, so the review runs in parallel with the build.
  4. Only if the customer asks.
Early contact surfaces blockers (data terms, regions, forbidden practices) while they are cheap to design around, and review steps proceed in parallel. Waiting turns every finding into rework on a built system and puts the longest-lead item last on the critical path.

The review finds that cross-tenant reads are possible through the retrieval index, and fixing it properly will take six weeks. The customer wants to go live in two. What should you do?

  1. Go live and fix it later without mentioning it.
  2. Mark it "not applicable" to keep the schedule.
  3. Accept the risk yourself on the customer's behalf.
  4. Treat it as a high-severity finding that blocks launch; offer options such as launching to a single tenant with no cross-tenant exposure or a temporary compensating control; and, if the customer still wants an earlier date for a lower-risk scope, record a risk acceptance signed by their accountable owner.
A cross-tenant data leak is a serious class of defect, and the decision to live with any part of it belongs to the customer's accountable person, informed and in writing. Hiding it, relabelling it or deciding for them leaves both sides exposed when it is discovered.
