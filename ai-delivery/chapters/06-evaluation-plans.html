Evaluation Plans Customers Trust

A score is only as persuasive as the process that produced it. This chapter is about the evaluation plan: a short document, agreed before the first run, that fixes what the customer's own work looks like as a gold set, which metrics count, how the current process is measured on the same cases, and what result will be accepted, rejected or called inconclusive.

Why this matters

The prototype told you which assumptions survive. Now the customer's sponsor, their risk team and perhaps a regulator must decide whether to rely on the system, and they will not take your word for it. They will believe a number if they helped define it, if it was measured on their own cases, if it is set beside what they do today, and if the rule for "good enough" was written before anyone knew the result. Evaluation is also where most disputes in AI projects are settled or inflamed: a disagreement about labels discovered after a bad score looks like an excuse, and the same disagreement agreed in week two is an ordinary piece of work. After this chapter you will be able to write a one-page evaluation plan, build a gold set from the customer's own work with a labelling procedure, choose and weight metrics, measure the current process as a fair baseline, compare the two with paired statistics, and state acceptance as a rule on an interval that returns accept, reject or "not enough evidence".

What an evaluation plan is

An evaluation plan is a short document, signed by both sides before the evaluation is run, that says how the system will be judged. The word plan matters more than evaluation. The evaluation techniques you met in the evals course (error analysis, harnesses, judges, statistics) are the tools; the plan is the agreement about which tools will decide the outcome. Think of it as the acceptance-test section of a contract, with the numbers filled in.

It has five parts, drawn in the figure. The order is deliberate, and the first four are fixed before the system is run on the test cases.

Part 1, the decision, comes first because it determines how much evidence is enough. "Should we replace the first-line check?" needs more proof than "should we run a four-week pilot with ten adjusters?". Say which decision the evaluation feeds, who takes it, and by when. Parts 2 to 4 are the rest of this chapter. Part 5 is short but strict: freeze the model, prompt, settings and retrieval index; run once on the sealed set; publish every failure alongside the score.

The gold set: the customer's own work

A gold set (also called a golden set, ground-truth set or test set) is a collection of real inputs, each paired with the correct output as decided by people the customer trusts. It is the single most valuable artefact in an applied AI project, because it turns an argument about whether the system is good into a measurement, and because it stays useful long after your involvement ends.

It must come from the customer's own work. Public benchmarks measure something else: different documents, different conventions, a different mix of easy and hard. A model's rank on a public leaderboard has uncertain relevance to a bank's loan files or a council's planning letters (reading leaderboards critically goes into why). The customer's own historical cases carry their vocabulary, their formatting quirks, their scanned fax from 2014, and the awkward edge cases that keep their experts up at night.

Where the cases come from

There are three honest sources, and the best sets mix them.

  • Historical cases with a known outcome. Claims that were investigated and settled, contracts that a lawyer reviewed, tickets that were resolved. The outcome is a label that existed before your project, produced by the real process. Beware that it may be noisy (a hurried decision, an unreviewed one) and may reflect past policy rather than current policy.
  • Fresh cases labelled by experts for this purpose. Slower, but you control the procedure. This is where the techniques of the expert knowledge chapter pay off.
  • Deliberately hard cases. The ones the experts name when you ask "what would worry you?". They are rare in a random sample and decisive for trust, so oversample them and report them as their own slice.

Sample the ordinary cases at random from a defined period, so the set represents the real mix, not the cases someone remembered. Then stratify: make sure every document type, language, channel and customer segment the system will meet appears often enough to be measured. Remove duplicates and near-duplicates. Prefer recent cases, because the future looks more like last month than like 2019. And check permission before copying anything: the gold set is a copy of customer data with extra labels, so it falls under the same rules as the rest. See data protection and privacy and data handling for AI.

How many cases?

The honest answer is "enough that the interval around the result is narrower than the decision needs", and you can calculate it. The uncertainty in a measured accuracy is described by a confidence interval: a range which, if the measurement were repeated on fresh random cases many times, would contain the true accuracy about 95% of the time. For accuracy on a proportion of right and wrong answers, a good interval is the Wilson interval (E. B. Wilson, 1927), which behaves sensibly near 0% and 100%, unlike the textbook formula; Brown, Cai and DasGupta (2001) compare the options. The statistics for evals chapter derives it; here is what it implies in practice.

# How wide is the uncertainty on a measured accuracy? Wilson interval, standard library.
import math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

print("observed accuracy 90%: how the 95% interval narrows with more cases")
print(f"{'cases':>6s} {'correct':>8s}   {'interval':>14s} {'half-width':>11s}")
for n in (25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800):
    k = round(0.90 * n)
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    print(f"{n:6d} {k:8d}   {lo:6.1%} to {hi:5.1%} {(hi - lo) / 2:10.1%}")

print("\ncases needed for a given half-width (planning at p = 0.90, z = 1.96)")
for h in (0.10, 0.05, 0.03, 0.02):
    n = math.ceil(1.96**2 * 0.9 * 0.1 / h**2)
    print(f"  +/- {h:.0%}: about {n} cases")

print("\nthe same, for a slice that is only 8% of the gold set of 200 (16 cases):")
lo, hi = wilson(14, 16)
print(f"  14/16 correct = 88%, interval {lo:.0%} to {hi:.0%}")

Two lessons fall out. First, precision is expensive: halving the interval needs about four times the cases, because the width shrinks with the square root of the count. A gold set of 100 can say "roughly 83% to 94%", and no cleverness changes that. Second, slices are fragile. Every slice you want to report separately (scanned documents, one language, one region) is its own small sample; with 16 cases the interval for an 88% result runs from 64% to 97%, which supports no conclusion either way. Decide up front which slices are gates (they must meet a bar, and so need enough cases) and which are only descriptions (reported with their intervals and no verdict).

Labels, agreement and the ceiling on accuracy

A label is a human judgement, and humans disagree. Write a labelling procedure before anyone labels: the instructions with examples, who labels, whether two people label each case independently, and how disagreements are settled (an adjudicator, usually the most senior expert, who sees both answers and decides). Measure agreement between independent labellers with a statistic that corrects for chance, such as Cohen's kappa, which the expert knowledge chapter covered and the human labels chapter details. Agreement sets a ceiling: if two senior adjusters agree on 88% of cases, then a system "scoring 95% against one adjuster" is claiming to be more consistent with that adjuster than the other adjuster is. Treat the label noise as a fact to report, not an embarrassment, and consider scoring against an adjudicated consensus.

Two rules protect the labels from bias. Label before the model runs, and never show the labeller the model's answer. People anchor on a suggestion and agree with it more than they would have chosen it, which makes the system look better and makes the gold set quietly depend on the system it judges. And seal a test half. Split the gold set into a development part, used freely while you build, and a test part that nobody consults until the final run. Many teams give custody of the test half to the customer, who releases the cases only when the configuration is frozen. This is the same device a data-science competition uses with its private leaderboard, and it solves the same problem: the more you look at a set while tuning, the less it measures anything but your tuning (the contamination chapter has the details).

Finally, version the gold set and keep a change log. Labels do turn out to be wrong, and fixing them is right. But a correction made after seeing results must say who made it, why, and what the score was before and after, or the log will look like score management. The signed plan should say how label disputes are handled: for instance, "a disputed label is reviewed by the adjudicator blind to the model's output, and the change is logged".

Common misconception

"The model scores 92% on a respected public benchmark, so it will do well on your documents." A benchmark measures how a model does on that benchmark's inputs, which were assembled by someone else for another purpose and may even have leaked into the training data. Customer trust comes from a measurement on the customer's own cases with their own definition of correct. A public score is a reasonable reason to shortlist a model (see Choosing Models) and no reason to accept one.

Metrics the customer agrees to

A metric is a promise about what will be counted. Choose it by starting from the decision the customer takes, then asking what a wrong answer costs. Accuracy alone treats every error alike, and customers do not. A claims handler who wrongly denies a valid claim causes a complaint and some rework; one who wrongly approves a claim pays out money that cannot be recovered; one who fails to refer a suspicious claim to a specialist may cause both. Three habits turn that into a metric both sides can sign.

Name the error types. List the ways the output can be wrong, in the customer's words, and agree which are critical errors: the kinds that must stay below a hard limit whatever the average does, because one of them is a serious incident, not a rounding error. Accuracy is then reported next to the critical-error rate, which has its own bar and its own interval. This is the same reasoning as the three-zone rule in the previous chapter, where the false approvals decided the outcome.

Weight them by cost. Where the customer can put figures on the error types, an expected cost per item is a more honest summary than accuracy, and it connects directly to the business case in cost models and return on investment. The figures below are illustrative assumptions, in the way a real plan would carry the customer's own estimates, and the two confusion matrices are invented to make a point.

# Accuracy is not the whole story: weight each kind of error by what it costs.
# Rows = the senior panel's decision (gold), columns = what the system decided.
# Confusion counts and costs are ILLUSTRATIVE assumptions, not real data.
LABELS = ["covered", "not_covered", "refer"]
COST = {  # EUR per claim, [gold][decision]
    "covered":     {"covered": 0,    "not_covered": 350,  "refer": 40},   # wrongly denied: complaint + rework
    "not_covered": {"covered": 1800, "not_covered": 0,    "refer": 40},   # wrongly paid: money out the door
    "refer":       {"covered": 1800, "not_covered": 600,  "refer": 0},    # skipped a case needing a specialist
}
SYSTEMS = {
    "model":           {"covered": [106, 2, 2], "not_covered": [4, 55, 1], "refer": [1, 1, 28]},
    "current process": {"covered": [100, 6, 4], "not_covered": [1, 56, 3], "refer": [1, 3, 26]},
}
for name, table in SYSTEMS.items():
    n = sum(sum(r) for r in table.values())
    right = sum(table[g][i] for i, g in enumerate(LABELS))
    cost = sum(table[g][i] * COST[g][d] for g in LABELS for i, d in enumerate(LABELS))
    wrong_pay = sum(table[g][0] for g in ("not_covered", "refer"))
    print(f"{name:16s} accuracy {right/n:5.1%}   wrongly approved {wrong_pay:2d} ({wrong_pay/n:4.1%})   "
          f"expected cost per claim {cost/n:6.2f} EUR")
model            accuracy 94.5%   wrongly approved  5 (2.5%)   expected cost per claim  52.10 EUR
current process  accuracy 91.0%   wrongly approved  2 (1.0%)   expected cost per claim  38.90 EUR

On accuracy the model wins by 3.5 points. On cost it loses by about 13 euros a claim, because its few mistakes are the expensive kind. Neither number is "the truth"; the point is that the customer, not you, decides which one governs, and that decision is written in the plan before any matrix exists. It may lead to a different design (for example, let the model auto-handle only the cases it marks confident, and send the rest to a person), and the plan should allow for that: report coverage (the share of items the system handles) beside the accuracy on those items.

Define correctness for generated text with a rubric. When the output is a paragraph and not a label, the plan needs a short list of yes-or-no criteria a reviewer can apply: every number matches the source, no claim is unsupported, nothing material is omitted, the tone follows house style. Binary criteria give far more consistent grading than a 1 to 5 score. If an LLM will apply the rubric at scale, the plan must also say how the judge was checked against human graders on a sample first (see LLM-as-judge); an unvalidated judge is an opinion with a model's confidence.

Keep process measures separate from quality measures. Minutes saved per claim, the share of suggestions adjusters accept, and the number of escalations are valuable, and they come from a pilot with real users, not from a gold set. Do not average them into one score. The plan can list them as things to be measured later, with their own owners.

The comparison customers care about: the current process

"Is it accurate?" is rarely the real question. The sponsor is asking "is this better than what we do now?", and the current process is not perfect. First-line staff are hurried, tired and inconsistent, as the expert-knowledge chapter showed, and yet the usual evaluation scores the model against a perfect answer key and leaves the human process unscored. That is unfair in both directions. If the model is held to 100% it may be rejected for failing to beat a standard no human meets; if the customer imagines the human process is flawless, no model can look good. The honest design measures both on the same cases.

  1. Pick the baseline(s). At minimum the current process as actually practised. Often also a trivially simple alternative (a keyword rule, last year's template) so you can show the model beats something cheap, as the feasibility ladder demanded.
  2. Run the baseline on the gold items. For a human process, have the people who normally do the job decide each case under normal conditions, from the same inputs, without seeing the answer key. Use staff who do not know which cases are in the gold set if you can, and make sure nobody who produced a label also acts as the baseline on it.
  3. Score everything against the same labels with the same metrics, including cost-weighted ones.
  4. Compare in pairs. The two systems are tested on the same items, which makes the comparison far more sensitive than comparing two separate averages.

The paired comparison is worth a moment, because it is a technique customers' analysts recognise and it removes an argument. Suppose both the model and the current process are run on 200 gold cases. Each case falls into one of four cells.

The next script builds a simulated set of 200 outcomes (the data are generated, with a fixed seed, to show the method; they are not a real evaluation) and compares the model with the current process three ways: an interval for the difference that ignores pairing, a paired bootstrap that resamples whole items, and an exact sign test on the disagreements.

# Compare the model with the current process on the SAME gold items.
# The 200 outcomes below are SIMULATED (seeded) to show the method; they are not real data.
import math, random

random.seed(7)
N = 200
# Each item is easy (70%) or hard (30%). Hard items trip both systems more often.
items = []
for _ in range(N):
    hard = random.random() < 0.30
    p_model = 0.76 if hard else 0.995
    p_human = 0.66 if hard else 0.975
    items.append((random.random() < p_model, random.random() < p_human))

m = sum(a for a, _ in items); h = sum(b for _, b in items)
both = sum(a and b for a, b in items)
b_only = sum(a and not b for a, b in items)      # model right, current process wrong
c_only = sum(b and not a for a, b in items)      # current process right, model wrong
neither = N - both - b_only - c_only
print(f"model right {m}/{N} = {m/N:.1%}   current process right {h}/{N} = {h/N:.1%}")
print(f"both right {both}, model only {b_only}, process only {c_only}, neither {neither}")

diff = (m - h) / N
se_unpaired = math.sqrt((m/N)*(1-m/N)/N + (h/N)*(1-h/N)/N)
print(f"\ndifference (model - process): {diff:+.1%}")
print(f"  unpaired 95% interval: {diff - 1.96*se_unpaired:+.1%} to {diff + 1.96*se_unpaired:+.1%}")

# Paired bootstrap: resample whole items, keeping each item's two outcomes together.
rng = random.Random(11)
boots = []
for _ in range(4000):
    s = [items[rng.randrange(N)] for _ in range(N)]
    boots.append(sum(a for a, _ in s)/N - sum(b for _, b in s)/N)
boots.sort()
lo, hi = boots[int(0.025*4000)], boots[int(0.975*4000)]
print(f"  paired   95% interval: {lo:+.1%} to {hi:+.1%}")

# Exact sign test on the disagreements only: if the systems were equally good,
# each disagreement would favour either side with probability 1/2.
d = b_only + c_only
k = max(b_only, c_only)
p_two = min(1.0, 2 * sum(math.comb(d, i) for i in range(k, d + 1)) / 2**d)
print(f"  sign test on the {d} disagreements ({b_only} vs {c_only}): two-sided p = {p_two:.3f}")

MARGIN = 0.03
print(f"\nnon-inferiority margin {MARGIN:.0%}: model is 'no worse' if the paired interval's low end is above {-MARGIN:+.0%}")
print("  non-inferior:", lo > -MARGIN, "  superior:", lo > 0)

Read the result as a statistician would, and as a sponsor needs it read. The model is ahead by 3.5 points, but the interval, about -1 to +8, includes zero, and the sign test (p = 0.21) cannot rule out that the two are equally good. So the data do not show the model is better. They do show something that may be what the customer actually needs: the model is no worse than the current process by more than 3 points, a conclusion called non-inferiority. For a system whose appeal is speed, cost or consistency, "at least as good as today, within an agreed margin" is often the right claim, and it needs a smaller sample than proving superiority. The margin (3 points here) is a business decision to be agreed in the plan, not a statistical one. Note too that pairing narrowed the interval only slightly in this example; it helps most when the two systems tend to fail on the same hard cases, and here they partly do.

One more fairness point. If the gold labels were produced by the same staff who form the current process, the process is being scored against its own answers and will look better than it is. Where possible, use a senior panel or independent adjudication for the labels, and say so in the plan.

Turning numbers into a verdict

Now the rule. A pass mark applied to a point estimate ("at least 90%") makes the verdict depend on luck: the same system can land at 88% on one sample and 92% on another. Apply the mark to the interval instead, and the rule has three outcomes, not two.

  • Accept if the lower end of the interval reaches the bar. Even on a pessimistic reading the system is good enough.
  • Reject if the upper end falls below the bar. Even on an optimistic reading it is not.
  • Not enough evidence otherwise. The honest answer is that the gold set is too small to say, and the plan states in advance what happens next (more cases up to an agreed limit, or a smaller scope).

The function is eight lines, and it also computes how many cases would settle the question if the observed rate held.

# A decision rule that uses the interval, not the point estimate.
import math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

def verdict(k, n, bar):
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    if lo >= bar:
        return "ACCEPT", lo, hi
    if hi < bar:
        return "REJECT", lo, hi
    return "NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE", lo, hi

def cases_to_settle(rate, bar, limit=5000):
    # if the observed rate held, how many cases until the interval clears the bar?
    for n in range(20, limit):
        k = round(rate * n)
        if wilson(k, n)[0] >= bar:
            return n
    return None

BAR = 0.90
print(f"acceptance rule: lower end of the 95% interval must reach {BAR:.0%}\n")
print(f"{'cases':>6s} {'correct':>8s} {'rate':>6s}   {'interval':>14s}   verdict")
for n, k in ((30, 28), (60, 56), (120, 113), (250, 235), (500, 470), (500, 430)):
    v, lo, hi = verdict(k, n, BAR)
    print(f"{n:6d} {k:8d} {k/n:6.1%}   {lo:5.1%} to {hi:5.1%}   {v}")

print("\nif accuracy stays at 94%, cases needed to clear a 90% bar:", cases_to_settle(0.94, 0.90))
print("if accuracy stays at 92%, cases needed to clear a 90% bar:", cases_to_settle(0.92, 0.90))

Step through

The same 94%, measured on more and more cases

  1. 50 cases, 47 right (94%). The 95% interval runs from about 84% to 98%. The point estimate is above the 90% bar, but the interval reaches well below it, so the plan's rule says: not enough evidence.
  2. 100 cases, 94 right. About 87.5% to 97%. Twice the cases, an interval about 30% narrower, and still straddling the bar.

All the gates, written down

The cost and latency gates connect to service-level objectives and to the model comparison in Choosing Models.

Analogy: a driving test

The examiner, the route type and the pass mark are fixed before you get in the car. The examiner records faults by category, and a serious fault such as running a red light fails the test whatever else you did well, which is a critical-error gate. Nobody grades you on how confident you felt, and the examiner does not change the pass mark halfway round because you seem nice. A pass says you are safe enough to be licensed, not that you will never have an accident, and a licence can be revoked.

Where it breaks down: candidates cannot see the test route in advance in the way an AI team can see its test set, so rehearsing the exam is not a risk for drivers. A system tuned on its test cases can "memorise the route", which is why the final run uses a sealed set. And a driving test is measured once on one day, whereas a deployed system faces a changing road and needs continuing monitoring.

Running it and reporting it

When the plan is signed, running it should be dull. Freeze the model version, the prompts, the retrieval index, the settings and the code, and record them (a commit hash and a configuration file will do). Ask for the sealed cases. Run them once, or, if the system is not deterministic, a fixed number of times, such as three, and report the spread; lowering the temperature reduces variation but does not guarantee identical outputs. Record every input, output, latency and cost so that any number in the report can be traced to a trace.

Then report in the order the sponsor will read it. First the verdict for each gate, using the plan's own words. Then the numbers with their intervals. Then, and this is what builds trust, the failures: a table of the wrong answers grouped by cause, with a handful of representative examples shown in full, and a sentence on what each group implies. The error analysis habit pays off here, because a customer who has seen what the system gets wrong will believe what it gets right. The kit that makes the run reproducible (the gold set with its labelling log, the scoring script, the frozen configuration, the plan itself) goes to the customer. In Lab 1 you will build exactly this kit from a brief and sample documents.

After sign-off

An accepted result is a statement about one frozen configuration on one set of cases at one time. Three events invalidate it, and the plan should say what happens for each. The system changes (a new model version, a prompt edit, a different retrieval index): rerun the gates on the sealed set, or on a refreshed one if the old set has been seen too often, and treat the change as a release. The world changes: new document formats, new policy wordings, a different customer mix; sample fresh production cases on a schedule, label them, and add them to the gold set, which is the loop in the improvement flywheel. The customer's standards change: a regulator tightens a rule or a new risk appears; revise the plan, with the same signatures as the first.

State of play, October 2026: what regulators already expect

Evaluation as described here is close to what supervisors ask for. In the United States, the Federal Reserve's supervisory letter SR 11-7 on model risk management (issued 4 April 2011, jointly with the OCC) describes model validation as conceptual soundness review, ongoing monitoring including benchmarking, and outcomes analysis comparing model results with actual outcomes, and asks for "effective challenge" by people independent of the builders. It predates modern language models but banks apply it to them; the finance chapter returns to it. In the European Union, Article 15 of the AI Act requires high-risk systems to declare their levels of accuracy and the relevant accuracy metrics in the instructions for use; the application timetable for the high-risk rules has been subject to change, which the feasibility chapter summarises, so check current dates. In both cases the evidence regulators want is the evidence a good plan already produces: stated metrics, independent data, a baseline, monitoring, and a record of changes.

Systems connection

An acceptance test with a stated tolerance is ordinary engineering: it is the test pyramid's top layer, with statistics because the output is a rate. The interval rule is the same reasoning as judging a canary release by its error rate with a confidence bound, as in deployment strategies, and the bar-plus-latency gates are service-level objectives from SLIs, SLOs and error budgets. A sealed test set is a held-out set in the sense of generalisation, and the leak rules are those of leakage. The paired comparison and the cost-weighted metric echo framing problems and choosing metrics. For the retrieval equivalent of a gold set, see building a test collection and building a domain benchmark.

Key takeaways

  • An evaluation plan is signed before the run and fixes five things: the decision, the gold set, the metrics, the pass rule and the run procedure. Disputes about labels settled in week two are work; discovered after a bad score they are excuses.
  • Build the gold set from the customer's own work: random cases from a defined period, stratified, with deliberately hard cases oversampled. Label before the model runs, never show the labeller the model's answer, and seal a test half.
  • Precision costs cases: the interval narrows with the square root of the count, and small slices have huge intervals. Declare which slices are gates and which are descriptions.
  • Name the error types, mark critical errors with their own bar, and weight by cost where the customer can. A more accurate system can cost more per item if its errors are the expensive kind.
  • Measure the current process on the same cases, compare in pairs, and consider non-inferiority with a margin the business agrees. Do not score humans against a perfect answer key while holding the model to it.
  • Apply the pass mark to the interval: accept if the lower end clears the bar, reject if the upper end falls below it, otherwise "not enough evidence" with a pre-agreed next step.
  • Report verdicts, numbers with intervals, and the failures; hand over the kit. Re-evaluate on any change to the system, the world or the customer's standards.

Check your understanding

A vendor's model scores 94% on 100 of the customer's cases, and the plan says "accept if accuracy is at least 90%". The sponsor wants to sign off. What does the interval-based rule say, and why?

  1. Accept, because 94% is above 90%.
  2. Reject, because 100 cases is too few for any decision.
  3. Not enough evidence: the 95% interval runs from roughly 87.5% to 97%, so a true accuracy below 90% is still plausible; more cases, or a lower bar agreed in advance, are needed.
  4. Accept, because accuracy above 90% on any sample proves the true value is above 90%.
The point estimate beats the bar, but the sampling uncertainty at n = 100 is about 6 points either side. The rule is applied to the lower end of the interval, which falls below 90%. Rejection would need the upper end to fall below the bar, and no sample size proves the true value from a single point estimate.

The team shows the expert labeller each case together with the model's answer and asks "is this right?". Labelling is faster and the score is 96%. What is the main problem?

  1. Anchoring: people tend to agree with a displayed suggestion, so labels depend on the model they are meant to judge and the score is inflated.
  2. The labeller will become tired.
  3. Nothing, because speed improves with assistance.
  4. The labels will be too strict.
Showing the answer turns an independent judgement into an approval, and humans approve more readily than they produce the same answer. Labels should be made blind to the model, with the comparison done afterwards. Fatigue and strictness are not the issue, and faster labelling is the symptom, not a benefit.

Model A has 95% accuracy and model B has 92%. The cost of a wrongly approved item is 40 times that of a wrongly rejected one, and A makes mostly wrongly approved errors while B makes mostly wrongly rejected ones. Which statement is best supported?

  1. A is better because accuracy is the standard metric.
  2. B is better because it is cheaper to run.
  3. They are equal because the sample is the same.
  4. B may be better for the customer: expected cost per item can favour the less accurate model if its errors are the cheap kind, so the cost-weighted metric should be agreed in the plan.
With very unequal error costs, a model that errs on the cheap side can cost less per item despite lower accuracy. Costs per run are not mentioned. Equal samples do not make equal systems. The right response is to agree the weighting with the customer in advance and compute both.

On 200 gold cases the model is right alone on 15, the current process is right alone on 8, both are right on 174 and both are wrong on 3. Why does a paired analysis use only the 23 disagreements to test for a difference?

  1. The other 177 cases were labelled less carefully.
  2. Cases where both systems agree cannot show which is better; under "equally good" each disagreement favours either side with probability one half, which gives an exact test.
  3. Discarding data always makes a test more powerful.
  4. The 23 cases are the hardest and so the most important by definition.
Where both are right or both wrong, the comparison has no information. Under the null that the systems are equally good, the 23 disagreements split as a fair coin, so 15 against 8 can be tested exactly (here p is about 0.21, not significant). The other options misdescribe why: labelling quality and difficulty are not the reason, and data is not discarded for power in general.

The customer's head of risk asks why the team plans to report a hard-case slice of 15 cases "with its interval but no verdict". What is the best answer?

  1. Because hard cases do not matter.
  2. Because slices cannot be measured.
  3. With only 15 cases the interval is very wide (a result of 13 right of 15 spans roughly 62% to 96%), so a verdict would be noise; the slice is shown for transparency, and a gate is reserved for slices with enough cases.
  4. Because the team does not want to report bad news.
The width of an interval at n = 15 means any pass or fail on that slice would mostly reflect luck. Hard cases matter a great deal, which is why they are reported, and the remedy for a small hard-case slice is to collect more of them. The point of pre-declaring gates is to avoid post hoc slice-fishing, not to hide results.

Three months after acceptance the provider releases a new model version and the team switches to it, with a modified prompt. Which action best respects the evaluation plan?

  1. Treat it as a release: rerun the gates on the sealed set (or a refreshed one), compare with the accepted configuration, and log the change.
  2. Keep the old score, because the accepted number still applies to the product's name.
  3. Skip testing because the new model is newer.
  4. Rebuild the whole gold set from scratch.
An accepted result describes one frozen configuration. A new model or prompt is a different system, so the gates are rerun and the change recorded. Reusing the old score confuses a product name with a measured configuration, "newer" is not evidence, and a full rebuild is rarely needed when the gold set remains representative.
