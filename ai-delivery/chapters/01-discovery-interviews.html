Discovery Interviews

Customers describe the solution they have imagined, not the problem they have. This chapter is about the conversation that gets from one to the other: asking about specific things that happened rather than opinions about things that might, listening for workarounds and numbers, talking to the right mix of people, and turning the notes into evidence you can defend.

Why this matters

Everything later in the course depends on the answer to "what is the real problem?". A beautifully evaluated system that solves the wrong problem is still a failed project, and the evaluation will not warn you, because you will have measured it against the wrong target. Discovery is also the cheapest stage: a week of conversations costs far less than a month of building. After this chapter you will be able to plan and run a customer interview, tell evidence from politeness in your own notes, put honest ranges on the value of a problem, and say what you have not yet learnt.

The request is not the problem

Suppose a claims team at an insurer tells you: "We need a chatbot over our policy documents." That sentence is a requested feature: a solution the customer has already chosen, usually the first one they thought of or saw demonstrated. It contains a hint about a problem, something is hard about finding answers in policy documents, but it hides nearly everything else: how often it is hard, for whom, what they do today, what a wrong answer costs, and why the documents are hard to use.

The real problem is the situation that causes the customer pain or cost, stated without reference to any particular solution. "Analysts lose about forty minutes on a policy lookup, and the answer sometimes differs depending on which PDF they open" is a problem statement. "We need a chatbot" is not. The distinction matters because different problems call for different solutions, and some of them are not AI at all.

Why simply asking does not work

The obvious way to learn what people need is to ask them what they want. It fails for three separate reasons, and it helps to keep them distinct because each calls for a different fix.

  • People cannot reliably report their own reasons. Nisbett and Wilson's review "Telling More Than We Can Know: Verbal Reports on Mental Processes" (1977) showed that people often give confident explanations of why they acted as they did that do not match the real causes. Asked "why is the process slow?", an expert gives a plausible story, not necessarily a true one. Chapter 3 returns to this for expert knowledge.
  • People are polite. If you describe your idea and ask whether it is good, the easiest response is yes. Compliments cost the speaker nothing and cost you the truth.
  • People cannot predict their own future behaviour. "Would you use this?" asks the customer to simulate a product that does not exist, in a future workload they cannot picture. The answer reflects mood and manners, not behaviour.

Rob Fitzpatrick's short book The Mom Test (2013) turned this into a working method. His name for the problem is bad data: compliments, fluff (generic claims such as "I usually" or hypotheticals such as "I would") and feature ideas the customer invents for you. His three rules are simple to state. Talk about their life instead of your idea. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future. Talk less and listen more. The title comes from the observation that even your mother could not lie to you if you asked about what she actually did last time instead of whether your business was a good idea.

Teresa Torres makes the same point from the product side in Continuous Discovery Habits (2021), recommending story-based interviews: ask for the story of a specific recent occasion, because a story has details that an opinion lacks and because details can be checked.

Analogy: a doctor taking a history

A patient arrives saying, "I need antibiotics." A good doctor does not hand them over, and does not argue either. She asks when it started, what it felt like, what they tried, what changed. The request tells her what the patient has been thinking; the history tells her what is wrong. She treats the second, and often the patient is relieved to learn that the thing they asked for would not have helped.

Where it breaks down: a body does not have politics. In a company, the person describing the problem may be protecting a team, a budget or their own reputation, and the person who pays is rarely the person who suffers. A doctor examines the patient; you also have to decide whose account to weight, which the people map below helps with.

Questions that produce evidence

You can sort any question you might ask along two axes: whether it is about their life (what they do, did, spent, tried) or about your idea, and whether it asks about specific past events or generic or future matters. Only one quadrant reliably gives you facts.

Commitment and advancement

Even a good conversation can end with warm words and no consequence. Fitzpatrick's remedy is to end every meeting by asking for something that costs the customer something. A commitment is a concrete next step: time (a second meeting), reputation (an introduction to a colleague), or money (a pilot budget). In AI delivery the currency is usually data and access: "Could you send me forty real past cases?", "Could I sit with the person who does this on Thursday?", "Could I see the data-flow rules your security team follows?" A meeting that ends with no commitment has advanced nothing, however pleasant it was. Compliments with no commitment attached are a signal too, but it points the other way.

Running the conversation

A discovery interview has a shape. Plan it in advance, because the risky moments, such as the temptation to pitch, come mid-conversation when it is hard to think.

Before

Write down the three things you most need to learn from this person. Not twenty questions: three unknowns, such as "how often do lookups go wrong?", "who decides when documents are updated?" and "what data may leave the building?". Different people can answer different unknowns, which is the reason to plan who you talk to. Read what exists first (the documents, the org chart, past incident reports) so you do not spend their time on facts you could find alone.

During

  • Open without a pitch. Say who you are, what you are trying to understand and that you are not selling anything. Ask whether you may record or take notes; in some workplaces consent is required by policy or law, and an unrecorded conversation you cannot cite is worth less.
  • Ask for a specific story, then follow it. "Tell me about the last time..." then "What happened next?", "How long did that take?", "Who else was involved?", "What did you do about it?". The follow-ups are the interview; the opening question only starts it.
  • Be quiet. Silence after an answer often produces the most useful second sentence. If you are talking more than a third of the time, you are probably pitching.
  • Ask to see the artefacts. The spreadsheet, the template, the email thread, the printout with handwriting on it. Artefacts cannot be flattering or forgetful.
  • Notice workarounds. A workaround is a makeshift routine people build to cope: a private spreadsheet, a copy-paste routine, a colleague everyone asks. People do not build workarounds for non-problems, so a workaround is among the strongest signals of real pain, and it often points to the solution the organisation has already invented.
  • Ask "why" gently, a few times. The five whys technique, associated with Taiichi Ohno and Toyota's production system, repeats the question to move from a symptom to its cause. It is a heuristic and not a proof: real problems often have several causes, so treat the chain as a hypothesis to check.

Who to talk to

In enterprise AI the person who asks for the system, the person who will use it, the person who pays and the person who can veto it are usually four different people. Interview them differently and expect them to disagree.

The red box deserves emphasis. These gatekeepers, the security, legal and compliance reviewers, are rarely in the room at the start, yet they decide what data you can use and where the system can run. Meeting them in week one, with a plain description of what data would flow where, is far cheaper than meeting them in month three. Chapter 15 covers what they will ask; threat modelling gives you the vocabulary now.

After

Write your notes within a day, while you remember what was said and what was merely implied. Keep three layers apart: the verbatim (quotes, with who said them), what happened (events, numbers, artefacts) and your interpretation. Mixing them is how an inference turns into a "fact" by the third meeting. Then update the list of unknowns: which are answered, which are new, who could answer the rest.

State of play, October 2026

Meeting recorders and AI note-takers are now routine, and it is tempting to hand the summary over to one. Two cautions that do not depend on any product. First, summaries tend to smooth hedges ("I think we probably do this about weekly") into flat statements ("we do this weekly"), which removes exactly the uncertainty you need. Keep the transcript and quote from it. Second, recording a conversation about a customer's internal process may be restricted by their policies, their employees' rights and local law (consent rules differ by country and, in some places, by state). Ask first, and ask in a way that makes no a comfortable answer.

From the request to the problem

Once the stories are in, the work is to climb from what was asked down to what is wrong. The ladder below follows the claims team. Each rung is something a person said or showed, and each question on the right is what produced it.

Step through

Climbing down from "a chatbot" to the problem

  1. The request. "We need a chatbot over our policy documents." It is a solution, and a plausible one. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a specification.
  2. A specific story. Asked for the last time, Marco describes last Tuesday: a flood-damage question, 40 minutes across three PDFs. It is a checkable event, not an opinion.
  3. A finding. "What happened next?" reveals that two of the PDFs gave different answers. Already, retrieval alone would not have helped: it would have surfaced both and let a model choose.
  4. A cause. Asking why they disagree leads to a 2023 update that was never merged into the older document, and to the discovery that no one is assigned to keep the documents current.
  5. A workaround. In the end he asked Priya, who answers from memory and keeps a spreadsheet of past rulings. About eight interruptions a day. This is the real system the team runs on.
  6. The reframed problem. The pain is stale sources plus rulings that exist only in one person's head and one private file. A chatbot may still be part of the answer, but it comes after fixing the sources and capturing the rulings (chapter 3), and its evaluation must test the contradiction case.

Putting a number on the problem

A problem you cannot size is a problem you cannot prioritise, and you cannot judge a solution's economics without one. The method is arithmetic: frequency × time × cost, with every factor traced to something you heard or saw. What matters is not getting a precise answer but showing which inputs you are unsure of and how much they matter.

The script below uses the claims scenario. Every input is a range, as it would be after a handful of interviews: the headcount from the org chart, search frequency from three interviews, search time from two shadowed sessions, hourly cost from the finance partner. (All figures are illustrative.) It prints the most likely annual cost, then swings each input alone between its low and high values to show which one matters most, then simulates all of them varying together.

import random
random.seed(7)
# Interview evidence, as (low, likely, high) ranges. Every number has a source.
facts = {
    "analysts":            (10, 12, 14),     # org chart
    "searches_per_week":   (6, 10, 20),      # three interviews, each told as a story
    "minutes_per_search":  (8, 15, 30),      # shadowed two sessions
    "cost_per_hour":       (45, 60, 80),     # finance partner
    "weeks_per_year":      (44, 46, 48),
}
def annual_cost(f):
    hours = f["analysts"] * f["searches_per_week"] * f["minutes_per_search"] / 60
    return hours * f["cost_per_hour"] * f["weeks_per_year"]

likely = {k: v[1] for k, v in facts.items()}
print(f"likely case: {annual_cost(likely):,.0f} per year")

# One-at-a-time sensitivity: swing each input to its low and high value
rows = []
for k, (lo, mid, hi) in facts.items():
    a = annual_cost({**likely, k: lo}); b = annual_cost({**likely, k: hi})
    rows.append((b - a, k, a, b))
for swing, k, a, b in sorted(rows, reverse=True):
    print(f"{k:20s} {a:>9,.0f} to {b:>9,.0f}  (swing {swing:,.0f})")

# Monte Carlo with triangular distributions
runs = sorted(annual_cost({k: random.triangular(lo, hi, mid) for k, (lo, mid, hi) in facts.items()})
              for _ in range(10000))
print("10th / 50th / 90th percentile:", *(f"{runs[int(len(runs)*q)]:,.0f}" for q in (0.1, 0.5, 0.9)))
likely case: 82,800 per year
minutes_per_search      44,160 to   165,600  (swing 121,440)
searches_per_week       49,680 to   165,600  (swing 115,920)
cost_per_hour           62,100 to   110,400  (swing 48,300)
analysts                69,000 to    96,600  (swing 27,600)
weeks_per_year          79,200 to    86,400  (swing 7,200)
10th / 50th / 90th percentile: 67,074 111,283 183,141

Three things are worth reading from the output. First, the single "likely" figure, 82,800, is not the middle of the plausible range: the simulated median is about 111,000. The ranges are skewed upwards (a search can take 30 minutes but not fewer than 8), so the likely case understates the typical case. Reporting only the likely case would have been misleading in the direction that hurts the customer. Second, the spread is wide: the 10th to 90th percentile runs from about 67,000 to 183,000. A claim of "saves 82,800 a year" would be false precision. Third, the inputs that matter are minutes per search and searches per week, not headcount or working weeks. That tells you where to spend your next interview: measure those two properly.

Common misconception

"A good interview is one where the customer loves my idea." Enthusiasm is the cheapest signal there is, and it is correlated with politeness more than with need. The interviews that move a project are the ones where your picture changes: you learn the documents are stale, or that the buyer is someone you had not met, or that the problem is real but small. Be suspicious of any interview from which you came away more confident and no better informed.

Turning notes into evidence

After five or six interviews, you will have pages of notes and a strong impression. Impressions are exactly what discovery is meant to protect you from, so impose structure. The simplest structure is to code each statement by what kind of statement it is.

Telling evidence from noise

The script below is a deliberately crude linter for interview notes. It flags compliments, hypotheticals, generic claims and feature wish lists as noise, and looks for specific past events, numbers and workarounds as evidence. A pattern matcher is no substitute for reading, and it will miss and mislabel plenty, but running it on your own notes is instructive, because the pattern in your own transcripts is often worse than you expected.

import re
# A crude "interview note linter": it flags the kinds of statement that are not evidence.
RULES = [
    ("compliment",  r"\b(great|love|amazing|awesome|brilliant|fantastic)\b|that would be (so )?(good|great|useful)"),
    ("hypothetical", r"\b(would|might|could|probably|maybe|i think i'?d|i'?d (use|pay|love))\b"),
    ("generic",     r"\b(always|never|usually|typically|everyone|nobody)\b"),
    ("wishlist",    r"\b(should have|needs? to have|it would be nice|feature|dashboard|chatbot)\b"),
]
EVIDENCE = [
    ("past-specific", r"\b(last (week|month|tuesday|quarter)|yesterday|on (monday|tuesday|friday)|this morning)\b"),
    ("number",        r"\b\d+(\.\d+)?\s*(minutes?|hours?|days?|%|people|times|tickets|pdfs?|claims?)\b"),
    ("workaround",    r"\b(spreadsheet|copy[- ]and[- ]paste|wrote it down|ask(ed)? (priya|a senior)|email(ed)? (it|them)|printout)\b"),
]
notes = [
    "That would be so useful, we should definitely have a chatbot.",
    "I'd probably use it every day, I think.",
    "Last Tuesday I spent 40 minutes hunting through three PDFs, then asked Priya.",
    "We usually lose time on policy lookups, everyone does.",
    "I keep a spreadsheet of the answers I had to ask a senior for, 120 rows so far.",
]
for line in notes:
    flags = [n for n, rx in RULES if re.search(rx, line, re.I)]
    gold = [n for n, rx in EVIDENCE if re.search(rx, line, re.I)]
    verdict = "EVIDENCE" if gold and not flags else "mixed" if gold else "NOISE"
    print(f"{verdict:8s} flags={flags or '-'} evidence={gold or '-'}\n         {line}")
NOISE    flags=['compliment', 'hypothetical', 'wishlist'] evidence=-
         That would be so useful, we should definitely have a chatbot.
NOISE    flags=['hypothetical'] evidence=-
         I'd probably use it every day, I think.
EVIDENCE flags=- evidence=['past-specific', 'number', 'workaround']
         Last Tuesday I spent 40 minutes hunting through three PDFs, then asked Priya.
NOISE    flags=['generic'] evidence=-
         We usually lose time on policy lookups, everyone does.
EVIDENCE flags=- evidence=['workaround']
         I keep a spreadsheet of the answers I had to ask a senior for, 120 rows so far.

Notice what the linter gets right and what it cannot. The third line is evidence because it has a date, a duration and a workaround. The fifth is evidence even with no number in it, because a private spreadsheet of 120 rows is an artefact that exists. The fourth, "we usually lose time on policy lookups, everyone does", is a generic claim: it may be true, but it is not usable until someone tells you about a specific lookup. If you use a language model to help code transcripts, apply the same discipline of reading a sample by hand and checking the model's labels against your own, as in Error Analysis.

Counting signals across interviews

The next step is to record what happened in each interview as columns, not adjectives. Did the person tell a specific story? Show a workaround? Give a number? Offer a commitment? Then count. This turns "everyone seemed keen" into "five of eight told a story, three showed a workaround, and four gave a number".

from collections import Counter
# One row per interview after coding the notes. Fields are things that HAPPENED, not opinions.
interviews = [
    # who,            role,       story, workaround, quantified, commitment
    ("Priya",         "senior",   True,  True,  True,  "sent 40 past questions"),
    ("Marco",         "analyst",  True,  True,  True,  "intro to team lead"),
    ("Dana",          "analyst",  True,  False, False, None),
    ("Wei",           "analyst",  False, False, False, None),          # only opinions
    ("Sam",           "manager",  True,  False, True,  "booked a 2nd call with data owner"),
    ("Lena",          "security", False, False, False, "asked for the data-flow diagram"),
    ("Omar",          "analyst",  True,  True,  False, "shared the spreadsheet"),
    ("Kay",           "buyer",    False, False, True,  None),
]
n = len(interviews)
c = Counter()
for who, role, story, work, quant, commit in interviews:
    c["story"] += story; c["workaround"] += work
    c["quantified"] += quant; c["commitment"] += commit is not None
for k in ("story", "workaround", "quantified", "commitment"):
    print(f"{k:11s} {c[k]}/{n}")
strong = [w for w, r, s, wk, q, cm in interviews if s and (wk or q) and cm]
print("strong signal (story + workaround or number + a commitment):", strong)
print("roles with no story at all:", sorted({r for _, r, s, *_ in interviews} - {r for _, r, s, *_ in interviews if s}))

The output says something no single interview could. The signal is strong from analysts and the senior adjuster, but the security reviewer and the buyer told no stories at all: they had opinions and constraints. That is not a flaw in them. It tells you which parts of your picture are evidence (the analysts' pain) and which are still assertions (the buyer's expectations), and it names a gap to close: you have not yet seen the approval path in action. Four interviews met the bar of a story, a workaround or a number, and a commitment. Those four are your design partners.

What goes wrong

  • Pitching mid-interview. The moment you describe your idea, the customer starts reacting to it, politely. Keep your idea in your pocket until you have the story, and then ask about their life, not your solution.
  • Interviewing only the sponsor. The sponsor knows the goal but not the workflow. Users know the workflow but not the goal. Neither knows the approval path. See the people map.
  • Confirmation hunting. You come with a hypothesis and hear only what supports it. Write down before each interview what answer would change your mind, and look for it.
  • Leading with technology. "We can use retrieval and an agent" tells the customer you have already decided. They will describe the problem you can solve rather than the one they have.
  • Treating one loud story as the pattern. A vivid anecdote may be a rare event. Follow with "is that typical? when was the time before?", and compare with shadowed data where you can.
  • Asking people to design the system. Users are experts in their work, not in software design. Collect problems and constraints from them; design is your job.
  • Forgetting the cost of a wrong answer. In AI projects this is the quantity that most determines feasibility (chapter 4), and customers seldom volunteer it. Ask for a time an answer was wrong and what happened next.

Systems connection

Discovery is requirements engineering for a system whose behaviour is statistical. The first step of any system design is the same: clarify the problem before drawing any boxes. Asking "tell me about a time it went wrong" is also the stance of a blameless postmortem: reconstruct what happened, step by step, rather than asking people for opinions on who was at fault. And the habit of reading real, messy data before choosing metrics is the same one you meet in A6's error analysis chapter: look first, then decide what to measure.

Where this leads

Key takeaways

  • A request is a solution the customer has already chosen. The real problem is the situation causing the pain or cost, stated without reference to any solution.
  • Asking what people want fails because they cannot report their reasons, are polite, and cannot predict their behaviour. Ask about specific events in the past in their life, not opinions about the future or about your idea.
  • Workarounds, artefacts and numbers are strong signals. Compliments, hypotheticals and generic claims are noise.
  • Talk to users, experts, sponsor, gatekeepers, operators and affected people. Meet security and legal early.
  • End each conversation by asking for a commitment, usually data or access. No commitment means no advance.
  • Size the problem with ranges and sensitivity, not a single figure, and report where the uncertainty lies.
  • Keep verbatim, events and interpretation apart in your notes, and count signals instead of trusting an impression.

Check your understanding

You ask a manager, "Would your analysts use a tool that answered policy questions?" She says, "Absolutely, they'd love it." What have you learnt?

  1. Strong demand, since a senior person endorsed the idea.
  2. Moderate demand, because the manager is not herself a user.
  3. Very little: it is a hypothetical answer with a compliment, about your idea.
  4. That the analysts need training to use such a tool.
The question is hypothetical and about your idea, so the answer is a polite guess. The tempting "moderate demand" option at least notices the manager is not the user, but it still treats the answer as weak evidence of demand; it is no evidence. The better next question asks her about the last time an analyst got stuck, and then asks to talk to that analyst.

Which question is most likely to produce usable evidence?

  1. "Tell me about the last time you could not find a policy answer. What did you do next?"
  2. "How often would you say you can't find policy answers?"
  3. "Which is more important to you, speed or accuracy?"
  4. "What would an ideal tool look like?"
Only the first asks for a specific past event in the person's own life, and its follow-up pulls out the workaround. "How often" asks for an average with no story behind it: useful once a specific case exists, weak on its own. The speed-versus-accuracy question forces an abstract ranking, and "ideal tool" invites invented feature ideas.

In the problem-sizing script, the "likely" case gives 82,800 a year but the simulated median is about 111,000. What does this tell you?

  1. The simulation is wrong, because the median should match the likely case.
  2. The headcount input is far too uncertain.
  3. The customer has exaggerated the time spent.
  4. The input ranges are skewed upwards, so the most-likely-values case understates the typical outcome.
The ranges run from low to a much higher maximum while the most likely value sits near the low end (for instance minutes per search: 8, 15, 30). Multiplying several right-skewed inputs pushes the middle of the distribution above the product of their most likely values. Nothing in the output suggests exaggeration, and headcount is not what drives the gap. The point is to report a range, not one number.

An interview ends warmly. The customer says they are excited and will "keep you in mind". What should you do before ending?

  1. Send a thank-you email and wait for them to come back to you.
  2. Ask for a concrete next step that costs them something, such as sample data, time with an expert or an introduction.
  3. Describe your proposed solution so that they can react.
  4. Offer a discount to secure the project.
A commitment tests whether the problem matters to them. Waiting passively lets warmth fade. Describing your solution switches the conversation from their life to your idea, and a discount is a sales tactic that tells you nothing about need.

After six interviews, analysts told detailed stories and showed workarounds, but the security reviewer and the sponsor only gave opinions and requirements. What is the sensible conclusion?

  1. The analysts' evidence is unreliable because the others did not confirm it.
  2. Ignore the security reviewer: they are not users.
  3. The user pain is well evidenced, while the approval path and the sponsor's expectations are still assertions that need testing.
  4. The project is ready for design because most interviewees were positive.
Different people supply different kinds of knowledge. Analysts' stories evidence the pain; gatekeepers hold constraints that can end the project, so ignoring them is the costly error. Counting positive vibes is how impressions replace evidence. The useful move is to say which claims are evidenced and which are not, and plan the next interviews around the gap.

Which situation is a sign that a workaround reveals a real problem?

  1. A senior adjuster keeps a private 120-row spreadsheet of rulings because the official documents don't give clear answers.
  2. A team lead says, "We should really have a dashboard."
  3. An analyst says, "I'm sure there's a better way to do this."
  4. The sponsor shows a competitor's demo and asks for the same.
A workaround is something someone has already spent effort on, which is a revealed preference. Wishes, vague optimism and a competitor's demo are about the future or about a solution, not about behaviour. The spreadsheet also hints at a solution the organisation has already half built.
