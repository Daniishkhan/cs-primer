Is AI the Right Tool?

Before anyone builds, ask whether this task suits a model at all. This chapter gives a short triage: is there a checkable right answer, can the customer survive a wrong one, do the data exist and may they be used, is the use regulated, and does something simpler already do the job. The best outcome of triage is sometimes "no", or "not yet".

Why this matters

You now have a picture of the real problem, a set of requirements and some expert judgement. Each of the next chapters will cost time. A project that fails the triage will fail later, more expensively, after a demo has raised hopes. Applied engineers who can say "a spreadsheet formula solves this" or "this decision must not be automated" earn trust that lasts across projects. After this chapter you will be able to run a feasibility triage in an afternoon, compute the accuracy a task needs to break even, recognise tasks that need a human regardless of model quality, and write a one-page recommendation that includes the conditions under which you would change your mind.

A triage before anyone builds

A feasibility triage is a short, structured assessment of a candidate task, done before building, that sorts it into build, fix something first, use a simpler tool, or stop. The name comes from emergency medicine, where a nurse sorts arrivals by urgency and available resources before treating anyone, because treating everyone in order of arrival wastes the scarce resource.

Five questions do most of the work, and the order matters, because early answers can end the conversation cheaply.

Question 1: can anyone say what a right answer is?

A task is suitable for evaluation, and therefore for responsible delivery, only if someone can look at an output and say whether it is good. "Extract the invoice total" has a right answer you can check against the document. "Draft a reply to this complaint" has no single right answer, but experts can say which replies are acceptable, and a rubric (chapter 3) captures that. "Make our communications more engaging" has neither, and no amount of modelling will fix an undefined target.

The test is blunt: could two competent people, shown the same output, mostly agree whether it is acceptable? If not, the first project is agreeing the criterion, which is the work of chapters 2 and 3, and the AI question can wait. Asking for the agreement statistic on a sample of ten real cases is usually enough to find out.

Question 2: how bad is a wrong output?

Every model sometimes produces wrong output, however good it is. What matters is what a wrong output costs when it occurs, and two properties shape that cost: how large the damage is, and how easily errors are caught before they cause it. Combine them and tasks fall into four kinds.

Notice that the grid is a design lever, not a verdict. You can move a task by changing how it is deployed: limit the amount a single wrong output can cost (a spending cap), make outputs reversible (hold before sending), or make them checkable (show the source passage next to every extracted value). The bottom-right cell is where AI should usually advise and a person decide. In some jurisdictions that is also the law, as the dated note below shows.

Break-even accuracy

You can put numbers on this. Automating a task replaces a manual cost M with a machine cost m, plus the cost of mistakes. A wrong output either reaches the customer, costing E, or is caught by a reviewer, costing another manual redo M. A review costs R and catches a fraction q of errors. The model must be right at least often enough for the total per-item cost to beat M. The script finds that break-even accuracy for three illustrative tasks, with and without review. (All inputs are made-up units; use your customer's.)

# When does automating a task pay? Per item, assume:
#   M manual cost, m machine cost, p chance the model is right,
#   E cost of a wrong item that reaches the customer, R cost of a human check,
#   q chance a human check catches a wrong item (a caught error is redone by hand at cost M).
def per_item(M, m, p, E, R, q, review):
    if not review:
        return m + (1 - p) * E
    return m + R + (1 - p) * (q * M + (1 - q) * E)

def breakeven(M, m, E, R, q, review):
    if per_item(M, m, 1.0, E, R, q, review) > M:
        return None                       # even a perfect model does not pay
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    for _ in range(40):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (lo, mid) if per_item(M, m, mid, E, R, q, review) <= M else (mid, hi)
    return hi

cases = [  # name, M, m, E, R
    ("Draft support replies",     4.0, 0.05,   25, 0.5),
    ("Code invoices to ledger",   2.0, 0.03,   60, 0.3),
    ("Triage clinical referrals", 6.0, 0.05, 5000, 1.0),
]
Q = 0.9
print(f"{'task':27s} {'no check':>9s} {'checked':>9s}   (reviewer catches {Q:.0%} of errors)")
for name, M, m, E, R in cases:
    cells = []
    for review in (False, True):
        b = breakeven(M, m, E, R, Q, review)
        cells.append("never" if b is None else f"{b:.1%}")
    print(f"{name:27s} {cells[0]:>9s} {cells[1]:>9s}")
task                         no check   checked   (reviewer catches 90% of errors)
Draft support replies           84.2%     43.4%
Code invoices to ledger         96.7%     78.6%
Triage clinical referrals       99.9%     99.0%

Reading the table. Drafting support replies without any check needs the model to be right about 84 per cent of the time just to break even, because a wrong reply costs 25 against a manual cost of 4. With a reviewer who catches 90 per cent of mistakes, the same task breaks even at 43 per cent. Human-in-the-loop review turns a hard accuracy bar into an easy one, which is why "AI drafts, person verifies" is such a durable pattern. Coding invoices is stricter because errors cost 60. And the referral triage task, with errors costing 5,000, needs 99.9 per cent unchecked and 99.0 per cent even when a good reviewer is in the loop. Two cautions follow. First, break-even is the floor, not the target: you want a margin for the uncertainty in every input. Second, high bars are not just hard to reach but hard to demonstrate. A common rule of thumb says that if you observe no errors in n cases, the 95 per cent upper bound on the error rate is about 3/n, so showing an error rate below 0.1 per cent needs roughly 3,000 error-free cases. For a task like the third, you may never be able to prove the system is good enough, which is itself a feasibility finding.

Analogy: triage in the emergency department

An emergency nurse does not diagnose; she sorts. Life-threatening cases go straight in, minor ones wait, and some can be sent home with advice. The sorting uses a few quick questions, takes minutes, and its value is that it stops the expensive resource being spent on the wrong case. A feasibility triage does the same for your team's weeks.

Where it breaks down: an emergency department must see everyone eventually. A feasibility triage can end in "no", and a "no" that comes with the conditions for a later "yes" is a perfectly good result.

A real case: when the chatbot's words are the company's words

In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149, decided 14 February 2024), British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal considered a passenger who relied on the airline's website chatbot for how to claim a bereavement fare. The chatbot gave advice that conflicted with the airline's own policy. The airline argued it should not be liable for what its chatbot said; the tribunal rejected that and held the airline responsible for information on its website, including the chatbot, finding that it had not taken reasonable care to ensure the chatbot was accurate. It is a small-claims decision of limited precedent value, and the sums were modest. The lesson for triage is still clear: the cost of a wrong answer lands on the deploying organisation, and here it was bounded only because the fare in dispute was small. A customer-facing assistant that states policy needs grounding in the actual policy and a way to check its claims; a city's chatbot case in this course shows the public-sector version of the same problem.

Question 3: is the data there, and can you use it?

Four separate data questions hide inside "do we have data?", and a project can fail on any of them.

  1. Inputs at run time. Will the information the model needs actually be available when the decision is made? A model that predicts churn from a field that is only filled in after the customer has left looks excellent in testing and is useless in use.
  2. Examples to evaluate on. Are there real past cases, with trusted outcomes or expert labels, in enough number to measure the success metric (chapter 2)? If not, creating them is the first task (chapters 3 and 6).
  3. Permission and location. May this data be sent to a model, and which ones? Personal data, privileged documents and regulated records often cannot go to a third-party service at all, which can decide the choice of model (chapters 7 and 13) before any other factor. See Data Protection & Privacy.
  4. Representativeness. Do the examples look like what the system will really see, including the messy and the rare? Gold sets built from clean historical cases overstate performance; chapter 6 returns to this.

A quick way to test several of these at once is a hand test: take twenty to thirty real, permitted inputs, run them through a capable model by hand with a plain prompt, and read the outputs with the customer's expert beside you. It costs an afternoon. If the model is plainly hopeless, you have learnt that cheaply. If it is plainly good, you know the task is within reach and can focus on the risks. Most often you learn something in between: that a third of inputs are scans, that a policy contradicts itself, that the reviewer disagrees with the "correct" answer. Only use data you are allowed to send; if you cannot, build the test on synthetic or redacted examples and say so.

Question 4: how regulated is the decision?

Some decisions about people are regulated whatever technology makes them. Credit, employment, education, essential public services, insurance pricing in some settings, and health decisions carry legal duties such as explanation, human oversight, non-discrimination and record-keeping. Whether the task falls into such a category is a feasibility question, because it changes the design (a human must decide), the evidence you must keep (documentation and logs) and the time it takes (approvals). Chapters 8 to 11 cover finance, healthcare, legal and the public sector in detail; here is the habit to build: ask early, in plain words, "does this output affect a person's rights, money, health or access to a service, and does anyone have to be able to explain or contest it?".

State of play, October 2026: the EU AI Act timetable

The EU's AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024. Its bans on certain practices applied from 2 February 2025, and the obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models from 2 August 2025. The rules for "high-risk" systems, including those used for credit scoring, recruitment and access to essential services, were scheduled for 2 August 2026. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the "Digital Omnibus on AI"), published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026, postponed them: to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk systems (Annex III) and to 2 August 2028 for those built into regulated products (Annex I), according to law-firm summaries (Cooley, Cuatrecasas, Hunton) read on 14 October 2026; see governance and regulation. Read the Regulation on EUR-Lex before relying on any date. Separately, Article 22 of the GDPR, in force since 2018, already gives people certain rights regarding decisions based solely on automated processing that have legal or similarly significant effects, and applies today. Dates and details change; the habit of asking early does not.

Regulation is not only an obstacle. For a regulated task, a human reviewer, an audit trail and a documented evaluation are things the customer needs anyway, and building them in from the start is cheaper than adding them under pressure. The triage outcome is often not "no" but "yes, as an advisory tool with a reviewer".

Question 5: does something simpler already work?

The cheapest, most reliable and most explainable solution is usually the simplest one that meets the bar. A useful habit is to climb a ladder of alternatives and stop at the first rung that does.

Two examples. A finance team wants an AI system to "read invoices". Discovery shows that 70 per cent of invoices come from fifteen suppliers with fixed layouts; a template per supplier extracts those fields exactly, and a model is needed only for the rest. The right design uses both, with rules first. A customer-support team wants an assistant to answer "where is my order?". That question has a precise answer in a database, so the solution is a lookup with a templated sentence, not a language model that might get the date wrong. The model's job, if any, is understanding the question and phrasing the answer. (Both scenarios are illustrative.)

A second form of this question is whether the real problem is the process. Chapter 1's analyst team had contradictory documents and an unwritten answer book. No model fixes that; clean-up of the documents might remove most of the pain, and a model on top later becomes a smaller and safer step.

A scorecard that forces the conversation

Teams find it helps to make the triage explicit in one table. Score each candidate task red, amber or green on each of the questions, use the first two as hard gates (a red on either means stop or redesign), and use weighted scores for the rest. The code below does exactly that for four candidate projects. It is a conversation tool, not an oracle: the value is that every score must be argued from evidence collected in chapters 1 to 3, and that disagreement shows where you know least.

# A feasibility scorecard: hard gates first, then a weighted score. 0 = red, 1 = amber, 2 = green.
GATES = ["data_allowed", "error_cost_bounded"]          # any red here means stop
WEIGHTS = {"task_defined": 3, "data_available": 3, "review_possible": 2, "simpler_fails": 2, "value_clear": 2}

projects = {
    "Summarise support tickets for agents": dict(data_allowed=2, error_cost_bounded=2, task_defined=1,
        data_available=2, review_possible=2, simpler_fails=2, value_clear=1),
    "Auto-approve small loans":             dict(data_allowed=2, error_cost_bounded=0, task_defined=2,
        data_available=1, review_possible=1, simpler_fails=1, value_clear=2),
    "Extract totals from invoices":         dict(data_allowed=2, error_cost_bounded=2, task_defined=2,
        data_available=2, review_possible=2, simpler_fails=0, value_clear=2),
    "Chatbot over 'all our documents'":     dict(data_allowed=1, error_cost_bounded=1, task_defined=0,
        data_available=1, review_possible=0, simpler_fails=1, value_clear=0),
}
def triage(s):
    stops = [g for g in GATES if s[g] == 0]
    if stops:
        return 0, "STOP: " + ", ".join(stops)
    pct = round(100 * sum(WEIGHTS[k] * s[k] for k in WEIGHTS) / (2 * sum(WEIGHTS.values())))
    if s["simpler_fails"] == 0:
        return pct, "use the simpler tool (rules or templates)"
    if pct >= 75: return pct, "build a prototype"
    if pct >= 50: return pct, "fix the weakest items first, then prototype"
    return pct, "do not build yet"
for name, s in projects.items():
    pct, advice = triage(s)
    print(f"{pct:3d}  {name:40s} {advice}")
 79  Summarise support tickets for agents     build a prototype
  0  Auto-approve small loans                 STOP: error_cost_bounded
 83  Extract totals from invoices             use the simpler tool (rules or templates)
 21  Chatbot over 'all our documents'         do not build yet

"Auto-approve small loans" is stopped by the error-cost gate however attractive the rest looks: no weighted score can buy back an unbounded downside. "Extract totals from invoices" scores 83 per cent, yet the recommendation is to use the simpler tool, because the score for "simpler tools fail" is zero, meaning rules or templates already work. "Chatbot over all our documents" scores 21 per cent because nobody can say what a correct answer is, how it would be reviewed, or what the value is: the project is not infeasible so much as undefined. Only "summarise support tickets for agents" reaches a prototype: errors are bounded, a person reads the summary, and the data are available.

Common misconception

"The demo worked, so it is feasible." A demo shows that a model can produce a good output on a hand-picked input. Feasibility asks whether it will do so often enough, on the customer's real messy inputs, at a cost of errors the customer can bear, with the data they are allowed to use. Almost any task has an impressive demo; the triage questions are the ones a demo cannot answer. A good habit is to ask of any demo: how were the inputs chosen, what did the failures look like, and who checked?

Writing the recommendation

The product of triage is a page, not a slide. It should state, in order: the task as the customer described it and the real problem as you now understand it; the answers to the five questions with the evidence for each; the recommendation (build a prototype, fix X first, use a simpler tool, or do not proceed); and, importantly, what would change your answer. "If the customer can supply 200 labelled cases and legal approves redacted data in a hosted service, this becomes a prototype" is a recommendation the customer can act on. A flat "no" is a dead end; a "not yet, here is what is missing" is a plan.

Be prepared for resistance. People attached to an AI project may hear "a simpler tool will do" as a criticism. Frame it as protecting their goal: the outcome they want is the problem solved, and the cheaper, more reliable route gets them there sooner. Showing your sums (the break-even table, the scorecard) turns an opinion into a shared analysis. And keep the door open: the same triage re-run in six months, with better data, a smaller error cost or a better model, may give a different answer.

Systems connection

Triage is the AI version of asking whether you need a distributed system at all, the habit that Designing with ML & LLMs applies to learned components. Bounding the cost of an error with caps, holds and reviews is the same idea as limiting blast radius. And the question "what is the worst thing that happens if this is wrong?" is the first step of threat modelling.

Key takeaways

  • Run a feasibility triage before building, with cheap questions first: a checkable right answer, a survivable error, usable data, regulatory exposure, and a simpler alternative.
  • Error cost depends on size of harm and how easily errors are caught. Reviews, caps and reversibility move a task toward feasibility; the bottom-right cell should be advice only.
  • Break-even accuracy shows how good a model must be. Review turns a hard bar into an easy one, but very high bars may not even be demonstrable with available test cases.
  • Data feasibility has four parts: inputs at run time, examples for evaluation, permission, and representativeness. Test them with a hand test on real, permitted inputs.
  • Decisions affecting people's rights, money or health are often regulated; ask early. Check current law, since dates change.
  • Climb the ladder of alternatives and stop at the first rung that meets the bar. A "no" or "not yet" with conditions is a valuable result.

Check your understanding

A team wants to auto-approve small consumer loans with a model. The model's offline accuracy is excellent. Under the triage, what is the main concern?

  1. Errors in decisions about people's credit are costly, hard to see and regulated, so the task needs a human decider or strong controls, whatever the accuracy.
  2. The model must be larger before the idea can be considered.
  3. Accuracy is never a relevant measure for loans.
  4. The only problem is that the data are not yet labelled.
The error-cost gate and the regulatory question fail before accuracy matters; a good average hides unfair or ruinous individual errors. Model size and labels are secondary. Accuracy is relevant, but it cannot buy back an unbounded downside or satisfy a duty to explain.

In the break-even table, adding a reviewer who catches 90% of errors lowered the required accuracy for drafting support replies from 84% to 43%. Why?

  1. Reviewers make the model more accurate.
  2. The model's cost falls when it is reviewed.
  3. Most errors become a cheap redo instead of a costly error reaching the customer, so the model can be wrong more often and still beat the manual cost.
  4. The reviewer's cost is ignored in the calculation.
The model's accuracy is unchanged. What changes is the cost of each error: caught errors cost a manual redo, not the full price of a leaked mistake. The reviewer's cost is included in the calculation, and it is part of why the bar is not lower still.

An invoice-extraction project scores 83% on the scorecard but the recommendation is "use the simpler tool". What explains this?

  1. 83% is below the threshold for building.
  2. The "simpler tools fail" item scored zero: rules or templates already meet the bar, which overrides the weighted score.
  3. The data permission gate was red.
  4. Invoices are always better handled by humans.
The score reflects how well the task suits a model, but if a simpler method already meets the bar, the model adds cost and risk without benefit. The permission gate was green for this project, 83 is above the build threshold, and nothing suggests humans beat templates.

Before building anything, a hand test with 25 real, permitted inputs is most useful because it:

  1. Replaces the need for an evaluation set.
  2. Proves that the model will work in production.
  3. Gives a precise accuracy figure to put in the contract.
  4. Cheaply reveals input problems, disagreements with the expert and whether the task is plausible, before committing effort.
Twenty-five cases are too few to give a trustworthy rate or to stand for production, and they do not replace a proper gold set. Their value is early, cheap information on the real inputs and on whether the expert accepts the outputs.

The EU's high-risk AI Act obligations were scheduled for 2 August 2026, then a provisional agreement in May 2026 proposed postponing them. What is the right way to use this fact in a project?

  1. Treat the dates as volatile; check the current legal text, and design to the underlying duties (oversight, documentation, explanation) regardless of the timing.
  2. Ignore the Act, because the deadline has moved.
  3. Assume the original date still applies in all cases.
  4. Wait until the rules take effect before planning any controls.
Dates shift but the duties remain, and building oversight and records in early is cheaper than adding them later. Ignoring the Act or assuming a stale date are both errors, and delaying controls until a deadline wastes the lead time.

A customer is disappointed that your triage recommends not building a chatbot yet. Which closing section of the memo is most useful to them?

  1. A list of AI projects that other companies have launched.
  2. A statement that AI is not suitable for their organisation.
  3. The specific evidence or changes (for example 200 labelled cases and approval for redacted data) that would turn the answer into a prototype.
  4. An offer to build it anyway at a reduced price.
A "no" with conditions gives the customer a plan and keeps the relationship intact. Blanket statements and examples of others are not tied to their situation, and building anyway reintroduces the risk the triage identified.
