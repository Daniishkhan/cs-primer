Why this matters

Most AI projects that fail do not fail because the model was too weak. They fail because the team solved a problem nobody had, agreed on no way to tell whether it worked, or built something the customer's security team would never let near real data. None of that is taught by a course on transformers, and all of it decides whether your skills turn into something that is used. After this chapter you will know what the applied AI engineer actually does, how the next nineteen chapters, four labs, six case studies and eight take-home studios fit together, and how to study them.

The job, in plain terms

An applied AI engineer builds AI systems for a particular customer or product need, and is responsible for the result being useful, not only for the code running. The customer may be an outside company, a department in your own organisation, or the product team down the corridor. What matters is that someone else owns the problem, someone else decides what "good" means, and your work has to live inside their constraints.

A close relative is the forward-deployed engineer (FDE): an engineer embedded with a customer, sometimes physically, who configures and extends the company's technology to fit that customer's real situation. The idea was popularised by Palantir, whose own engineering blog describes its "Delta" engineers as the people who deploy the software to customers, are assigned to one customer team and are measured by their impact on that customer's goals ("Dev versus Delta", Palantir blog). AI companies and consultancies adopted the pattern as models became general enough that the hard part moved from building the technology to fitting it to a particular business. Titles vary widely: applied AI engineer, solutions engineer, deployment engineer, AI engineer on a services team. This course is about the shared work, not any one title or employer.

State of play, October 2026

Job postings for forward-deployed and applied roles have multiplied since 2025, and most large AI labs and many enterprise software firms now hire for them. Published figures on how many postings there are and what they pay come mostly from recruiters and vendors with something to sell, and they disagree with each other, so this course quotes none. What has stayed steady across sources is the skills list: shipping production code, running a discovery conversation without a sales script, designing evaluations, and explaining a failure honestly. Those are the chapters ahead. Titles, pay and team structures change quickly; check current postings rather than trusting any summary, including this one.

A project, start to finish

Every applied project passes through the same five stages, though real projects loop back constantly. The course is organised around them.

  1. Discover. Discovery means working out what the customer needs, which is often not what they asked for. Interviews, requirements, expert knowledge and an honest "should this use AI at all?" check. Chapters 1 to 4.
  2. Prove. Build the smallest thing that could show the idea is wrong, and agree an evaluation the customer trusts before you tune anything. Chapters 5 to 7.
  3. Fit the domain. Finance, healthcare, law and government each have rules about what a system may do and what evidence must exist. Chapters 8 to 11.
  4. Deliver. Move a pilot into production, inside restricted networks, within a budget, past a security review. Chapters 12 to 15.
  5. Communicate. Turn research into a product, write for the people paying, and show your own judgement to a hiring team. Chapters 16 to 19.

The core idea in miniature: a project is a stack of bets

Here is the idea that the rest of the course keeps returning to. Every project rests on assumptions: statements that must be true for the work to pay off. "The scanned PDFs are legible." "Legal will allow a hosted model." "Reviewers will accept five per cent misses." You cannot remove the uncertainty, but you can choose the order in which you resolve it. Test first the assumption that is most likely to be wrong, most expensive if wrong, and cheapest to check. This is the riskiest-assumption principle, which chapter 5 develops fully; the other chapters in Part 1 are ways of finding the assumptions in the first place.

A scoring rule makes the idea concrete. For each assumption, estimate the chance it is wrong, the cost of building on it if it is wrong, and the cost of testing it. Rank by expected waste avoided per day of testing. The numbers below are illustrative guesses, which is the point: writing them down exposes disagreements.

# Rank the assumptions behind a project by how urgently to test them.
assumptions = [
    # name, P(wrong), cost if wrong (person-weeks wasted), cost to test (person-days)
    ("Users will trust and read AI-written summaries", 0.35, 12, 3),
    ("The scanned PDFs are legible enough to read",     0.20, 10, 1),
    ("A model can find the clause in under 10 s",       0.10,  4, 1),
    ("Legal will approve sending text to a hosted API", 0.40, 16, 2),
    ("Reviewers will accept 5% misses",                 0.30,  8, 2),
]
def urgency(p_wrong, cost_wrong, cost_test):
    # expected waste avoided per day of testing (weeks converted to days at 5 d/week)
    return p_wrong * cost_wrong * 5 / cost_test
for name, p, cw, ct in sorted(assumptions, key=lambda a: -urgency(*a[1:])):
    print(f"{urgency(p, cw, ct):6.1f}  {name}")
  16.0  Legal will approve sending text to a hosted API
  10.0  The scanned PDFs are legible enough to read
   7.0  Users will trust and read AI-written summaries
   6.0  Reviewers will accept 5% misses
   2.0  A model can find the clause in under 10 s

The assumption that scores highest is not a modelling question. "Legal will approve sending text to a hosted API" has a 40 per cent chance of being wrong, would waste 16 person-weeks if it is, and a two-day conversation settles it. The lowest-ranked assumption, whether a model can find the clause quickly, is the one engineers instinctively test first because it is the interesting one. In applied work, the interesting assumption is rarely the risky one.

Common misconception

"Customer-facing roles are for people who cannot code, and the real work is the model." In applied AI the opposite tends to hold: the model is a component you can often swap, while the problem definition, the evaluation and the deployment constraints decide the outcome. Customer-facing does not mean sales. It means you read the customer's documents, sit with their experts and own whether the result works. The technical bar is high, because you must make real systems work in unfamiliar environments with little help.

Key takeaways

  • The applied AI engineer owns whether an AI system is useful to someone else, inside their constraints. Titles vary; the work is shared.
  • Projects run through five stages: discover, prove, fit the domain, deliver, communicate. They loop, and what you learn late can change the brief.
  • A project is a stack of assumptions. Test first the one most likely to be wrong, most costly if wrong and cheapest to check, which is rarely the modelling question.
  • This course reuses A0 to A7 and the engineering they assume. It teaches the judgement that connects them.
  • Study by doing: labs for mechanics, case studies for pattern, take-home studios for judgement under time pressure.

Check your understanding

A customer asks you for "an AI assistant that answers questions about our policies". What is the most useful first move?

  1. Pick a model and build a demo over the policy documents so they can react to something real.
  2. Ask about the last few times someone needed a policy answer, to learn what the problem actually is.
  3. Write a requirements document listing the features of the assistant.
  4. Ask the customer which model vendor they prefer.
Discovery comes first. A demo built on the stated request tests your solution and not their problem; the feature list repeats the request; and a vendor preference is a constraint to record, not a starting point. The past-behaviour conversation is the subject of chapter 1.

In the assumption-ranking script, one assumption outscored a technically interesting modelling assumption. Why did it?

  1. Non-technical assumptions are always more important than technical ones.
  2. The script rewards assumptions with the highest probability of being true.
  3. Models are never the risky part of a project.
  4. It combined a high chance of being wrong, a high cost if wrong, and a low cost to test.
The score is (chance wrong × cost if wrong) per day of testing. The legal-approval assumption was moderately likely to fail, very costly if it did, and cheap to test. It is not a rule that non-technical risks win: a model that cannot do the task would score high on a project where that was uncertain. The tempting "models are never risky" claim is false; the ranking just asks about expected waste.

A pilot passes every accuracy test but the customer's security team blocks it from touching real data. In terms of the five stages, what most likely went wrong?

  1. The evaluation in stage 2 was too easy.
  2. The model chosen in stage 2 was too weak.
  3. A constraint that should have surfaced in discovery (stage 1) was not found until delivery (stage 4).
  4. The communication in stage 5 was too slow.
What data may be used, and where, is a requirement and constraint question that belongs in discovery. Finding it late is the expensive case the loop in the diagram warns about. Accuracy was fine, so neither the evaluation nor the model is the cause, and better communication afterwards would not have unblocked the data.
