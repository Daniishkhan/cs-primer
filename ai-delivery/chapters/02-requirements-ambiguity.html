Requirements Under Ambiguity

After discovery you know what hurts. Now you must agree, in writing, what you will build, within what limits, and how both sides will decide it worked. For AI systems that is harder than for ordinary software, because the output is a rate and not a yes or no. This chapter turns "make it better" into decisions, constraints and success metrics that both parties sign.

Why this matters

Most disputes at the end of an AI project are disputes about requirements nobody wrote down. The customer expected "accurate", you delivered 91 per cent, and neither of you had ever said what the number should be, on what data, or at what cost per mistake. Fixing this is cheap on day five and expensive on day ninety. After this chapter you will be able to convert a vague goal into a measurable target with honest uncertainty, choose an operating point when errors have different costs, record constraints and decisions so they can be revisited, and explain to a customer why a single accuracy figure is not a promise.

Why AI requirements are different

An ordinary software requirement says "the system shall reject passwords shorter than twelve characters", and a test either passes or fails. An AI system's behaviour on a given input is not fully specified in advance, and it varies across inputs. "The system shall flag risky clauses" will be true for some clauses and false for others, so the requirement is really about a rate on a population: how often, out of how many, of what kind, measured how.

That produces four distinct kinds of ambiguity, and it helps to name them because each is fixed in a different way.

  • Goal ambiguity. "Improve the review process." Improve what, for whom, by how much? Fixed by tracing the goal to an outcome the customer already measures.
  • Quality ambiguity. "It should be accurate." What counts as a correct output, on which cases, and what is an acceptable error rate? Fixed by defining metrics, datasets and thresholds.
  • Scope ambiguity. "Contracts" might mean English supplier contracts or every document in the legal department. Fixed by explicit in-scope and out-of-scope lists, with examples.
  • Constraint ambiguity. Nobody has said where the data may go, which models are allowed, or how fast it must answer. Fixed by asking, and by recording what you were told and by whom.

The deliverable that resolves them is a short document, which this course calls the requirements restatement: your account of what you have understood, with the assumptions and open questions marked, which both sides sign. It has three load-bearing parts.

Analogy: planning a mountain climb

A climbing party does not promise to reach the summit. It agrees on the objective, the turnaround time after which they descend whatever the weather, the gear and the route's known hazards, and who decides on the mountain. The plan survives surprises because it contains the rules for changing it. A requirements restatement for an AI project should be like this: a destination, limits, and agreed rules for what to do when reality disagrees.

Where it breaks down: a mountain does not change shape because you looked at it. A customer's goals can move once they see early results, and sometimes the right call is to move the summit. That is why the change process is part of the document.

From a vague goal to a number

Start from the outcome the customer already cares about, then walk down. Each step makes the statement narrower and more testable; stop when a stranger could run the check.

What a metric needs

"Accurate" is an adjective, not a metric. A success metric is a number that can be computed, and a usable one has six parts:

  1. Name and measure. The exact formula: "found risky clauses divided by labelled risky clauses".
  2. Threshold. The value that counts as success, and whether it is a floor or a ceiling. Say how uncertainty is handled (below).
  3. Dataset. The inputs it is computed on: where they came from, how many, who labelled them. A metric without a dataset is not reproducible. Chapter 6 shows how to build one.
  4. Baseline. The performance of what exists today, measured the same way. A target of 90 per cent means little if the current process scores 95. The baseline is often the single most useful number in the project, and customers usually have never measured it.
  5. Owner. The person who will accept the number. If nobody on the customer's side owns it, nobody will believe it later.
  6. When and how it is measured. Offline on the gold set before release; online during the pilot; how often afterwards.

It is easy to forget these and hard to notice that you have. The script below is a small spec linter that checks each metric in a requirements file has all six, and flags names that are adjectives. It is crude, but it catches the commonest gaps, and it is the kind of check you can run on every draft.

VAGUE = {"accurate", "fast", "good", "reliable", "user-friendly", "high quality", "robust", "secure"}
REQUIRED = ("name", "measure", "threshold", "dataset", "baseline", "owner")

spec = [
    {"name": "accurate", "measure": "", "threshold": None, "dataset": "", "baseline": "", "owner": ""},
    {"name": "Risky-clause recall", "measure": "found / labelled risky clauses",
     "threshold": ">= 0.90 (95% lower bound)", "dataset": "gold-v1, 120 risky clauses",
     "baseline": "paralegal first pass: 0.84", "owner": "Head of Legal Ops"},
    {"name": "Median review time", "measure": "minutes per contract, pilot logs",
     "threshold": "<= 120", "dataset": "pilot weeks 3 to 6", "baseline": "", "owner": "Ops lead"},
]
for m in spec:
    problems = [f"missing {f}" for f in REQUIRED if not m.get(f)]
    if m["name"].lower() in VAGUE:
        problems.append("name is an adjective, not a measurement")
    print(("OK    " if not problems else "FIX   ") + m["name"] + ("" if not problems else ": " + "; ".join(problems)))
FIX   accurate: missing measure; missing threshold; missing dataset; missing baseline; missing owner; name is an adjective, not a measurement
OK    Risky-clause recall
FIX   Median review time: missing baseline

The first entry, "accurate", fails five times over. The second is a complete metric. The third is almost complete but has no baseline, so nobody can say whether 120 minutes is an improvement. Running a check like this on a draft restatement takes seconds, and each failure is a conversation you would rather have this week than at the final review.

Metrics need uncertainty

Suppose you agree "recall of at least 90 per cent" and test on a gold set. You find 95 of 100 risky clauses. Have you met the target? The point estimate says so, but with 100 positives the true recall could plausibly be anywhere from about 89 to 98 per cent. The honest way to write a threshold is as a bound on the confidence interval: "the lower end of the 95 per cent interval must be at least 0.90". This protects both sides, because the customer is not accepting luck and you are not failing for bad luck. The standard interval for a proportion is the Wilson score interval (Wilson, 1927), which behaves well for small samples and for rates near 100 per cent, where the simple textbook formula breaks down. A6 covers the full treatment in Statistics for Evals; here is what it means for sizing a gold set.

from math import sqrt
def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    half = z * sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return centre - half, centre + half

print("Observed recall 95%, by number of high-risk clauses in the gold set:")
for n in (20, 40, 100, 200, 400):
    k = round(0.95 * n)
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    print(f"  n={n:3d}  {k}/{n} found   95% interval {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}")

def n_needed(target_lo, p_hat):
    n = 10
    while True:
        k = round(p_hat * n)
        if wilson(k, n)[0] >= target_lo:
            return n
        n += 1
print("Gold-set positives needed so the LOWER bound stays above 0.90 if true recall is 0.97:", n_needed(0.90, 0.97))
Observed recall 95%, by number of high-risk clauses in the gold set:
  n= 20  19/20 found   95% interval 0.764 to 0.991
  n= 40  38/40 found   95% interval 0.835 to 0.986
  n=100  95/100 found   95% interval 0.888 to 0.978
  n=200  190/200 found   95% interval 0.910 to 0.973
  n=400  380/400 found   95% interval 0.924 to 0.967
Gold-set positives needed so the LOWER bound stays above 0.90 if true recall is 0.97: 69

The same 95 per cent observed recall comes with very different guarantees depending on the sample: from 0.76 to 0.99 with 20 positives, to 0.92 to 0.97 with 400. The last line answers a practical question you will face in every contract negotiation: if the system truly achieves 97 per cent recall, you need about 69 labelled risky clauses before the lower bound clears 0.90 reliably. (The number is not smooth, because proportions are discrete; treat it as "about 70".) If the customer can only provide 30 examples, you have a conversation to have before signing: either the threshold is softer, or the dataset is bigger, or you agree the first release is evidence-gathering and not acceptance.

Common misconception

"We will agree the accuracy target after we see what the model can do." That sounds flexible and is the commonest cause of acceptance disputes. If the target is set after seeing results, it is set to match the results, and the customer will reasonably suspect it. Agree the form of the target (metric, dataset, baseline, interval) before building, and agree the value after the first baseline measurement on the real gold set, but before any tuning against it. Write down which of the two you are doing.

When errors have different costs

Most AI tasks make two kinds of mistake, and they rarely cost the same. A model that flags risky clauses can miss one (a false negative) or flag a safe one (a false positive). A missed risky clause may mean a bad contract is signed; a false flag costs a few minutes of human reading. A single accuracy number averages these as if they were equal, which is almost never the customer's view.

The remedy is to ask the customer for the cost of each kind of error, in money, time or harm, and to treat the decision threshold as a business parameter. A classifier produces a score; items above the threshold are flagged. Lowering it catches more risky items and flags more safe ones. The choice of where to sit is the operating point. It also depends on a constraint that is easy to forget: the reviewers have finite time. If the team can read 500 flagged clauses a batch, a threshold that flags 1,000 is not "more thorough"; it is a queue that does not get read.

Here is the arithmetic on a simulated batch of 2,000 clauses, 10 per cent genuinely risky, with illustrative costs: 400 units for a missed risky clause and 6 for each human look. The scores come from a seeded random generator standing in for a real classifier.

import random
random.seed(11)
# Synthetic review queue: 2,000 clauses, 10% genuinely risky. Scores from a pretend classifier.
items = []
for _ in range(2000):
    risky = random.random() < 0.10
    score = random.gauss(0.70 if risky else 0.35, 0.15)
    items.append((min(max(score, 0), 1), risky))

COST_MISS, COST_REVIEW = 400, 6          # illustrative: a missed risky clause vs one human look
CAPACITY = 500                            # clauses the team can read per batch
print("thr  flagged  caught  missed  precision  recall   cost   within capacity")
best = None
for thr in (0.35, 0.45, 0.50, 0.55, 0.60, 0.70):
    flagged = [r for s, r in items if s >= thr]
    caught = sum(flagged); missed = sum(r for s, r in items if s < thr and r)
    total_risky = caught + missed
    cost = missed * COST_MISS + len(flagged) * COST_REVIEW
    ok = len(flagged) <= CAPACITY
    print(f"{thr:.2f} {len(flagged):7d} {caught:7d} {missed:7d} {caught/len(flagged):9.2f} {caught/total_risky:7.2f} {cost:7d}   {'yes' if ok else 'NO'}")
    if ok and (best is None or cost < best[1]):
        best = (thr, cost)
print("cheapest threshold that fits the team's capacity:", best)
thr  flagged  caught  missed  precision  recall   cost   within capacity
0.35    1079     206       1      0.19    1.00    6874   NO
0.45     639     202       5      0.32    0.98    5834   NO
0.50     471     189      18      0.40    0.91   10026   yes
0.55     338     177      30      0.52    0.86   14028   yes
0.60     240     161      46      0.67    0.78   19840   yes
0.70     128     117      90      0.91    0.57   36768   yes
cheapest threshold that fits the team's capacity: (0.5, 10026)

The cheapest threshold in the whole table is 0.45 (cost 5,834), but it flags 639 clauses, above the team's capacity of 500, so it is not available. The best feasible choice is 0.50, which costs about 70 per cent more and misses 18 risky clauses. Notice also that precision, the share of flags that are really risky, is only 0.40 at that point, which means reviewers will meet many false alarms. That is not a defect to fix; it is the price of high recall, and the customer should hear that before the pilot, not from the reviewers in week two.

Constraints: the things you cannot trade

A constraint is a limit you must stay inside, not a goal you trade against others. The distinction matters because a goal can be partly met ("faster is better"), but a constraint is met or broken ("data does not leave the EU"). Customers state constraints implicitly, and you find them by asking the right questions in the right rooms. A checklist helps.

ConstraintWhat to askWhy it bites AI projects
Data use and locationWhich data may a model see? May any of it leave your network or country? Which fields are personal data?Rules out hosted APIs, changes which models are possible, forces redaction (privacy and data handling).
Approved models and vendorsWhich vendors have passed review? Who approves a new one, and how long does it take?Shrinks the model menu, sometimes to a few options (chapters 7 and 13).
Latency and volumeHow many items a day, in which peak hour? What wait is tolerable, and at which percentile?Sets model size and architecture; see tail latency.
Cost per taskWhat does a task cost today, and what budget exists per task and per year?Prices out some designs, such as long reasoning on every item (chapter 14).
Human oversight and accountabilityWho reviews outputs? Who is accountable when an output is wrong?Changes error tolerance, and in regulated settings may be a legal requirement (chapter 4).
Audit and explanationWhat must be logged, for how long, and who may read it? Must a decision be explained to the person affected?Requires traces and retention that must themselves obey privacy rules.
Language and accessibilityWhich languages and scripts? Which accessibility standards?Model quality varies by language; see multilingual documents.
Integration and operationsWhich systems must it plug into? Who runs it and who is on call?A prototype nobody can operate is a demo (chapter 12).
Time and peopleWhen is the decision point? Who on the customer side has time for you?Expert time is usually the scarcest input to evaluation (chapter 3).

Two further questions are worth asking explicitly because customers rarely volunteer the answers. "What would make you switch this off?" gives you the kill criteria: the conditions, written in advance, under which the project stops, such as a serious error in a regulated process, a cost per task above a stated figure, or reviewers rejecting more than a set share of outputs. Having them agreed protects both sides from a slow death in which a failing pilot is kept alive for politics. And "What have you tried before, and what happened?" surfaces the history, including the vendor who promised everything and the earlier pilot that was never evaluated.

Decisions and assumptions you keep on paper

The restatement is one page of fixed things. Alongside it, keep two living logs.

The decision log

A decision record captures a choice that shapes the work: the context, the options considered, what was chosen, who decided and the consequences. A few lines each is enough. "English only for v1; decided by the head of legal operations on 12 March; revisit after pilot; consequence: Spanish contracts go to manual review." Software teams call a similar format an architecture decision record, and Trade-offs, Communication & Design Docs shows how to write them. The value lies in the "who" and the "revisit when": three months later, nobody remembers that the English-only choice was deliberate.

The assumptions and open questions log

Every statement that is not yet known goes here, with an owner and a date: "Contracts are searchable PDFs, not scans: owner Dana, check by Friday". Each entry is either confirmed (and promoted to a constraint), refuted (and the plan changes), or still open. The log is the working list for the prototype in chapter 5, because the riskiest assumption in it is what you build first. A useful technique for filling it is the premortem of Gary Klein (2007): imagine the project has failed a year from now, and ask each person to write down why. The reasons people give are, almost word for word, the assumptions and constraints you have not yet written down.

The one-page restatement

Put it together on one page, in the customer's words where possible. A restatement that runs to twenty pages will not be read, and what is not read is not agreed. A workable outline:

  1. Problem and evidence. Two or three sentences stating the real problem from discovery, with the number and the source ("about 30 analyst-hours a week, from three interviews and two shadowed sessions").
  2. Users and the decision supported. Who uses the output, and what they do with it. This fixes whether a human reviews before anything leaves the building.
  3. In scope and out of scope. With two or three concrete examples of each, because "contracts" means different things to different people.
  4. Success metrics. The table of measure, threshold, dataset, baseline, owner and timing, plus the guardrail metrics.
  5. Constraints and kill criteria. From the checklist, each with the name of the person who stated it.
  6. Decisions so far, each with who and when.
  7. Assumptions and open questions, each with an owner and a date.
  8. Change process and sign-off. Who may approve changes, and who signs.

The restatement is deliberately written by you, not the customer. Writing "this is what I understood" invites correction in a way that "please send requirements" does not, and it exposes misunderstandings while they are cheap. The A8 take-home studios ask for exactly this document as the first deliverable, with the assumptions and questions you would send back.

Contracts, change and honest scoping

The shape of the commercial agreement should match the uncertainty. A fixed-price, fixed-scope contract assumes you know what you are building; for AI work with an unmeasured baseline and an untested data pipeline, that is rarely true, and the vendor ends up either padding the price or cutting quality. A common alternative is to stage the work: pay for a short discovery and baseline, then for a pilot with an agreed gate, then for production. Each stage has its own signed metrics and a decision to continue, change or stop. (Legal terms differ by country and organisation; this is a way to think about the structure, not legal advice.)

Requirements will change, and the document should say how. The simplest change process is a loop: someone proposes a change; you state its effect on the metrics, cost and dates; the owners decide; the restatement is updated and re-signed.

The hardest requirement skill is saying "that is outside what we agreed, and here is what adding it would cost" without making it sound like a refusal. Having the loop agreed in advance turns it from a confrontation into a procedure.

Metrics get gamed

Once a number is agreed, behaviour shifts toward it. This is often paraphrased as Goodhart's law, in Marilyn Strathern's wording: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. A system tuned to the recall target alone will flag everything; a reviewer measured on contracts per hour will skim. The remedy is to agree guardrail metrics alongside the target: recall paired with the review queue length and precision; speed paired with a sampled quality audit. Keep the gold set separate from tuning data, so that you are not grading your own homework, and review the metrics themselves on a schedule. The same hazard in automated form, a system exploiting its evaluator, is the subject of A7's Evaluator Hacking & Reward Hacking.

Systems connection

A success metric with a threshold and a measurement window is a service-level objective by another name, and the room for error is an error budget. Constraints about where data may go are the subject of data protection and privacy, and the people-and-systems questions in the checklist are what a secure design review will later ask. The scale numbers (items a day, peak hour) are the same back-of-the-envelope estimates you used in System Design Studio.

Key takeaways

  • AI requirements are rates on populations, not pass/fail statements. Resolve goal, quality, scope and constraint ambiguity separately.
  • Sign three things: decisions, constraints and success metrics. Keep assumptions and open questions in a log with owners and dates.
  • A usable metric has a measure, threshold, dataset, baseline, owner and timing. "Accurate" is an adjective.
  • State thresholds as interval bounds, and size the gold set to support them. Small samples cannot back strong claims.
  • Errors cost different amounts, and reviewers have finite time. The threshold is a business parameter chosen under a capacity limit, and two reasonable promises can leave only a narrow window or none.
  • Constraints are limits, not goals. Ask about data, models, latency, cost, oversight, audit, language, operations and time, and agree kill criteria.
  • Stage the commitment to match the uncertainty, and agree the change process in advance. Pair every target with a guardrail metric.

Check your understanding

A customer asks for "an accurate contract-review assistant". Which revision is the most useful way to make the requirement testable?

  1. "The assistant should be at least 95% accurate."
  2. "Recall of risky clauses of at least 0.90 (95% lower bound) on a 120-clause gold set labelled by two lawyers, compared with the paralegal baseline of 0.84."
  3. "The assistant must be as accurate as a human."
  4. "Accuracy will be reviewed after the pilot."
The second gives a measure, threshold with uncertainty, dataset, labelling procedure and baseline. The first names a number but not what is counted, on what data, or against what. "As accurate as a human" has no measurement, and deferring the target invites a target chosen to fit the result.

In the operating-point table, the cheapest threshold (0.45) was not chosen. Why?

  1. Its recall was too low.
  2. Its precision was the highest of all thresholds.
  3. It missed more risky clauses than any other threshold.
  4. It flagged 639 clauses, more than the 500 the team can read in a batch.
At 0.45 recall was 0.98, higher than the chosen threshold, and misses were few. The problem was the review queue. Precision was low there (0.32), not highest. The constraint is on reviewer capacity, a reminder that the best threshold on paper may be unusable in practice.

You observe 95 of 100 risky clauses found. The agreed target is "recall of at least 90%". What is the most defensible statement?

  1. The point estimate is 95%, but the 95% interval runs from about 89% to 98%, so the target is probably met but not demonstrated at this sample size.
  2. The target is met because 95 is greater than 90.
  3. The target is not met because 5 clauses were missed.
  4. Nothing can be said without testing on 10,000 clauses.
With 100 positives the lower bound (0.888) falls just below 0.90, so a bound-based target is not demonstrated yet, though it is likely to be met. Stating "met" ignores sampling error; "not met" misreads a rate as perfection. A larger sample is useful, but you do not need thousands: the wilson.py output shows about 200 positives is enough at this observed rate.

With review capacity of 500 flags and a model of fixed quality, the customer raises the required recall from 90% to 95%. According to the widget and chart, what is the likely result?

  1. The threshold just moves slightly and everything still works.
  2. Costs fall because recall is higher.
  3. No threshold satisfies both numbers, so something must change: capacity, model quality, or the target.
  4. The model automatically becomes more precise.
Higher recall needs a lower threshold, which flags more items than the reviewers can read. Two reasonable promises that cannot both be kept are the case to find before signature. Cost does not fall: more flags means more review cost. Precision does not improve by itself.

Which of these is a constraint, not a goal?

  1. "Reviews should be as fast as possible."
  2. "Customer data must stay in the customer's EU region."
  3. "We would like high recall."
  4. "The tool should be pleasant to use."
A constraint is met or broken and cannot be partly traded for other benefits. The other three are goals: they can be satisfied to a degree, and are normally traded against cost or each other.

Halfway through the pilot, the sponsor asks to add a new document type. What does the change process recommend?

  1. Add it quietly, since it is a small change.
  2. Refuse, because the scope was agreed.
  3. Add it and tell the customer about the delay later.
  4. State its effect on the metrics, cost and dates, let the named owners decide, and update and re-sign the document.
Silent additions are how targets slip and trust erodes; blanket refusals ignore that learning legitimately changes needs. The loop makes the decision visible and puts its costs in front of those who decide.
