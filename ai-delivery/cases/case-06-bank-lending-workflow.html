An Expert Workflow for Bank Lending

Sakana AI and MUFG Bank spent about six months building an agent system, called the AI Lending Expert, that helps corporate-lending staff analyse a borrower and draft the internal approval document. The companies' own accounts are unusually specific about how: they did not ask a model for the finished document in one step, they broke the banker's thinking into steps, wrote down the veterans' unstated knowledge as rules, and tuned the system against roughly 1,500 pieces of feedback from the people who would use it. They are also unusually silent on results, which is part of what this case teaches.

The pattern in one sentence

To automate expert work, copy the expert's sequence of steps rather than the expert's output: decompose the task into stages the expert would recognise, store the tacit rules the expert applies as editable knowledge, and run a feedback loop in which the experts' ratings, not the builder's intuition, decide what to fix and whether any automatic judge can be trusted.

Context

In Japanese corporate banking, a loan to a company goes through an internal approval document called a ringi-sho (often shortened to ringi), a written proposal that circulates for sign-off. Before it exists, a relationship manager (RM) who knows the client gathers financial statements and background, and an analyst or credit officer works out what the numbers imply. The same kind of work exists in banks everywhere under names such as credit memo or credit approval paper. It is expert, document-heavy and judgement-heavy, which is why it is attractive to automate and risky to get wrong. The finance chapter's section on lending covers the regulatory side in the US and EU; I did not research Japanese banking regulation and nothing below should be read as a statement about it.

MUFG's press release of 19 May 2025 says MUFG Bank and Sakana AI, a Tokyo AI research company founded in July 2023, entered a "long-term strategic technology partnership for a period of more than three years", and that it would initially apply the expertise of Sakana's The AI Scientist system "to automate the creation of internal and external documents that require complex thinking and advanced specialized knowledge". It also retained Sakana's chief operating officer, Ren Ito, as an AI advisor. The release names no value for the contract. Some press coverage reported a figure; MUFG's release does not state one, so I do not use it.

Ringi (稟議) document
The internal proposal that must be approved before a loan is made. The AI Lending Expert drafts it; officers review and correct it.
Tacit knowledge
What experienced people know and apply but cannot easily state: what makes a borrower's story hang together, which risks matter for which client. See capturing expert knowledge.
Rulebook
The sources' word for the written instructions that tell the system how to do a step; here it holds what was learned from bankers' feedback and is revised as feedback arrives.
AI as judge
Using a model to score another model's outputs automatically. It scales review but must be checked against people. See LLM-as-judge.
Calibration
Comparing an automatic score with human ratings so you know when, and for which criteria, the automatic score can be trusted. See human labels.
Proof of concept (PoC)
A limited trial to show a system can work in real conditions, before a wider rollout. See from proof of concept to production.

What the sources say happened

Pieced together from Sakana's two 2026 articles and the secondary reports, the sequence is:

  1. May 2025. The partnership is announced (MUFG's release). Sakana's Applied Research Engineer on the project, Masato Ota, joins that month, and the project manager, Takato Iida, in July 2025, according to the interview.
  2. About six months of PoC on domestic corporate lending at "some sites" the release does not name. Sakana's release of 6 March 2026 says about 100 people took part, including relationship managers, credit examiners, digital and planning staff, and Sakana's engineers and project managers, with about 30 core members driving development. The system used past approval documents, internal rules and manuals, in what the release calls a secure environment.
  3. March 2026. The system moves to a phase of verification on real cases. MUFG plans a phased start at selected branches and departments, then wider expansion; the release gives no dates.

The release states what the PoC established in general terms: that the system can help streamline a series of complex steps across the main lending types, and that MUFG valued it as more than a document generator, as a support partner for staff from junior to veteran. It gives no numbers. It also says explicitly that AI-generated drafts are premised on verification and correction by bank staff before use. The StartupHub report adds that the stated aim is to free staff for conversations with clients and critical judgements, and that nothing I read says the AI makes the credit decision. In other words, this is a drafting and analysis assistant for expert users, as in the law-firm case, with a person accountable for what is approved.

The mechanism: decompose the expert's reasoning

The interview's central design statement, as the tool translated it, is a policy of not having the AI produce the final deliverable in one go. Ota is quoted by the English reports as saying the team "didn't aim for the AI to produce the final output in one go". Instead the system is a workflow that follows the order in which a person thinks the problem through. The articles list the supported stages as initial analysis, organising information, financial simulation and drafting the ringi. At each step, according to the interview, the AI works autonomously while checking consistency as it goes, an approach borrowed from Sakana's AI Scientist work, which structures research as hypothesis and verification. The detailed steps are described as confidential and are not published.

Two further design choices are worth noting. First, the interview says the veterans' unstated, experience-based judgements were organised into a form the AI can use and accumulated as knowledge, adapting an idea from Sakana's ALE-Agent in which the AI writes out what it learns as it iterates. The recurring practitioner pattern is the one taught in capturing expert knowledge: do not ask experts to describe their rules in the abstract; show them a draft and let their corrections reveal the rule. Second, the design accepts qualitative input. According to the interview, a relationship manager's impressions from talking to the client, such as the client's enthusiasm or intent, can be fed in alongside the documents. That matters because the interview reports one early criticism of the drafts was that they lacked the perspective of the bank's relationship with the client.

The mechanism: a feedback loop calibrated to the reviewers

The interview reports that the early output was criticised bluntly. As the tool rendered the Japanese, the drafts read as lists of facts, were hard to read, and lacked a view informed by the relationship between the bank and the client. Over the evaluation period the team received about 1,500 individual feedback items. They did not process these by hand. The interview says they used AI to classify the issues and extract improvements by theme, then used the themes to update the prompts and rulebooks. They also had the AI score its own answers (AI as judge), and compared those scores with the bankers' ratings to calibrate the judge; that comparison, the interview says, made it clear which problems to fix first. A dedicated accuracy-evaluation owner within the engineering team kept the quality measurement consistent.

Analogy: an apprentice and a master

The loop resembles how an apprentice learns in a craft. The apprentice drafts, the master marks it up, the apprentice's habits change, and over months the drafts stop needing the same corrections. A junior analyst who reads 1,500 comments from senior colleagues improves in the same way, and the interview's line that the system went from a junior's level to something experienced bankers valued has the same shape.

Where it breaks down: an apprentice generalises from a correction to situations the master never mentioned, with judgement the master can later check. The system's rulebook changes only where someone wrote a rule, and it will fail silently on cases no feedback covered. Also, a person who changes their habits can tell you why; a rulebook edit made by an AI classifier may not be understood by anyone.

A toy of the calibration step

The script below is mine and uses invented data; it has nothing from MUFG or Sakana. It does two things the interview describes in general terms. It sorts free-text reviewer comments into themes and ranks them. Then, for three criteria, it compares an AI judge's pass/fail verdicts on 60 drafts against the reviewers' verdicts, using raw agreement and Cohen's kappa (agreement beyond what chance would give; 0 is chance, 1 is perfect). I built the judge to be reliable on factual tie-out and unreliable on the "relationship" criterion, because the interview's early criticism suggests that is the kind of criterion where a model judge would be lenient.

"""Toy: two steps of a reviewer-calibrated feedback loop, on INVENTED data (nothing here is MUFG's or Sakana's).
Step 1: sort free-text reviewer comments into themes and rank them.
Step 2: before trusting an AI judge for a criterion, compare it with the reviewers' own labels (Cohen's kappa)."""
import random
from collections import Counter

# Step 1: a keyword classifier over 12 made-up reviewer comments.
THEMES = {"fact list, no story": ["list of facts", "just numbers", "hard to read"],
          "missing relationship view": ["relationship", "history with the client", "our position"],
          "wrong or untraced figure": ["does not match", "which page", "source"]}
comments = ["Reads as a list of facts", "No view of our relationship with this client", "Which page is this figure from?",
            "Just numbers, no conclusion", "Ignores our position as main bank", "Figure does not match the statements",
            "Hard to read, too long", "Nothing on the history with the client", "Source missing for the ratio",
            "List of facts again", "Does not say what we should do", "Our position as lender is not discussed"]
counts = Counter()
for c in comments:
    hit = [t for t, keys in THEMES.items() if any(k in c.lower() for k in keys)]
    counts[hit[0] if hit else "unclassified"] += 1
print("theme counts:", dict(counts.most_common()))

# Step 2: judge vs reviewer, per criterion, on 60 invented drafts.
def kappa(a, b):
    n = len(a); po = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    pa, pb = sum(a) / n, sum(b) / n
    pe = pa * pb + (1 - pa) * (1 - pb)
    return po, (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

rng = random.Random(7)
# (criterion, share the reviewers pass, chance the judge flips a reviewer 'fail' to 'pass', chance it flips a 'pass' to 'fail')
CRITERIA = [("figures tie to source", 0.70, 0.05, 0.05),
            ("reads as an argument, not a fact list", 0.55, 0.15, 0.10),
            ("reflects the bank-client relationship", 0.40, 0.50, 0.10)]
print(f"{'criterion':40} {'agree':>6} {'kappa':>6}  use the judge?")
for name, p_pass, up, down in CRITERIA:
    human = [rng.random() < p_pass for _ in range(60)]
    judge = [(rng.random() < up) if not h else (rng.random() >= down) for h in human]
    po, k = kappa(human, judge)
    print(f"{name:40} {po:6.0%} {k:6.2f}  {'yes' if k >= 0.6 else 'no, keep reviewers on it'}")
theme counts: {'fact list, no story': 4, 'missing relationship view': 4, 'wrong or untraced figure': 3, 'unclassified': 1}
criterion                                 agree  kappa  use the judge?
figures tie to source                       97%   0.90  yes
reads as an argument, not a fact list       93%   0.84  yes
reflects the bank-client relationship       58%   0.21  no, keep reviewers on it

Raw agreement alone would mislead: 58% sounds middling, but the kappa of 0.21 shows that the judge hardly tracks the reviewers on that criterion. The 0.6 cut-off in the script is my assumption, not a standard; the LLM-as-judge chapter discusses how to choose one. The practical rule: automate the scoring only for criteria where the judge has been shown to agree, and keep reviewers on the subtle ones, which are the criteria the customer cares most about.

Common misconception

"If the AI judge's scores go up, the drafts are getting better." Only if the judge agrees with the reviewers on that criterion. A judge that is lenient on the hardest criterion will report steady progress while the bankers still find the drafts shallow. That is why the comparison with the bankers' ratings is the heart of the loop, not an add-on.

The engagement around the system

The interview is as informative about the project's shape as its technology, and the points are all things a delivery engineer controls. Sakana's team, more than ten people, mixed three roles: Applied Research Engineers who understood the lending work and designed the workflow, a project manager who ran delivery, and software engineers who built the system. The two sides moved toward each other's expertise. The contract was a partnership rather than a commissioned build, which the interview says allowed the team to take on problems beyond the original specification and to change course boldly; MUFG's digital strategy department handled data collection, governance and security requirements and, the interview says, did so unusually fast with strong backing from top management. This is the forward-deployed shape, with expert users inside the build.

The interview also reports a constraint: for security and infrastructure reasons the team could not freely use the newest high-end models, and it says it reached its target accuracy by workflow design rather than by switching to a stronger model. This is the situation of the restricted environments chapter, and it is why decomposition is more than a quality trick: stepwise workflows with narrow tasks and explicit rules let a more modest model reach a bar that a single prompt would not. The target accuracy is not stated.

Systems connection

The staged workflow is a pipeline whose intermediate results can be stored and compared, the same property that lets error analysis find the first failing step in a trace. Reviewer feedback that updates a rulebook is the improvement flywheel with the rulebook as the artefact being versioned. The "secure environment" and the handling of confidential credit files are the questions of security reviews for AI systems.

What is and is not established

  • Reported by the companies: a PoC of about six months with about 100 participants and a core of about 30; a staged workflow that mirrors how bankers reason; about 1,500 feedback items; AI-assisted classification of feedback; an AI judge compared with bankers' ratings; operation under model-selection constraints; human review of every draft; a phased rollout starting in 2026.
  • Not published in anything I read: any accuracy or time-saved figure, the size or design of the evaluation set, how the judge was calibrated (which agreement statistic, which criteria, what threshold), the models used, how many drafts were reviewed, how reviewers' disagreements were settled, the cost, or any error found after launch. "From a junior's level to something veterans valued" is a qualitative impression reported in a company interview.
  • Reasonable cautions, which are mine: the interview and releases are partly recruiting and marketing material (the release ends with a recruitment call), so they emphasise success. The 1,500 items measure effort, not quality. Reviewers who gave feedback and then judged the improvements are not independent of the system. And because humans approve each draft, a plausible but wrong draft could be accepted when the reviewer is busy; how the project guards against this (trajectory review, sampling, second reviewers) is not said.

Lessons for a delivery engineer

Mirror the process, then inspect each step

Decompose by the expert's own stages so each can be graded and fixed. A lender can say "the simulation was wrong" more easily than "the document was bad". See prototype the riskiest assumption.

Let experts' corrections write the rules

Show drafts early and treat each correction as data about a rule nobody stated. Keep the rules readable so a human can audit them: capturing expert knowledge.

Calibrate before you automate scoring

Compare any automatic judge with the reviewers, per criterion, with an agreement statistic. Keep people on the criteria it fails: LLM-as-judge.

Plan for the report you cannot write. The sources give the structure of the work and no results. A customer's validation or audit team will ask for results with intervals on a held-out set from their own cases (evaluation plans customers trust). Decide early what you will be able to show, and keep the evidence, because a "very successful PoC" with no numbers will not survive a risk committee.

Staff the evaluation. The interview singles out a dedicated owner for accuracy evaluation. In small teams the same person builds and grades, which is how optimistic numbers happen.

Name what stays human. The drafts are premised on staff verification; the decision remains with the bank. Say so in the scope document, as in requirements under ambiguity, so the system is not later judged as if it were an autonomous underwriter.

Try it yourself

In judge_calibration.py, change the "relationship" criterion's chance of the judge passing a failing draft from 0.50 to 0.20 and see the kappa. Then change the number of drafts from 60 to 15 and rerun with several seeds: how much does the kappa move? Decide how many drafts the reviewers would need to rate before you would believe a kappa of 0.6, and write down what you would tell a customer who asks why the judge is only used on two of the three criteria.

$ python3 judge_calibration.py

Use it in an interview

Say: "For an expert workflow like credit memos I would decompose the task into the stages the analysts already use, capture their unstated rules from corrections to real drafts, and run a feedback loop in which an AI judge is calibrated against the reviewers criterion by criterion, with agreement reported, before I let it score anything on its own. Sakana and MUFG describe this pattern, with about 1,500 feedback items, though they publish no accuracy numbers."

Don't: present the project as proven to save time or improve accuracy, or call the system an automated lending decision. The sources describe a drafting assistant with human review and no published metrics.

Check your understanding

Why does a staged workflow (analyse, organise, simulate, draft) help more than a single "write the approval document" prompt when working with expert reviewers?

  1. It always makes the final text longer and more detailed
  2. Reviewers can say which step went wrong, so each step can be graded and fixed separately
  3. It removes the need for human review
  4. It guarantees the model never makes an arithmetic error
A staged design makes intermediate results inspectable, which is what lets feedback be specific and fixes be targeted. It does not remove review (the sources say officers review every draft) and it does not guarantee correctness at any step.

An AI judge agrees with reviewers on 58% of drafts for one criterion, and the pass rate is 40%. Another criterion shows 93% agreement. What is the most defensible conclusion?

  1. Use the judge on both, because 58% is better than a coin toss
  2. Drop automatic scoring entirely
  3. Trust the 58% criterion because it is the more important one
  4. Check chance-corrected agreement (kappa) on each; use the judge where it is high and keep reviewers on the weak criterion
Raw agreement is inflated by chance, especially when one outcome is common. A kappa close to zero means the judge barely tracks reviewers on that criterion. Dropping all automation (option 2) wastes the criterion where the judge is reliable.

The sources say about 1,500 feedback items were collected. What does that figure establish?

  1. The scale of the feedback effort, but nothing about final accuracy
  2. That the drafts are right 99% of the time
  3. That an independent audit was done
  4. That every ringi document is now written without human review
A count of feedback items measures effort and input volume. The sources publish no accuracy or time-saved figures, no independent audit, and say drafts are premised on staff review.

The interview reports the team could not freely use the newest high-end models and still reached its target accuracy. Which design choice is the most plausible reason this was possible?

  1. Training a new foundation model for the bank
  2. Using one very long prompt with all the documents
  3. Breaking the work into narrow stages with explicit, improvable rules so a modest model handles each step
  4. Replacing the reviewers' ratings with the model's own
The interview attributes the result to workflow design. Narrow stages and written rules reduce what each model call must get right. A single long prompt (option 2) is the approach the team says it avoided, and replacing reviewers with the model (option 4) would remove the calibration that the loop relies on.
