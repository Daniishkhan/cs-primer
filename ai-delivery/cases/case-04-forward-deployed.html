The Forward-Deployed Engineer

Palantir says it pioneered placing its own software engineers inside customer organisations. From 2025, AI companies and the venture capitalists who back them began hiring "forward-deployed engineers" in large numbers. This case separates what the sources establish about the model (where it came from, what the job is, why it appears now) from the claims that are opinion, and works through the economics that decide whether embedding engineers builds a product or a consultancy.

The pattern in one sentence

Put engineers inside the customer's organisation to find the real problem and make the product work in their environment, and treat what they build as discovery for the product team rather than as a custom deliverable, or the company drifts into being a consultancy.

Context

Enterprise software has an old problem: the product works in the demo and then meets the customer's data, permissions, legacy systems and unwritten processes. Sales engineers and consultants have always bridged the gap. A forward-deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer employed by the product company who works at, or tightly with, the customer to build and configure the solution in situ and carries what is learnt back to the product team. Applied AI makes the gap wider than usual: a language model needs the customer's documents, evaluation data, permissions and a definition of "good", and the customer rarely has these ready.

The role is the one this capstone teaches. Most chapters in this course describe an FDE's tasks without the title: discovery interviews, prototyping the riskiest assumption, evaluation plans and moving from proof of concept to production.

Forward-deployed engineer (FDE)
A vendor engineer embedded with a customer to configure, build and ship a solution on that customer's data and systems.
Embedded
Working inside the customer's team and tools (on site, or in their chat and repositories), not delivering from a distance.
Productisation
Turning a one-off piece of customer work into a reusable product feature. See turning research into products.
Services-led growth
Revenue and learning driven by people-intensive implementation work rather than by software alone. High-touch, lower-margin, and slow to scale unless the work is folded back into the product.
Generalisability
The extent to which what was built for one customer works for the next. The PostHog post says this is secondary for an FDE and primary for a product engineer.

Where the model comes from

Palantir's own job posting calls the role "The Original Forward Deployed Software Engineer", says it "isn't just a job title: it's the blueprint", and says Palantir pioneered the position by "embedding talented engineers directly with our customers". The responsibilities it lists include working alongside engineers on architecture, "wrangling massive-scale data and using AI to accelerate and enhance critical operations", "developing custom applications tailored to customer needs", "engaging directly with customer stakeholders, from technical teams to executives" and owning delivery end to end. That is the company's description of itself, written to recruit.

The PostHog post gives an account of why Palantir did it: even simple demos for intelligence customers could take weeks of agreements and security clearances, so the answer was to place engineers on the customer's site, with the right approvals, to do product discovery and development together. I could not verify that origin story against a Palantir primary source, so read it as the blog's account. What I can confirm from Palantir's August 2020 Form S-1 is that the filing refers, in its risk factors, to "the ability of our forward-deployed engineers to assist our customers in identifying new use cases, modernizing their data architectures, and achieving success with data-driven initiatives." That shows the role was part of how Palantir described its business to public-market investors in 2020, and that the company treated it as a source of both new use cases and risk.

Restricted settings are where this model started, and they remain where it is hardest to replace: classified or air-gapped environments, regulated data, customers who cannot copy their data out. The practical issues are in restricted environments and security reviews for AI systems.

Why it spread to AI companies

Storyboard18, relaying a Financial Times report, said on 3 November 2025 that Indeed data showed postings for applied-AI roles rising more than 800% between January and September 2025, that OpenAI, Anthropic and Cohere were recruiting forward-deployed engineers, that OpenAI had formed a forward-deployed team earlier in 2025 and planned about 50 engineers, and that Anthropic projected a fivefold increase in its applied-AI team by the end of the year. I could not check the underlying FT or Indeed figures, and other outlets quote different growth numbers over different windows, so I use only this one and label it as relayed.

The Pragmatic Engineer issue (apparently May 2026) reports a second wave. Its author cites announcements that on 11 May OpenAI launched "The OpenAI Deployment Company", a standalone entity funded by $4 billion of private equity at a $14 billion valuation, and that it acquired a UK AI company, Tomoro, which has 150 FDEs. He also cites a 4 May Anthropic announcement of a new enterprise AI services company with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman and Goldman Sachs among its investors. Google Cloud's chief executive announced FDE hiring for a new AI organisation. These are the author's reports of company announcements; I did not read the announcements. The author's inference, which is his own, is that FDEs at the external companies are seen as not "core" to the labs.

The reason for the surge that the sources give is consistent: businesses want AI but struggle to adapt it to their own contexts (Storyboard18), and companies selling to large customers can absorb the cost of onsite work when contracts are large (PostHog). The mechanisms of why adaptation is hard are the subject of this course: the customer's real problem is not the stated one, data is messy, and "good" is not defined.

What the job is, and what is only opinion

The PostHog post lists what an FDE does: meets customers to understand the problem, designs, writes and tests software for those needs, configures existing products for a customer, and relays customer feedback to core product teams. It says roles now vary from product development to sales to revenue operations, with some closer to technical consultants or sales engineers. The Pragmatic Engineer author, drawing on conversations with FDEs at OpenAI and Ramp about a year earlier, describes the work as a mix of platform engineering, software engineering and solutions engineering, and estimates (his estimate, not measured data) about 25% coding, 50% integration and plumbing, and 25% meetings and customer work. The PostHog post offers a comparison table, unattributed, in which FDEs spend 60% to 80% of their time with customers against 10% to 20% for software engineers, and travel 20% to 50% of the time. Neither set of numbers comes from a survey I could check.

Both posts are candid that the model is not free. PostHog says the initial costs are large and can take years even with one customer; that if the usual product-market-fit playbook is working you should not hire FDEs; and that the model fits best when the product needs deep integration and has large margins, when the industry is heavily regulated, or when entering a new vertical where the company "doesn't yet know what it doesn't know". The Pragmatic Engineer author argues that, at external companies, the role is close to a solutions architect or consultant, and that it suits people who like shipping end to end more than people who want well-engineered, long-lived systems. These are opinions from people with their own interests, which I report as such.

Common misconception

"An FDE is a consultant who codes." The difference is the direction of travel. A consultant delivers a customer-specific result and leaves; a forward-deployed engineer's work is meant to feed a product, so success is judged partly by how much of it the next customer does not need. The Pragmatic Engineer author argues that, in practice, many external FDE roles drift towards the consultant end, which is exactly the risk. The sources do not provide measurements of how often that happens.

The economics: custom work that folds back, or does not

Whether embedding engineers builds a product or a consultancy comes down to one parameter: how much of each customer's custom work is absorbed into the product so that the next customer needs less. The script below is an illustrative model with assumed inputs (24 FDE-weeks for the first customer, $6,000 per FDE-week); nothing in it is taken from a company's data. It shows what the PostHog post implies: if nothing is folded back, eight customers cost eight times the first; if 60% of each customer's custom work is absorbed, the total is a small multiple of the first.

"""ILLUSTRATIVE model (all inputs are assumptions): embedded engineers vs the product.
Each customer needs custom work; the product team folds a share of it back into the product."""
custom_weeks_first = 24      # ASSUMED FDE-weeks the first customer needs
for fold_back in (0.0, 0.3, 0.6):
    total, work = 0.0, custom_weeks_first
    row = []
    for customer in range(1, 9):
        total += work
        row.append(work)
        work *= (1 - fold_back)       # share absorbed into the product, so the next customer needs less
    print(f"fold-back {fold_back:.0%}: FDE-weeks per customer {[round(w) for w in row]}  total {total:.0f}")
print()
weekly_cost = 6000           # ASSUMED fully loaded cost per FDE-week
for fold_back in (0.0, 0.6):
    work = custom_weeks_first; total = 0
    for c in range(8):
        total += work; work *= (1 - fold_back)
    print(f"fold-back {fold_back:.0%}: 8 customers cost ${total*weekly_cost:,.0f}; per customer ${total*weekly_cost/8:,.0f}")
fold-back 0%: FDE-weeks per customer [24, 24, 24, 24, 24, 24, 24, 24]  total 192
fold-back 30%: FDE-weeks per customer [24, 17, 12, 8, 6, 4, 3, 2]  total 75
fold-back 60%: FDE-weeks per customer [24, 10, 4, 2, 1, 0, 0, 0]  total 40

fold-back 0%: 8 customers cost $1,152,000; per customer $144,000
fold-back 60%: 8 customers cost $239,843; per customer $29,980

Real life is lumpier: each customer has a share of unique work that never folds back, product teams have a limited capacity to absorb requests, and some customer requests would make the product worse for everyone else. That last point is the delivery engineer's judgement call: when you say no to a request because it does not generalise, you should be able to say why, in terms the customer's sponsor accepts. The conversation is part of requirements under ambiguity.

State of play, October 2026

The structures described in the Pragmatic Engineer issue (a standalone OpenAI deployment company, an Anthropic-backed services company, Google Cloud FDE hiring) are announcements from around May 2026 as reported by one newsletter author; I did not read the announcements. Hiring-growth figures differ by source and window (800% for January to September 2025 as relayed from the Financial Times; higher figures elsewhere that I could not trace). Job titles, pay and the split between "product" and "services" FDE roles change quickly. Check current postings before you describe the market to anyone.

What is and is not established

  • Established by company documents I read: Palantir describes embedding engineers with customers as its model (job posting), and told investors in 2020 that its forward-deployed engineers help customers find use cases and modernise data architecture (S-1).
  • Reported, relayed: a sharp rise in FDE-style postings in 2025, and new organisational structures at AI labs in 2026.
  • Opinion: the time split, the claim that the role is drifting towards consulting, who it suits, and the economics. These are plausible and useful but not measured.
  • Unknown from these sources: how often embedded work actually folds back into products, FDE retention, and the margin of FDE-led deals.

Lessons for a delivery engineer

Find the real problem on site

Embedding gives you the access to ask about past behaviour, watch the work and see the data. See discovery interviews and capturing expert knowledge.

Send learning back

Write down what generalises and hand it to the product team. See turning research into products.

Exit with the customer able to run it

Production gates, support, change management and handover are the finish line, not the demo. See proof of concept to production.

Scope is the main risk. An embedded engineer hears every request first-hand, and the pressure to say yes is high. The remedy is the same as in any engagement: written requirements, a prioritised list, and an agreed definition of success from requirements under ambiguity and evaluation plans. The "white glove" tone some job advertisements use should not mean "never refuse".

Security travels with you. An FDE often holds access to a customer's data and systems. Least privilege, audit logging, and handling of secrets are not optional; see security reviews and authorization.

Write for the sponsor. Your customer's executive sponsor reads status reports, not code. Learn the genre in writing for customers.

Hiring and careers. If you aim for the role, the portfolio and interview material in take-homes, portfolios and interviews is the practical preparation, and the take-home Productise a Research Prototype rehearses the loop in the figure above.

Try it yourself

Edit fde.py. Change the first-customer effort to 12 weeks and the fold-back rate to 15%, 30% and 45%. At what fold-back rate does the total for eight customers drop below twice the first customer's effort? Now list three requests a customer might make (a new report format, a connector to an obscure system, a rule specific to their industry) and decide for each whether it would fold back. What does that tell you about the realistic rate?

Use it in an interview

Say: "The forward-deployed model, which Palantir says it pioneered, embeds vendor engineers in the customer to find the real problem and make the product work on their data. The test of whether it is working is the cost of the next deployment: what we learn should fold back into the product."

Don't: describe the role as glamorous or claim figures such as 25% coding as fact; they are one commentator's estimate.

Check your understanding

What most distinguishes a forward-deployed engineer from a consultant, in the sense these sources use?

  1. The FDE works for the customer
  2. The FDE never writes code
  3. The FDE's work is meant to feed the vendor's product, so learning is carried back and the next deployment needs less custom work
  4. The FDE is paid more
The distinguishing loop is productisation. Without it the engineer is delivering custom work, which is consulting. The first option is wrong because the FDE is employed by the product company.

In the illustrative model, why does the total cost for eight customers fall so sharply with a 60% fold-back rate?

  1. Engineers work faster
  2. Each customer's custom work is 60% smaller than the previous one's, because the product absorbed it
  3. There are fewer customers
  4. The weekly cost falls
The effort for customer n is the first customer's effort times 0.4 to the power n−1. The inputs are assumptions, so the shape, not the numbers, is the lesson.
