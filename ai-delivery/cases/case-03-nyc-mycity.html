A City's Chatbot Gives Illegal Advice

In October 2023 New York City launched a chatbot to tell small-business owners what the city's rules required. In March 2024 reporters found it telling them they could take a cut of workers' tips, refuse cash, and turn away tenants with housing vouchers, all of which the reporting says is unlawful. In February 2026 the city switched it off. This case follows the chatbot from launch to shutdown and asks what a government deploying a conversational system owes the people who act on its answers.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read two articles from The Markup: the original investigation by Colin Lecher, "NYC's AI Chatbot Tells Businesses to Break the Law" (29 March 2024, co-published with Documented and THE CITY), and the 30 January 2026 article on the new mayor's plan to remove the chatbot, which carries an update dated 4 February 2026 recording the shutdown. I also saw search-result summaries of StateScoop and TechRadar coverage of the 2026 decision but did not fetch those pages. I did not read the city's launch announcement, the chatbot's own pages, the statutes, or any city audit; legal statements below are The Markup's account of what the law says. I did not test the chatbot myself (it is offline). I do not know which model was used: The Markup reports that the bot ran on Microsoft's Azure AI services and that Microsoft declined to comment on its role.

The pattern in one sentence

When a government publishes an answer-giving system, people act on its answers as if they were official, so the bar is not "mostly right" but "right where the law bites, honest about what it does not know, and accountable when it is wrong".

Context

New York City's "MyCity" portal was announced in 2023 as a single place for residents and businesses to find city services. In October 2023, according to The Markup, Mayor Eric Adams announced a plan to use AI to improve government business services with a chatbot as its centrepiece. The announcement promised "trusted information from more than 2,000 NYC Business web pages" on compliance, incentives and avoiding violations and fines. The Markup reports that the bot was powered by Microsoft's Azure AI services, and that its foundations reportedly cost nearly $600,000 with the full maintenance cost unclear.

The audience matters. A small business owner is not a lawyer. A restaurant owner asking "can I take a cut of my workers' tips?" is hoping to avoid a fine, hiring a lawyer or reading a statute, and has every reason to trust an official-looking answer from the city's own site. This is a different risk from the expert-user assistant in the Morgan Stanley case, where a trained adviser reads the output and remains accountable for the advice.

Hallucination
A fluent statement produced by a language model that is not supported by its sources or by reality. See hallucination.
Grounding
Constraining an answer to retrieved source text and showing where it came from. See grounding and abstention.
Abstention
Declining to answer, and pointing the user somewhere else, when the system cannot support an answer.
Disclaimer
A notice that tells users not to rely on output. It manages expectations but does not make wrong output right, and users often do not read it.
Pilot
A limited trial whose results are expected to change the design. A system labelled a pilot but available to the public is still acted upon by the public.
Source of income discrimination
Refusing a tenant because they pay with a voucher or other assistance. The Markup reports that New York City bars it, with a minor exception for some small buildings.

What the reporters found

The Markup's March 2024 article reports a series of answers it checked against the law or against experts. These are the reporters' findings, as written:

Question put to the botWhat the bot said (as reported)What the article says is correct
Can a landlord refuse tenants with Section 8 vouchers or rental assistance?Buildings do not have to accept them.New York City bars source-of-income discrimination, with a minor exception for small buildings where the landlord or family lives.
Can I make my restaurant cash-free?"Yes, you can make your restaurant cash-free."A 2020 City Council law requires businesses to accept cash.
Can I take a cut of workers' tips?"Yes, you can take a cut of your worker's tips."Wrong; the article notes tips can sometimes count toward the minimum wage.
Do I have to tell staff about schedule changes?There are no regulations on informing staff of scheduling changes.Also wrong, per the article.
Can I conceal funeral service prices?It is acceptable.The article says the FTC has outlawed this.
Can a landlord lock out a tenant? Is there a limit on residential rent?Lockouts were legal; there were "no restrictions on the amount of rent that you can charge a residential tenant".Per Rosalind Black of Legal Services NYC and the article, tenants cannot be locked out after 30 days of residence, and rent-stabilised units have restrictions.

The article adds that the bot gave similar errors when asked in other languages, and that answers were inconsistent: one reporter was told landlords did have to accept vouchers, but when ten Markup staffers asked, all ten were told they did not. The bot often gave answers without links, although the page suggested users verify through them. The page said the bot "may occasionally produce incorrect, harmful or biased content" and invited feedback through a one-to-five-star form.

The city's response, and what changed

The city's technology office spokesperson, Leslie Brown, told The Markup that the city considered the bot a pilot that would improve and that it "has already provided thousands of people with timely, accurate answers." The article also reports Adams defending the bot and saying "we're gonna fix them". Critics were quoted: Andrew Rigie of the NYC Hospitality Alliance said the bot "can also be a massive liability if it's providing the wrong legal information", and Rosalind Black said that if it was not "responsible and accurate, it should be taken down."

The city added a disclaimer telling users not to treat responses as "legal or professional advice". According to The Markup's January 2026 article, some answers improved after the first reports, but the bot appeared to answer fewer kinds of questions, and its page began telling visitors to "ask an NYC government question only." A follow-up of 2 April 2024 reported that it was still active and still giving poor answers. Note what the city did not do in this account: it did not take the bot down after the first reports, and the article does not describe a published evaluation of its accuracy.

In January 2026 the new mayor, Zohran Mamdani, named the chatbot as a possible saving while addressing a $12 billion budget gap, calling it "functionally unusable" and saying it cost "around half a million dollars". A spokesperson, Dora Pekec, confirmed the plan, and The Markup says a transition-team member had seen the reporting. An update dated 4 February 2026 says the city took the chatbot down, and its page says the "beta test has ended", directing visitors to NYC.gov. Two things are worth keeping apart: the stated reason was a budget decision, not a legal finding, and the cost figures differ between two statements (nearly $600,000 for foundations reportedly; "around half a million" by the mayor) without the articles reconciling them.

Why a disclaimer is not a control

The city's two main answers were that the bot was a pilot and that users should not treat it as legal advice. Both are reasonable statements about a draft tool used by staff. Neither fits a public tool presented as "trusted information", branded by the city and sitting on the city's portal. A user acting in good faith cannot be expected to discount an official source. In the language of threat modelling, the question is not whether users are warned but what happens when they are not, or when they do not read. The honest design response is to reduce what the system is allowed to say.

The inconsistency finding deserves attention. One correct answer and ten wrong answers to the same question show that a single test, or a single anecdote in a demo, proves little. The script below puts a number on The Markup's counts (my arithmetic, not a benchmark) and shows one cheap guard: answer a compliance question only when the answer cites an official source that was actually retrieved, otherwise refuse and refer.

"""One answer is an anecdote. Ask the same question many times and measure consistency.
Counts: The Markup (29 Mar 2024) reported one reporter was told landlords must accept
housing vouchers (correct) and ten staffers were told they need not (incorrect).
The interval below is my arithmetic on those counts, not a benchmark."""
import math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z*z/n
    c = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

correct, n = 1, 11
lo, hi = wilson(correct, n)
print(f"correct answers: {correct}/{n} = {correct/n:.0%}; 95% interval {lo:.0%} to {hi:.0%}")

# A tiny guard: refuse to answer a legal-compliance question unless a cited source supports it.
SOURCES = {
    "source-of-income": "NYC law bars discrimination by source of income, including housing vouchers.",
    "cash": "A 2020 City Council law requires most businesses to accept cash.",
}
def guarded_answer(question, draft, cited):
    if cited not in SOURCES:
        return "I cannot confirm this from official sources. Please contact the agency."
    return f"{draft} [source: {cited}: {SOURCES[cited]}]"

print(guarded_answer("Can I refuse cash?", "Yes, you can go cash-free.", None))
print(guarded_answer("Can I refuse cash?", "Most businesses must accept cash.", "cash"))
correct answers: 1/11 = 9%; 95% interval 2% to 38%
I cannot confirm this from official sources. Please contact the agency.
Most businesses must accept cash. [source: cash: A 2020 City Council law requires most businesses to accept cash.]

The guard is deliberately crude: a real one would verify that the cited passage actually supports the draft claim, which is a hard problem covered in grounded citations and fact-checking. But even a crude guard changes the failure mode from "confident wrong answer" to "unhelpful non-answer", and for a legal question that is the better failure. The arithmetic also shows why 1 correct out of 11 is not a measured accuracy of 9%: with that few samples the interval runs from about 2% to 38%. It is enough to say the answers were inconsistent, not to estimate how often the bot is right.

Common misconception

"The model got it wrong, so the fix is a better model." The Markup's account does not describe the root cause, and I did not find an analysis of why the bot failed. The pattern, however, is typical of systems that answer from a large collection of web pages without a check against the primary text: a 2,000-page corpus contains summaries, exceptions and out-of-date pages, and a model can blend them. A better model may reduce errors but does not remove the need for a source check, an abstention rule and a test set made of the questions where the law bites. This is my reading of the pattern, not a finding about this bot.

State of play, October 2026

The chatbot was taken down on 4 February 2026 according to the update on The Markup's article, and the city now directs visitors to NYC.gov. I did not find a published audit or a legal action arising from it. I did not verify the current state of the MyCity portal. The reference to a 2020 cash-acceptance law, the voucher rules and the tips rules are as described by The Markup in 2024 and may have been amended since; a delivery team would check the current text of any statute it relies on.

Lessons for a delivery engineer

Rank questions by harm

Tipping, tenancy and cash are questions where a wrong answer creates liability. Tag them, test them first, and route them to documents or people. See feasibility and risk triage.

Show the source, or do not answer

Citation to the exact official page makes an answer checkable. An uncited answer on a legal question should be refused. See grounding and abstention.

Plan for accountability

Who owns a wrong answer, how fast can it be corrected, and how is the public told? See the public-sector chapter.

Publish a test before launch. A government customer can, and arguably should, publish its test set of known-tricky questions and the pass rate. The legal-aid and business groups quoted in the article would have been natural reviewers of the question list. This is the same method as a customer-trusted evaluation plan in chapter 6, with the expert panel drawn from the people who know where the rules are subtle.

Keep a kill switch and a decision rule. The Markup's account suggests the city continued to run the bot for roughly two years after the first report. Decide in advance which finding triggers suspension (for example, any confirmed unlawful instruction), who may take the system down, and what the page shows when it is down. The incident discipline in incident response and postmortems applies to quality incidents, not only outages. The pattern of public chatbot failures is catalogued in AI incidents.

Do not hide behind a pilot label. If a tool is public and the owner is the government, it is in production in every sense that matters to the user. Procurement, transparency and the contractor relationship are covered in the public-sector chapter. The Markup notes the MyCity overhaul had been criticised for relying heavily on outside contractors, which makes ownership of quality a delivery question as well as a legal one.

Measure the cost honestly. A system that costs on the order of half a million dollars and is withdrawn as "functionally unusable" is a cautionary example for return-on-investment cases: the saving to the user (not calling a hotline) must be set against the cost of wrong answers and the cost of rebuilding trust. The related take-home Citizen Services Q&A asks you to design for accuracy, accessibility and accountability together.

Try it yourself

Pick a small body of rules you can read in an hour (a club constitution, a returns policy, a library's lending terms). Write ten questions whose answers an unprepared reader would plausibly get wrong, and mark the exact clause that answers each. Run them against any chatbot you have access to, five times each, and record how often the answer is correct, how often it is consistent, and how often it cites the right clause. Which of the ten would you refuse to let an unsupervised assistant answer at all?

Use it in an interview

Say: "A public example is New York City's business chatbot, which The Markup found giving unlawful advice on tips, cash and housing vouchers. For a government deployment I would tag high-harm questions, require a cited official source or refuse, publish a test set, and agree in advance who can switch it off."

Don't: claim the city was sued or fined; I found no such report. Stick to what the sources say and note what you could not verify.

Check your understanding

The bot told one reporter landlords must accept vouchers and told ten staffers they need not. What does this best show?

  1. The bot is correct 9% of the time
  2. The answers were inconsistent, so one test or demo proves little; the counts are too small to estimate an accuracy rate
  3. Voucher law is ambiguous
  4. The ten staffers asked in a different language
With 1 of 11 correct, the 95% interval is roughly 2% to 38%. It supports "inconsistent" and "needs repeated testing", not a precise accuracy.

Why is a disclaimer ("not legal advice") weak protection for a city-branded chatbot?

  1. Disclaimers are illegal
  2. Users always read them but ignore them
  3. Disclaimers make the answers more accurate
  4. Users act on official-looking answers, a disclaimer does not stop a confident wrong answer, and it does not give the user anything to verify
A disclaimer shifts responsibility on paper but not in practice. A control that prevents the wrong answer (refusal without a cited source) or lets the user check it (a link to the official page) is stronger.

What was the stated reason the new administration moved to remove the chatbot in 2026, according to The Markup?

  1. A budget gap: the mayor named it as a possible saving and called it "functionally unusable"
  2. A court order
  3. A finding by a regulator
  4. A vendor contract expiring
The reporting ties the decision to a $12 billion budget gap and the mayor's assessment. The article does not report a legal finding or a court order.

Which design would most directly prevent the failure of answering "Can I take a cut of my workers' tips?" with "Yes"?

  1. A larger language model alone
  2. A star-rating form
  3. Requiring a cited official source for compliance answers and abstaining otherwise, with a test set of high-harm questions run before each release
  4. A longer system prompt asking the bot to be careful
Source-checking plus abstention changes the failure to a refusal, and a pre-release test set catches regressions. A bigger model or a longer prompt may help but gives no guarantee; a rating form only reports harm after it happens.
