A Research Assistant for Financial Advisers

Morgan Stanley's wealth-management division put a GPT-4 assistant over roughly 100,000 of its own research documents and, according to OpenAI's account, reached more than 98% adoption among adviser teams. The interesting part for a delivery engineer is not the model but the gate: a set of evaluations built with the firm's own experts that decided what was good enough, found where the system was actually weak, and kept running after launch.

The pattern in one sentence

Before a model is allowed near professional users, build an evaluation with their experts that names the tasks, the grading rubric and the failure you fear most, and keep it running as a regression test, because it will tell you where the system is weak, and that is rarely where you expected.

Context

Morgan Stanley's wealth-management business has thousands of financial advisers who answer client questions about products, markets and procedures, and who draw on a large internal library of research and process documents. The firm has a long history of managing model risk (the regulatory discipline is covered in the finance chapter), and it is a bank where a wrong statement to a client can become a compliance matter. That makes it a good case of an assistant deployed to expert users in a regulated setting, where the question is not "can it chat?" but "who decides it is good enough, and how?".

The assistant is internally called "AI @ Morgan Stanley Assistant". Qorus's article (20 September 2023) reports that an initial GPT-4-based assistant was introduced in March 2023 and that by September 2023 it was fully operational for all financial advisers and their support teams, answering from a repository of about 100,000 research reports and documents. The firm had spent months curating the content and used human experts to test it, according to that article, which gives no detail on grading criteria. Advisers are asked to write full-sentence questions rather than keywords.

Eval (evaluation)
A repeatable test of a system on a defined set of tasks with a defined way of scoring the results. See eval harnesses.
Golden set (gold set)
A collection of inputs with answers or judgements agreed by experts, used to score the system. See building benchmarks.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
Answering with a model that is first given passages fetched from a document collection. See RAG end to end.
Document recall
The share of the relevant documents for a question that the retrieval step actually returns. If a needed document is not retrieved, the model cannot use it.
Regression test
A test that runs after every change to catch quality that used to be good and has got worse.
Adviser team
A financial adviser plus support staff. OpenAI's wording is that over 98% of adviser teams actively use the assistant, which is not the same as 98% of individual advisers.

The mechanism: an evaluation framework built with experts

As quoted from OpenAI's story, Morgan Stanley "ran summarization evals" to test GPT-4 against its experts' work, and advisers and prompt engineers graded the model's responses for accuracy and coherence. Those grades fed prompt refinements. The story is quoted as adding that "the eval framework wasn't static; it evolved as the team learned", growing to cover other tasks such as translation for multilingual clients. A search summary of the same story says daily regression testing was part of the compliance controls; I could not confirm that wording, and the ZenML summary of the OpenAI talk puts it differently, saying the evaluation system was integrated with continuous integration and deployment, reaching a daily deployment and testing cadence.

The ZenML summary, again a secondary account of a talk, describes how the evaluation matured:

  1. Five prototype test cases that the speaker called "vibes-based". Each specified the kinds of documents to retrieve, the analytical insight an adviser would want, and the expected length and detail. One example asked for a comparison of the investment outlook for two companies.
  2. Observation of real use, then limited rollouts with thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback. The summary says thumbs were used as a way to find problem cases, not as the quality metric, and that human review of flagged cases mattered because some negative feedback came from people misunderstanding what the tool was for.
  3. Small curated datasets labelled by humans, plus automated recall metrics for retrieval.

This sequence is worth copying because it is the one that works in practice: start with a handful of hand-checked cases to make the task concrete, harvest real failures from limited use, then promote the failures into a labelled set that gates changes. It is the loop taught in error analysis and the improvement flywheel, and the customer-facing version is evaluation plans customers trust.

What the evaluation found: retrieval, not the model

The most useful claim in the sources is about where the problem was. The ZenML summary says the evaluation showed document recall, not summarisation, was the main problem, and that recall (it also uses the phrase "document corpus utilization" for the same change) improved from 20% to 80% after the team worked on retrieval: whole-document versus paragraph-level embeddings, chunk sizes, and ways to retrieve complete documents versus specific passages with enough surrounding context. I would treat the 20% and 80% as indicative, since it is a third party's summary and the metric definition is not given.

Whatever the exact numbers, the shape is general and easy to demonstrate. End-to-end quality is roughly the product of retrieval and generation. If the model summarises correctly 95% of the time when it has the right document, a retriever that finds the right document 20% of the time caps the system near 19%, and improving the retriever to 80% lifts it to about 76%. You would never find that by grading the summaries alone. See relevance metrics, chunking and RAG failures.

"""Why an end-to-end eval beats a model-only eval: quality = retrieval x summarisation.
Recall figures 20% and 80% are those reported in a ZenML summary of an OpenAI talk;
the summarisation accuracy and sample sizes are ILLUSTRATIVE assumptions."""
import math, random

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z*z/n
    c = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

summ = 0.95  # ASSUMED: summariser is right 95% of the time when given the right document
for recall in (0.20, 0.80):
    print(f"retrieval recall {recall:.0%} x summary accuracy {summ:.0%} -> end-to-end ~{recall*summ:.0%}")

print()
print("How many graded questions before a 'pass' means something? (observed 90% correct)")
for n in (10, 30, 100, 300):
    k = round(0.9 * n)
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    print(f"  n={n:>3}: {k}/{n} correct -> 95% interval {lo:.0%} to {hi:.0%}")

print()
random.seed(7)
print("Gate check: release only if the LOWER bound of the interval clears 85%")
for n, true_p in ((30, 0.90), (300, 0.90), (300, 0.84)):
    k = sum(random.random() < true_p for _ in range(n))
    lo, hi = wilson(k, n)
    print(f"  n={n:>3}, true rate {true_p:.0%}: {k}/{n}, lower bound {lo:.1%} -> {'ship' if lo >= 0.85 else 'hold'}")
retrieval recall 20% x summary accuracy 95% -> end-to-end ~19%
retrieval recall 80% x summary accuracy 95% -> end-to-end ~76%

How many graded questions before a 'pass' means something? (observed 90% correct)
  n= 10: 9/10 correct -> 95% interval 60% to 98%
  n= 30: 27/30 correct -> 95% interval 74% to 97%
  n=100: 90/100 correct -> 95% interval 83% to 94%
  n=300: 270/300 correct -> 95% interval 86% to 93%

Gate check: release only if the LOWER bound of the interval clears 85%
  n= 30, true rate 90%: 28/30, lower bound 78.7% -> hold
  n=300, true rate 90%: 267/300, lower bound 85.0% -> hold
  n=300, true rate 84%: 258/300, lower bound 81.6% -> hold

The second half of the script addresses the other question a sceptical risk committee asks: how many graded questions are enough? With ten, a 90% pass rate has a 95% interval from about 60% to 98%, which is no evidence at all. With three hundred the interval narrows to roughly 86% to 93%. The last block shows a decision rule that I would propose to a customer (my suggestion, not Morgan Stanley's): ship only if the lower bound clears the agreed bar. Notice that even with 300 questions and a true rate of 90%, one seeded run produced a lower bound of 85.0% against a bar of 85%, and the gate said hold. A gate with a statistical rule will sometimes hold a system that is truly fine; that is the price of not shipping systems that are not. See eval statistics.

Common misconception

"98% adoption means the assistant is 98% accurate." Adoption is how many adviser teams actively use the tool (OpenAI's wording, as quoted: over 98% of adviser teams). It is a usage measure. It tells you the tool is useful enough to open and says nothing about the rate of wrong answers, which the sources do not report. In regulated work, adoption and error rate must be tracked on separate lines.

Adoption and later expansion

In October 2024 CNBC (republished by NBC News on 23 October 2024) reported that Morgan Stanley had rolled out "AskResearchGPT", a GPT-4-based tool over the bank's research, in its institutional securities group in summer 2024. The bank produces more than 70,000 research reports a year, and the tool is embedded in browsers, Microsoft Teams and Outlook. Per that report, close to half of the bank's 80,000 employees use generative AI tools built with OpenAI, employees ask about three times as many questions as they did with a traditional AI tool used since 2017, and the bank says a salesperson takes one-tenth of the time to answer an average client inquiry with the tool. These are the bank's figures as reported by CNBC. The article describes no formal testing process, so it supports the adoption story, not the evaluation story. The head of research, Katy Huberty, is quoted as saying she does not see a near-term path to machines writing research reports or generating investment ideas.

As quoted from OpenAI's story, David Wu, head of firmwide AI product and architecture strategy, said the firm went from being able to answer 7,000 questions to a place where it can "effectively answer any question" from its library. Jeff McMillan, head of firmwide AI, is quoted as saying the technology "makes you as smart as the smartest person in the organization", and the story adds that the evaluation framework "has also unlocked a flywheel for future solutions and services". Read these as the vendor's and client's marketing voice: they are useful for showing what the sponsors cared about, not as evidence of quality.

What is and is not established

  • Established by sources I read: the assistant exists, it answers over a curated library of roughly 100,000 documents, the firm used human experts to test it for months before the September 2023 rollout, and OpenAI and the bank report very high adoption among adviser teams.
  • Reported, unverified by me: the evaluation design (summarisation evals graded by advisers and prompt engineers), the 20% to 80% recall improvement, and the daily testing cadence.
  • Not published in anything I read: accuracy scores, the size of the gold set, how disagreements between expert graders were resolved, and incident rates. A reader should not infer that the system is accurate to any stated level.

Lessons for a delivery engineer

Build the eval with their experts

Advisers graded the outputs, so the standard was theirs, not the vendor's. In capturing expert knowledge you turn that judgement into a rubric. A customer will trust a result they helped define.

Evaluate the pipeline, not only the model

Score retrieval and generation separately, then together. The reported finding that recall limited quality is a common pattern in RAG systems.

Keep the gate after launch

Models, prompts and documents all change. A gold set that runs on every change is a monitoring asset, and a basis for the audit trail that finance customers require.

Curate before you retrieve. The Qorus article stresses months of content curation. A research library contains superseded reports, drafts and conflicting views; if the corpus is not governed, the assistant will faithfully quote the wrong one. Access control matters too: an assistant that can retrieve a document the adviser is not allowed to see leaks it (see filters and permissions).

Keep the human accountable. The assistant answers an adviser, not a client. The adviser still owns the advice, which is a lower-risk deployment than a chatbot talking to the public. Compare the public-sector case where the user is a member of the public with no expert in the loop.

Reference the regulation, not just the practice. In US banking, supervisors expect effective challenge and ongoing monitoring of models (the Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 guidance on model risk management, discussed in chapter 8). A gate like the one above is the practical form of that expectation; I did not find a source that says Morgan Stanley framed its framework that way, so that link is my reading.

Try it yourself

Choose a document set you know, such as the documentation of a tool you use. Write five questions, with the document that answers each. Count how often a search (even Ctrl-F) puts the right document in the top five. That is your document recall. Then change the retrieval (split documents by page versus by heading) and measure again, before you touch any model. Finally take the numbers from ms.py, change the summary accuracy to 99%, and see how little the answer changes while recall is low.

Use it in an interview

Say: "In a regulated, expert-user deployment I would agree the tasks and rubric with the users first, start with a handful of hand-checked cases, grow a labelled set from real failures, score retrieval and generation separately, and gate each change on the lower confidence bound. Morgan Stanley's reported experience, that retrieval was the weak link, is why."

Don't: quote "98% adoption" as a quality number, or present the 20% to 80% recall figure without saying it comes from a third-party summary of a talk.

Check your understanding

The reported evaluation showed document recall rather than summarisation was the main problem. If summary accuracy given the right document is 95% and recall is 20%, roughly what end-to-end quality should you expect?

  1. About 95%
  2. About 58%
  3. About 19%
  4. About 75%
The product is 0.20 × 0.95 = 0.19. Quality is capped by the weaker stage, which is why grading summaries alone would have hidden the problem. Raising recall to 80% gives about 76%, not 95%.

OpenAI's wording is that over 98% of adviser teams actively use the assistant. What does that tell you?

  1. How widely the tool is used, but nothing about the rate of wrong answers
  2. That 98% of answers are correct
  3. That 98% of individual advisers were surveyed
  4. That the evaluation gold set had 98% agreement
Adoption is a usage measure. It would be plausible even for a tool with a meaningful error rate if advisers verified its answers before using them.

A team grades 10 answers, gets 9 right, and wants to ship. What is the best response?

  1. Ship, since 90% beats the 85% bar
  2. Ask for 9,000 answers
  3. Ignore the sample and use thumbs-up counts
  4. Hold: with 10 samples a 90% result has an interval from about 60% to 98%, so grade more and gate on the lower bound
The point estimate hides large uncertainty at small n. Three hundred questions narrows the interval to roughly 86% to 93%. Thousands are not needed to make a decision; the wrong answer is to treat ten as conclusive.

According to the ZenML summary, what role did thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback play?

  1. It was the final quality metric
  2. It was a way to find problem cases that humans then reviewed, because some negative feedback reflected misunderstanding of the tool
  3. It replaced the gold set
  4. It was used only for legal sign-off
Thumbs are a cheap sampling signal, biased by who bothers to click and by expectations. They are useful for harvesting failures, not for measuring accuracy.
