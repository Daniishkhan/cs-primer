Klarna's AI Assistant

In February 2024 Klarna announced that an OpenAI-powered assistant had handled two-thirds of its customer-service chats in its first month, work it equated to 700 full-time agents. In May 2025 its chief executive said the company would hire humans again. This case reads both statements side by side, asks what each number actually measures, and draws out what a delivery engineer should promise, measure and keep when automating a customer-facing job.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read Klarna's own press release of 27 February 2024, which is the source of the launch numbers. I also fetched and read three trade-press articles: Customer Experience Dive on the Q3 2025 earnings call (20 November 2025), Maginative's account of the May 2025 interview with Bloomberg, and Customer Experience Dive's February 2026 report of a podcast interview. I could not read the Bloomberg interview itself (the site returned an access error), so the May 2025 statements below come from Maginative and from search-result summaries of other outlets, and I say so where it matters. I did not read Klarna's quarterly filings or earnings slides directly; the Q1 and Q3 2025 claims are as reported by Customer Experience Dive. Nothing here is independently audited: customer satisfaction, savings and agent-equivalents are all the company's figures.

The pattern in one sentence

A headline automation number (chats handled, agents replaced) is a claim about volume and cost, and a customer-facing system also has to be judged on the conversations the automation cannot or should not handle, so the human route has to be designed, staffed and measured from the start.

Context

Klarna is a Swedish payments company best known for "buy now, pay later" credit. Its customer service handles questions that arise whenever money moves: where is my refund, why was I charged, how do I dispute an invoice. Those are high-volume, repetitive, and tied to a customer's balance, which is why they were an early target for a large language model (LLM) assistant, a text-generation model that can follow instructions and call tools (see the tool loop). They are also a place where a wrong answer costs money or trust: a promise to refund that is not honoured, or a loop that never reaches a person.

Two features of the case make it valuable for a delivery engineer. First, it is unusually well documented from the company's side over two years. Second, the story is easy to retell wrongly, as either "AI replaced 700 people" or "AI failed and humans came back". The sources support neither. What they support is a more useful reading: a real automation gain, a headline that invited an over-simple conclusion, and a company correcting its operating model when the cost-first framing ran into customer experience.

Containment
The share of conversations that the automated assistant finishes without handing over to a person. A volume measure, not a quality measure.
Full-time-agent equivalent (FTE)
A conversion of workload into the number of full-time staff who would be needed to do it. It depends on assumptions about handling time and working hours, which the announcement did not publish.
Escalation
The handover of a conversation from the assistant to a human. A good system makes this easy and records why it happened.
Repeat inquiry
A customer who contacts support again about the same issue. Klarna used its fall as a quality signal.
Elapsed resolution time
The clock time from a customer's first message to a resolved chat. It includes waiting and is not the same as the labour spent.

The claim: February 2024

Klarna's press release, datelined New York on 27 February 2024, announced that its assistant, which it said was powered by OpenAI, had been live globally for one month. The company reported that it had handled 2.3 million conversations, which was two-thirds of Klarna's customer-service chats, doing work it described as equivalent to 700 full-time agents. It said customer errands were resolved in under two minutes, down from eleven, that repeat inquiries fell by 25%, and that the assistant was available in 23 markets, around the clock, in more than 35 languages. It also said the assistant was "on par" with human agents on customer satisfaction score, and estimated a $40 million profit improvement in 2024. Customers could still choose a live agent, the release said.

These are the company's statements. The release does not describe how satisfaction was measured, how the agent-equivalent was calculated, or how "resolved" was defined. That is normal for a press release, but it matters for how much weight each number can bear.

Reading the numbers: what "700 agents" requires

An agent-equivalent is a conversion, so it can be checked for plausibility. The release gives enough to try. Spread 2.3 million conversations over 700 agent-equivalents and each handles about 3,300 conversations a month. The script below shows what that implies, using the release's figures plus one labelled assumption (160 productive hours per agent per month). It also shows a point that is easy to miss: the eleven-minute figure is elapsed resolution time, which includes waiting, not labour. If you multiplied 11 minutes by 2.3 million chats and called that human work, you would need about 2,600 agents. The 700 therefore implies a much smaller labour share per chat, which is plausible (many chats are quick lookups, and human agents can run several chats at once) but is a modelling choice the reader cannot see.

"""Sanity-check a headline: what must be true for '700 agents' to hold?
Inputs are figures from Klarna's 27 Feb 2024 press release; assumptions are labelled."""
conversations = 2_300_000      # press release: first month
agents_equiv = 700             # press release: 'equivalent to 700 full-time agents'
elapsed_before_min = 11        # press release: resolution time before (elapsed)
elapsed_after_min = 2          # press release: 'under 2 minutes'

per_agent = conversations / agents_equiv
print(f"conversations per agent-equivalent per month: {per_agent:,.0f}")

hours_available = 160          # ASSUMPTION: productive hours per agent-month
for label, concurrency in [("one chat at a time", 1), ("2 chats at once", 2), ("3 chats at once", 3)]:
    minutes_per_chat = hours_available * 60 * concurrency / per_agent
    print(f"{label:>18}: {minutes_per_chat:5.1f} agent-minutes available per chat")

print()
print("If resolution time were pure labour, 11 min x 2.3M chats =",
      f"{conversations*elapsed_before_min/60:,.0f} hours =",
      f"{conversations*elapsed_before_min/60/hours_available:,.0f} agents")
print("So the '700' figure implies a smaller labour share than elapsed time,")
print("e.g. concurrency, waiting time, or conversations too easy to need 11 minutes.")

# Cost-of-error view (ILLUSTRATIVE assumptions, not Klarna data)
print()
print("Illustrative: automated share vs hidden cost of escalation")
auto_cost, human_cost, redo_cost = 0.40, 5.00, 5.00   # ASSUMED $ per chat
for fail in (0.05, 0.15, 0.30):   # share of automated chats that fail and reach a human anyway
    blended = auto_cost + fail * redo_cost
    print(f"  automated chat fails {fail:4.0%}: cost per chat ${blended:.2f} vs human-only ${human_cost:.2f}")

None of this says the 700 figure is wrong. It says the number is a modelling output, and that the second half of the script, which is purely my illustration with assumed dollar amounts, is where the delivery risk lives: the cheaper the automated path looks per chat, the more tempting it is to let its failures pile up unmeasured. If 15% of automated chats fail and the customer then reaches a person anyway, the cost saving shrinks but, more importantly, the customer has had a worse experience for the same eventual cost. Cost per conversation is not cost per resolved problem.

Common misconception

"The assistant replaced 700 people." The release says the assistant did work equivalent to 700 full-time agents. That is a statement about workload, not about who was dismissed. In February 2026 Klarna's chief executive said, on a podcast reported by Customer Experience Dive, that nobody was laid off: Klarna stopped using outsourced agents, and those workers moved to other customer-service companies. Separately, the same report says headcount fell from 7,000 to 3,000 through AI use, while Maginative's 2025 account gives a goal of going from 5,000 to 2,000 by attrition. The baselines differ between sources, so treat headcount figures as the company's rough statements, not a measured series.

What happened next: 2025

The timeline below is assembled from the three trade-press sources. It is not a complete history, only the points they report.

May 2025. Maginative, writing on 8 May 2025 from Bloomberg's interview at Klarna's Stockholm headquarters, reports that chief executive Sebastian Siemiatkowski said the automation push "went too far", that cost had been too dominant a factor in organising support, and that "really investing in the quality of the human support is the way of the future for us." He said customers should know "there will be always a human if you want". Klarna was piloting an "Uber type of setup": remote workers, drawn from students, rural residents and passionate Klarna users, who log in to handle support. The same article says customers complained about the assistant's limits and that service quality declined. That last claim is the outlet's summary; I could not trace it to a measurement.

Q1 and Q3 2025. Customer Experience Dive reports that Klarna's first-quarter 2025 report claimed "no drop in consumer satisfaction" after the rollout, and that its third-quarter presentation said satisfaction with the assistant was on par with human agents, with a net promoter score of 73. On the Q3 call (reported 20 November 2025) the chief executive said the assistant now did the work of more than 853 full-time agents, up from 700 at the start of the year, and that it had saved $60 million. The same article notes that customer-service and operations costs were $50 million in the quarter, up from $42 million a year earlier. That is a total-cost line affected by many things, including a business that grew (revenue up 26% and active users up 32% year on year, per the article), so it does not show that automation failed. It does show why "savings" needs a baseline.

The critic. Kate Leggett, a vice-president and principal analyst at Forrester, told Customer Experience Dive that Klarna "overpivoted on their AI strategy" and "overpivoted to cost containment, without thinking about the longer-term impact of customer experience." She also said the questions Klarna gets are "highly repetitive and fairly simple", which is "a perfect candidate for AI", and that simple inquiries would be fully automated while nuanced ones would fall to humans. She wondered how much of the cost saving was timed for the initial public offering. Note what she is and is not saying: the use case was a good fit, but the operating model was unbalanced. She is a commentator, not a party with access to Klarna's data.

February 2026. Customer Experience Dive, reporting a podcast with the chief executive, says Klarna's plan is for the assistant to answer easy questions and for human help to be a premium option. He is quoted: "We said the future of VIP experience will be the human connection." He argued that when AI makes service cheap and universal, human contact becomes the scarce good. Klarna recruits agents from its own customers for part-time support work, and he called customer satisfaction with Klarna-hired agents "through the roof", a claim I treat as the company's.

State of play, October 2026

The most recent account I found is the February 2026 podcast report; I did not find later primary statements from Klarna, so I cannot say how the mix of automated and human service looks today. The 853-agent and $60 million figures were stated on the Q3 2025 earnings call and I have not seen independent verification. Check Klarna's latest quarterly materials before quoting any of these figures in a customer conversation.

What the sources do and do not support

  • Supported: an LLM assistant took a large share of chat volume in a repetitive, well-bounded domain, and the company reports faster resolution and fewer repeat contacts. The Forrester analyst agrees the domain is a good candidate.
  • Supported by the company's own words: in 2025 the chief executive said cost had been over-weighted and that the human option needed investment.
  • Not established by these sources: that satisfaction fell because of the assistant (the company reports parity, and the "quality declined" claim is not traced to data), or that the cost savings were illusory (the company reports growing savings, the cost line is confounded by growth).
  • Unknowable from outside: how the agent-equivalent was computed, how satisfaction was sampled, and how many conversations were escalated.

That last point is the lesson in miniature. A delivery team that wants to be believed, by its customer's finance team or by a regulator, publishes how each number was produced. See evaluation plans customers trust for what an agreed metric definition looks like, and human labels for why a satisfaction score from a different population (people who stayed in the chat) is not a like-for-like comparison.

Lessons for a delivery engineer

Agree what "done" means

"Handled" is not "resolved". Define resolution with the customer (no repeat contact within, say, seven days), and report containment and resolution as separate lines. See chapter 6.

Design the human route first

Escalation is a product feature with staffing, routing and context hand-over. Ship it before the assistant takes volume, not after complaints. See chapter 12.

Publish the arithmetic

Any equivalent or saving needs a baseline, an assumption list and an interval. Cost per resolved problem beats cost per chat. See chapter 14.

Watch for the cost-first frame. The chief executive's own diagnosis was that cost became too predominant an evaluation factor. A project steering group that sees only a cost dashboard will optimise it. Counter this by putting a quality metric and an error budget beside the cost metric from the first week, as in SLIs, SLOs and error budgets, and by defining what triggers a roll-back. The generic technique for controlling quality, cost and latency together is in cost, latency and quality.

Match automation to the shape of the work. The Forrester comment about "highly repetitive and fairly simple" questions is a feasibility triage: send the head of the distribution to the assistant and keep the long tail for people. That is the framing of Is AI the right tool? The assistant in this case also acts on accounts (refunds, disputes), which makes it an agent with permissions, not only a text generator; the risks of that are in agent security and the refund-handling take-home Support Automation with Refunds.

Be careful what you announce. A first-month number invites a conclusion the data cannot yet support. If the pitch is for an investor audience, the risk is higher. The practical rule for customer communication is in writing for customers: say what was measured, over what period, and what you do not yet know.

Try it yourself

Take a workload you know (a support queue, an internal help desk, a document intake). Write down the conversations per month, the share an assistant could plausibly handle, and the share of those that would fail and still need a person. Run the numbers from klarna.py with your own inputs, then write one sentence that a customer's finance lead could repeat without overstating the result. Finally list the two quality metrics you would put beside the cost line, and the value of each that would make you stop the rollout.

Use it in an interview

Say: "A public example is Klarna's assistant. The company reported two-thirds of chats handled and an agent-equivalent figure, then said in 2025 that cost had been over-weighted and it was hiring humans again. I would report containment and resolution separately, show how any equivalent was computed, and staff the human route before launch."

Don't: say "Klarna's AI failed" or "AI replaced 700 people". Neither is what the sources say, and an interviewer who has read them will notice.

The February 2024 release says the assistant did work equivalent to 700 full-time agents. What is the most accurate reading?

  1. Klarna dismissed 700 employees in 2024
  2. It is a workload conversion that depends on unpublished assumptions about handling time and hours
  3. It is a measured count of agents who left the company
  4. It proves customers were as satisfied as before
An agent-equivalent converts volume into staff using assumptions the release does not publish. It is not a dismissal count (Klarna's chief executive later said no one was laid off, with outsourced agents moving to other providers), and it says nothing about satisfaction.

Why can the 11-minute "before" resolution time not be multiplied by 2.3 million chats to size the human workforce?

  1. Because the figure was in seconds
  2. Because humans work faster than the assistant
  3. Because the press release gave no chat volume
  4. Because it is elapsed time including waiting, not hours of labour, and agents may handle several chats at once
Elapsed resolution time is clock time. Doing the multiplication anyway gives about 2,600 agents, far above the 700 claimed; the gap shows how much the conversion depends on how labour is defined.

Which diagnosis did Klarna's chief executive give in May 2025, as reported by Maginative?

  1. Cost had been too dominant a factor in organising support, so quality of human support needed investment
  2. The OpenAI model had been withdrawn
  3. Regulators had banned automated support
  4. Customers preferred longer waits
The reported diagnosis was a balance problem, not a technology ban. The same chief executive continued to describe AI as central, and Klarna kept the assistant.

Customer-service and operations cost was $50 million in Q3 2025 against $42 million a year earlier, as reported. What can you conclude?

  1. The assistant failed to save money
  2. The $60 million saving claim is false
  3. Very little, because the total cost line is affected by growth and other factors, so you need a baseline for the specific workload
  4. Human agents are cheaper than the assistant
The business grew over the same period (revenue up 26%, per the article), so a rising total says nothing alone. The tempting first option confuses a total with a counterfactual comparison.
