Rolling Out AI in a Law Firm

In February 2023 Allen & Overy announced that a GPT-based assistant was going to more than 3,500 of its lawyers. Four months later a New York court sanctioned two lawyers for filing cases that a chatbot had invented. The next two years produced a Stanford measurement of how often legal research tools are wrong, an American Bar Association opinion on what lawyers owe their clients when they use such tools, and an English High Court judgment telling managing partners to do more than issue guidance. Read together, these sources describe a rollout problem that is mostly not about the model: when a professional signs every answer, adoption is a verification culture.

What I could and could not read

I fetched and read four primary documents: the court's 43-page Opinion and Order on Sanctions in Mata v. Avianca (22 June 2023); the Stanford preprint "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools" (Magesh et al., submitted 30 May 2024); ABA Formal Opinion 512 (29 July 2024); and the Divisional Court's judgment in Ayinde and Al-Haroun, [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), on the National Archives case-law site. The two court documents and the ABA opinion were read in full text; for the Stanford paper I read the abstract, introduction, results and discussion sections. The Ayinde judgment was fetched through a summarising tool, so paragraph numbers below are as that tool reported them and the quotes should be checked against the original.

I did not read Allen & Overy's own announcement. The firm's rollout is known from the Law Gazette report of 15 February 2023 (fetched and read), which relies on the firm's and OpenAI's statements. I read the Choi, Monahan and Schwarcz randomised trial only through its abstract page. No source I found publishes adoption rates, accuracy audits or incident counts from inside a law firm, and I have not invented any; the "What is and is not established" section says what that leaves open.

The pattern in one sentence

When every output must be checked by a professional who is personally accountable for it, a rollout succeeds or fails on the verification rule (what must be checked, by whom, against what source, at what cost) far more than on the model's average accuracy, so design that rule first, make it cheap to follow, and measure whether the net time saved survives it.

Context: why law is the hard case for expert users

Most of this course deals with systems whose users are customers. A law firm is different in three ways that all point the same direction. The users are experts who know the work better than the tool does. They are individually accountable: a lawyer who signs a filing owns every sentence in it, whatever helped draft it. And the work product is text, so a plausible mistake is hard to see by skimming. The chapter on legal and compliance covers what the engineering looks like (citation checking, document review recall, confidentiality architecture). This case is about the other half, which that chapter's "Rolling out in a firm" section only summarises: what happened when firms and courts met these tools, and what rules came out of it.

The earlier case on Morgan Stanley's adviser assistant is the closest sibling: expert users, a regulated setting, a gate built with the experts. The difference is the failure cost. An adviser's wrong answer reaches a client who may complain; a lawyer's wrong citation reaches a judge who can sanction.

Hallucination (in the Stanford paper)
A response that is incorrect (a false statement of law) or misgrounded (it cites a source that does not support the claim). A real case cited for something it does not say still counts.
Verification rule
A written statement of what must be checked before AI output is relied on, by whom, and against which independent source. This is the firm's rule, not the vendor's.
Rule 11
The US federal rule under which a lawyer who signs a court filing certifies, after reasonable inquiry, that its legal contentions are warranted. It is the basis of the sanctions in Mata.
Duty of competence
The professional obligation (ABA Model Rule 1.1) to have the knowledge, skill and thoroughness the work requires, which the ABA reads as including understanding the technology you use.
Wasted costs order
An English court order making a lawyer personally pay costs incurred through improper, unreasonable or negligent conduct. It was used in Ayinde.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
Answering with a model that is first given passages retrieved from a trusted collection. See RAG end to end.

The rollout that started the conversation: Allen & Overy

The Law Gazette reported on 15 February 2023 that Allen & Overy (A&O), a London-headquartered international firm, was introducing Harvey, a platform built on an OpenAI model adapted for legal work. According to the report, the firm had trialled Harvey in beta since November 2022 under its markets innovation group; by the end of the trial about 3,500 of its lawyers had asked it around 40,000 queries for client work; and it would now be available to more than 3,500 lawyers across 43 offices. David Wakeling, who headed the group, called it "a game-changer", and the report says output needs careful review by an A&O lawyer.

What the report does not contain is as informative as what it does. There are no accuracy numbers, no time-saved figures and no cost figures; "we saw some amazing results" is the only evidence offered. The figure "3,500" appears both for people who tried it and for people who would receive it, and the report does not say whether these are the same group. That is not a criticism of the firm, which was making a launch announcement, not publishing a study. It is a reminder that access numbers are not adoption numbers and neither is a quality number, the same warning as in the Morgan Stanley case. If a customer asks "what did the pilot show?", "40,000 queries" is an answer to a different question.

What happens when the verification rule is missing: Mata v. Avianca

The clearest public record of a verification failure is a court opinion. Roberto Mata sued Avianca over an injury on a flight. His lawyers' opposition to a motion to dismiss, filed on 1 March 2023, cited decisions that did not exist. The sanctions opinion of 22 June 2023, by Judge P. Kevin Castel of the Southern District of New York, sets out the sequence, and it is worth reading as an incident timeline.

The facts the opinion establishes are these. The lawyer who did the research, Steven Schwartz, used ChatGPT. His firm, Levidow, Levidow & Oberman, normally practised in New York state courts and subscribed to a legal research service called Fastcase but not to Westlaw or LexisNexis; for this federal matter the firm's Fastcase account had limited access to federal cases. Schwartz said he had assumed ChatGPT was "a super search engine". After the defendant questioned the cases, he asked ChatGPT "Is Varghese a real case" and "Are the other cases you provided fake"; it answered that the cases were real and could be found through Westlaw, LexisNexis and the Federal Reporter. He filed those screenshots with his May affidavit.

Three points matter to a delivery engineer. First, the court was explicit that using an AI tool was not the offence: "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance", but the rules "impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys". The sanction rested on the failure to check and on the conduct afterwards: the court found bad faith based on "acts of conscious avoidance and false and misleading statements to the Court". Second, asking the model to verify itself is not verification; the independent source was the thing missing. Third, the firm's available independent source was weak for this task, a pattern where the sanctioned individual had no cheap way to check, which is a rollout failure as well as a personal one. The court imposed a $5,000 penalty, jointly and severally, on the two lawyers and the firm, and required letters to the judges whose names had been attached to invented opinions.

Common misconception

"Using a legal-specific tool with real sources solves this." It reduces the problem; it does not remove it, as the next section shows. And the Mata failure was not about which tool. It was about a signature on a filing that nobody had checked against a source that exists.

Measuring the tools: how often are they wrong?

Vendors responded to the hallucination problem by grounding answers in their own legal databases with retrieval, and some described the result in strong terms. The Stanford paper quotes marketing that promised "100% hallucination-free linked legal citations" (Lexis), and that a product "does not make up facts, or 'hallucinate'" (Casetext), and that Thomson Reuters said it avoided hallucinations by relying on trusted content. Magesh, Surani, Dahl, Suzgun, Manning and Ho (Stanford RegLab and Human-Centered AI, 2024) tested the claims.

They built a preregistered set of 202 legal queries covering general research questions, jurisdiction- or time-specific ones, false-premise questions (where the question contains a mistaken view of the law) and others, ran them through Lexis+ AI, Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research and Thomson Reuters's Ask Practical Law AI, and compared GPT-4 with no retrieval. Lawyers graded each response on two dimensions: whether it was correct and whether it was grounded (whether the cited source supports the claim). Their evaluation of the legal tools was completed in April 2024, and the authors note that LexisNexis later released a second-generation product their results do not cover.

The paper's error taxonomy is as useful as its headline. Westlaw's answers were the longest (an average of 350 words, against 219 for Lexis+ AI and 175 for Ask Practical Law AI), so they contained more claims that could be wrong, and, as the authors put it, lengthier answers "require substantially more time to check", because every proposition and citation has to be evaluated. They also observed a design pattern that worked against checking: in one case Westlaw's tool stated a rule from a case that had been overruled without citing the case, which the authors suspect results from suppressing citations that a citator flags. The authors call this suppression behaviour dangerous because it "impedes verification of the claims most likely to be false".

The authors give the dilemma this creates for a firm in one sentence: lawyers must "either verify by hand each and every proposition and citation produced by these tools (thereby undercutting the efficiency gains that AI is promised to provide), or risk using these tools without full information about their specific risks and benefits (thereby neglecting their core duties of competency and supervision)." They also note that a firm's own assessments struggle for the same reason: citing a 2024 report on law-firm evaluations, they quote that the need to verify "makes any efficiency gains difficult to measure", and they report that one large firm's internal evaluation of Harvey had published no quantitative results. The authors' own caveat is a sample of 202 queries, which is small, from a benchmark of research questions that is not a firm's actual workload.

What a verification rule costs

The efficiency argument is testable with arithmetic. The script below combines the paper's Figure 4 rates with a time model I made up: 10 minutes to do a research task by hand, 3 to prompt and read an answer, 6 to verify one answer, and a full redo by hand whenever the answer is hallucinated or incomplete. The minutes are my assumptions and the rates are from 2024 products on a research benchmark; the point is the shape, not the numbers.

"""Toy: what a verification rule costs, using the Stanford rates (Magesh et al., 2024, Figure 4)
and an illustrative time model. The minutes are my assumptions, not measurements."""
RATES = {  # (accurate, incomplete, hallucinated) shares of 202 queries, from the paper's Figure 4
    "Lexis+ AI":        (0.65, 0.18, 0.17),
    "Westlaw AI-AR":    (0.42, 0.25, 0.33),
    "Ask Practical Law": (0.20, 0.63, 0.17),
    "GPT-4":            (0.49, 0.08, 0.43),
}
HAND, PROMPT, CHECK = 10.0, 3.0, 6.0   # minutes: do it by hand / ask and read / verify one answer

print(f"{'tool':18} {'halluc':>6} {'P(>=1 bad in 10)':>17} {'min per task':>13}")
for name, (acc, inc, hal) in RATES.items():
    p_batch = 1 - (1 - hal) ** 10
    # Verify every answer. A bad or incomplete answer is caught and redone by hand.
    redo = hal + inc
    minutes = PROMPT + CHECK + redo * HAND
    print(f"{name:18} {hal:6.0%} {p_batch:17.0%} {minutes:13.1f}")
print(f"{'by hand':18} {'':>6} {'':>17} {HAND:13.1f}")

# Break-even: the longest verification time (minutes) at which using the tool still beats doing it by hand.
print("\nbreak-even minutes to verify one answer")
for name, (acc, inc, hal) in RATES.items():
    be = HAND - PROMPT - (hal + inc) * HAND
    print(f"{name:18} {be:5.1f}")

Lexis+ AI             17%               84%          12.5
Westlaw AI-AR         33%               98%          14.8
Ask Practical Law     17%               84%          17.0
GPT-4                 43%              100%          14.1
by hand                                              10.0

Lexis+ AI            3.5
Westlaw AI-AR        1.2

GPT-4                1.9

Under these assumptions a "check everything" rule makes every tool slower than working by hand, and even the best one only breaks even if verifying an answer takes 3.5 minutes. The middle column explains why skimming fails: with a 17% hallucination rate, 84% of a batch of ten answers contains at least one bad one. Change CHECK and HAND to what you believe about your own work, and the sign can flip, which is exactly why the next two sources focus on which checking is proportionate.

The rule-makers: what the ABA and an English court asked of firms

ABA Formal Opinion 512 (29 July 2024) applies existing Model Rules of Professional Conduct to generative AI; it creates no new rule, and a state bar is not bound by it. Several of its positions are directly about rollout design.

  • Verification is proportionate, not absolute. The opinion says the appropriate amount of independent verification "will necessarily depend on the GAI tool and the specific task". Its worked example: a lawyer using a tool to summarise many lengthy contracts would not necessarily have to review every document if the lawyer "had previously tested the accuracy of the tool on a smaller subset of documents by manually reviewing those documents, comparing them to the summaries produced by the tool, and finding the summaries accurate".
  • Responsibility cannot be delegated. "Regardless of the level of review the lawyer selects, the lawyer is fully responsible for the work on behalf of the client." Lawyers may not leave tools alone to give legal advice or perform functions that need their personal judgement.
  • Firms must have a policy and train people. Managerial lawyers "must establish clear policies regarding the law firm's permissible use of GAI", and supervisory duties include training on the tools' capabilities and limits. A footnote suggests marking all material produced by AI tools as such when it is stored in a client or firm file, so later users know it may be fallible.
  • Confidentiality and fees. The opinion says informed client consent is required before inputting client information into certain tools (the self-learning ones), and that hourly billing must reflect time actually spent: 15 minutes of prompting plus review time is billable, but not the hours the work would have taken.

The English High Court went a step further on who must act. In R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey and Al-Haroun v Qatar National Bank, [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), a Divisional Court considered two cases in which submissions contained fictitious authorities. According to the judgment as I read it, five cited cases in Ayinde did not exist, and 18 of the 45 citations in a schedule in Al-Haroun did not exist. The court said freely available generative AI tools such as ChatGPT "are not capable of conducting reliable legal research"; that lawyers must check AI research against authoritative sources; and that people with "individual leadership responsibilities (such as heads of chambers and managing partners)" must take "practical and effective measures" so everyone understands their duties, adding that issuing guidance alone is "insufficient" and that the court can be expected to ask in future whether those measures were taken. In Ayinde a first-instance judge made wasted costs orders of £2,000 each against the individual lawyer responsible and the law centre; the Divisional Court declined contempt proceedings but referred lawyers to their regulators.

Put the two documents beside the Stanford paper and a design brief emerges. The ABA says verification should scale with task and prior testing. Stanford says the tools' error rates are high enough that the testing has to be done by the firm, because vendors publish no benchmarks. The English court says the firm's leaders are the ones accountable for making sure it happens.

The mechanism: a verification policy that people can follow

A policy that says "check everything" produces either abandonment (the time model above) or box-ticking. The sources point to a tiered design: classify tasks by what a mistake costs, decide what evidence of checking each tier needs, and make the evidence cheap to produce.

Tier 1 is where the ABA's sampling idea does its work, and statistics gives a number to it. If you review n outputs and find no errors, you have not shown the error rate is zero, only that it is probably below some bound. The script computes the exact 95% upper bound (the largest true error rate still consistent with what you saw) for a few review sizes. It is the same reasoning as the confidence intervals in statistics for evals.

"""Toy: how many reviewed outputs justify trusting a summariser on the rest?
Exact one-sided 95% upper bound (Clopper-Pearson) on the error rate after n reviewed outputs."""
from math import comb

def upper_bound(errors, n, alpha=0.05):
    """Largest error rate p still consistent (at level alpha) with seeing <= `errors` in n."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    for _ in range(60):
        p = (lo + hi) / 2
        cdf = sum(comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p) ** (n - k) for k in range(errors + 1))
        if cdf > alpha: lo = p
        else: hi = p
    return hi

print("errors found / reviewed -> 95% upper bound on the true error rate")
for errors, n in [(0, 10), (0, 20), (0, 59), (0, 100), (1, 100), (3, 100), (0, 300)]:
    print(f"  {errors} / {n:<4} -> {upper_bound(errors, n):6.1%}")

  0 / 20   ->  13.9%
  0 / 59   ->   5.0%
  0 / 100  ->   3.0%
  1 / 100  ->   4.7%
  3 / 100  ->   7.6%
  0 / 300  ->   1.0%

Twenty clean reviews cannot rule out an error rate near 14%; about 60 clean reviews are needed to say it is probably under 5%, and 300 to get to about 1%. That is a sound basis for an internal first draft that a lawyer will read anyway, and no basis at all for a filing, where one error is one too many. The tier decides which statistic is appropriate, and the sample must be drawn from the firm's own tasks (see evaluation plans customers trust), because Stanford's 202 research questions say little about a particular firm's contract summaries.

Where the gains are

None of this means the tools are not useful. Choi, Monahan and Schwarcz ("Lawyering in the Age of Artificial Intelligence", Minnesota Law Review, 2024) ran what they describe as the first randomised controlled trial of AI assistance on legal analysis: law students were randomly assigned to complete realistic tasks with or without GPT-4, and the work was blind-graded and timed. From the abstract: GPT-4 gave only slight and inconsistent improvements in quality, but large and consistent gains in speed, and where it helped quality at all, the lowest-skilled participants benefited most. A later study by Schwarcz and colleagues on newer tools reports larger quality effects, but I have seen only secondary summaries of it and do not rely on its figures. The honest reading is that speed gains are real in studied tasks; whether they survive a firm's verification rule is the question the Stanford paper raises and nobody I read has answered with firm data. See also cost models and return on investment, including the billing point: under hourly billing a faster lawyer earns less unless pricing changes.

State of play, October 2026

The Stanford numbers describe products tested in April 2024; both vendors have since released new versions, and models have changed more than once. Treat the 17% to 33% range as a measured baseline for a moment in time and as a reason to measure again, not as the current rate. The Ayinde judgment's appendix lists fabricated-citation cases from several jurisdictions after Mata, so the problem has not gone away. Check your jurisdiction's current rules, since they are changing, and nothing here is legal advice.

What is and is not established

  • Established by documents I read: the facts and timeline of Mata and the sanction imposed; the Stanford error rates for 2024-era tools on a 202-query set; what ABA Opinion 512 and the Ayinde judgment say; that A&O announced a rollout to over 3,500 lawyers in February 2023.
  • Reported, with a caveat: A&O's trial query counts (from the firm's statements via a news report); the Choi et al. findings (abstract only).
  • Not published in anything I found: how many lawyers at any firm use these tools weekly, how many errors firms' own checks catch, how much time verification takes in practice, or how any firm's verification policy is written. The tier diagram above is therefore a design proposal, and the time model is an assumption set.

Lessons for a delivery engineer

Write the rule before the pilot

ABA 512 expects clear policies and training. Get the sponsor to sign a tiered verification rule, and decide what evidence of checking is recorded, before anyone is given access. See legal and compliance.

Make the check cheap

Show the source passage beside every claim, link each citation to the document, and block output with an unverifiable authority. Design so checking is the easy path, using citation verification and grounded citations.

Measure the net, not the draft

Time saved after verification, on matched tasks, with errors found on a sample by senior lawyers. Report intervals, as in eval statistics.

Do not let the model grade itself. In Mata, ChatGPT's assurance that the cases were real was submitted as evidence. For an independent check use a source outside the model, and read why models hallucinate for why self-reports are unreliable.

Give the person a legitimate tool. The sanctioned firm turned to a consumer chatbot because its approved research service lacked federal coverage. A firm that withholds a good tool does not stop people reaching for a bad one; this is the shadow-IT problem of security reviews and the confidentiality point in privacy and data handling.

Put leaders on the hook. The English court's point about managing partners and heads of chambers is a stakeholder map: the person accountable for the policy is rarely the person who tries the tool. Plan the sponsor conversation as in from proof of concept to production.

Use it in an interview

Say: "In a firm, I would start by agreeing a tiered verification rule with the partners: sampling for internal drafts once the tool is tested on the firm's own tasks, a reviewer for client work, and every authority opened for anything filed. Then I would measure net time saved after verification, because Stanford's 2024 study found 17% to 33% hallucination even in retrieval-based legal tools."

Don't: quote A&O's "40,000 queries" as evidence of value, or say a tool with citations is safe to skim because the citations are real. A real case cited for the wrong proposition counts as a hallucination in the Stanford paper.

Check your understanding

In Mata v. Avianca, the lawyer asked ChatGPT whether the cases it had supplied were real, and it said they were. What does this show for a rollout?

  1. The check was sound but the model gave a wrong answer on that occasion
  2. Asking the model about its own output is not independent verification, so the rule must name an outside source
  3. Consumer chatbots should be replaced by a vendor tool and then no check is needed
  4. The lawyer should have asked the question twice and compared answers
A model's assurance comes from the same process that produced the error. The court's concern was the missing check against a source outside the model. Repeating the question (option 3) still asks the same system, and a vendor tool (option 2) still hallucinated at 17% to 33% in the Stanford study.

Lexis+ AI hallucinated on 17% of queries in the Stanford study. About how likely is it that a batch of ten independent answers from a tool with that rate contains at least one hallucination?

  1. About 17%
  2. About 50%
  3. About 66%
  4. About 84%
The chance all ten are clean is 0.8310, about 16%, so at least one bad answer appears about 84% of the time. Option 1 confuses the per-answer rate with the per-batch rate, which is exactly why skimming a batch fails.

You review 59 outputs of a contract summariser and find no errors. What can you say?

  1. The true error rate is probably below about 5%, enough to justify sampling for internal drafts but not for filings
  2. The true error rate is zero
  3. The tool is accurate on all documents the firm will ever process
  4. Nothing, because a sample cannot say anything about a population
Zero errors in 59 gives an exact 95% upper bound of about 5%. It does not prove zero errors (option 2) and applies only to documents like the ones sampled (option 3). A sample does support a bounded claim (option 4 is wrong), which is the logic behind the ABA's example of testing a tool on a subset.

What did the English Divisional Court say about firm leaders in Ayinde?

  1. Individual lawyers alone are responsible, and leaders have no duty
  2. Issuing guidance is enough to discharge leaders' duties
  3. Leaders such as managing partners must take practical and effective measures, and guidance alone is insufficient
  4. Firms should ban generative AI entirely
As reported from the judgment, the court said that those with leadership responsibilities must take practical and effective measures, that guidance alone is insufficient, and that it can be expected to ask whether they did. It did not ban AI use; it said AI use needs appropriate oversight.
